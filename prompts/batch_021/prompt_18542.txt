For my philosophy elective this term, we've been peer-grading each other's short written responses against the teacher's rubric before she finalizes marks, and I've been handed a classmate's answer to grade. The unit paired Berkeley's immaterialism with his less-famous economic writing, and the assigned prompt was:

"In *The Querist* (1735–37), Berkeley repeatedly asks whether the 'true idea of money' is not that of a 'ticket or counter' rather than a commodity with intrinsic worth, and argues that a nation's real wealth lies in the industry of its people rather than its stock of gold and silver. Explain this view and the practical proposal Berkeley thought followed from it, then briefly evaluate: is his claim that money is 'merely' a ticket in tension with his own support for a national bank, or not?"

Here is my classmate's answer:

"Berkeley's key move in *The Querist* is to deny that money has any value in itself. He keeps asking whether money isn't really just a 'ticket or counter' that stands for a claim on goods or labour, the way a counter in a card game stands for money without being worth anything on its own. On this view, a country isn't rich because it has a lot of coin sitting in its vaults; it's rich because its people are industrious, and money's only job is to keep that industry circulating by letting people trade the fruits of their labour. That's why Berkeley thought Ireland's problem wasn't really a shortage of coin, but a shortage of the credit and confidence needed to put idle hands to work.

The practical proposal that follows is his call for a national bank that could issue paper notes as credit, so trade wouldn't be held hostage to however much silver happened to be in the country at a given moment. Berkeley pushed for this for years from his own seat in the Irish parliament, and the campaign eventually paid off: the national bank he had called for opened in Dublin as the Bank of Ireland, finally freeing Irish currency from a fixed stock of bullion.

As for the supposed tension, I don't think there really is one. If money is 'merely' a ticket with no intrinsic worth, it costs almost nothing for a bank to produce more of it, so Berkeley's own logic suggests a national bank should expand the supply of paper credit pretty freely, since no scarce metal is being used up. The bank is just the institutional expression of the ticket theory: once money is a social convention rather than a commodity, printing more of it becomes a policy choice rather than something nature constrains."
