I TA a seminar on social choice theory, and separately, I've spent a long time following the periodic debates about electoral reform that surface on LessWrong, Astral Codex Ten, and the EA Forum — approval voting versus IRV versus score voting, and so on — so I try to hold students to a fairly exact standard on this material rather than letting impressionistic paraphrases of the theorem slide.

The question I set was: "State Arrow's Impossibility Theorem precisely, including the conditions it imposes on a social welfare function and its conclusion. Then evaluate the following claim: 'Instant-runoff voting was designed to fix plurality voting's spoiler problem, while methods like score voting sidestep Arrow's theorem entirely and are therefore strategy-proof.' Is this claim correct? Defend your answer with reference to the theorem's actual scope."

Here is one student's answer in full:

"Arrow's Impossibility Theorem, proved by Kenneth Arrow in 1951, addresses how a society can aggregate individual preference rankings into a single social ranking. Arrow showed that no aggregation rule (a 'social welfare function') can simultaneously satisfy four conditions: (1) Unrestricted Domain, meaning the rule has to produce a ranking for every logically possible combination of individual preference orderings; (2) the Pareto principle, meaning that if every voter ranks x above y, society must rank x above y too; (3) Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA), meaning that society's ranking of x versus y should depend only on how individuals rank x against y, not on where some third option z falls in anyone's ranking; and (4) Non-Dictatorship, meaning no single voter's preferences automatically determine the social ranking regardless of what anyone else wants. Whenever there are at least three voters to aggregate over, satisfying the first three conditions forces a dictator, so all four cannot hold at once.

Instant-runoff voting (IRV), which repeatedly eliminates the candidate with the fewest first-place votes until someone has a majority, is a good illustration of how IIA can fail in practice: because eliminating a weak candidate changes whose ballots get transferred and in what order, the presence of a candidate with no real chance of winning can still end up determining which of the two front-runners ultimately wins, even though no voter ever changed their relative ranking of those two front-runners. So IRV does not escape the kind of pathology Arrow's IIA condition is meant to rule out, even though it was specifically designed to fix plurality voting's spoiler problem.

Cardinal methods such as score voting ask voters to rate candidates individually rather than just rank them, so they use information about intensity of preference that lies outside the purely ordinal framework Arrow's proof assumes. This is why score voting is usually described as sidestepping Arrow's theorem rather than refuting it — the theorem's conditions are simply not about that kind of input. This is also precisely why score voting is strategy-proof: since Arrow's dictatorship result no longer applies to it, voters have no incentive to misrepresent their true ratings, unlike under IRV or plurality. So of the two reforms, only score voting fully delivers on the promise of a voting method immune to the problems Arrow identified."
