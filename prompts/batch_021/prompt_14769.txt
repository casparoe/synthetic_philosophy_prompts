Two of my coworkers -- whose actual names happen to be Alice and Bob, a coincidence that did not go unremarked on by the rest of the team -- got into a drawn-out disagreement that started at a sprint retrospective and continued in Slack for most of the afternoon. Ahead of a database migration, each had privately estimated the probability that it would finish without a customer-visible incident by Friday; afterward, they spent close to an hour walking each other through their full reasoning, and while their estimates moved closer together, they never fully converged. I don't have any background in decision theory or philosophy, so I looked up the bare statement of Aumann's agreement theorem afterward just to make sure I'd followed the argument correctly, but I'd still like a more careful outside read on the whole exchange. Below is roughly how it went.

1. Alice: We've now spent an hour comparing every piece of evidence we each have. Aumann's theorem says that if we're both rational Bayesians and we each know the other's number, we're stuck agreeing. We don't agree, so one of us isn't being rational.

2. Bob: That's not what the theorem needs. It requires a common prior -- we'd have to have started from the same distribution over outcomes before any evidence about this specific migration came in. I've been burned by three migrations like this one; you've been burned by none. That difference isn't something you can hand me in an hour of talking -- it's baked into how skeptical I am of any migration going smoothly, full stop.

3. Alice: A prior is supposed to represent what you'd believe before seeing evidence, not a standing grudge. If your low number is really just scar tissue from unrelated migrations, then once we've both looked at this migration's actual evidence, a genuinely rational updater should land in roughly the same place no matter where they started. Otherwise "my prior" becomes an excuse for never changing your mind.

4. Bob: Some priors don't wash out that fast, and that's not irrational -- it's just what happens when a prior is concentrated rather than spread out. You're also skipping the theorem's harder requirement: it needs our posteriors to be common knowledge, meaning I know your number, you know mine, I know that you know mine, and so on without end. We've exchanged numbers, sure, but I don't fully believe you're not anchoring on the deadline, and you probably have the same doubt about me. That kind of mutual trust doesn't get built over Slack in an afternoon.

5. Alice: Fine, but then you don't get to invoke the theorem selectively. You can't say "we lack common knowledge" to wave away tonight's gap while still telling the team, the next time our estimates diverge, that this is just how forecasting goes. Either the theorem has teeth for cases like ours or it doesn't.

6. Bob: It has some teeth, just not the ones you first reached for. You moved from 90 to 75, I moved from 45 to 60 -- honestly comparing notes did pull us together. Still ending up fifteen points apart doesn't prove either of us is irrational; it's more likely a sign that one of our priors is quietly tracking something other than this migration -- your general optimism about migrations, or my last postmortem -- and neither of us has actually surfaced that.
