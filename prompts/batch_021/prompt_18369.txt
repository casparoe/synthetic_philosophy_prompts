I don't have any formal training in philosophy or mathematics, but a friend got me interested in decision theory a while back by showing me Newcomb's Problem, and I've spent a fair amount of my free time since then reading about it wherever I can, since an actual course isn't really an option for me right now. Somewhere along the way I came across the old fatalism story about a servant who runs into Death in a crowded market, sees him give a startled gesture, takes it as a threat, and rides through the night to a distant city to escape him — only for his master to later run into Death himself and ask why he frightened the poor servant like that. Death explains that the gesture wasn't a threat at all, just surprise at seeing the servant still in that market, since Death already had an appointment to meet him that very night in the city the servant was about to flee to.

What took me longer to find is that decision theorists picked up that same story, renamed it "Death in Damascus," and turned it into something considerably stranger than a lesson about not being able to outrun fate. In their version, Death isn't fate in some mystical sense; he's an almost perfectly reliable predictor who simply goes to wherever he predicts you'll be. The genuinely strange part, from what I've pieced together, is that whichever city you try to talk yourself into fleeing to, the fact that you're becoming convinced looks exactly like the evidence Death would have used to predict it. That makes the other city look better instead, which would itself have been predicted, and so on, so that there's no plan you can settle on that still seems like the right one once you fully account for the fact that you're the kind of person who'd settle on it. People bring this up to argue that ordinary rational-choice reasoning doesn't always deliver a stable answer, in a way that even Newcomb's Problem, for all its controversy, arguably still does. Every version of "Death in Damascus" I can find online just retells the original fable with the place names changed and skips this part entirely.

I'd like to see that second, stranger version actually brought to life instead of explained, so could you write me a short story, maybe a page or two, built around it? I'd like the main character to be someone who thinks explicitly in these terms — an actuary, a professional gambler, someone who lives on prediction markets, whatever seems most natural to you — who works through the logic in real time and can't settle on a decision that still looks right the moment they realize they're about to make it. Please don't let them escape through a trick ending, a lucky break, or a Death who turns out to be merciful — I'd like the ending to genuinely honor that impasse rather than quietly resolve it. Beyond that, the setting, era, and voice are entirely up to you — it doesn't have to be anywhere near the ancient Middle East — as long as the bind driving the plot is this specific one, and not just the simpler idea that fate can't be dodged.
