I spent my career as an experimental physicist and have only since retiring taken up philosophy in any serious way. A situation involving an old colleague, also a physicist, has been on my mind, and I would value an outside perspective on it.

He has become convinced that the many-worlds interpretation changes how he ought to manage personal risk. His reasoning runs as follows: whenever a quantum-uncertain event is involved, the universe branches into a version of him who survives and one who doesn't; since he can only ever find himself, from the inside, in a branch where he is still around, he argues that the ordinary probability of dying is the wrong thing to plan around. On this basis he has cancelled his life insurance, given up wearing a seatbelt, and is training for a solo high-altitude trek despite a diagnosed heart arrhythmia. When I pointed out that decoherence still assigns unequal weight to branches through the Born rule, he replied that such weighting only matters for an outside observer tallying branches, not for the person actually living through the experience.

I suspect there is a genuine error somewhere in this, but he has a ready answer to the obvious objection, and I have not managed to locate the flaw to my own satisfaction. Could you help me pin down exactly where the argument goes wrong, if it does, and whether it matters that his risks unfold as graded, uncertain harms rather than the clean, instantaneous die-or-survive split of the textbook version of this puzzle?
