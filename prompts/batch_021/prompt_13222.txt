Random research tangent that's eaten my whole week: I'm drafting a novel with a subplot set among freethinkers in 1840s Coventry, so I've fallen back down the George Eliot rabbit hole — the evangelical childhood, the "holy war" with her father after she fell in with Charles Bray's circle and stopped going to church, the anonymous Strauss translation, then Feuerbach's The Essence of Christianity a few years later (the one book she ever published under her own name, weirdly enough). What strikes me this time through is that she didn't land on plain atheism so much as this very specific, Comte-inflected humanism — all the moral seriousness of her evangelical years, just transplanted onto humanity itself. You can hear it in "O May I Join the Choir Invisible," and in that anecdote — Myers records it, I think from a walk through a Cambridge garden — where she takes "God, Immortality, Duty" as her subject and pronounces the first inconceivable, the second unbelievable, and the third still utterly binding.

So here's my actual question. If Eliot somehow read The God Delusion, or wandered into a Sunday Assembly meeting, or picked up Alain de Botton's Religion for Atheists, what do you reckon her actual reaction would be? I don't want the easy "she was a skeptic, so she'd obviously side with other skeptics" answer — actually reason it out from her specific intellectual formation. Would the New Atheist temperament (religion as basically a mind-virus, nothing worth keeping once you've refuted the metaphysics) grate against everything her own path taught her about why religion mattered even after you'd stopped believing it was literally true? Or would she see something like de Botton's project as her own Positivist instincts finally catching up with the wider culture, a century and a half late? Get properly into the weeds of her actual religious biography for this rather than just the novels — its the underlying philosophical throughline I keep losing hold of.
