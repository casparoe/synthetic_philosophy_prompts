I'm at work on a novel structured as a series of imagined encounters between historical philosophers and the intellectual problems of the present, in the tradition of the philosophical dialogue but staged against contemporary rather than classical concerns. The chapter I'm drafting now puts Descartes across the table from a present-day AI safety researcher, who wants him to grapple with what researchers in that field call the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents. The obstacle, put plainly, is this: Löb's theorem shows that no sufficiently powerful, consistent formal system can license the rule "if I can prove P, then P is true," for an arbitrary proposition P, without already being able to prove P outright — the trust one might hope to buy cheaply for a future or successor version of oneself turns out to be unpurchasable except at the price of already knowing the answer. This is why, as researchers at places like MIRI have discussed under headings like the "tiling agents" problem and "Vingean reflection," an AI cannot straightforwardly certify in advance that a successor system, reasoning in the very same formal framework, will only ever endorse true or safe conclusions.

A friend who works in AI safety first pointed out to me, over dinner, that this sounded to her like a formalized version of the so-called Cartesian Circle: Arnauld's charge, in the Fourth Objections to the Meditations, that Descartes needs a clear and distinct perception — the proof that God exists and is no deceiver — to underwrite the general reliability of clear and distinct perception as such, including the very perceptions that produced that proof. I haven't been able to decide whether the resemblance runs deeper than a pun on the word "trust," and I'd like your help thinking it through carefully enough that I can put a defensible version of the reasoning in Descartes' own mouth, rather than a caricature of him.

So: would Descartes, presented with Löb's theorem and its role in AI safety, recognize it as a formal sharpening of Arnauld's objection — perhaps even as vindicating a worry his own Replies never fully laid to rest? Or do his actual resources — in particular, his distinction between the immediate, attention-bound certainty available while a clear and distinct perception is actually being entertained, and the more mediated certainty required for propositions recalled from memory or reasoned about only in general terms — give him grounds to think the two problems are only superficially alike? And when, in the scene, the researcher asks him directly what an AI's designer should do about getting a system to trust its own future reasoning, what would he actually say, given everything else he held about method, doubt, and the natural light of reason? I'd be grateful if you could be precise about where the analogy is doing real philosophical work and where it may simply be overstated.
