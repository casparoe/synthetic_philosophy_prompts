I'm teaching Principia Ethica this week and we got a bit stuck on the thought experiment in the chapter on "The Ideal" -- section 50 or thereabouts, don't quote me on the exact number -- where Moore asks you to imagine two worlds, one unbelievably beautiful and one just a disgusting heap of filth, and stipulates that no one, human or otherwise, will ever perceive either one, not now and not in the future. He then says we have to admit the beautiful world is better then the ugly one, even though by hypothesis this makes zero difference to anyone's actual experience -- which he's using as a club against hedonism. My class immediately split into two camps: half found this totally convincing, the other half said the whole thing is a cheat, because you cant actually picture "an unobserved beautiful world" without smuggling in an imagined observer (namely you, right now, enjoying picturing it), so the intuition is really just tracking your own pleasure in contemplating it rather than some experience-independent value out there in the world.

Could you write me a dialogue between these two camps where they actually engage with each other rather than just restating their opening positions? I want the Moore-defender to have a real answer to the "smuggled observer" objection instead of just re-asserting the intuition louder, and I want the hedonist to seriously grapple with Moore's organic unities idea -- e.g. that correctly admiring a beautiful thing is worth more than the value of the object and the value of the admiring just added together -- since Moore leans on that same idea a few pages later to argue that personal affection is one of the two greatest goods there are. Both sides should be argued by someone who really knows the material and is pushing as hard as they can -- I don't want either one to be a pushover.
