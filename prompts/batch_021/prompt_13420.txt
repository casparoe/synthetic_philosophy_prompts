I'm a PhD student in philosophy (my own dissertation is in ethics, but I spent a formative first year in a seminar built around David Lewis) and I've been asked to give the toast at my older sister's wedding next month. The set-up is a little unusual: my sister is a game theorist and her fiancé is a philosopher of language, and the two of them met and started dating in a graduate seminar on Lewis's Convention. Ever since, they've had a running bit where they describe their own relationship in the book's terms, half as a joke and half, I suspect, sincerely. Naturally, a good portion of the wedding guests will be their academic colleagues, who will catch every reference; the rest is our extended family, who have never heard the phrase "coordination equilibrium" and would be a little alarmed to hear their niece's marriage described as "arbitrary."

That word is exactly my difficulty. A Lewisian convention is a regularity that a population conforms to, expects everyone else to conform to, and prefers to keep conforming to given that others do, in a recurring situation where some other regularity would have done just as well. What fixes on this particular regularity rather than another is not some deeper fact about the two of them being right for each other, but precedent, common knowledge, and simply its being the one they actually settled into and kept renewing. That really is the vocabulary my sister and her fiancé use about themselves, and I don't want to flatten it into the usual toast-talk of soulmates and people being "meant for" each other — language their own theory rules out, and language they would (fondly) heckle me for reaching for. At the same time, I don't want the toast to read like a seminar with a sentimental postscript stapled on: I want the coordination-and-common-knowledge picture itself to be the reason the toast is moving, arbitrariness and all, rather than something to get through before the real sentiment starts.

Could you write the toast for me? It should run about four to five minutes read aloud, work as a genuine toast for relatives who will never open Convention, reward the ones who have, and land on at least one moment built out of the theory's own vocabulary that both halves of the room can enjoy without either side feeling like the joke was really meant for someone else.
