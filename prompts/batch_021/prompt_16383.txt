So this came up at an ACX meetup -- we were nominally there to discuss Harsanyi's aggregation theorem -- and it spun off into a side-argument between two people that I can't adjudicate myself.

The case that kicked it off: a hospital has exactly one kidney and two patients who are stipulated to be identical in every ethically relevant respect. The hospital flips a coin.

1. Mara said the coin flip is theater. Run the actual math Harsanyi's theorem gives you -- social utility as a fixed weighted sum of individual expected utilities -- and the coin flip comes out exactly as good, in expectation, as just handing the kidney to patient A outright. So there's no real moral difference, only a psychological one for whoever looses.

2. Dan said that can't be the whole story: basically every institution that faces this exact problem (transplant lists, draft lotteries, oversubscribed school places) randomizes precisely because arbitrary assignment feels wrong even when expected utility is identical, and that's not superstition -- ex ante fairness is doing work an ex post utility tally can't see.

3. Mara said the real reason institutions randomize is to prevent corruption and keep the process legible -- remove any chance of bias, or of anyone ever finding out, and the intuition should evaporate too.

4. Dan said it doesn't evaporate: people still prefer a coin flip over an equally uncorruptible but non-random rule, like going by whoever's surname comes first alphabetically. So it can't just be an anti-corruption story.

5. Mara said fine, that just shows a raw taste for randomization, not a gap in Harsanyi's framework -- you can always fold "I value being decided by a coin" into the individual utility functions, and the theorem goes through exactly as before.

6. Dan said that move proves too much: if any counterexample can be neutralized by redefining the utilities after the fact, the theorem stops ruling anything out.

Neither of them budged, and honestly I can see both sides.
