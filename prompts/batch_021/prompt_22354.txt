I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have spent much of this past year working through Pascal's Pensées on my own, using an old public-domain translation I found online. In the section titled "Justice and the Reason of Effects," I came across a fragment that is not the famous line about justice without force being powerless and force without justice being tyrannical, but seems to circle the same territory from a different angle, and it is the one I keep returning to without being able to resolve it:

"Custom creates the whole of equity, for the simple reason that it is accepted. It is the mystical foundation of its authority; whoever carries it back to first principles destroys it. Nothing is so faulty as those laws which correct faults."

I cannot decide between two readings of it. On one, Pascal is saying that justice simply is whatever custom has settled on, with no deeper standard behind it at all. On the other, he still believes there is a real justice, one that human beings have lost the ability to reliably perceive, so that custom is only the best substitute fallen reason can manage, not a replacement for justice itself. The wording seems to me to support either reading, and I cannot settle the matter from the text alone. I would also value help with the middle sentence: why would tracing a custom back to its actual origin destroy its authority, rather than simply explaining it? Could you go through the fragment with me, sentence by sentence, and help me work out what Pascal is actually committing himself to?
