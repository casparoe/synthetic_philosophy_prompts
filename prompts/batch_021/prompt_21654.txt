I'm writing my IB Extended Essay in Biology, and although the paper is officially about evolutionary theory, it has turned into almost as much a philosophy-of-science project as a biology one. One issue keeps coming up in my research that most of my sources treat as settled but that still bothers me: Karl Popper's notoriously unstable position on whether the principle of natural selection is, in a phrase he came back to more than once, "almost tautological." In several works from the 1960s and 70s, Popper argued that if "fitness" just means whatever traits happen to correlate with actually surviving and reproducing, then "the fittest survive" collapses into something close to "those that survive, survive" — not a genuine empirical law at all — and he went so far as to describe the whole Darwinian framework as a "metaphysical research programme" rather than a proper scientific theory. What makes the story more interesting is that Popper later partly reversed himself, even using the word "recant" in his intellectual autobiography, and tried to argue that natural selection does make testable predictions after all.

A friend from my school's philosophy club insists this whole debate is a non-issue by now, mainly because philosophers of biology have since defined fitness independently of actual survival through what is sometimes called the "propensity interpretation of fitness" (I have seen it attributed to Susan Mills and John Beatty): on this view, fitness is a probabilistic disposition an organism has in a given environment, fixed in advance, rather than something read backward off of who happens to survive. I am not convinced this settles things, so please argue the position I have been defending in these debates: that Popper's original tautology worry was basically correct, and that neither his own partial recantation nor the propensity-based reply actually rescues "survival of the fittest" from being uncomfortably close to empty. If you can, I would also be curious whether the same worry resurfaces whenever a biologist explains some particular trait as an adaptation.
