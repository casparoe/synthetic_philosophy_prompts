A friend of mine and I spent most of last night's dinner arguing about consequentialist cluelessness, and neither of us gave any ground. She donates a fixed share of her income to interventions like bed-net distribution, and my objection was that she has no real basis for believing such donations are net positive once their effects are traced out over centuries rather than years: any act that shifts who is conceived, when, and with whom will, through ordinary chaotic amplification, eventually reshape the entire future population, so the good she thinks she's doing could easily be swamped by downstream effects whose very sign she cannot guess. Her reply was that this so-called cluelessness problem is no special defeater for consequentialism: since we have no more reason to expect those unforeseeable long-run effects to be bad than good, they cancel out in expectation, leaving her free to act on the foreseeable, intended effects alone. I don't buy that "no more reason to expect bad than good" is actually true here, rather than a convenient assumption needed to keep the theory usable. Settle this for us: write an essay that first distinguishes cluelessness of this systematic kind from garden-variety empirical uncertainty about outcomes, then critically weighs whether appeals to symmetry or cancellation-in-expectation genuinely license ignoring unforeseeable effects. Don't hedge -- end with a definite verdict on whether cluelessness is a decisive problem for consequentialism as a guide to action, a containable nuisance, or something in between, and if you think it is containable, say exactly what does the containing.
