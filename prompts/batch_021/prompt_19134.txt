I'm a retired analytical chemist, and for the last year or so I've been happily falling down a philosophy rabbit hole in retirement -- working through introductory metaphysics most evenings instead of, I dunno, taking up golf like a normal person. Lately I've been stuck on the literature about how to properly define "dispositional" properties -- solubility, fragility, that kind of thing, properties that only show themselves under certain conditions -- and all the clever counterexamples people have cooked up to break the obvious-looking definitions of them. It's apparently rotting my brain in a good way, because I've started re-litigating arguments I overhear at parties like they're homework problems.

Case in point: a barbecue at my neighbor Frank's place last month. A few years back an arborist looked at Frank's big old white oak and said it was structurally unsound -- likely to fail in a bad storm -- so instead of taking it down, Frank paid to have it cabled and braced (steel cables strung between the main limbs, basically holding things together if it starts to go). We had a nasty windstorm a few weeks back, the tree was totally fine, and Frank's wife Carol needled him about it at the barbecue: the arborist was clearly wrong, she said, the tree obviously isn't going anywhere, and he'd wasted his money. Frank said no -- the tree is exactly as unsound as the arborist found it; the cables are just doing the work of holding it up, and if you cut them tomorrow it'd come down in the next real storm. Carol answered that if the cables hold every single time, forever, there's no actual difference between "unsound-but-cabled" and just plain "sound" -- and besides, she'd read on a gardening forum once that trees mostly build up trunk and root strength by responding to the stress of actually swaying in real wind, so if the cables have been doing that job for the tree for seven years, its own wood and roots probably haven't developed the way they normally would've, meaning the tree might now be even more prone to falling on its own than when the arborist first looked at it -- not just quietly protected from some fixed, unchanging problem. Frank had a quick answer to the first half of that (an actually sound tree wouldn't care if a cable snapped, his tree would, so there's a real difference and the tendency is real even if it never shows up), but didn't have much of a comeback for the growth point, beyond muttering that it still didn't mean Carol was right back at the start.

I couldn't settle this on the spot and it's been bugging me since, because underneath a dumb yard argument there seems to be something with actual philosophical bones to it, not just two people talking past each other. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who's got the better of it at each stage, rather then just handing out an overall winner? I'm especially curious whether Carol's growth-and-roots point actually rescues or strengthens her very first claim, or whether its really doing separate work that just happens to embarrass Frank for a different reason -- and if you think there's something both of them are missing entirely, I'd love to hear that too.
