For a feature I'm writing on the intellectual history of "agent foundations" research in AI safety — the strand most associated with the Machine Intelligence Research Institute (MIRI) and the broader rationalist AI-safety scene of the early-to-mid 2010s — I keep running into the same conceptual sticking point, which insiders call the Löbian obstacle. As I understand it, the problem follows from Löb's theorem: a proof-based agent reasoning inside a fixed formal system F can never write down, within F itself, a general warrant for trusting whatever a future or modified version of itself proves, even when that successor reasons inside the very same system. Instantiate the natural "whatever F proves is true" reflection schema at the sentence "false," and you get F asserting its own consistency, which Gödel's second incompleteness theorem forbids; full self-trust is therefore provably unattainable for any consistent proof-based agent, and there is no clean, general way inside classical logic to build (or become) something that reasons exactly as you do and still trust it going forward. I've read the original 2013 tiling-agents paper that coined the term, along with a handful of the MIRI technical reports that followed it, and every researcher I've interviewed for the piece seems to hold a different, strongly argued view about how much genuine progress has actually been made since then, as against the problem simply being redescribed in fancier terms.

I'd like your own independent, reasoned judgment on this, not a summary of what others think. Please rate each of the following four families of response to the Löbian obstacle on how fully it actually dissolves the underlying problem — an agent's inability to formally vouch, from inside its own system, for a successor that reasons exactly as it does — rather than merely relocating or softening it: (a) hierarchies of formally distinct proof systems, in which each level is strong enough to certify the soundness of a strictly weaker system below it, the move behind the original tiling-agents proposal; (b) logical induction, which drops binary provability for a convergent, market-like assignment of probabilities to logical sentences, including sentences about the system's own future beliefs; (c) probabilistic reflection principles and "reflective oracles," which let a system assign coherent probabilities to sentences referring to its own outputs by trading finitary proof for calibrated credence; and (d) pragmatic, bounded responses — such as proof-length discounting or Vingean reflection on a smarter successor's likely-correct conclusions — that treat irreducible, partial self-trust as a fact about real agents rather than an obstacle to be engineered away.
