So I've fallen down a bit of a rabbit hole reading old rationalist-community stuff (LessWrong, the Center on Long-Term Risk crowd, that general orbit) for a grad seminar on rational agency and threats I'm putting together, and there's one move in the acausal-blackmail literature I can't fully make sense of: the "surrogate goal" trick. As I understand it, an agent worried about being acausally threatened -- by predictors, distant superintelligences, whatever agent is able to model it well enough to make a credible threat with no causal contact at all -- can protect its actual values by manufacturing an extra goal it doesn't really care about, and then behaving as though it cares about that fake goal a great deal. The hope is that a rational threatener goes after the decoy instead of the real thing, since the decoy is the only lever that actually gets a response out of the agent.

What I keep getting stuck on is why this doesnt just unravel the moment a threatener is sophisticated enough to have modeled the agent that precisely in the first place. If it can predict the agent well enough to threaten it acausally, why can't it also tell the surrogate goal is fake and go straight for the real target? And theres a regress worry on top of that: if I can just gin up an extra "goal" out of nothing to absorb threats, doesn't that suggest my supposedly ironclad refusal to ever negotiate over my real goals might also be softer than advertised, in a way a sufficiently good adversary could exploit? Could you walk me through the actual game-theoretic mechanics of why surrogate goals are supposed to work as a defense here, ideally with a toy example where the incentives are spelled out? The one-paragraph gloss you get in blog posts is about as far as I've gotten on my own, and it's not cutting it.
