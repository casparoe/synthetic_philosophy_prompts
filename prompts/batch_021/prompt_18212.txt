Last night a friend and I got into a long, slightly heated argument over dinner that I still haven't been able to let go of. It started when I brought up a finding from perceptual psychology, associated with Dennis Proffitt's research program, that a hill looks and feels noticeably steeper to someone who is fatigued, elderly, unfit, or carrying a heavy backpack than to someone who is rested — the idea being that visual perception of one's surroundings is calibrated to one's capacity to act on them. I took this as a striking case of cognitive penetration: one's bodily state reaching in and altering the character of visual experience itself, rather than merely one's later judgment about that experience. My friend, who has some background in vision science, pushed back hard, insisting that this case, like most of the findings in that research program, is better explained by demand characteristics or response bias: subjects give a different number when asked to estimate the slant, she said, but show no corresponding difference on tasks that bypass conscious report, such as visually guided action, which suggests that nothing about the perceptual experience itself has actually changed.

We went back and forth for over an hour without ever resolving the more general question sitting underneath our specific disagreement: what, in principle, would distinguish a genuine case of belief, desire, or bodily state penetrating the actual content of perceptual experience from a merely apparent one that is really just an effect on attention, on perceptual learning, or on the judgment or reporting stage that comes after perception is already complete? Neither the hill-and-backpack case nor the handful of standard philosophical examples we tried to recall (the angry-face case, the wine-connoisseur case, and so on) settled the matter for us, since each one seemed redescribable either way depending on how much charity one extended to the skeptic.

So here is what I would like from you, since neither of us could produce it on the spot: construct an original hypothetical scenario — not the hill-and-backpack case, and not one of the standard textbook examples — that would count as a clean, relatively unambiguous instance of genuine cognitive penetration of perceptual experience. Please then explain explicitly which features of your scenario do the work of ruling out the three deflationary alternatives just mentioned (mere reallocation of attention, mere perceptual learning or differentiation, and mere post-perceptual judgment or response bias), since that is exactly the point at which my friend and I kept talking past one another.
