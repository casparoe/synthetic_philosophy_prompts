I have spent a good deal of time with Simone Weil's short essay "On the Abolition of All Political Parties," and the longer I sit with it, the less convinced I am that she is wrong. Her claim is not merely that parties tend to be corrupt or poorly run, but that any party whatsoever, simply by virtue of its structure, does three things: it manufactures a collective passion around a doctrine, it exerts a pressure on its members that makes independent judgment about what is true or just extremely difficult, and it ends up treating its own growth and survival as its real objective, whatever cause it was originally founded to serve. On this basis she concludes that a healthy democracy would be better off with no political parties at all, only individuals and their representatives reasoning things out on the merits, case by case.

I would like you to write a dialogue between two people equally well-versed in this debate: one who has come to accept Weil's argument and defends the abolition of parties, and one who grants much of her diagnosis but thinks that organized parties are nonetheless indispensable to democratic life, and that her proposal would leave us worse off rather than better. Neither should have a ready answer to everything the other raises; I want real engagement, including some account of where the "collective passion" Weil worries about is likely to go if it is not channeled through parties, and how a convinced Weilian would respond to that.
