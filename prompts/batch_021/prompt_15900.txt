So in my epistemology reading group I made an offhand joke that Lob's theorem basically finishes off Descartes, and now my advisor's making me defend it in a term paper section. Here's the thought: in Meditation 5, Descartes admits that once he stops actively attending to a clear and distinct perception, doubt can creep back in, so he needs a general rule -- "whatever I clearly and distinctly perceive is true" -- validated via the non-deceiving God, so he can trust his own past and future judgments without re-deriving them every time. Arnauld's circularity charge is the classic complaint about that move, but Lob's theorem seems to be making a sharper point than just "circular": a consistent system can't prove that general a reflection schema about its own outputs unless it already proves the specific claim in question, on pain of inconsistency -- meaning no bootstrapped route to that kind of self-trust could ever have worked, not just Descartes's route through God specifically. Can you build that argument out carefully, and handle the obvious objection that Descartes is doing informal, psychological epistemology rather than formal provability, so maybe Lob's theorem just doesn't transfer to his case at all?
