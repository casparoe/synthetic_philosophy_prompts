Address: (a) whether each thinker treats personhood as something given at birth or something achieved over time; (b) how Kwame Gyekye's notion of 'moderate communitarianism' responds to Menkiti's position; and (c) one implication of this debate for the moral status of infants or of strangers who stand outside the community."

"Mbiti's slogan reverses Descartes: instead of grounding identity in individual thought, it grounds identity in the community, so that the 'I' only exists because it is embedded in a web of relationships with the living, the dead, and the yet-to-be-born. Menkiti pushes this further into a normative claim: for him, personhood is not a metaphysical given that comes automatically with being born human, but a status acquired gradually through moral maturation and participation in communal life, which is why he can say that children possess personhood only incompletely while elders who have lived well possess it more fully. Gyekye, worried that this makes the individual nothing more than a product stamped out by the community, keeps Menkiti's picture of personhood as something gradually achieved through communal life, but insists that even the newest members of the community arrive already equipped with certain inborn rights that no amount of communal shaping can revoke; moderate communitarianism, on this view, is essentially Menkiti's theory of personhood plus a guaranteed floor of individual rights. One consequence of the Mbiti/Menkiti view is that a newborn, or a stranger with no ties to the community, would seem to have a diminished moral status, which is presumably why critics have pushed communitarian African philosophers to clarify how far this logic is meant to extend."
