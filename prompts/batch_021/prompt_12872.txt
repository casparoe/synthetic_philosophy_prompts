I'm writing a historical novel set in Edinburgh in the early 1750s in which David Hume appears as a secondary character, and two pivotal scenes turn on his running argument with a skeptical merchant, Fenwick, about money and trade. In the first, Hume tells Fenwick that his hoarding of gold and his worry over the balance of trade rest on a confusion: money, he says, is "none of the wheels of trade" — merely the oil that eases the wheels' motion without adding any force of its own — so that once prices catch up, a kingdom with twice the gold is no richer, only differently reckoned. Some months later, after a windfall of Spanish silver reaches the port, the same Hume tells a delighted banker that the influx will be a genuine boon: merchants will hire more freely, workmen will labor with new alacrity, and the whole country will enjoy a spell of heightened industry before prices rise far enough to absorb it.

Two friends who read the draft, Marta and Owen, fell into an argument at dinner over whether this makes Hume look like a coherent thinker or one caught contradicting himself, and neither would give ground.

Marta said the two scenes cannot depict the same mind: if money is truly oil rather than a wheel, a silver windfall cannot be a genuine tonic for industry, since the whole point of calling something a lubricant is that it supplies no motive force of its own. Owen replied that this confuses two timeframes any economist would distinguish: money's quantity is irrelevant once wages and prices have fully adjusted, which is the state Hume describes to Fenwick, but during the adjustment itself, the state he describes to the banker, an increase can quicken real activity without ceasing to be, in the end, a veil.

Marta granted the distinction but said it costs Owen more than he admits: if the benefit lives only in the interval before prices catch up, the sole way to keep it is to keep the money supply rising forever, so the economy is always "during" and never "after" — which is just Fenwick's own policy of endless accumulation, dressed up. Owen answered that this conflates Fenwick's actual programme, hoarding specie and restricting imports to protect a surplus, with the narrower, non-mercantilist recommendation that a magistrate should prefer a money stock that grows steadily rather than one that is simply large; the second view forbids the trade restrictions Fenwick wants even as it welcomes the windfall.

Marta then raised a subtler point: the interval only helps because merchants and workmen are slow to notice that their money buys less than before, and keep hiring and laboring at close to the old rates until prices overtake them. But this is the same Hume who tells his students, elsewhere in the book, that all expectation is custom worn into us by repetition, never reasoned out in advance — so a population freshly settled into one price level should be especially slow to revise its guesses upward, which is a consequence of Hume's own psychology, not a lapse in it. Owen conceded the point but noted that custom cuts both ways: the same habituation that keeps a baker from raising his prices should equally keep a workman from suddenly laboring longer or a farmer from suddenly planting more, since extra effort is as much a departure from custom as a new price would be — and nothing in "custom" alone explains why habit should grip one kind of behavior while releasing the other.
