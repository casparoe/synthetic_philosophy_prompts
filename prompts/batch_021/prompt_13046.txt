I spent about thirty years running an analytical chemistry lab before retiring, and if you'd asked me back then what my "philosophy" was, I'd have said something like: never believe more than the data licenses, and when the evidence is thin, don't make up your mind yet. Turns out that's more or less W.K. Clifford's whole position, which I only just discovered actually has a name and a source -- I read "The Ethics of Belief" a couple weeks back, including that opening story about the shipowner who talks himself into a comfortable belief that his rickety emigrant ship is sound, sends it off anyway, and is guilty of the drownings even in the possible world where it happens to limp home safe, because the crime was in how carelessly he formed the belief, not in how things turned out. Felt pretty airtight to me. Then I read James's reply, "The Will to Believe," and I've read alot about it since, but I'm less sure of anything than when I started. He's got this whole apparatus of "forced," "living" and "momentous" options, and says that when a real ("genuine," his word) option of that kind can't be settled either way by the evidence, our "passional nature" gets to cast the deciding vote -- and he's even got a nice example of a guy stuck on a mountainside who has to leap a gap, where believing he'll make it actually helps him make it, while hesitating and doubting dooms him. What I can't tell is whether James found a genuine hole in Clifford's net, or just picked one hard case and is smuggling a much bigger permission slip in behind it.

Could you write me a dialogue that really puts the two positions through their paces? Set it as two hikers stuck at exactly that kind of gap in the rock: one of them has to jump and has talked himself into believing he'll clear it, the other is a committed Cliffordian who thinks that's just wishful thinking with fancier vocabulary and is trying to talk him out of jumping until he has better grounds. I want the Cliffordian to press on whether "forced/living/momentous" is doing real philosophical work or is just loose enough to rubber-stamp whatever the believer already wants to do, and to bring back something like the shipowner case as a test of the Jamesian's principle. And I want the Jamesian to have an actual answer to that, not just restate the theory -- plus get a turn on offense, by pushing on whether Clifford's own rule (always proportion belief to the evidence) can itself be justified by evidence, or whether it's a passional commitment quietly posing as a neutral, evidence-only one. Have them genuinely respond to each other's points rather than talk past one another, and don't let either one be the obvious strawman -- I'd like to end up about as torn as I am now.
