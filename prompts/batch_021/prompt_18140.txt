I write about science and technology for a magazine, and I've spent the past few weeks reporting on a trend I keep running into: platforms quietly replacing small teams of professional fact-checkers with large volunteer panels that vote on whether a given post is misleading. In one interview, a data scientist who helped design such a system told me that even though any single volunteer rater is only a hair better than a coin flip on ambiguous claims, say, right about 51 percent of the time, this stops mattering once you pool enough of them: with several hundred thousand raters weighing in, she said, the majority verdict becomes "close to mathematically guaranteed" to be correct, because individual errors simply wash out. It struck me as almost too convenient, though I couldn't immediately say what was wrong with it. When I put this to a researcher who studies online misinformation, she was much more skeptical, arguing that the identical reasoning could just as easily "prove" that a large enough crowd will converge on a confidently wrong verdict, and that the data scientist's confidence quietly depends on some assumption about the raters that she doubted any real online crowd actually satisfies.

Before I write this up, I'd like help figuring out who actually has the better argument. Is the data scientist's underlying math sound as far as it goes, and if so, what exactly would need to be true of the raters, beyond simply having a lot of them, for her confidence to be justified? Is the researcher's objection a precise, checkable point, or just a vaguer "crowds can go wrong" intuition dressed up in technical language? And purely so I understand the mechanics: what should happen to the platform's confidence in its own verdicts if raters turned out to be right less than half the time, rather than more, on some especially deceptive category of claims? I'd rather get the actual logic right than fall back on a "wisdom of crowds, it's complicated" line in the piece.
