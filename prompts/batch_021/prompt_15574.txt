And here, verbatim, is the student's answer:

"Van Fraassen's Reflection Principle says that an agent's current credence in a proposition A should equal whatever credence they expect their future self to assign to A, conditional on that future credence: formally, Cr_t(A | Cr_t'(A) = x) = x, for any t < t'. Intuitively, you should treat your future, presumably more informed, self as an expert on A: if you knew in advance exactly what you were going to believe tomorrow, you should just believe that now, conditional on that information.

Van Fraassen supports the principle with a diachronic Dutch book argument: if your current credence conditional on your anticipated future credence diverges from that future credence, a bookie can sell you a combination of bets today and tomorrow that guarantees you a loss regardless of how the world turns out. Since this holds for any agent whose conditional credences fail to track their own anticipated future credences, rationality requires Reflection to hold, in just the way that avoiding a synchronic Dutch book requires your credences at a single time to satisfy the probability axioms. In effect, Reflection is just conditionalization applied to the special case where your evidence at t' is your own future credence: since a rational agent updates by conditionalizing on whatever they learn, and learning your own future credence is itself a piece of evidence, Reflection follows immediately, and the two principles stand or fall together.

The most influential objection to Reflection involves cases of anticipated memory loss or belief change that isn't driven by evidence at all. Suppose I know that tomorrow, because of a memory-wiping drug, I will become irrationally certain that A is true regardless of the facts. Reflection would then require me to already be certain of A today, which seems clearly wrong: I have no reason to defer to a future self whose confidence is due to a drug rather than to evidence. Cases like this show that Reflection is only plausible once we assume our future credences will themselves be formed rationally -- which is exactly the sort of assumption Rawls has in mind when he talks about beliefs settling into a reflective equilibrium over time."
