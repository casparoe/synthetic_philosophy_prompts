At work I've been put in charge of designing the scoring system we'll use to evaluate our analysts' probabilistic forecasts, which sent me down a rabbit hole into the accuracy-first epistemology literature — Joyce's original argument, the Predd et al. result on coherence and proper scoring rules, and Pettigrew's book. All of it insists that whatever rule is used to measure a credence's inaccuracy must be strictly proper, or the argument from "minimize expected inaccuracy" to "credences should obey the probability axioms" falls apart, but I don't yet have a real feel for why this property is so load-bearing. Concretely: if I scored forecasts using the plain absolute difference between the stated probability and the eventual outcome (0 or 1), instead of something like the Brier score, what exactly would break — both in the incentives this creates for forecasters and in the philosophical argument for probabilism that's supposed to rest on accuracy-dominance? I'd like an explanation that builds up the intuition rather than one that just restates the proof.
