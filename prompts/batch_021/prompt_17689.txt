So this has been bugging me for weeks now and I cant decide if it's an actual unsolved problem or if I'm just missing something obvious. I'm a backend engineer, and every sprint planning session our PM wants us to estimate new features by looking at how long "similar" past projects took, instead of just gut-feeling a number off the top of our heads (fair enough, we've been burned by that before). Problem is, "similar" is doing a ton of work in that sentence, and nobody on the team agrees on what it actually means. Do I average over literally every project the company has shipped in the last five years (tons of data points, but half of them have nothing to do with what I'm building)? Do I narrow it down to just the handful of projects that involved integrating a third-party API, like this one does (feels more relevant, but now I've got maybe three data points, and one of those blew up for reasons that had nothing to do with the integration part)? Or do I just go off my own personal track record on similar-ish tickets, which is an even smaller and probably biased sample? Depending on which bucket of past projects I use, I've literally watched the "same" feature get estimated anywhere from two weeks to four months, and everyone can argue their bucket is the sensible one. The weirdest part is that if you keep narrowing the bucket to make it more specific to this exact ticket, you eventually get down to a group of one -- this project, right here -- which seems to break the whole idea of averaging over past cases to begin with.

I brought this up with a friend who works in data science and she just said "that's what base rates are for, use whichever one's closest," which didnt really help since it just pushes the question back to "closest by what standard, exactly." So what I actually want to know is: is there some principled, non-arbitrary way to figure out which group of past projects is the right one to lean on here, or is this kind of estimating always going to have an irreducibly arbitrary step in it no matter how much historical data we pile up? And if there's genuinely no clean answer, is there at least a defensible way to narrow it down that I could bring back to my team, instead of everyone just eyeballing it and calling whatever number they like "data-driven"?
