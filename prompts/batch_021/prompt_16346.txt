I host a podcast on science and ideas, and next week's guest is a philosopher known for defending Kripke's "sceptical solution" to the rule-following paradox. His view is that there is no fact about whether a speaker means addition or some deviant "quus"-like function by a symbol, and that talk of meaning survives only because an individual's use is checked against the wider community's agreement. He tends to wave away objections by calling this "Humean humility, not meaning-nihilism," and I don't want him simply repeating that line on air. Draft eight to ten questions that push past it: at least one should press the worry that a solitary rule-follower — Crusoe on his island, or the last speaker of a dying language — becomes an impossible or indeterminate case on this account; one should ask whether the very underdetermination argument used against the individual doesn't apply equally to the community's own collective practice, since that practice is just as finite; and one should ask whether cashing out correctness as communal agreement preserves any genuine normative "ought" or just redescribes conformity as if it were one. For each of these, add a follow-up tied to the deflection he's most likely to offer. If it fits naturally, work in one question on whether language models, whose outputs are shaped entirely by aggregate communal usage, count as a vindication of the sceptical solution or as a fresh instance of the same paradox.
