Since retiring I've been reading a lot of philosophy for the first time in my life -- I'm a biologist, spent about three decades doing fieldwork on baboon dominance hierarchies before I hung up my binoculars -- and I've recently fallen down a Pascal rabbit hole. Got stuck on the Pensées fragment about justice and force (the one where he says, more or less, justice without force is powerless and force without justice is tyranny), and I wrote up some notes trying to connect it to my own field. Would love some honest comments before I show this to people who actually trained in philosophy.

Here's the gist: Pascal's move isn't just cynical Realpolitik, I think -- it starts from an epistemic claim, that justice is "sujette à dispute" (contested -- I think that's the actual phrase, my French is rusty) while force is plain for everyone to see and nobody argues about who's got it. So rather than making the just side strong, we've made the strong side count as just, and custom quietly launders that arrangement so it feels principled instead of arbitrary. He makes basically the same point elsewhere with the line about truth on one side of the Pyrenees being error on the other -- what's "lawful" flips at a mountain range, so justice is tracking jurisdiction, not principle.

What got me hooked, coming from watching actual baboon troops, is how much this sounds like a dominance heirarchy dressed up in moral language. The alpha isn't legitimate in any deeper sense, he's just whoever currently wins fights -- an ESS (evolutionarily stable strategy), more or less -- and then everyone's deference behaviour is what manufactures the appearance of a rightful order sitting on top of a plain power fact.

Two things I can't settle on my own:
1. Am I flattening Pascal into a proto-game-theorist he just isn't? He's writing as a Jansenist, and I suspect "nobody agrees on justice" is supposed to carry real moral shame, reason broken by the Fall and all that, not a neutral coordination story the way an ESS description is neutral.
2. Does the dominance-hierarchy comparison actually explain anything, or does it just relabel the phenomenon and dodge the actually interesting question, which is why force gets dressed up as justice at all instead of just ruling openly as force?

Keen to hear where this holds up and where it's naive, on those two points especially.
