"Transworld identity is the thesis that one and the same individual can exist in, and have properties in, more than one possible world. On this view, the very same Humphrey who actually lost the 1968 election is also the man who wins it in some other world. Kripke's version of this works by looking across at the other worlds, picking out whichever person there matches Humphrey closely enough in the relevant respects to count as him, and then checking what happens to that individual -- since that just is Humphrey, if he wins there, then Humphrey could have won. So the claim 'Humphrey could have won' comes out true because Humphrey himself, the actual man and no one else, wins in that other world.

Counterpart theory, David Lewis's alternative, denies that anything exists in more than one world at all: every individual is confined to a single world, so the actual Humphrey never turns up anywhere else. What makes 'Humphrey could have won' true instead is that Humphrey has a counterpart -- someone else, in some other world, who resembles him closely enough in the relevant respects -- who wins there. Since the counterpart relation just tracks overall similarity, in any given world it singles out one individual as Humphrey's counterpart (or none, if no one there is similar enough), so despite being a similarity relation rather than strict identity, it still ends up working world-by-world much like a restricted identity relation would.

Kripke's objection to counterpart theory is that this changes the subject: when we say Humphrey could have won, we want to know something about Humphrey himself, and learning that someone else, however similar, wins in another world shouldn't be any comfort to him, since strictly speaking it isn't a fact about him at all.

The problem of accidental intrinsics is Lewis's own argument against transworld identity. Shape properties like having five fingers seem to be had by a thing simpliciter rather than relative to a world, so if Humphrey is wholly present in two worlds, five-fingered in one and four-fingered in the other after an accident, then Humphrey simpliciter both has and lacks five fingers -- a contradiction. Counterpart theory escapes this because the five-fingered and four-fingered individuals are just numerically distinct counterparts of one another rather than one thing with contradictory properties, so nothing ever comes out both F and not-F simpliciter."
