Here is the question as it appeared on the exam:

"State the Stalnaker Thesis precisely. Reconstruct the core structure of Lewis's triviality result against it, making explicit which assumptions about the relevant class of probability functions are doing the real work, as opposed to what would follow just from the Thesis holding for a single credence function on its own. Then explain how EITHER Adams's OR Jackson's response to the triviality result is meant to preserve something like the tight link between conditionals and conditional probability while escaping Lewis's conclusion, and briefly assess whether it succeeds."

And here, verbatim, is the student's answer:

"The Stalnaker Thesis holds that the probability of an indicative conditional 'if A, C' equals the corresponding conditional probability, P(A > C) = P(C | A), for any A with P(A) > 0. On Stalnaker's own semantics this is meant to be a fact about a genuine proposition: 'if A, C' is true at a world w iff C is true at the closest A-world to w, so the Thesis says that ordinary probability, applied to that proposition, always coincides with conditional probability.

Lewis's triviality result shows this cannot hold in general, once we ask for more than the Thesis being true of our actual credence function alone. Pick A and C so that A, C, A-and-C, and A-and-not-C are all possible, with probabilities strictly between 0 and 1 -- so A and C are the kind of contingent, evidentially connected propositions ordinary indicative conditionals are actually used for. Suppose the Thesis holds not just for our actual P but also for the functions we get by conditionalizing P on C and on not-C -- this closure assumption, that any function we could rationally come to have by updating on new information must also satisfy the Thesis, is really doing all the work; nothing this strong follows just from asking the Thesis to hold once, for the P we happen to have. Conditionalizing on C forces P(A>C | C) = 1, since once C is already true, 'if A, C' can hardly fail. Conditionalizing on not-C forces P(A>C | not-C) = 0, since C is now impossible. By the law of total probability, P(A>C) = P(A>C|C)P(C) + P(A>C|not-C)P(not-C) = P(C). But the Thesis applied to P itself says P(A>C) = P(C|A). So P(C|A) = P(C): A and C come out probabilistically independent, for any A and C meeting the non-triviality conditions we started with. That is absurd for A, C that are supposed to be evidentially connected -- which is most of the interesting cases -- so something in the setup has to be given up.

Adams's response is to keep something like the Thesis while denying that 'if A, C' is simply the material conditional 'A ⊃ C,' understood as an ordinary truth-functional proposition subject to the standard probability calculus. On his view, 'if A, C' really is materially true or false -- true whenever A is false or C is true -- but our willingness to assert or accept it is governed by a further constraint that is conventional rather than truth-conditional in nature: an indicative conditional is fit to assert only when it is robust with respect to its antecedent, i.e., when learning A would not undermine one's confidence in it. That is why assertability tracks P(C|A) so closely even though, strictly, the conditional's truth conditions are just those of 'A ⊃ C.' Since, on this view, the truth and probability of 'if A, C' really are just the truth and probability of 'A ⊃ C,' and the Thesis-like equation is recovered only at the level of assertability rather than truth, Adams's view was never committed to the premise Lewis's proof targets -- that one and the same proposition's probability must equal P(C|A) -- so the triviality result leaves it untouched.

This is, in effect, why Stalnaker himself eventually gave up the selection-function semantics for indicative conditionals in response to Lewis: if no truth-conditional theory can supply a genuine proposition satisfying the Thesis without triviality, and Stalnaker had no pragmatic fallback of Adams's kind available to him, the natural conclusion was that indicative conditionals cannot be treated as expressing propositions in his original sense after all. The cost of the material-conditional-plus-implicature strategy, though, is the familiar one that originally motivated non-truth-functional accounts: since Oswald did in fact shoot Kennedy, both 'if Oswald didn't shoot Kennedy, someone else did' and 'if Oswald didn't shoot Kennedy, no one did' come out equally true as material conditionals, and the view then has to say that the second is merely inappropriate to assert rather than false, when ordinary judgment says it is simply false."
