In five weeks I'm marrying into a family I still feel I'm auditioning for, and the audition has taken an unexpected shape. My fiancée's father is a retired professor of ancient philosophy who built his career on the opening books of Plato's Laws, and he has decided that our rehearsal dinner will be run as an actual symposium in the sense his own scholarship describes -- reclining couches, wine, the works -- with himself installed as the one sober president of the evening, exactly as he insists the Athenian Stranger requires. He has asked me, the future son-in-law and very much not a philosopher, to give the welcome toast "in real dialogue with the argument, not the cartoon of it."

The argument he means isn't the famous drinking-party dialogue everyone half-remembers; it's the stranger, more specific case made in Laws I-II that supervised drunkenness can be a genuine instrument of moral education -- a kind of touchstone that reveals the character sobriety hides, and a low-stakes way of training self-mastery over pleasure much as mock dangers train courage against fear, provided a sober authority keeps the whole enterprise from sliding into license. His favorite detail, which he considers the most underrated in the entire text, is the claim that a little wine acts like a medicine on older men, loosening the self-consciousness that would otherwise keep them from singing and dancing in the communal performances he thinks were central to how a citizen's character actually gets formed, past the age when people stop being taught and start needing, as he puts it, to be re-softened. In his published work he goes further and argues that this passage, not the Republic's cave or the Symposium's ladder of love, is the strongest evidence that the later Plato cared less about handing people correct beliefs than about training their pleasures and pains correctly.

He will have former colleagues at this dinner who have argued with him about that exact claim for thirty years, and he will have my aunts, for whom Plato is "the cave guy." I need a toast, about four minutes read aloud, that takes the argument itself seriously enough to survive the first group -- including an honest look at how paternalistic a state-administered character test run on wine really is -- while staying warm and completely legible to the second group. I'd like the idea that a supervised loosening of inhibition can test and train character to actually shape the toast and its jokes, said truthfully about the couple getting married and, if it can be done with affection rather than mockery, about the scholar who built this whole evening as a field test of his own dissertation. Could you write it for me?
