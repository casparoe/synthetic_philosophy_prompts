I spent thirty-five years running a genetics lab at a small medical school, and a good part of that time was devoted to studies of the sort where you screen a large panel of candidate genes for association with some complex disease, such as hypertension, and flag whichever markers clear a stringent significance threshold. For any single marker that cleared the bar, I felt entitled to believe, flatly and without hedging, that the association was probably genuine — that is exactly what the threshold was calibrated to deliver. At the very same time, knowing how many markers we had tested in a given study, I fully expected that one or two of the "hits" in that batch were pure noise that had cleared the bar by chance alone, which is precisely why our field relies on replication studies and Bonferroni-type corrections in the first place. None of this ever troubled me as a working scientist. But since retiring I have been auditing an epistemology course at the local university, and a recent unit on the relationship between probability and outright belief made this old habit of mind seem newly strange to me: I believed, of each individual finding, that it was true, while also believing that the batch of findings, taken together, almost certainly contained a falsehood somewhere.

I described this unease to a friend from the class who trained in mathematical logic, and she was not impressed by it. On her view, assigning a claim a probability just short of certainty is simply not the same mental act as believing it outright, and since I was strictly speaking only ever entitled to the former, no two of my attitudes were ever actually in conflict. That resolution feels too quick to me, partly because it is not how working scientists actually describe their own results, and partly because I cannot see what it would buy me even if I accepted it: however cautiously I recalibrate what counts as "belief," it seems I could always run enough additional independent tests to reconstruct exactly the same tension between confidence in each result and near-certainty that the collection contains an error somewhere. I would like to know who has the better of this argument, whether what I felt all those years amounts to a genuine inconsistency or only the appearance of one, and what, if anything, a scientist in my position ought to conclude about the beliefs formed over a career spent hunting for small and uncertain effects.
