My department's history-of-analytic-philosophy reading group has its one outside speaker of the year lined up for next month, and she's someone whose spent a long stretch in the Frege Nachlass, including the diary he kept for those few weeks in spring 1924 -- the one full of antisemitic, antidemocratic bile that only surfaced decades after Dummett had already crowned him analytic philosophy's patron saint. Her actual position, which she's published on, is the strong one: that the diary isn't some ugly footnote sitting next to the Grundgesetze but continuous with it, that the same picture of thought as objective and mind-independent -- the third-realm stuff from "Der Gedanke," which you either grasp with reason or you don't -- that drives his whole war on psychologism in logic is exactly what's driving his contempt for parliamentary vote-counting and his views on who should and shouldn't get a say in how Germany is run. I find the boldness of the claim appealing but I'm not sure it survives contact with the obvious objections, and I'm moderating the Q&A after her talk.

Could you draft me eight or nine questions for that Q&A that actually engage the thesis instead of just inviting her to restate it? I want at least one question that presses the counterexample problem -- there were other committed anti-psychologistic logicians, Russell for one, who were realists about propositions and were, if anything, committed democrats, so the inference from the logic to the politics can't be doing much work on its own -- one on the standard genetic-fallacy complaint that none of this should touch how we assess the definition of number or the Begriffsschrift regardless of what's in the diary, one on how her story is even supposed to be falsifiable rather than a just-so tale you could tell about almost any philosopher's politics after the fact, and one on why Dummett himself, who read the diary and was clearly shaken by it (I think he wrote about it in the preface to one of his Frege books, can't remember which one), still stopped short of drawing the line she's drawing. For each one, sketch the reply I should expect from her and give me a sharp follow-up keyed to it, and end with a question asking what would actually get her to give up the thesis.
