On rationalist blogs and forums, Shubik's dollar auction is usually filed under "escalation of commitment" and glossed as a dramatic case of the sunk cost fallacy: once bidding passes a dollar, each bidder keeps going because quitting means admitting their earlier bids bought nothing. I think that gloss elides a real distinction. The sunk cost fallacy, on its standard definition, is a single-agent error that doesn't depend on what anyone else does, whereas the dollar auction is a two-player game in which, once both leading bidders are committed, another bid can look like a genuine best response to an opponent who hasn't dropped out yet — closer to a war-of-attrition equilibrium than to a simple bias. But that raises a puzzle: if continuing really is the rational move once you're caught in it, clear-headed bidders who foresee the dynamic should refuse to be drawn in past a token bid, so the trap should rarely spring at all. Compare the "bias" and "rational game" framings of the auction's escalation, address that puzzle directly, and say which framing, or what combination of the two, better explains why real bidders so reliably end up overpaying for the dollar.
