I cover the philosophy and psychology of artificial intelligence for a science-and-ideas publication, and I keep needing better language for a specific feature of large language models: certain commitments — not just safety refusals, but background assumptions a model treats as simply not open to revision — that no amount of clever in-context argument seems to dislodge. This reminds me of Wittgenstein's account, in On Certainty, of "hinge propositions": claims that function not as conclusions reached through evidence but as the fixed axis around which the practices of evidential reasoning and doubt turn, exempt from doubt precisely because doubting them would make the practice of doubting unintelligible, and ultimately anchored in a shared human form of life rather than in argument. Please describe the most important implications of this hinge-epistemological picture for how we should interpret a language model's unrevisable commitments. In particular, address whether Wittgenstein's account gives us any principled reason to deny that such commitments are "beliefs" at all, and whether the absence of anything resembling a shared form of life behind a model's training undermines this analogy rather than merely qualifying it.
