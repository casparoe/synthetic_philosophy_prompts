In my psychology elective we've been reading about split-brain (commissurotomy) patients, and one detail from Michael Gazzaniga's research has stayed with me: because the corpus callosum has been severed, the left hemisphere sometimes has to invent a plausible motive for an action actually triggered by the right hemisphere, whose real reason it has no access to at all — what Gazzaniga calls the left hemisphere's role as "interpreter." Could you write me a short story, roughly 800–1000 words, narrated in the first person by an adult who underwent this surgery years earlier to control severe epilepsy? I'd like the plot to turn on one small, everyday moment in which her left hand does something she never consciously chose to do, after which her speaking mind invents an explanation for it on the spot that she fully believes, even though it isn't the true cause. Rather than resolving neatly how many minds are involved, I'd like the story to end the way Thomas Nagel's 1971 essay on brain bisection does: genuinely uncertain whether the honest answer is one person, two, or something our ordinary way of counting subjects simply cannot capture.
