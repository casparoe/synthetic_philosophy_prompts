I've spent the last few weeks reading around eighteenth-century France for a novel I'm drafting, and I fell down a rabbit hole I hadn't expected: Rousseau's quarrel with the theatre. A friend in my writing group, hearing me go on about it over dinner, joked that every working novelist ought to have to answer Rousseau's charges before being allowed to publish anything. I haven't been able to shake the joke since, because the more I read, the less it sounds like one.

In the Lettre à d'Alembert sur les spectacles — his furious response to d'Alembert's suggestion, in the Encyclopédie's article on Geneva, that the city acquire a playhouse — Rousseau argues that the pity a tragedy wrings from its audience is spent entirely inside the theatre, leaving spectators feeling they have discharged their humanity for the evening and are owed a rest from it; he reads Molière's Misanthrope as a machine for teaching audiences to laugh at Alceste's rigor and warm to Philinte's easy compliance, to prefer, in other words, a comfortable vice to an inconvenient virtue; and he treats the actor's trade, the nightly counterfeiting of passions to order, as something that hollows out whatever genuine character an actor might otherwise have kept. What stops me from simply admiring all this from a safe distance is that a few years later this same man publishes Julie, ou la Nouvelle Héloïse, one of the best-selling novels of the century, reportedly moving many of its readers to real tears — and prefaces it by pre-empting exactly the objection I would want to raise against him: he allows that theatres suit corrupt capitals and novels suit a corrupt age in general, so that the damage, if any, is already done before the curtain rises or the book is opened, and adds, for good measure, that no properly innocent girl has any business reading a novel at all.

I'd like an essay that takes Rousseau's anti-theatrical arguments seriously on their own considerable merits, rather than dismissing them as mere puritanism, and then presses them hard against his own practice as a novelist. Work out the strongest principled distinction available to him — something about solitary, re-readable, page-at-a-time reading versus collective, embodied, one-night spectatorship, perhaps, or about writing for an already-corrupted public in general versus licensing a theatre in a still-uncorrupted Geneva in particular — and test whether it actually survives contact with Julie, or whether it just relocates the problem (a corrupt "age in general," after all, presumably includes Genevans too). I don't want a survey that politely declines to choose; I want a real verdict on whether Rousseau's position is coherent, or whether he is, in the end, special-pleading on behalf of the one art he could not give up — and I want that verdict argued from the texts themselves rather than from his biography or his psychology.
