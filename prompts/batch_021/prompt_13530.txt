A friend and I spent most of a long dinner last week arguing about peacocks, of all things, and neither of us has quite let it go since. It started when I mentioned that Darwin was genuinely troubled by the evolution of the peacock's tail: I recalled — imperfectly, I'm sure — that he once wrote to a colleague, I think Asa Gray, that simply looking at one of those feathers made him feel ill, precisely because such a cumbersome, predator-attracting ornament seemed so hard to reconcile with a theory built on survival advantage. He eventually resolved this for himself through sexual selection and female aesthetic preference, and that move is part of why Alfred Russel Wallace broke with him so sharply on this particular question, preferring to keep male ornamentation tied to something more measurable, like general vigor or condition, rather than to female taste for beauty as such.

Neither of us could put the matter to Darwin directly, obviously, but that only pushed the argument one step further: what would he have made of Amotz Zahavi's handicap principle, proposed almost a century later, which holds that an ornament's very cost and impracticality is precisely what makes it a reliable signal, since only a genuinely high-quality individual could bear such a handicap and still survive? My friend is convinced Darwin would have seized on this as the missing piece that finally vindicated his side of the dispute with Wallace. I suspect, on the contrary, that he would have found it more congenial to Wallace's instincts, since it grounds the signal's meaning in a real, checkable underlying quality rather than in taste for its own sake. Reasoning only from what Darwin actually believed and argued at the time, and not from anything he couldn't have known, could you help us adjudicate this? I'd like your best reconstruction of how he would actually have reacted to Zahavi's proposal, and which of our two readings of him seems better supported.
