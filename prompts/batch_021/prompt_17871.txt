I'm several chapters into a historical novel set around the Congregatio de Auxiliis, the papal inquiry into grace and free will that ran from the closing years of Clement VIII's pontificate into the opening years of Paul V's, and I've arrived at a scene I've been putting off. My two central theologians — a young Jesuit peritus who has staked his career on defending Luis de Molina's Concordia, and an older Dominican consultor trained by Domingo Bañez himself and sent to Rome to keep the Society from carrying the day — corner each other in a corridor the night before they are due to testify. I've been reading Molina and the surrounding secondary literature on and off for a year, but almost everything available to me in English is sympathetic to the Jesuit side, and I don't yet trust myself to give the Dominican his due.

Could you draft that corridor scene for me, roughly a page of dialogue? I don't want either man reduced to the textbook line that Jesuits championed free will while Dominicans championed predestination; I need them arguing like two people who have each spent years with the other's books. The Jesuit should be made to explain, in his own terms, how scientia media is supposed to fall logically between God's knowledge of the merely possible and His knowledge of the actual, and what a counterfactual of creaturely freedom is doing when God is said to consult it before decreeing which world to create. The Dominican should press him on what makes such a counterfactual true prior to any decree, or to the creature's even existing to choose anything — an objection close in substance to what philosophers would only much later name the grounding objection. And I want the Jesuit to return the favor by making the Dominican answer, in scholastic terms rather than by appeal to authority, whether physical premotion — for all its insistence that God moves the will "according to its own nature" — doesn't simply relabel predetermination under a gentler name. Neither man should concede more than his system allows, and neither should land a clean knockout blow: one of them is going to lose this fight in Rome, but I haven't decided yet which, and I'd like a reader who already knows the material to feel that both sides were argued as well as they can be.
