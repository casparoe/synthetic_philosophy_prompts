I spent about forty years doing solid-state physics, and it's only since I retired that I've actually sat down and read some of the philosophy papers everyone used to casually name-drop at conferences instead of just nodding along. Case in point: Wigner's old essay on the "unreasonable effectiveness" of mathematics. Reading it properly for the first time, what grabbed me wasn't the general headline (fine, math describes nature, we all knew that) but the more specific claim buried in there — that there are basically two seperate miracles going on: first, that nature has laws we can find at all, and second, that a piece of mathematics cooked up for its own internal, aesthetic reasons (his favorite example is matrix mechanics) keeps turning out decades later to be exactly the right tool, valid way outside whatever narrow domain it was originally built for. That second part is the bit that acutally seems spooky to me, more than the vague "math is useful" observation people usually flatten the essay down to.

So naturally I went looking for who'd argued back, and found Hamming's essay from 1980 with the almost mischievous identical title, where he tries to talk Wigner down off the ledge with a string of pretty down-to-earth, "you're overthinking it" type explanations — something like: we mostly notice the mathematics that happened to work and quietly forget the mountain of stuff that didn't (survivorship bias, basically); our mathematical taste was shaped by the same evolutionary pressures as the physical world we're trying to describe, so a fit is less surprising than it looks; and physics has actually only ever tackled a thin slice of all possible phenomena, so the "success rate" looks more miraculous than it is once you remember the denominator. I ran this by a couple of old colleagues over coffee and we mostly just talked past each other. What I can't work out on my own is whether Hamming's deflationary points genuinely answer Wigner's sharper claim about theories keeping their validity way beyond the original domain they were derived in, or whether he's quietly swapped in an easier question and answered that instead. Could you lay out both positions properly, point by point, and then actually tell me — with real argument, not a wishy-washy "both have a point" — whether Hamming's naturalism dissolves Wigner's puzzle, or whether the hard part survives untouched?
