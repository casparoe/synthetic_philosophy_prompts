I help run my high school's AI safety reading group, and this month we worked through Irving, Christiano, and Amodei's original paper proposing debate between two AIs, judged by a human, as a way to oversee systems that might already be smarter than we are, along with Barnes and Christiano's later follow-up describing the "obfuscated arguments" problem they ran into once they actually tried the scheme out. Half the group came away thinking debate is basically a solved approach to scalable oversight, on the strength of the complexity-theoretic argument that optimal-play debate should let a bounded judge adjudicate a far larger class of claims than an undebated answer ever could; the other half thought the obfuscated-arguments issue shows that guarantee falls apart once you swap in an actual resource-limited human for the idealized judge. We ran out of time before anyone changed their mind.

To help me write up discussion notes for next week, could you write a dialogue between two AI safety researchers arguing this out at a workshop poster session -- one who has built her career on debate as the most promising route to scalable oversight, and one who thinks the obfuscated-arguments problem is close to fatal for it? I'd like the skeptic to press specifically on why a time-limited, non-ideal judge should be expected to reward the debater who is actually correct, rather than whichever debater's argument happens to bottom out somewhere the judge can't audit in the allotted time, and I'd like the defender to answer with something more substantive than restating the original completeness argument -- for instance, by appealing to mechanisms like cross-examining earlier copies of the opponent. Somewhere in the exchange, have the skeptic ask whether this is really a problem specific to debate or whether it would just as easily sink rival scalable-oversight proposals like recursive reward modeling, and have the defender give a real answer instead of dodging. I don't want either side to land a clean, uncontested win -- I want both of them to have to work for every point, the way I imagine it would actually go if two people who'd each spent years on this sat down together.
