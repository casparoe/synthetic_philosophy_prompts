After thirty years running a laboratory on animal sensory systems, I retired a few years ago and have been reading my way through the teleosemantics literature that grew out of Ruth Millikan's Language, Thought, and Other Biological Categories. I have talked myself into a worry about the whole project, and I would like you to talk me back out of it rather than indulge it.

Millikan resolves the disjunction problem for a state such as a frog's fly-snapping response by fixing its content via the mechanism's selected effect: the response means 'fly' rather than 'small dark moving speck', because catching actual flies, and not merely triggering the retina, is what the ancestral mechanism was selected for. That move relies on exactly the sort of selection explanation that Elliott Sober's distinction between selection for a property and mere selection of a coextensive one was designed to discipline, and on Sober's own account that distinction only yields a determinate verdict when the candidate properties could in principle have been decoupled and tested against one another under selection. In a lineage where being a fly and being a small dark moving speck never once came apart, there seems to be no historical fact, even in principle, about which property was selected for, and so none about which content Millikan's apparatus is entitled to assign.

Please argue that this does not show etiological theories of content fail as a naturalization project. Engage with whatever resources you think a Millikan-style theorist genuinely has available, such as her notion of a Normal explanation or a willingness to accept some coarseness or disjunctiveness in content, rather than simply insisting by stipulation that selection histories are always fine-grained enough to do the job.
