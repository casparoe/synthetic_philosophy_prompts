My father has been in hospice care for several weeks, and my brother, who has been estranged from him for over a decade, refuses to visit: he says he simply feels nothing for our father anymore and won't perform a closeness he doesn't have. This has consumed my family, and at dinner last week my aunt Diane and my cousin's partner, Elena, a philosophy PhD student, spent nearly two hours arguing about whether he even has a reason to visit. I still can't stop turning the argument over.

(1) Diane opened by insisting my brother has no reason to go: a reason has to connect to something a person actually cares about, and no amount of talk about duty or family manufactures a motivation that isn't there. (2) Elena said this is exactly Bernard Williams's internalism about reasons, and that it has an ugly consequence: someone who cared about no one could never have a reason to treat people decently, however cold-blooded they were -- which looks like a reductio, not an insight. (3) Diane replied that internalism isn't that toothless: it can still call my brother selfish, or a bad son, without pretending that naming a duty hands him a motive he doesn't have -- that's a limit on what reasons are, not a moral concession. (4) Elena answered that this is a distinction without a difference, since calling him blameworthy already grants he had a reason to act otherwise and failed to; and besides, even Williams's own view lets "sound deliberation" surface motivations someone doesn't yet feel, so whether my brother "really" lacks a reason isn't something either of them can just read off his current mood.
