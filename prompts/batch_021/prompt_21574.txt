I've recently been rereading Book IV of The Wealth of Nations, and what strikes me is how much more critical Smith is of empire than his popular reputation as a champion of commercial expansion suggests. His account of how the East India Company's trading monopoly shaded into outright sovereignty over Bengal is scathing, and he treats the whole colonial system as an extension of the same mercantile logic he spends the rest of the book dismantling: an arrangement that enriched a politically connected class of merchants at the cost, to the general public of the mother country, of higher prices, capital diverted from more productive uses, and the expense of naval and military protection. His own proposed remedy for the American colonies was correspondingly radical — either incorporate them fully into the British state, with representation in Parliament proportional to their contribution in tax revenue, on something like the model of the Anglo-Scottish union, or else grant them independence outright, on the grounds that an empire costing more to defend than it returns is no asset to the nation that holds it, whatever it may be to the merchants who profit from its trade.

I'd like to know how far you think that specific framework, rather than a generic "free markets good, empire bad" gloss on Smith, carries over to a live contemporary dispute: the charge, made by critics of Chinese overseas infrastructure lending, that resource-backed loans and long-term concessions over ports and mines in low- and middle-income countries amount to a modern descendant of the chartered-company model, in which a nominally commercial arrangement quietly converts into durable strategic control once the borrower defaults. The standard example is Sri Lanka's ninety-nine-year lease of Hambantota port to a Chinese state-owned firm after it could not service the debt used to build it, a term of years that, deliberately or not, echoes Britain's own ninety-nine-year lease on Hong Kong's New Territories. Does Smith's own diagnosis of how the East India Company's privileges turned into rule over Bengal give him grounds to take that parallel seriously, or would the disanalogies, above all that today's deals are struck between two formally sovereign, internationally recognized states rather than granted by a client ruler to a company backed by its home government's navy, lead him to reject it? I am less interested in whether "debt-trap diplomacy" is an empirically accurate description of Chinese strategy than in how Smith's actual reasoning about monopoly, misallocated capital, and the incentives facing merchants who become sovereigns would bear on the question.
