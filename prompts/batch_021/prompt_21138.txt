I'm currently taking a course on formal epistemology, and I've been asked to chair the Q&A after a visiting speaker's talk on the epistemology of chance. She defends a broadly Lewisian, Humean account of objective chance and handles the "undermining" problem for the Principal Principle by replacing Lewis's admissibility clause with unconditional deference to the complete theory of chance, essentially a version of the New Principle. Whenever someone raises a case where this looks like it breaks down, her go-to move is to insist that whatever evidence seems to unseat the current chance hypothesis simply wasn't part of the true chance theory to begin with -- which looks suspiciously like Lewis's old admissibility dodge wearing a new coat. Draft seven to nine questions for the session that make that move progressively harder to use. Include at least one question on whether individuating the complete theory this way is any less circular than the admissibility clause it replaced, one that forces her to state exactly how deference to that theory is supposed to block a future frequency from counting as evidence against a present chance, and one asking whether an ordinary epistemic agent could ever be in a position to condition on the complete theory the way her view requires, or whether that just relocates rather than dissolves the original puzzle. Close with a question asking why we should prefer her Humean picture at all, given that a propensity theorist's chances don't supervene on the pattern of outcomes and so never generate an undermining problem in the first place. For each question, add a brief follow-up keyed to the reply she's most likely to give.
