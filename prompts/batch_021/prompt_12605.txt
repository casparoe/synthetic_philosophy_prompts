I have Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, and somewhat randomly ended up filling the "patient/community" seat on my hospital system's new value-assessment committee (basically a spinoff of the regular Pharmacy and Therapeutics committee), mostly because I'd been loud about access issues in a support group. Next month we're voting on whether to formally adopt a cost-per-QALY cutoff — something like $150k per QALY, in line with the upper end of what ICER publishes — as the tiebreaker whenever the drug budget can't cover everything the doctors want approved. Our health economist keeps framing this as basically just rational allocation: get the most health for the money, full stop. But when we brought in a disability-rights advocate to comment, she said something that's been bugging me since — that to even calculate a QALY you have to decide a year of one person's life with their condition is worth, say, 0.6 of a year of someone elses life without it, and then stack those numbers on the same ruler as if that were unproblematic. She called it an "interpersonal comparison of utility" problem and said theres no neutral, scientific way to make that comparison, that it's a value judgment wearing a lab coat. I went and read a bit about this afterward (apparently there's a whole fight between an economist named Robbins, who thought comparing utility across people isn't even scientifically meaningful, and Harsanyi, whose famous utilitarian aggregation result kind of needs it to be meaningful to go through) and now I feel like I understand the disagreement less than when I started, not more.

I have to circulate a short memo to the committee before we vote and I really don't want to just punt with "reasonable people disagree." Could you draft it for me? I need it to land on an actual recommendation — adopt the cutoff as proposed, adopt some modified version of it, or scrap it in favor of a different allocation rule (lottery, strict need-based priority, whatever actually makes sense) — and I need the reasoning spelled out well enough that both the economist and the advocate would feel like their strongest point got taken seriously, including whichever one of them ends up on the losing side.
