I have been wrestling with how to teach the closing paragraph of Smith's *Wealth of Nations*, and I would welcome your help thinking through its tone and argumentative function. Here is the passage, which is also the literal final paragraph of the entire book (Book V, Chapter III, "Of Public Debts"):

"This empire, however, has hitherto existed in imagination only. It has hitherto been, not an empire, but the project of an empire; not a gold mine, but the project of a gold mine; a project which has cost, which continues to cost, and which, if pursued in the same way as it has been hitherto, is likely to cost, immense expense, without being likely to bring any profit; for the effects of the monopoly of the colony trade, it has been shewn, are to the great body of the people, mere loss instead of profit. It is surely now time that our rulers should either realize this golden dream, in which they have been indulging themselves, perhaps, as well as the people; or that they should awake from it themselves, and endeavour to awaken the people. If the project cannot be completed, it ought to be given up. If any of the provinces of the British empire cannot be made to contribute towards the support of the whole empire, it is surely time that Great Britain should free herself from the expense of defending those provinces in time of war, and of supporting any part of their civil or military establishment in time of peace; and endeavour to accommodate her future views and designs to the real mediocrity of her circumstances."

What makes this passage hard to place is what comes just a few pages earlier in the same chapter. There Smith sketches, at some length, a scheme for uniting Britain and the American colonies under a single taxing authority, with colonial representatives admitted to Parliament in proportion to what the colonies contributed - yet he introduces even that scheme as "a new Utopia... not more useless and chimerical than the old one." By the time we reach the final paragraph, then, it is unclear how much weight "completed" is meant to bear. Is Smith seriously presenting Britain with two live and dignified options - consummate the union, or let the colonies go - and declining to choose between them? Or has he already tipped his hand, via the Utopia remark, that completion is not a real possibility, so that the "golden dream" language is really doing the work of making separation look like the only responsible course, dressed up as even-handedness? It is worth remembering that the book appeared in March 1776, several months before the Declaration of Independence, so this was not an academic hypothetical for its first readers.

How would you adjudicate between these two readings, and does it matter that this passage is not just one argument among many, but the note on which Smith chose to end the entire treatise?
