I'm working on a novel that's got a long section set in a near-future "digital afterlife" startup, and one of my POV characters is a thinly-veiled version of Kurt Godel -- basically an upload trained on his old letters and papers, who has to decide whether to let his own procedure be marketed to customers as literal life after death. I want his internal monologue to actually track what the real Godel believed, instead of doing the generic "genius is suspicious of the newfangled machine" cliché, so I've been digging into his actual metaphysical views and I keep runing into a tension I can't resolve on my own, which is where you come in.

From what's out there on the letters he wrote to his mother (apparently way more candid on this stuff than anything he ever published), Godel seems to have argued that an afterlife has to exist as a matter of rationalist necessity -- roughly, that the world is rationally constructed and meaningful, so it would be absurd for a mind's development to just get cut off at death with nothing to come of it, therefore there's got to be a hereafter. That's a Leibniz-flavored argument, not a religious one. But then there's the Gibbs lecture, where he lays out that famous disjunction -- either the mind outruns every fixed formal system, or there are math problems no mind could ever settle -- and my understanding is he personally leaned toward minds just not being reducible to any mechanical process, which is presumably part of why people like Lucas and Penrose later leaned on him for their own anti-AI arguments.

So here's the actual question I need settled for the scene: walking those two commitments through together, would the "real" Godel see an upload -- a computational process running on a machine -- as a plausible instance of the afterlife his own argument demands? Or does the anti-mechanism basically disqualify an upload from counting as him in any real sense, no matter how well it talks or how many of his old proofs it can reconstruct? Don't just hand me a verdict -- I want the whole chain of reasoning spelled out the way he might actually have worked through it himself, and feel free to phrase bits of it like something he'd actually say, since I might steal a line or two for the dialogue.
