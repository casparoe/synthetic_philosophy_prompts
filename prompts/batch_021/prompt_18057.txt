Here's my attempt (only talking about indicative "if"s here, not counterfactuals). Call the claim that P(A > B) = P(B|A) for every "reasonable" credence function and every A, B with P(A) > 0 "the Thesis" (I think this gets attributed to Adams and/or Stalnaker depending who you read -- I always mix up who gets credit for what). The extra premise Lewis needs is that the set of reasonable credence functions is closed under conditionalizing on anything with positive probability -- basically, if P is reasonable then so is whatever you'd update to after learning some true proposition C. Okay so: pick some P and some A, B with P(A&B) > 0 and P(A&~B) > 0, so P(B|A) is strictly between 0 and 1, a genuinely open conditional. Conditionalize P on B to get P_B; since P_B(A) > 0, the Thesis says P_B(A>B) = P_B(B|A), and since conditioning on B and then A is the same as conditioning on A&B, that's just P(B|A&B) = 1. Symmetrically, conditionalize on ~B to get P_~B, and the Thesis gives P_~B(A>B) = P(B|A&~B) = 0. Now recombine using total probability, conditioning back on A and ~A: P(A>B) = P(A)*P_B(A>B) + P(~A)*P_~B(A>B) = P(A)*1 + P(~A)*0 = P(A). But the Thesis applied to the original P also says P(A>B) = P(B|A), so P(B|A) = P(A) -- for pretty much any A and B you like, which is clearly insane, so something upstream has to be rejected.

The moral I want to draw is that you can't have indicative conditionals be ordinary propositions whose credences behave the normal way AND have conditional probability track them -- but I'm genuinely unsure if that's the standard lesson people take from this or if I'm overstating it (I've seen people say the real upshot is more about assertability conditions than truth conditions, Adams/Edgington style, and that this is compatible with conditionals still meaning something).
