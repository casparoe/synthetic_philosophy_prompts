I'm taking a seminar this term on realism and anti-realism in the philosophy of language, and we just spent two sessions on Fitch's paradox of knowability. As we reconstructed it in class: let K abbreviate "is known by someone at some time," and consider the knowability principle (KP) that every truth is knowable in principle — for any p, if p then it is possible that Kp — together with the modest assumption of non-omniscience (NO), that there is some truth p that is not known, i.e., p and not-Kp. Fitch's lemma shows that the conjunction "p and p is not known" can never itself be known, on pain of contradiction: knowing it would mean knowing p and also knowing that p is not known, and since knowledge is factive, that second piece of knowledge would make it true that p is not known, directly contradicting the first. Now apply KP to that very conjunction: if "p and not-Kp" is ever true, KP says it must be possible to know it — but the lemma says it is never possible to know it. So no instance of "p and not-Kp" can be true, which just is the claim that every truth is known, flatly contradicting non-omniscience. Since that conclusion is absurd, the paradox is usually read as a direct threat to Dummett-style semantic anti-realism, which needs exactly the sort of knowability principle that generates the trouble.

My professor's line, following what she takes to be the best defense of anti-realism, is that the natural way out — restricting KP so that it governs only "basic" statements that do not themselves contain epistemic vocabulary, rather than every statement whatsoever — is not just a face-saving patch but is independently motivated by the anti-realist's own theory of meaning, on which the assertibility conditions of logically complex statements are already built out of, and parasitic on, those of basic statements. A couple of us in the reading group think this gets things backwards: that the restriction is really reverse-engineered to deliver the result anti-realists need, and the "independent" motivation is supplied after the fact rather than doing any genuine work. I want to press on this before our next meeting, so I am after the strongest version of our side of the argument rather than a survey of the debate.

Could you argue, as rigorously as you can, that restricting the knowability principle to basic statements is an ad hoc rescue of anti-realism rather than a principled solution to Fitch's paradox? Please engage seriously with the parasitism motivation rather than dismiss it out of hand — granting, if it helps your case, that the assertibility conditions of complex statements really are built out of those of basic statements — and then show specifically why that general architectural fact fails to license the exact restriction needed to block Fitch's derivation. Don't simply assert the "ad hoc" verdict; locate precisely where the gap between the general motivation and the specific technical fix lies.
