My mother, an analytic metaphysician, died ten days ago, and I have been asked to give her eulogy at the memorial service this coming Saturday. I teach philosophy myself, though in ethics rather than metaphysics, which is partly why I'd like help getting this right rather than trusting my own instincts. For the last quarter-century of her career she defended what most people in the field would call a fairly restrictive answer to the special composition question: that some simples compose a further thing only when their joint activity constitutes a life, in more or less the way van Inwagen argues in Material Beings. On her view, strictly speaking, there are no chairs, no rocks, and no corpses -- only simples arranged chairwise, or rockwise, or, as she liked to put it at conferences whenever someone raised the standard objection, arranged corpsewise. Living organisms were the one real exception. Unlike van Inwagen, she was not religious, and she had no interest in his move of softening the view's implications with an appeal to bodily resurrection; she held, quite calmly, that when the life running in her stopped, the thing that had been her would stop existing too, not merely change state, and that nothing afterward would answer to her name.

A good third of the people at the service work in metaphysics, several in mereology specifically, and know exactly what she thought about all this; at least two have published on the so-called "corpse problem" as an objection to views like hers, and I suspect they'll be listening for whether I quietly borrow the comforting, ordinary-object language -- "her body," "she's at peace now," "part of her lives on in this house" -- that her whole career was spent refusing to use loosely. The rest of the room, including my father and her undergraduates from thirty years of teaching, knew her as a warm and very funny woman, not as a position in a debate, and they need the eulogy to actually comfort them.

Could you write it for me? It should run about seven minutes aloud, be recognizable to the philosophers in the room as taking her actual view seriously rather than quietly hedging around it, and still work, for everyone else, as a eulogy rather than a seminar. I'd like you to draw out whatever real comfort her own position makes available -- about the simples that persist and get taken up elsewhere, about what it actually took for a life to be running in the first place and how definite a thing that was, about what does compose something now, in the people she taught and raised -- rather than either dodging the metaphysics or undercutting it with exactly the consolations she spent her career declining to offer herself.
