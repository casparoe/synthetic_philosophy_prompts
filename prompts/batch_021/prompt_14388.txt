I'm about two-thirds through a novel built around a slow-motion constitutional crisis in an unnamed republic, and one of my characters — a former constitutional court judge who has made an uneasy peace with the coup that ended his career — is exactly the sort of man who would keep Pascal's Pensées on his shelf and misquote it to justify himself. I don't want to put a line in his mouth that I've mangled the way people mangle "survival of the fittest," so I went back past the one sentence everyone knows to read the whole fragment usually filed under "Justice, force" in the section on the "reason of effects." The two English editions I own render it slightly differently, so rather than quote either one and risk flattening the argument, I've worked out my own close rendering from the French, which I'll give you here:

"Justice, force. — It is right that what is just should be followed, and necessary that what is strongest should be followed. Justice without force is powerless; force without justice is tyrannical. Justice without force is contradicted, because there are always wicked men; force without justice is condemned. Justice and force must therefore be brought together, by making what is just, strong, or what is strong, just. Justice is open to dispute; force is easily recognized and admits of none. So it was not possible to give force to justice, because force contradicted justice and said that justice was unjust, and said that it itself was the just one. And thus, unable to make what is just strong, men have made what is strong just."

What I can't work out is the joint in the last third, and I'd like your help reconstructing it rather than a précis of "Pascal thought might makes right." The opening reads like a genuinely open design problem — satisfy the pairing either by strengthening the just or by moralizing the strong — but Pascal then argues as if only the second branch had ever been live, on the ground that force is undisputed while justice is not. I don't see why that asymmetry forces the outcome rather than merely making it likelier; couldn't a sufficiently undisputed justice have done the same work? And how would you weigh the tone of the closing sentence: is it cold description of how legitimacy actually gets manufactured, a specifically Christian claim about what fallen self-love does with power (something closer to Augustine's libido dominandi than to Machiavelli), or is Pascal quietly setting up an inference he expects an attentive reader to complete on their own, given his other remarks about custom and the danger of telling people the truth about where their laws come from? My judge needs to be arguing against a real position here, not a slogan, so I'd rather you push on the argument itself than summarize it.
