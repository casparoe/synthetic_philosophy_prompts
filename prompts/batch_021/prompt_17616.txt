I'm a software engineer, and over the past few months I've been building a small side project in my spare time: a bot that plays repeated betting games against friends for fake money. Working on it left me stuck on a disagreement I haven't been able to resolve, and neither have a couple of people I've argued about it with on algorithmic-trading forums. The setup is the classic one: you're repeatedly offered the same favorable bet — a coin that comes up heads, paying even money, with probability 55% — and before each round you choose what fraction of your current bankroll to wager. Plain expected-value reasoning says you should wager the largest fraction you're allowed every single round, since expected terminal wealth after any number of rounds is strictly increasing in the fraction wagered whenever the bet has positive edge. But everything I've since read about the Kelly criterion and Ole Peters' "ergodicity economics" says that betting anywhere close to your whole stake drives your probability of eventual ruin toward 1, and that the fraction you actually want is the Kelly fraction (here, 10%) — the one that maximizes the expected logarithm of wealth, i.e., the almost-sure long-run growth rate — even though that fraction yields strictly lower expected wealth at every finite round count than betting more aggressively would.

What I can't settle is whether this is a genuine disagreement about what "rational" betting even means, or just a dressed-up restatement of ordinary risk aversion. One person on the forum insisted it's the latter: if you already have a concave (say, logarithmic) utility function over wealth, the Kelly fraction just falls out of ordinary expected-utility maximization, and there's a whole tradition — going back to Samuelson's famously dismissive critiques of "growth-optimal" investing — showing that treating the Kelly fraction as uniquely rational, independent of anyone's actual utility function, is a mistake. Someone else pushed back hard, arguing that this dodges what's distinctive about the ergodicity framing: in a genuinely non-ergodic, multiplicative process, the "expected value" being maximized is an average over an ensemble of parallel, non-interacting copies of you that no single person ever actually lives through, so treating it as the benchmark of rationality is close to a category error, independent of anyone's risk preferences — and that this same preference for geometric (multiplicative) over arithmetic (additive) aggregation shows up elsewhere under the banner of "geometric rationality," for instance when pooling several forecasters' probability estimates by averaging their odds rather than their raw probabilities.

Could you write me a substantive dialogue between these two positions, staged at the point where they're actually arguing over what fraction my bot should wager? Give one character (call her the Expected Value theorist) the strongest version of the "this is just utility theory" position, and the other (call him the Ergodicity theorist) the strongest version of the geometric-rationality position, and have them genuinely engage with each other's reasoning — pressing on real weak points, conceding minor ground where it's actually due, and returning to the concrete coin-bet numbers rather than resting on slogans. I'd rather the dialogue end in honest, unresolved tension than have either side simply concede, since my own suspicion is that people who understand this material thoroughly still disagree about it.
