I'm a philosophy PhD student working on formal epistemology, and after last week's reading group session on games with imperfect recall, two of the participants, Mara and Devesh, got into a long argument over coffee about the absent-minded driver problem that neither would concede. For anyone reconstructing this cold: a driver is on a highway with two consecutive, visually identical exits, X and Y. Exiting at X pays 0, driving past X and exiting at Y pays 4, and driving past both pays 1. Because the intersections are indistinguishable and the driver has no way of telling, on arrival, whether this is his first or second encounter with one, any strategy must assign the same probability p of "continue" to both. Maximizing the payoff computed before setting out gives p = 2/3, for an expected payoff of 4/3 — the standard answer to this problem.

Mara opened by saying that this settles the matter: 2/3 just is the rational continuation probability, since a driver who commits to it in advance does better on average than one who commits to any other fixed rule, and nothing further should be demanded of "rational" here.

Devesh pushed back. He noted that the standard way of computing "the probability I'm at X rather than Y," once you're actually at an intersection, gives 3/5 and 2/5 respectively (X is reached on every trip, Y only two-thirds of the time, so you weight by how often each is reached). He then pointed out that plugging those numbers into an expected-payoff comparison of exiting versus continuing right now, holding the rest of the plan fixed, yields exactly 8/5 for either action. His complaint: this tie is not evidence that the beliefs support the plan, since 2/3 was chosen precisely to make the two actions come out equal given those beliefs. The credence was reverse-engineered to fit the plan rather than independently derived and then found to recommend it, so presenting it as a Bayesian vindication of p = 2/3 gets the order of justification backwards.

Mara answered that there is nothing improper about that structure: it is the same as a mixed-strategy equilibrium, where the whole point of the mixture is to make the other side indifferent, and no one objects that a matching-pennies equilibrium is "circular" on those grounds. A plan and the beliefs it induces being jointly pinned down by a fixed-point condition is just what optimality under uncertainty about one's own location looks like.

Devesh replied that the analogy fails: mixing in matching pennies is an instrumentally chosen action, justified by its effect on an opponent, whereas "credence 3/5 that I'm at X" is supposed to be a factual belief about which physical intersection is in front of him. Making that belief true only in virtue of which plan he has adopted reverses the normal direction of fit — beliefs are supposed to answer to the world and then guide action, not be tailored after the fact to keep the theory consistent.
