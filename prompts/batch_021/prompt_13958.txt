I'm plotting a novel with a washed-up actuary character who can't get through a conversation without turning it into a probability lecture, and while I was researching his voice I fell down a rabbit hole of decision theory paradoxes. Last month I tried one out on my Thursday game night crowd just to watch peoples faces -- it's called the Pasadena game: you flip a coin until it lands heads for the first time, and if that happens on toss n you win 2^n/n dollars when n is odd and loose 2^n/n dollars when n is even (so +2, then -2, then +8/3, then -4, and so on, alternating and blowing up forever). It kicked off a real argument between two friends of mine, Theo and Elena, that I still can't stop turning over.

Theo's first move was that since the payoffs only add up to ln(2) (about 0.69) if you sum them in the order n=1,2,3..., and that particular sum is only "conditionally" convergent, you can rearrange the exact same set of payoffs to add up to any number you want, or to infinity, or to nothing at all -- so there's no expected value here, full stop, and since there's no number to speak of, the rational price to pay to play has to be $0. Elena said undefined and zero are not the same thing and he was quietly smuggling one in for the other, like insisting an undefined slope must be flat, and that the honest conclusion is just that expected-value reasoning has nothing to say about this game at all, which is a different and more uncomfortable conclusion than "it's worth nothing." Theo came back with: fine, but the rearranging is a bit of a magic trick anyway, because there IS a natural order to add these up in, namely by n, the toss on which the game actually ends -- that's how the payoffs get generated, we don't get to shuffle cards that haven't been dealt yet -- so ln(2) should just be the answer and the "paradox" is overblown. Elena's comeback was that "generated in order" is sneaking in an assumption: the payoffs aren't sequential events happening in time, they're just a big table of possible worlds with probabilities attached to them, and nothing in probability theory says the label n we use for bookkeeping is privileged over any other way of pairing those worlds up with the natural numbers -- the fact that n is convenient for us to talk about isn't a fact about the game.

Nobody budged, they went around this twice more, and I ended up just quietly taking notes for the actuary character instead of refereeing. Could you go through those four moves one at a time and say which ones actually hold up and which don't, rather than just declaring an overall winner -- and is there anything important that both Theo and Elena are missing entirely?
