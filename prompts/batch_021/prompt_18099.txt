I cover science and ideas for a living, and for the past year I've served as the sole outside "public member" on the exhibit-content advisory committee at my city's natural history museum, a seat I inherited after profiling the museum's renovation for my publication. The committee is finishing a new permanent gallery on perception and the brain, informally called "The Guessing Brain," whose centerpiece wall panel — drafted by the in-house science-education staff together with a cognitive-neuroscience consultant — describes ordinary seeing as "a controlled hallucination: your brain's best guess about what's out there," and treats optical illusions as simply cases where the guess misfires. On this account, correctly seeing a red apple and mistakenly seeing a bent stick in water are, at bottom, the same kind of event — one lucky, one not. When the draft went out for the routine outside academic review the museum requires before fabrication, a philosophy professor who works on perception sent back a pointed letter arguing that the panel had quietly settled a live dispute in her field in the neuroscientists' favor: she invoked naive realism, the classical argument from illusion, and disjunctivism by name, and argued that treating accurate perception as merely a luckier instance of the same guessing process, rather than as a categorically different achievement, is a substantive philosophical claim dressed up as settled science. The designers and their consultant consider this an academic quibble that would leave the panel hedgy and no more useful to the visitors who will actually read it; when I ran the letter by a philosopher friend over dinner, she thought the reviewer had a genuine point.

As the committee member with no stake in either camp, I've been asked to write the short memo that will anchor our final sign-off meeting next Tuesday, after which the text is locked for fabrication and any later change means an expensive reprint. Please draft that memo. It should come down on one specific outcome for the panel — keep the existing language, replace it with alternative wording you supply, or adopt some defensible third option — with reasons the design team could actually act on by Tuesday, and it should treat the accessibility worry as a real cost of precision rather than dismiss it as anti-intellectualism.
