Alongside writing and repeatedly revising The Methods of Ethics, Henry Sidgwick spent much of his career founding and twice presiding over the Society for Psychical Research, sifting testimony about telepathy, apparitions, and trance mediumship for evidence of personal survival after death. It is tempting to treat this as a charming Victorian eccentricity, essentially separable from the technical argument of the Methods. Please argue against that separation. Sidgwick held that the "dualism of practical reason" — the standoff between the claims of rational egoism and those of rational benevolence (utilitarianism) — could be resolved only by weakening his own standards of self-evident truth or by securing some independent guarantee that duty and self-interest ultimately coincide. Make the case that his psychical research was a rationally motivated attempt to secure the latter empirically, so that, in his own phrase, the Cosmos of Duty need not be "reduced to a Chaos." In building this case, explain why a contingent empirical discovery about ghosts or telepathy could even bear on settling what looks like a purely normative dispute.
