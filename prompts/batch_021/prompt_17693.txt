I don't have any formal training in philosophy or religious studies, but over the past few months I've been slowly working through Maimonides' Guide for the Perplexed on my own, mainly because I wanted to understand why the Torah devotes so much space to the details of animal sacrifice when the practice plays no part in how Judaism, or Christianity for that matter, is actually lived today. When I reached the section of Book III that most translations number as chapter 32, where Maimonides explains the sacrificial cult as a kind of divine concession, I found it genuinely satisfying. His idea, as I understand it, is that the Israelites, having grown up in a world where every nation worshipped through sacrifice, could not simply be commanded to abandon the practice overnight in favor of pure prayer, so God let them keep offering sacrifices, redirected exclusively toward Him, as a way of gradually weaning them toward something purer, much as a physician weans a patient off a harmful habit rather than forcing sudden withdrawal. I was even more convinced once I saw that he backs this up with the passage in Jeremiah where God says that when He brought the people out of Egypt, He had not actually commanded them concerning sacrifices at all, only obedience.

Since then, I've come across enough passing references to Nahmanides' reaction to this theory to suspect that it's far more contested, among people who actually know the sources, than it first seemed to me. As best I can piece together, Nahmanides thought the chronology alone was a problem, since sacrifice goes back to Abel and to Noah, long before Israel could have absorbed the practice from any surrounding idolatrous nation, and, more fundamentally, that Maimonides' account empties the sacrificial rites of any meaning of their own, reducing a large part of the Torah to scaffolding for a lesson that has nothing to do with sacrifice as such. My understanding is that Nahmanides instead reads the specific details of an offering, such as the laying on of hands, the slaughter, and the burning of particular organs, as standing in symbolically for the sinner's own body and life, so that the ritual carries a positive meaning in its own right rather than serving merely as a concession to primitive habit.

I'd like to think through this disagreement properly, since on my own I can't tell how much of it comes down to two equally defensible readings of an ambiguous text, and how much one side is simply getting Maimonides, or the Torah, more right than the other. Could you lay out both positions carefully, including how each side would handle the Jeremiah passage and the problem of sacrifice predating any exposure to idolatry, and then tell me which of the two views you find more defensible as an account of why the Torah commands animal sacrifice in the first place, and why? I'd rather have a real verdict, with reasons, than a diplomatic "both sides have a point."
