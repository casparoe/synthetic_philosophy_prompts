I spent almost forty years doing condensed matter physics before I retired, and a couple years back I started sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group at the university near me. Last month we read Wigner's old essay on the "unreasonable effectiveness" of mathematics, and it kicked off a real argument between two of the regulars, Marta and Owen, that I still can't stop chewing on.

Marta's opening move: the whole "unreasonable" framing is just survivorship bias. We remember the handful of cases where math invented for no physical reason later fit nature perfectly, and quietly forget the mountains of math that never found any use, plus all the physics that had to invent its own bespoke math from scratch. Cherry-pick the hits and of course it looks miraculous.

Owen: fine for alot of cases, but not the famous ones. Matrix algebra was decades-old pure math before Heisenberg needed exactly that structure for quantum mechanics. Riemann's geometry of curved space sat around for something like sixty years before general relativity needed exactly that, not some rough cousin of it.

Marta: not as independent as advertised, though -- Riemann's own lecture on the foundations of geometry already wonders whether physical space itself might be curved, and whether that shows up as a kind of force at small scales. So even the "zero physical motivation" story oversells it.

Owen: sure, but a hunch about curvature is a long way from a field equation precise enough to predict exactly how much starlight bends around the sun, or from an abstract equation that predicted a whole new particle before anyone had seen one. Those are novel predictions confirmed by observation after the fact, not old math getting reinterpreted to fit. You can't blame survivorship bias for a prediction that came before the evidence.

Marta: maybe, but physicists also only end up calling something "fundamental" once it's turned out to be mathematically clean, and quietly shelve the messy stuff as detail or engineering. Thats a selection effect too, just operating on what counts as core physics rather than on which old math gets reused.

I can see something right in each move but keep flip-flopping on who's ahead at any given point. Could you go through the exchange step by step, say who has the better of it at each stage, and flag anything important that neither of them is raising?
