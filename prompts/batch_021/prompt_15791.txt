"Standard Bayesian probability theory idealizes agents as 'logically omniscient': the moment you have some beliefs, you're assumed to already believe every logical consequence of them, so you can never be logically surprised. That's clearly wrong for any real agent -- a chess engine doesn't instantly know whether a given position is a forced win, and a mathematician doesn't instantly know whether Goldbach's conjecture is true just because she knows the axioms of arithmetic. Any reasoner with finite compute has to be uncertain about plenty of things that are, in fact, logically settled one way or the other.

Logical induction tries to fix this by setting up a kind of prediction market in logical sentences instead of just bolting on an ad hoc patch. Each sentence is treated like a stock; 'traders' can buy and sell shares in it, and a trader profits if it correctly predicts that the market's current price (i.e. probability) is wrong. The logical induction criterion says that no trader can make unbounded profit off this market over time -- basically, no Dutch book is possible. From this one requirement you can derive a whole family of desirable behaviors: the market's probabilities converge as it deliberates longer, they become calibrated (so if you look at a batch of sentences it assigns 70% to, roughly 70% of them really are true), and probabilities assigned to theorems and their negations head to 1 and 0 respectively as proofs are found. In effect, the market becomes logically omniscient in the limit -- it just gets there gradually, at a rate no efficient trader can front-run, instead of instantaneously the way classical Bayesian agents are assumed to be. That's the real innovation: logical omniscience is recovered as a limit behavior rather than a starting assumption, which is a much more honest model of how mathematicians and computers actually converge on the truth over time."
