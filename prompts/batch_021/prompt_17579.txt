I am drafting a chapter of a novel in which a mountain guide, caught mid-storm, must decide which of two roped climbing parties to try to reach before a ridge becomes impassable. She has radioed an identical promise to each group — "stay where you are, I'm coming" — and she knows that reaching one by nightfall means the other will almost certainly not survive the exposure. I have deliberately made the two parties equivalent in every respect I can control (same size, same experience, no prior acquaintance with her, nothing that would let triage logic simply pick out a "more salvageable" group), because I want the chapter to sit with a specific question rather than quietly resolve it: is she genuinely bound, all things considered, to save each of two groups of people even though she cannot save both — so that whichever she reaches, she has actually failed an obligation and not merely paid a regrettable cost — or is that just a dramatic way of describing a situation that careful moral reasoning would always cash out into one determinate duty, with her later guilt being natural but mistaken?

Since I don't trust myself to represent both sides fairly, I'd like you to write me a dialogue between two moral philosophers who take her case as their working example and disagree sharply about it. One should argue, in the spirit of Ruth Barcan Marcus and Bernard Williams, that this is a genuine moral dilemma: that both obligations remain in force right up to the moment of choice, that the agent-regret she will feel afterward is a fitting response to a real remainder rather than a confusion to be argued out of her, and that this needn't make her underlying principles inconsistent — only that a perfectly coherent set of principles can turn out, given sufficiently unlucky circumstances, to be jointly unsatisfiable. The other should argue something closer to Alan Donagan's position: that because she could have kept either promise on its own, "ought implies can" is satisfied for each taken separately, but that agglomerating the two into a single requirement to keep both would violate it, and that a rational moral code cannot coherently do that to an innocent agent — so either the case really does resolve into one determinate duty, or her sense of remaining guilt needs some other explanation. I don't want either side handed an easy win: the first philosopher should have a real answer to the agglomeration argument, not just an appeal to intuition, and the second should engage seriously with why the guilt feels irreducible rather than simply insisting it shouldn't. Feel free to give them invented names rather than writing them in as the historical figures themselves.
