I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have been turning over a distinction in my head ever since an argument I witnessed at a family dinner some months ago. My uncle criticized my cousin for gossiping about our grandmother's declining health behind her back, and my cousin brushed him off as a hypocrite for saying so, even though, as far as anyone in the family knows, my uncle has never gossiped about anyone in his life. That struck me as the wrong charge to level at him, and it left me wondering whether we too casually treat two different things as though they were the same: being a hypocrite, and simply lacking the right to criticize someone in the first place. In everyday speech, phrases like "you have no right to judge me" and "you're such a hypocrite" seem to function as near-synonyms, but it seems to me that a person could fail to have the first without being guilty of the second.

I would like your help testing that thought with a concrete case. Could you construct a detailed, realistic scenario involving two people — call them A and B — with the following features: B has genuinely done something wrong (nothing invented, exaggerated, or merely a matter of differing opinions); A calls B out on it directly; A has never done anything resembling that particular wrong and applies no double standard whatsoever; and yet most people, on hearing the full story, would still feel that A had no business saying what they said. Please fill in enough concrete detail — who A and B are, what connects them (if anything), what the wrong consists in, and the setting in which the criticism is delivered — that the missing standing obviously cannot be chalked up to some hidden form of hypocrisy on A's part. Once you have laid out the example, I would be grateful if you could identify, in a sentence or two, exactly what feature of the situation is doing the standing-undermining work, given that hypocrisy has been ruled out by construction.

Finally, I am curious whether the relationship also runs the other way. Is it possible to construct a case in which A is a hypocrite by the ordinary meaning of the word — condemning B for precisely the sort of thing A has done, or still does — and yet A plausibly keeps full standing to blame B all the same? If such cases exist, it would suggest that hypocrisy and the loss of standing to blame are connected in a much looser and more interesting way than everyday usage assumes, and I would like to understand that connection better.
