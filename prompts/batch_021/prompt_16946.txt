A few months back I fell down a rabbit hole after stumbling on an old news story about researchers who had taken Kurt Gödel's modal ontological argument for God's existence -- the one he apparently never published himself and only showed to a handful of colleagues -- and formalized it using an automated theorem prover, confirming that the conclusion really does follow validly from the axioms. As a software engineer, that hooked me immediately: the notion that you could run a proof about God's existence through something like a proof assistant the way you'd verify a piece of code. Reading past the headlines, though, I learned that the same axioms are also blamed for producing something called "modal collapse" -- I believe the objection traces back to a paper by Howard Sobel, though I'm not fully certain I have the name right -- where the system ends up entailing that every truth that merely happens to be actual is also necessarily true, so that nothing could ever have been otherwise. That struck me as a far bigger cost than the popular coverage ever let on.

Curious how serious the problem really is, I started reading up on Alvin Plantinga's modal ontological argument as a point of comparison, since it appears to run on similar machinery -- necessity, possibility, and an S5-style move from "possibly necessarily true" to "necessarily true" -- except applied to a "maximally great being" rather than Gödel's primitive "positive properties." I mentioned all this to a friend who minored in philosophy, and she dismissed the comparison by saying Plantinga's argument "just defines God into existence the same way Anselm did, dressed up in modal logic." That struck me as too quick, and it didn't explain why Gödel's version is treated as uniquely saddled with modal collapse while Plantinga's is rarely accused of the same thing, even though both arguments are trying to squeeze necessary existence out of little more than a possibility premise.

What I'd like from you is a close comparison of the two arguments' logical skeletons. First, could you identify exactly which axiom or inferential step in Gödel's system is doing the work that produces modal collapse, and then check whether Plantinga's argument genuinely avoids that step, or whether it relies on something structurally similar that has simply attracted less scrutiny? Then, having laid that out, tell me which of the two you consider the more defensible attempt to derive God's necessary existence from modal logic alone -- Gödel's or Plantinga's -- and justify that judgment rather than just asserting it.
