Inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) attempts to recover an agent's reward function from its observed behavior, typically by assuming that the agent acts optimally, or near-optimally, with respect to that reward. As has been clear since Ng and Russell's original formulation of the problem, this is radically underdetermined: any policy is compatible with infinitely many reward functions, including the degenerate constant reward under which every policy counts as trivially "optimal." A sharper result due to Armstrong and Mindermann (2018) shows that the indeterminacy survives even the most natural fix for it: if we allow the agent to be systematically irrational, and try to jointly infer its reward together with its "planner" -- its rationality or bias profile -- no simplicity prior can privilege the intended decomposition over alternatives that assign the very same behavior wildly different preferences and correspondingly different biases.

Please argue that these results show something more than a practical obstacle awaiting better priors or richer data: that talk of an agent's "true," interpreter-independent preferences is confused, in essentially the way, and for essentially the reason, that Quine held facts about meaning and translation to be indeterminate. Your argument should spell out the structural analogy in some detail rather than merely gesturing at it, and should address the natural objection that an agent's reward, unlike meaning, might simply be some further physical or computational fact about its cognition that behavioral evidence merely fails to reveal.
