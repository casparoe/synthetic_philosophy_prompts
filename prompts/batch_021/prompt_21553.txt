I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have spent a good deal of time lately turning over a short, strange essay the philosopher David Lewis wrote asking whether penal substitution — the idea that Christ was punished in the place of sinners — is even a coherent thing to believe. What has stuck with me is his move of comparing it to letting someone else pay off your fine, then worrying how much of that comparison survives once the debt in question is a prison sentence or an execution rather than money: a fine can be discharged by anyone, but a punishment seems to belong essentially to the person who earned it. He flirts with the idea that guilt might have to stay fixed on the original wrongdoer while something like the liability to be punished gets passed on to a willing substitute, without ever quite convincing himself that this rescues the doctrine. I would like to see that tension worked out directly: please write a dialogue between two equally capable people, one who thinks this guilt/liability split is enough to make penal substitution coherent, and one who thinks it fails because punishment, unlike a debt, cannot be pried loose from the culpable person without ceasing to be punishment at all. They should engage with each other's actual arguments as the exchange develops, and neither should simply come out on top by the end.
