I'm several drafts into a historical novel set in Ireland in the mid-1730s, one strand of which follows George Berkeley during his years as Bishop of Cloyne, while he is writing "The Querist" -- that strange little pamphlet composed entirely as a string of numbered questions about money, credit, and banking rather than as an ordinary treatise. My editor, who knows Berkeley only as the "esse est percipi" philosopher from her undergraduate survey course, was genuinely startled to learn he had anything to say about economics at all, and her reaction convinced me I need to get the substance right rather than treat it as quaint local colour. To that end I've arranged to record a research conversation with a historian of eighteenth-century economic thought who has built her career on Berkeley's monetary writings, meant to run as a companion piece when the novel is published, and I'd like your help preparing for it.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that push past the level of "Berkeley thought money wasn't wealth" and get into the actual joints of the position. I want at least one question on why he chose to write the whole work as unanswered queries rather than argued propositions, and what that form is doing philosophically as well as rhetorically; one on his "counters" or "tickets" account of currency, and what, if anything, he thought fixed the value of a ticket once the metal backing it was denied any intrinsic worth; one on his proposal for a national bank extending paper credit to a country chronically short of specie, and how that credit was supposed to be secured against collapse; and one on the disputed link between this monetary nominalism and his immaterialism -- my guest tends, when students raise it, to wave the question off as "a family resemblance, not a family tree," and I'd like a question built specifically to make that dismissal harder to sustain. For each of the ten, add a short note on the answer she is most likely to give and a pointed follow-up that doesn't let her simply rest on it -- and please, nothing generic like what first drew her to Berkeley.
