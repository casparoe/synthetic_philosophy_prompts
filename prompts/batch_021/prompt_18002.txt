Over the past year, since retiring after three decades as an experimental physicist, I have been reading my way through the metaphysics of individuation, and Max Black's 1952 thought experiment has stayed with me longer than almost anything else in that literature: a universe containing nothing but two qualitatively identical iron spheres, a mile apart, offered as a counterexample to the Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles. What has really occupied me lately, though, is not Black's original argument but a more recent complication of it. Simon Saunders and others distinguish three grades of discernibility: absolute discernibility, where some monadic property separates two things; relative discernibility, where an asymmetric relation holds one way and not the other; and weak discernibility, where an irreflexive relation holds between them even though it holds symmetrically and no monadic property distinguishes either one. On this taxonomy, Black's spheres turn out to be weakly discernible after all, since being a mile from something is an irreflexive relation; and Saunders presses the same point about fermions, such as two electrons in a singlet state, each truly describable as having opposite spin to the other. He takes this to rescue a defensible version of the Principle rather than concede Black's counterexample.

I am nevertheless stuck on the rejoinder associated with Katherine Hawley and others, to the effect that this gets the direction of explanation backwards: to call being a mile from, or having opposite spin to, an irreflexive relation, one already has to know that there are two objects rather than one, so the irreflexivity looks like a consequence of there being two spheres, or two electrons, rather than a ground for it. On this view, weak discernibility is a symptom of numerical diversity rather than a source of it, and Black's spheres, together with the fermion case meant to vindicate them, remain a genuine counterexample to anything worth calling the identity of indiscernibles.

Rather than have the disagreement summarized for me, I would like to see it fought out properly. Could you write a dialogue, staged as though two philosophers had been set down inside Black's imagined universe, standing exactly between the two spheres? Let one of them defend weak discernibility as a genuine rescue of the Principle, drawing on the fermion case as independent support, and let the other press the circularity worry as hard as it will go, not just against the spheres but against the fermion case as well. I want each to engage with the other's strongest move rather than simply restate their own position, and I want them evenly matched: if one of them says something that sounds like it settles the matter, the dialogue should make clear why the other does not agree that it does. They need not resolve the question by the end, but I would like them to arrive at a precise statement of the point at which they stop agreeing about what would even count as evidence either way.
