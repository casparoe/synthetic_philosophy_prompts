I recently retired from international-level rifle competition and, somewhat to my surprise, was just elected to my national shooting federation's therapeutic use exemption panel for a three-year term — a seat that had always seemed like a formality when I was competing, and that I only really understand now that I'm the one voting. My first case landed on my desk last week, and after several days of back-and-forth with the other panel members by email, I've realized none of us can actually articulate a principled basis for the position we're each leaning toward, so I want to work out something more careful before we reconvene.

The case: a nineteen-year-old member of our national air-rifle team was diagnosed two years ago, well before she made the national team, with an anxiety disorder that produces panic symptoms in ordinary life — she describes it flaring up during university exams and at family gatherings, nothing to do with sport. Her physician, who has no connection to shooting, prescribed a low daily dose of propranolol, a beta-blocker, which she has now taken for two years with clear benefit to her day-to-day functioning. Beta-blockers happen to be on our sport's prohibited list at all times, because they steady the hand and slow the heart rate in exactly the way that decides a 10-metre air rifle final; without an exemption she cannot compete while medicated. Our exemption standard, which tracks the international anti-doping code fairly closely, is supposed to require a genuine diagnosed condition, no reasonable alternative treatment, and — this is where we keep getting stuck — reasonable confidence that the substance won't enhance performance beyond what a return to ordinary health would give any competitor anyway. Half the panel thinks this is a straightforward case, no different from letting a diabetic athlete use insulin: she has a real, independently documented illness and takes an ordinary therapeutic dose, so it would be indefensible to make her choose between her mental health and her sport. The other half points out that in most sports the condition being treated is irrelevant to the skill being scored, whereas here the whole discipline is a competition in who can hold still and stay calm, so "restoring her to normal function" and "improving the exact trait we are judging" might just be the same event described two different ways — and no amount of sincerity about her diagnosis changes that.

Could you draft the memo I'll bring back to the panel? I need an actual recommendation — grant, deny, or grant with specific conditions (dose caps, a requirement to exhaust non-pharmacological alternatives first, an independent annual re-certification, a defined review period, or whatever you think defensible) — with reasoning developed enough that whichever side loses can recognize that its strongest point was actually engaged rather than just waved at.
