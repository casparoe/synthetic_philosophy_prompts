I've been reporting a longer piece on the intellectual pedigree of "might makes right" arguments about law and authority—tracing a line from Thrasymachus through Hobbes and into contemporary arguments about the rule of law—and an academic I interviewed for it kept steering me back to a single fragment of Pascal's Pensées that, in her view, gets flattened every time someone turns it into a pull-quote. It's usually catalogued as fragment 298 in Brunschvicg's edition of the Pensées (103 in Lafuma's later reordering), though as with most of that text the exact wording shifts a little between translations. In one standard English rendering, the passage runs:

"Justice, force.—It is right that what is just should be followed; it is necessary that what is strongest should be followed. Justice without force is powerless; force without justice is tyrannical... Justice is subject to dispute; force is easily recognizable and is not disputed. So we have not been able to give force to justice, because force has contradicted justice, and has said that it was unjust, and that it was itself that was just. And thus, being unable to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just." (Pascal's own line, often quoted alone in French, is: "La justice sans la force est impuissante ; la force sans la justice est tyrannique.")

What I can't quite settle, even after sitting with this for a while, is the logical and emotional register of that closing move. The opening sentences read like a diagnosis of something gone wrong—justice is powerless, force is tyrannical. But the last sentence, "we have made what is strong just," sounds less like a complaint than a flat report of a completed, almost satisfactory arrangement, with no obvious residue of protest. Is Pascal offering a purely descriptive, proto-Hobbesian genealogy of how legal authority actually gets manufactured—force retroactively dressed up as justice—or is there a normative undertow here, some sense that substituting the strong for the just is the right, or at least the only survivable, response to a fallen, self-loving human reason that can't reliably track justice on its own? And how should this fragment be read against Pascal's separate, more explicit worry elsewhere in the Pensées that ordinary people must never be told outright that existing laws aren't truly just, since they obey those laws only because they believe them just? I'd be grateful for help pulling apart exactly what's being claimed in these few lines, and how far a "cynical realism" reading of the passage can be pushed before it runs up against the rest of Pascal's religious anthropology.
