Me and my friend Theo got into this two hour argument last night that started over something dumb — he said he "knew" our other friend's flight had landed on time because the tracking app showed it, and then it turned out the flight was delayed, so I said "well then you didn't know, you just believed it," and he got kind of annoyed and said that's way too strict a standard for normal conversation. Somehow that spiraled into a full debate about what you actually need in order to properly assert something out loud — is having a justified or reasonable belief enough, or do you really need to know it, or does knowledge/belief not even matter as long as it happens to be true? Theo's view is that requiring knowledge is way too demanding for everyday talk and reasonable belief should be plenty. I pushed back with the classic lottery-ticket case: if you own one ticket in a million-ticket lottery, you've got amazing justification for believing your ticket lost (a 99.9999% chance!) but it still feels off to just flatly assert "my ticket lost" before the draw, rather than something hedged like "it probably lost" — which made me think assertion needs something stronger than mere justified belief, more like actual knowledge. Then Theo threw out a case that kind of got me stuck: picture a devout creationist biology teacher who personally doesn't believe evolution happened, but is a totally competent and honest teacher who accurately tells her class "species evolved via natural selection," fully representing what the evidence shows, with zero intention to deceive anyone. That still seems like a perfectly fine, non-improper assertion, even though she doesn't believe it (so she definately doesn't know it either) — which seems to show that knowledge, and maybe even belief, isnt actually required.

Either (1) defend a knowledge norm of assertion and show how you'd handle the creationist-teacher case without giving up the norm, or (2) defend some kind of justified/reasonable-belief norm (or another non-knowledge option, your call) and show how you'd handle the lottery case without just biting the bullet and saying "my ticket lost" is totally assertable in that situation. Get as technical and jargon-heavy as you need to (safety, sensitivity, Moore's paradox, whatever) — I just want an actual, committed argument that grapples honestly with the case that's inconvenient for whichever side you pick, not a wishy-washy both-sides-have-a-point answer.
