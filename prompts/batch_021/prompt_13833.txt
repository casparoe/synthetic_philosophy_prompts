The question, verbatim, is: "Nyaya recognizes exactly four pramanas: pratyaksa, anumana, upamana, and sabda. (a) Using the standard gavaya example (a gavaya being a kind of wild ox), explain what upamana is supposed to establish. Be precise about exactly what new piece of knowledge is produced, and for whom. (b) The Bhatta Mimamsakas accept two further pramanas that Naiyayikas deny are genuinely independent. Name each one, illustrate it with the standard example, and explain how a Naiyayika would try to show that it really reduces to one of the four accepted pramanas."

And here's the student's answer, copied exactly:

"(a) The classic case is the man who has never seen a gavaya. Someone familiar with the forest tells him that a gavaya is an animal similar to a cow. Later, in the forest, he comes across an animal that strikes him as resembling a cow in the relevant respect, and putting this together with what he was told before, he arrives at a new cognition: that this particular animal in front of him is a gavaya. Nyaya treats this as a separate pramana because neither ingredient could deliver that result on its own — the testimony only gave him a general description in advance, with no particular animal to apply it to, and the perception by itself only gives him a resemblance to a cow, not the concept 'gavaya.' Only the two working together, which is what upamana names, produce the new knowledge that the animal standing in front of him is a gavaya.

(b) The Bhattas add arthapatti (postulation) and anupalabdhi (non-apprehension) to the shared list of four. Arthapatti is illustrated by the stout Devadatta who is never seen eating during the day: since his being fat is inconsistent with his not eating, we're forced to postulate that he eats at night, and that postulation is arthapatti. Anupalabdhi is illustrated by our knowledge that there's no pot on the floor, which we get simply by failing to perceive a pot under conditions where we'd expect to perceive one if it were there. Naiyayikas deny both of these independent status, and in both cases their strategy is the same: show that the apparent new pramana is really just inference in disguise. Devadatta's case becomes an ordinary syllogism once you supply the implicit premise 'whoever is fat without eating by day eats by night,' and the pot case likewise reduces to an inference from 'all the conditions for perceiving a pot are satisfied, yet no perception of a pot occurs' to 'there is no pot.' So in Nyaya's view ordinary anumana already covers both cases, and there's no need for a fifth or sixth pramana."
