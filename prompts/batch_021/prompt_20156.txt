Since retiring from a forty-year career as an experimental physicist working on turbulence in fluids, I have finally had the time to take up philosophy, and lately the philosophy of mathematics in particular, more seriously than I ever could while I was working. For the past several months, a former colleague and I have been working through a set theory textbook together once a week, partly for fun and partly to shore up the technical background that the philosophical literature seems to assume. Cantor's proof that the real numbers cannot be placed in one-to-one correspondence with the natural numbers was one of the results that most impressed me as a young student, and I had always regarded it as about as settled a piece of mathematics as exists. It was correspondingly unsettling to reach the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem and its well-known corollary, Skolem's paradox: if the axioms of ZFC are consistent, they have a countable model, and since ZFC proves that uncountable sets exist, that very model must contain some set that the model itself certifies as uncountable, even though, from where we stand outside the model, the entire model — and hence every set inside it — is nothing more than a countable collection.

Our textbook insists that this is a harmless curiosity rather than a genuine contradiction, but the more I turn it over, the less convinced I am. If a single set can be uncountable as judged from inside a model and merely countable as judged from outside it, then "uncountable" does not seem to name an absolute property of the set at all, only an artifact of whichever model of the axioms one happens to be occupying. That would seem to leave Cantor's theorem with considerably less content than I was taught it had, and it makes me wonder whether the measure theory and real analysis I relied on for four decades of continuum mechanics has, at bottom, only ever held relative to a model, rather than of the real numbers as such. Why do working mathematicians not treat this as a serious crisis for the foundations of analysis, and how, if at all, has the profession resolved it rather than simply agreeing not to discuss it?
