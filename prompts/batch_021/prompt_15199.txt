Q: Explain Fodor's Language of Thought Hypothesis. What is the systematicity argument for it, and why did Fodor think it implies that most lexical concepts (ordinary nouns like DOG or DOORKNOB) must be innate rather than learned?

Student's answer: "Fodor's Language of Thought Hypothesis says thinking happens in an inner mental code, 'Mentalese,' which has a compositional syntax and semantics: complex thoughts are built from simpler constituents by combinatorial rules, the way sentences are built from words, and thinking itself is a computational process of manipulating these structured symbols. The systematicity argument says our ability to think certain thoughts comes bundled together - nobody can think 'John loves Mary' without also being able to think 'Mary loves John' - which is best explained if thoughts have inner constituent structure that can be recombined. Fodor and Pylyshyn pressed this against connectionism in their well-known 1988 paper, arguing that unstructured neural networks only capture systematicity by covertly implementing a classical symbolic architecture underneath. Since Mentalese is the actual code meanings are represented in, and different languages carve up experience differently, Fodor took this systematicity to show that a person's native language feeds back into and shapes the structure of their Mentalese, so thought is never fully independent of whichever language a thinker grew up speaking. On acquisition, Fodor argued that 'learning' a concept by forming and testing hypotheses about it already presupposes the very concept being tested for, so strictly primitive concepts can't be learned at all - they must be innate. He didn't push this to absurd lengths though: the innateness claim was really meant for basic sensory concepts like RED, while more complex, culturally specific concepts like DOORKNOB or CARBURETOR were, on his view, genuinely built up out of simpler learned parts."
