A friend and I argued all through dinner last week about whether you can ever be justified in believing something purely on someone's word, with no independent way to check it. She said you can't, not without some track record or independent corroboration; I said that proves too much, since nearly everything either of us believes beyond direct experience rests on somebody's say-so. We never resolved it, and this week it became the question facing a committee I sit on.

I'm on my university's extenuating circumstances committee, which grants coursework extensions for illness, bereavement, and similar hardships, but currently only on the strength of supporting documentation such as a doctor's note. The policy is under review after a student was denied an extension because her grandmother died abroad and she could not obtain a death certificate in time. A proposal now before us would replace documentation with self-certification: a student's own consistent, unchallenged statement, accepted by default, with no evidence required absent a specific reason for doubt. Some members call this an abandonment of due diligence; others say demanding corroboration for every claim already treats students' word as inherently suspect in a way we would not tolerate if it were applied to ourselves.

Please draft the short memo I will circulate before next week's vote. It should make a specific policy recommendation, propose a time frame for reviewing it, and engage honestly with the strongest objection to whatever it recommends.
