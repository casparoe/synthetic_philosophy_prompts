A friend and I spent much of a long evening after a philosophy of language seminar arguing about the semantics and pragmatics of slurs. The crux was whether a slur's offensiveness belongs to its encoded meaning - something that ought to survive quotation and other embeddings, as the "prohibitionist" view that slurs are simply taboo word-forms, quite apart from any special descriptive or expressive content, would predict - or whether it is instead tied to a speaker's use of the word to derogate an actual target, in which case citing it for illustrative purposes might reasonably carry less weight. We never settled the question, and a case has just landed on a committee I sit on that seems to turn on exactly that distinction.

I serve on my university's Academic Freedom and Classroom Conduct Committee, which hears complaints where our harassment policy - defined, as usual, around conduct a "reasonable person" would find severe or pervasive enough to create a hostile environment, and silent on anything resembling a use/mention distinction - runs up against faculty claims of pedagogical necessity. A philosophy of language professor, presenting the standard literature on pejoratives, wrote an ethnic slur unredacted on the board and then uttered it aloud embedded under negation ("It is not true that so-and-so is a [slur]") to run the classic projection test used in that literature to argue that a slur's derogatory force survives cancellation in a way an ordinary conversational implicature would not. A student in the room, a member of the group the term targets, filed a complaint: she says the harm of hearing the word was not mitigated by the negation, the theoretical framing, or the professor's evident lack of intent to derogate anyone present, and that the policy's effect-based standard was written precisely so that intent and use/mention niceties would not settle such cases by themselves. Roughly half the committee wants to dismiss on academic-freedom grounds; the other half thinks dismissing on those grounds just assumes the answer to the question her complaint actually raises.

Could you draft the short decision memo I'll circulate before next Tuesday's meeting? It should recommend one concrete resolution - dismissal, a misconduct finding with some sanction, or an intermediate outcome such as a non-disciplinary recommendation about how such examples are presented going forward - with reasoning substantial enough that the losing side could recognize its own best argument inside it, rather than seeing the use/mention distinction or the policy's text simply asserted past them. It should also leave the committee with criteria we could reuse in the next comparable case, whether that one involves a different slur, a reclaimed term used by a speaker from the in-group, or a historical document quoted in a literature seminar.
