I'm a PhD student in philosophy specializing in philosophy of psychiatry, and this term I've been serving as the graduate-student representative on the committee that oversees my university's student health insurance plan and its academic-accommodations policy: five faculty members, two administrators, the director of Counseling and Psychological Services (CAPS), and two student representatives. A specific case has pushed a broader policy question onto our agenda. A student petitioned for a retroactive, penalty-free withdrawal from last semester on the grounds of "Gaming Disorder," supported by a letter from an outside psychiatrist applying the WHO's ICD-11 criteria (impaired control over gaming, increasing priority given to gaming over competing activities, and continuation of the behavior despite clear harm). The registrar denied the petition because our accommodations list still tracks DSM-5 categories, where the closest construct, "Internet Gaming Disorder," appears only in Section III as a condition proposed for further study rather than an approved diagnosis. Rather than resolve this one appeal in isolation, our chair wants the committee to adopt a standing policy on whether to add ICD-11's Gaming Disorder to our recognized list for medical leave, late withdrawal, and insurance reimbursement, and to vote on it at our meeting in three weeks.

Predictably, the committee's disagreement keeps tracking a debate I've been reading for my qualifying exams: naturalism versus normativism about disease. The CAPS director argues that withholding recognition just because there is no isolable biological marker is inconsistent with how we already treat other behavioral and substance addictions, and needlessly leaves impaired students worse off than straightforward deference to the WHO would. Our chair, a kinesiology professor, thinks the word "disorder" is doing normative work the biology can't support here: absent some independently specifiable dysfunction of an evolved capacity, "impaired control" and "harm" just encode a prior judgment that this particular way of spending sixty hours a week is bad, in a way none of us would say about competitive chess or an all-consuming research habit -- a worry sharpened by how notoriously difficult it already is to specify the relevant evolved function for higher-level motivational capacities, even within a broadly Wakefieldian framework. A third member, from disability services, is sympathetic to the affected students but worried about setting a precedent for medicalizing any intense hobby a family member or professor happens to disapprove of.

Could you draft the short memo I'll circulate to the committee ahead of the vote? It should land on one concrete recommendation -- recognize the diagnosis outright, decline and instead route these students through existing categories such as comorbid mood or impulse-control diagnoses, or recognize it subject to specified conditions -- defended with reasoning the committee could actually vote on, rather than a report of the ICD-11/DSM-5 split as though that settled things. I'd especially like it to engage whether Boorse's biostatistical theory, Wakefield's harmful-dysfunction analysis, or something closer to Nordenfelt's welfare-based account actually gives us a principled way to decide this case, and to state plainly, and as charitably as possible, the strongest version of whichever position the memo ends up not adopting.
