I recently moved from a general ML engineering role onto the small team at my company that maintains the reward model we use to fine-tune our production assistant via RLHF. Because our annotator pool is split across several contractor groups with noticeably different rating norms, we don't train one reward model on pooled comparisons; instead we fit a separate reward model to each group's comparisons and then combine the group-level scores into a single aggregate before training against it. At a planning meeting last week, two of my more senior colleagues, Daniel and Nadia, got into a real disagreement about whether this design is principled or just a comfortable hack, and since we're about to decide whether to keep it or rebuild it from scratch, I haven't been able to stop turning their exchange over in my head.

Daniel opened by defending the status quo on theoretical grounds. (1) He argued that Harsanyi's aggregation theorem shows that if every group's preferences over outputs, along with the aggregate's own preferences, satisfy the expected-utility axioms, and the aggregate is never allowed to disagree when every group is indifferent, then the aggregate score must be representable as a weighted sum of the group-level utilities—so their linear blend isn't an engineering shortcut, it's close to the only coherent option, and they should stop apologizing for it. Nadia pushed back: (2) the theorem, she said, is being asked to prove more than it can, since each group's reward model is only meaningful up to its own positive affine rescaling, so "weighted sum" is empty until a common scale across groups has already been fixed—and fixing that scale is exactly the value-laden step the theorem stays silent on, which echoes a complaint Amartya Sen made about Harsanyi's use of the theorem. Daniel conceded the point in principle but said it doesn't bite in practice: (3) they already z-score every group's reward model against a shared held-out set before blending, which pins down a common scale in a way that's symmetric across groups rather than arbitrary. Nadia then raised a different worry, adapting, she said, an idea from Peter Diamond's 1967 reply to Harsanyi: (4) even once the scale is fixed, an aggregate built purely from expected utilities can't distinguish a policy that flips a coin to decide whose preferred output is served whenever two groups disagree from a policy that always, deterministically, serves whichever output scores higher on the blend, in cases constructed so both policies yield the same expected combined score—yet most people would call the coin flip fairer, since it gives every group a chance on each disputed case, whereas the deterministic rule could in principle favor the same group every single time the numbers happen to be close. Daniel's reply was that this is an objection to expected utility as a currency of fairness in general, not to their pipeline specifically, and that (5) at the volume of traffic they serve, the law of large numbers means any single-decision gap between "ex ante" and "ex post" fairness washes out across the whole user base, even if it wouldn't for a one-off case involving just two people.

Neither of them budged, and I don't know this literature well enough to referee it myself. Could you go through moves (1) through (5) and, for each, say whether the point is basically right, basically wrong, or right but weaker than its author seems to think, with a sentence or two of justification? I'd also like to know whether either of them is misdescribing what Harsanyi's theorem actually delivers, and whether there is something about aggregating large, shifting groups of annotators—rather than the two fixed individuals of the textbook case—that neither Daniel's nor Nadia's framing quite captures. Since we do have to make an actual call here, close with one sentence on whether you think an appeal to Harsanyi's theorem, by itself, is good grounds for sticking with a linear blend of group-level reward models.
