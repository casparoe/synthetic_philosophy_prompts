I am several drafts into a novel whose protagonist underwent a corpus callosotomy in her twenties as a last resort against intractable epilepsy, and whose creeping suspicion that she is no longer straightforwardly one person becomes the engine of the book's second half. I want the philosophical backbone of this to be sound rather than borrowed from the version of split-brain science that circulates in popular treatments—the cartoon in which a mute, spatially gifted right hemisphere and a chatty, rationalizing left hemisphere are simply two little people who happen to share a skull. So I have been working through the more serious literature: Sperry and Gazzaniga's original lateralized-testing experiments, Nagel's old essay on brain bisection, and some of the philosophical responses to it, including work defending a much more conservative reading of what the data actually show.

What I cannot quite pin down, and what I would like you to help me think through, is the substance of the disagreement between two camps that accept the same experimental results yet draw very different conclusions from them. One camp—in the spirit of Roland Puccetti's old arguments for "mental duality"—takes the lateralized dissociations to show that there are, quite literally, two separate subjects of experience sharing one skull, each with its own stream of consciousness. A more conservative camp, associated with philosophers such as Tim Bayne, denies that this follows: on their view, what the tests demonstrate is a breakdown in the patient's capacity for unified access, report, and control, not necessarily a doubling of whoever it is that is having the experiences in the first place. I would like you to explain, at a fairly granular level, what is actually at stake in that disagreement—whether it is ultimately a verbal dispute about how permissively to use the word "consciousness," or a substantive one about the facts of the matter—and why the conservative camp thinks the inference from disunified behavior to disunified experience is too quick.

I would also like you to fold in a complication I ran across while researching this: a 2017 study led by Yair Pinto, published in the journal Brain, reporting that split-brain patients could indicate awareness of simple stimuli confined to a single hemisphere even on tasks that the classical two-streams picture predicted they should fail, which its authors took as evidence for a single underlying conscious system despite the patients' disunified access. Does a result like that actually count as evidence for the conservative, one-subject view, or is it something the two-streams camp could absorb on its own terms—by appealing, say, to inter-hemispheric leakage, confabulation, or a sufficiently permissive notion of what a "stream" of consciousness can accomplish on its own? I am less interested in a survey of the literature than in a clear, worked-through account of the concepts and the stakes, since a couple of plot decisions I am about to commit to will implicitly side with one camp or the other.
