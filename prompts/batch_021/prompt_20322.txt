So I'm a phil PhD student (my own work is mostly in philosophy of language, not epistemology) and this term I've been sitting in on a formal epistemology seminar because a friend talked me into it, and we spent two whole sessions on van Fraassen's Reflection Principle from "Belief and the Will" and I still feel like I've only got the formula memorized, not the actual intuition for it. I get that the basic idea is something like: my credence now in some claim A, conditional on the supposition that my credence in A at some later time will be x, is suppose to just equal x -- and that van Fraassen backs this with a diachronic Dutch book argument, which is supposed to be a different beast from the usual Dutch book argument for conditionalization, since Reflection doesnt care what evidence you're going to get, only what credence you'll end up with. What I can't manage to explain to myself (let alone the friend who dragged me into this) is why that Dutch book is supposed to show Reflection is a genuine constraint on rationality, rather than just a cute fact about agents who happen to update well.

The part that really breaks my brain is the standard-seeming counterexample about anticipated future irrationality -- like, I know for a fact that in an hour I'm going to be pretty drunk, or that tomorrow someone's giving me a drug that messes with memory, so I have every reason to think my future credence will be a worse guide to the truth than my current one, not an equally good one. Reflection still seems to tell me to defer to that future credence anyway, which feels exactly backwards. Someone in the seminar mentioned van Fraassen has some way of restricting the principle to dodge this -- something like, it's only meant to hold when you have no specific reason to doubt your future rationality -- but I couldn't follow whether that actually rescues the principle or just hollows it out. Could you walk through, fairly intuitively: why the Dutch-book argument is supposed to actually force Reflection on a rational agent rather than merely describe one, how exactly the drunk/drugged cases generate a real counterexample rather than a case Reflection was never meant to cover in the first place, and whether the restricted version of the principle keeps enough content to still be interesting once you've built that escape clause in?
