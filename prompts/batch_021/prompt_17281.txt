I'm a PhD student in philosophy of biology, and I run a small interview series for our department's working group on evolutionary theory. Next week's guest is an evolutionary theorist known for defending strong gene selectionism -- the view that the gene is the only genuine unit of selection, and that any apparently group- or organism-level selection story is ultimately just a convenient redescription. His stock reply whenever someone raises a group-selection-flavored case is that it's "kin selection wearing a group-selection costume." Draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having him restate the view: I want at least one question that uses the Price-equation equivalence between gene-level and group-level partitions of fitness to ask what, if anything, makes the gene the "real" unit rather than merely the most convenient bookkeeping choice; one on eusocial superorganisms such as ant or naked-mole-rat colonies, asking whether his account can explain colony-level organization without quietly reintroducing the higher-level individuality it's supposed to dissolve; and one that uses Sober's selection-for/selection-of distinction to ask whether his position has in effect shifted from a causal claim about where selection acts to a merely pragmatic claim about which bookkeeping is more convenient. For each, add a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give.
