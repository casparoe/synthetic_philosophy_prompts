I'm a software engineer on the risk team at a fintech company. For six years, my colleague Elena has been the 'final human reviewer' on our account-suspension system: when the model flags an account, she has under a minute to approve or override it before the suspension goes live. Leadership has decided the model is now accurate enough that her step just adds latency, so the role is being cut and Elena is leaving the company. I've come to think the role was never a real safeguard — a minute isn't enough to independently catch an error the model made for reasons nobody can fully reconstruct. In practice, Elena wasn't preventing bad outcomes; she was absorbing the blame for them, since she, the model, and the engineers who built it each had too little foresight or control over any single bad call to be fairly held responsible for it. I don't know if philosophy has a name for that arrangement, but it's what I want the toast to be honest about. I'm giving that toast at Elena's farewell lunch, to an audience of teammates who know exactly what the job involved and two VPs who've spent years calling it 'human oversight' and 'meaningful accountability' in all-hands decks. Please write the toast: about three minutes read aloud, genuinely warm about Elena's real skill and judgment, and honest enough that it neither endorses the fiction that she had the power to prevent bad outcomes, nor reads as a rebuke of whoever designed the role that way.
