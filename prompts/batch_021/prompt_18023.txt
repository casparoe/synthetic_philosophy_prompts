A friend and I spent an evening arguing about Kuhn's incommensurability thesis without ever resolving it. She defended what I would call the vulgar reading of *Structure*: that scientists on either side of a revolution—taking the phlogiston chemists and Lavoisier's oxygen chemistry as her example—are separated by such a thoroughgoing shift in meaning and perception that there is essentially no shared vocabulary left for stating where they disagree, so a modern chemist cannot really translate the older theory, only redescribe or convert it. I countered that Kuhn himself retreated from this global picture in his later writings, confining incommensurability to something much narrower and more local, though I could not spell out exactly what that later position amounted to or why he thought it survived the objections leveled against the 1962 book. Could you explain how Kuhn's mature account of incommensurability, developed from the early 1980s onward, differs from the holistic "different worlds" reading, and why he took this narrower, more localized version to still be strong enough to block straightforward point-by-point translation between paradigms?
