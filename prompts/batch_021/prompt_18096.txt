This semester I'm taking a seminar cross-listed between the philosophy and economics departments on the epistemic foundations of game theory, and we recently devoted a week to Aumann's agreement theorem and the common prior assumption it depends on. The basic result — that two Bayesians who share a prior cannot knowingly persist in disagreement once their posteriors on some event become common knowledge — resurfaced in an argument after class between two classmates from the seminar, Marcus and Elena, which I observed without joining in.

It went roughly as follows. (1) Marcus pointed out that he and his study partner still disagree, even after laying out their full reasoning to each other, about whether the central bank will cut rates next quarter, and argued that by Aumann's theorem this means at least one of them must be irrational, since two people who trust each other's rationality and have common knowledge of each other's posteriors cannot remain in genuine disagreement. (2) Elena objected that this only follows if the two of them actually share a prior to begin with, which is never stated and shouldn't just be assumed: priors are supposed to be the subjective starting point that precedes any evidence, so nothing is irrational about two people simply starting out with different priors and never converging. (3) Marcus replied that this is exactly what the Harsanyi doctrine is meant to rule out: a rational agent's prior is supposed to reflect a common, pre-informational starting point, so any difference in belief between two people should ultimately trace back to differences in the information they've received rather than to some brute, unexplained difference in priors — meaning that if you traced both of their beliefs back far enough, they had to have agreed at the outset, so their present disagreement is itself evidence that someone is mishandling their information. (4) Elena answered that this move doesn't resolve the puzzle so much as relocate it: it posits an unobservable, pre-evidential state that can never actually be checked, which makes the doctrine unfalsifiable, and she added that real financial markets are full of trades between people who simply interpret the same public information differently — exactly the kind of trade that no-trade theorems say shouldn't occur if traders genuinely had common priors and common knowledge of each other's rationality. (5) Marcus answered that this proves less than Elena thinks: no-trade theorems describe idealized traders whose only motive is exploiting private information, whereas real markets are full of hedging, liquidity needs, and noise traders with no stake in being right, so aggregate trading volume can't be read off as evidence against the common prior assumption as a norm for genuinely truth-seeking agents like him and his study partner.
