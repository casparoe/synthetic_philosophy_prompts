Since retiring from a research career in physiology—most of it spent on organ preservation for transplantation—I have taken up philosophy in earnest, and I find myself repeatedly drawn back to a question that used to be, for me, simply a matter of clinical protocol: what actually entitles us to treat circulatory death and whole-brain death as two routes to the very same event, rather than as two distinct events we have simply agreed to treat as interchangeable. A former colleague who now teaches a graduate seminar in bioethics has enlisted my help grading short written responses this term, on the strength of that background, and I would like an independent assessment of this one before I finalize my own comments.

Below is the question, followed by one student's answer.

Question: The Uniform Determination of Death Act allows death to be established either by irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory function, or by irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem. Explain the philosophical rationale for treating these as two ways of establishing the same underlying event, and discuss one significant challenge to that rationale.

Student's answer:

Death has traditionally been understood as the permanent loss of the organism's capacity to function as an integrated whole, and both prongs of the UDDA are meant to track this same underlying event. The circulatory-respiratory standard holds that once the heart and lungs permanently stop working, the body can no longer sustain itself as a unit, so the organism has died. The whole-brain standard holds that once the entire brain, including the brainstem, permanently stops working, the patient has irreversibly lost all capacity for consciousness and awareness, and should be regarded as dead even if a ventilator keeps the heart beating and blood circulating.

The two standards are treated as equivalent because, outside the ICU, each failure quickly brings on the other: without brain function the brainstem can no longer drive breathing, so the heart soon stops from lack of oxygen, while without a heartbeat the brain is starved of blood and dies within minutes. So although the two criteria look at different organs, they pick out the same moment, just approached from different ends of the same collapse.

The most serious challenge to this picture comes from the neurologist Alan Shewmon, who documented cases of patients correctly diagnosed as whole-brain dead whose bodies nonetheless continued, with ventilator support, to grow, heal wounds, fight infections, and, in one case, gestate a fetus to term, sometimes for years. Shewmon argued that this showed that the real criterion for death should be the permanent loss of the capacity for consciousness and mental life, since that is what is irreversibly lost in these patients despite their persisting bodily integration; he used the evidence to argue for shifting the legal definition of death away from the whole brain and toward a higher-brain, personhood-based standard.
