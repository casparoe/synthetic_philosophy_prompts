I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, but I've been reading around in philosophy of probability, and I've gotten stuck on something in David Lewis's account of objective chance.

Lewis's Principal Principle says a rational agent's credence in an event should equal the objective chance of that event, conditional on all "admissible" evidence -- which reads like a fairly intuitive calibration constraint. The trouble is the "undermining" problem, which Lewis himself called the "big bad bug" for his own Humean, best-system theory of chance. On that theory, the chance of an event at a time t is fixed by the best system for the entire history of the world -- future included -- not simply by what's happened up to t. That leaves room for a possible future which has some positive chance of happening right now, but which, were it to happen, would mean the chances at t were never quite what we thought they were. So the Principal Principle tells me to set my credence in that future equal to today's chance value, while my very knowledge of that value seems to let me reason my way to its negation.

Could you walk me through exactly where the contradiction bites, using whatever simple example makes it clearest? I'd also like to understand why simply calling the chance information "inadmissible" doesn't trivially dissolve the problem, how Lewis's later "New Principle" is supposed to fix things without giving up on best-system chances altogether, and whether that fix is generally regarded as having succeeded.
