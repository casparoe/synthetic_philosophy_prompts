Ok so I'm plotting a scene in my new novel where my protagonist wakes up after an accident totally cut off from her senses -- no sight, no touch, can't even feel where her limbs are -- and the first coherent thought she has is "well, at least I know I'm still here." Did some digging and it turns out this is basically Avicenna's old flying man thought experiment: a guy created hovering in mid-air, blindfolded, limbs spread so they don't even touch each other, no memories to draw on, and Avicenna says he'd still affirm his own existence even though he couldn't affirm having a body at all. Before I decide how my character's scene actually plays out I want to stress-test the argument, so could you write me a dialogue between two philosophers going at it over whether it actually holds up?

One should defend it -- that it genuinely shows self-awareness is immediate, doesn't depend on the body, and that this can be leveraged into the soul being a separate thing from the body. The other should be a real critic, not just "obviously you need a body to think" but something sharper: even on Avicenna's own psychology, the internal senses that would be doing any actual imagining or remembering are seated in bodily organs, so how's he so sure its the immaterial intellect doing the "I exist" work and not some leftover bodily residue -- basically, there's a gap between being aware of yourself without being aware of your body, and your self actually being separate from your body (people raise the same complaint about descartes). I want real back and forth, both equally sharp, actually engaging each other's points instead of talking past each other or folding.
