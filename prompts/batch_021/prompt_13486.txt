"Molinism takes its name from the sixteenth-century Jesuit Luis de Molina, who introduced the doctrine of middle knowledge specifically to solve the logical problem of evil later given its classic formulation by Mackie and addressed by Plantinga: how an omnipotent, perfectly good God could permit the amount and kinds of suffering we find in the world. Molina's solution rests on dividing God's knowledge into three logical, not temporal, moments. First, God's natural knowledge covers every necessary truth and every metaphysically possible state of affairs, including every possible free creature and everything it could possibly do; this is settled independently of anything God decides. Third, after creation, God's free knowledge covers the contingent truths about the one world he has actually brought about. Sandwiched between these is middle knowledge: God's knowledge of what every possible free creature would do in every possible circumstance it could be placed in, expressed in 'counterfactuals of creaturely freedom' like 'if Adam were in circumstances C, Adam would freely eat the apple.' Since God determines which of these counterfactuals are true, he can survey all the resulting complete scenarios and freely decree that the one history matching his providential purposes become actual, while each creature's choice within that history remains entirely its own. This is how Molinists can say God providentially controls even free actions without causing them.

The most serious problem is the grounding objection, pressed by Robert Adams: since counterfactuals of freedom are supposed to be true logically prior to God's decree and before the creature in question ever exists to make them true, it's unclear what could possibly make them true in the first place, since they're neither necessary truths nor the product of anybody's choice."
