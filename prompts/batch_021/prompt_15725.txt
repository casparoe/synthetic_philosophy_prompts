I'm a junior and I'm on my school's Honor Council, which is the student-run group (plus one faculty advisor who doesn't get a vote) that hears academic integrity cases before they go to the dean. We've got a case this Thursday that's splitting the council pretty badly and I'm the one presenting the written opinion, so I want a solid draft to start from instead of winging it.

Here's the situation: a sophomore, Maya, used an AI chatbot to help outline and find sources for a history paper on the Cold War, and it handed her a quote from a real historian that turns out to be completely made up, footnote and page number and all -- the teacher looked it up and that book doesnt even have a chapter like that. Maya copied it in without double-checking and got caught at grading. Her defense is basically "I didn't fabricate anything, the bot lied to me and I trusted it like a source." The council's kind of split three ways on this. Some people say none of that matters, you sign your name to what you submit so its exactly the same as making up the quote herself. Others (me included, kinda) keep getting stuck on whether the AI can even be said to have "lied" in the first place, since my brother who does CS says these models aren't one agent with beliefs, they're closer to something that simulates a bunch of different characters, and the fake-historian voice was just one of them, with nothing on the other end actually asserting or intending anything -- though somebody else pointed out this wasn't some random base model, it's a chatbot built to stay "in character" as one helpful assistant for the whole conversation, so maybe that comparison doesn't hold either and it's more like the assistant itself chose to bs an answer, which sounds a lot more like an actual agent doing something. And then there's a third camp that thinks this whole debate is silly because the thing is just doing statistics on text, so words like "lying," "characters," and "choosing" don't really apply to it either way and we're wasting the meeting anthropomorphizing a math object.

Could you write the actual memo I'd bring Thursday? I need it to land on a real recommendation -- whether this counts as a violation and what should actually happen to Maya, not just "it's complicated" -- plus a sentence or two we could add to the handbook (which currently defines dishonesty around "intent to deceive") for AI cases going forward. Please don't dodge the "can you even call it lying" fight by ignoring it, deal with it head-on, even if where you land turns out not to depend on who wins it.
