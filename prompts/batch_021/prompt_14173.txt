"Billy and Suzy each throw a rock at a glass bottle. Suzy's throw is a little faster, so her rock arrives first and shatters the bottle; a split second later, Billy's rock flies through the space where the bottle used to be. Had Suzy not thrown, Billy's rock would have shattered the bottle instead. Using this case, explain how (a) Lewis's counterfactual theory of causation and (b) an interventionist theory in the tradition of Woodward and Pearl each try to establish that Suzy's throw, rather than Billy's, is what caused the bottle to shatter. Do the two theories reach this verdict by the same route, or do they diverge at some point? (Around 500 words.)"

And here is the student's answer:

"Lewis's 1973 theory analyzes causation as the ancestral of counterfactual dependence: c is a cause of e if there is a chain of events linking them such that each event in the chain counterfactually depends on the one immediately before it. This chain requirement, rather than a single direct dependence between c and e, is exactly what lets Lewis handle Suzy and Billy. The shattering does not directly counterfactually depend on Suzy's throw, since even without her throw the bottle would still have shattered courtesy of Billy. But if we track the intermediate stages of Suzy's throw -- the rock leaving her hand, its flight, its impact -- each stage depends on the one before it, and the shattering depends on the impact of her rock specifically. No analogous chain connects Billy's throw to the shattering, since his rock never impacts anything. So the chain of stepwise dependence, unlike the simple one-step version, correctly picks out Suzy rather than Billy as the cause.

The interventionist approach recasts the same case using structural equations and Pearl's do-operator. We build a causal graph with a variable for each throw and one for the shattering, and ask a comparative question: does the value of the shattering variable change if we alter the value of the variable for Suzy's throw, holding fixed the actual values of the other variables, in particular Billy's throw? Operationally, this comes down to comparing the probability of shattering given that Suzy throws to the probability of shattering given that she doesn't, with Billy's throw controlled for. Since Billy's rock would shatter the bottle regardless, holding Billy's throw fixed while flipping Suzy's does not change the outcome, so on the most straightforward reading, the do-operator test says Suzy's throw is not a cause -- the wrong verdict. More careful interventionists such as Halpern and Pearl repair this by not holding every other variable fixed at its actual value, but instead letting some variables, such as which rock actually reaches the bottle first, be treated as part of the contingency under which the test is run, which restores the intuitively correct verdict that Suzy's throw is the cause.

So although Lewis and the interventionists ultimately agree that Suzy's throw is the cause, they get there differently: Lewis needs nothing beyond the original 1973 machinery of stepwise counterfactual chains, whereas the interventionists have to move past the simplest version of the do-operator test to a more refined notion of holding some, but not all, variables fixed. If anything, this suggests that Lewis's theory was already in good shape on this point, since the graph-theoretic apparatus needed a comparably significant patch to reach the same conclusion."
