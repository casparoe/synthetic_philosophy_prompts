In my seminar on the philosophy of machine learning, the assigned readings have pushed a fairly optimistic line: that mechanistic interpretability is gradually vindicating realism about neural network representations, since sparse autoencoders can recover clean, monosemantic "features" out of what were previously polysemantic neurons. For my next response paper, I want to argue against that consensus. Please argue that the superposition phenomenon it presupposes—networks that have far fewer neurons than there are task-relevant features, and which cope by encoding those features as overlapping, non-orthogonal directions in activation space, so that individual neurons end up firing for several unrelated concepts at once—actually supports a deflationary, instrumentalist reading of "representation" talk about neural networks, rather than the realist reading the field seems to be converging on. The argument should directly confront the obvious rejoinder that sparse-autoencoder-recovered features are still "real patterns" in Dennett's sense: compressible, predictively exploitable regularities that don't need a uniquely privileged decomposition in order to count as real.
