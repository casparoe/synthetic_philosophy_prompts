I spent the better part of four decades as a theoretical physicist working mainly in cosmology, and only since retiring have I had the leisure to read philosophy properly rather than in the odd stolen hour. I have recently been working through David Lewis's *On the Plurality of Worlds*, and his modal realism -- the thesis that absolutely every way a world could possibly be is a way some world actually is, with all of these worlds equally concrete and equally real, ours being distinguished from the rest only by our being part of it -- struck me immediately as a philosopher's rediscovery of ideas I had already met in my own field: the endlessly budding pocket universes of eternal inflation, the string theorists' landscape of vacua, and, in its more expansive readings, the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics. When I raised this parallel at a philosophy reading group I have recently joined, I was told, kindly but firmly, that this was a rather naive conflation, and that philosophers are careful to keep Lewis's picture distinct from any physical multiverse hypothesis.

I would like your help setting me straight. Is the difference between Lewis's plurality of worlds and these physical multiverses really just one of degree -- more worlds, more variety, less empirical constraint -- or does it run deeper than that? I am especially keen to understand how the two pictures compare on three counts: first, what each view is actually trying to accomplish (a physical theory extending known laws to their natural conclusion, versus a metaphysical analysis meant to ground the truth conditions of modal claims as such); second, whether the worlds involved are meant to be isolated from one another in the same strict sense, given that Lewis individuates worlds by spatiotemporal and causal connectedness rather than by anything a cosmologist would recognize as a "universe"; and third, just how much variation each view actually permits from world to world -- different constants and initial conditions on the one hand, versus, on Lewis's view if I understand him rightly, worlds with wholly different laws of nature or even different fundamental ontologies altogether. The many-worlds interpretation is the case I personally find hardest to keep apart from Lewis's picture, so I would welcome your using it as a running example if that would help.
