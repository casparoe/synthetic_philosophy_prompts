I spent thirty-five years as a paleontologist working mostly on the functional morphology of brachiopod and gastropod shells, and only since retiring have I had the leisure to read the philosophy of science that grew up around debates I lived through as a working scientist without ever reflecting on them properly. Below is a short essay I have drafted for a reading group of former colleagues on Gould and Lewontin's 1979 spandrels paper — I actually heard Gould present a version of the argument at a conference around that time, though I trust my memory of the occasion less than I once did. Before circulating it, I would value your comments on three things in particular: whether I have reconstructed the argument and its reception fairly; whether the distinction I draw between a "methodological" and a "metaphysical" reading of the critique is genuine or a false dichotomy of my own devising; and whether my closing example actually supports my thesis or quietly undermines it. Please be candid rather than encouraging.

Spandrels, Pendentives, and the Limits of a Metaphor

Gould and Lewontin's "Spandrels of San Marco" is probably the most famous two-word phrase to come out of twentieth-century evolutionary biology, and I suspect its staying power owes as much to the vividness of its central image as to the rigor of the argument that image is meant to carry. The image is architectural: the four curved triangular spaces created when a dome is raised on rounded arches are not, on this telling, independently designed; they are a forced by-product of setting a round dome over a square base, afterward decorated as though they had been the whole point. Biological structures, the authors argued, are too often explained the same way in reverse — treated as adaptations finely tuned by selection for their present role, when they may really be by-products ("spandrels") of some other adaptation, or of developmental and structural constraints having nothing to do with fitness.

Two things have nagged at me on rereading the paper. First, the architecture may be shakier than the metaphor needs it to be: I recall, though I can no longer trace the source, architectural historians objecting that the spaces pictured at San Marco are better described as pendentives than as spandrels proper, and that a dome can in any case be mounted without generating any such "obligatory" space at all — squinches being the standard alternative in Byzantine and Islamic building — which would make the necessity Gould and Lewontin lean on a matter of stylistic choice rather than structural law. Second, and more importantly, I think the paper does two different jobs that its critics and champions alike tend to run together: a methodological one (do not accept an adaptive story merely because it is plausible; test it against explicit alternatives, including non-adaptive ones) and a metaphysical one (a substantial fraction of the traits organisms have are not themselves targets of selection at all). The methodological point seems to me nearly unanswerable and has aged very well; the metaphysical one is doing far more work than the architecture actually licenses, since a shaky filler space is at best a picture of how a by-product could arise, not evidence of how common by-products actually are.

As a test of my own thesis, take the human chin, which I have seen treated in the recent literature as a serious candidate spandrel: on one reading, its shape falls out as an incidental consequence of reductions in jaw robustness driven by changes in diet and chewing stress over the course of human evolution, the chin itself never having been selected for anything; on a rival reading, it is defended as a genuine target of sexual selection or social signaling. We can only settle which reading is right, it seems to me, by doing exactly the comparative, hypothesis-testing work that Gould and Lewontin's methodological point demands of us — which would suggest that the lasting content of their paper survives the collapse of its own showcase example. I would like to know whether that conclusion is actually warranted, or whether I am simply being generous to two authors whose rhetoric I otherwise find rather overbearing.
