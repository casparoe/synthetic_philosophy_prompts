Okay so this has been bugging me since our postmortem last week and nobody on my team gave me an answer that actually satisfies me, so I'm bringing it here.

I'm a backend engineer at a ~40 person startup. We had an outage a couple weeks back — a Postgres box filled up its disk over a long weekend and checkout was down for about 5 hours before anyone noticed. Extra annoying because a smaller version of this basically already occured last year and got waved off the same way. Anyway, in the postmortem doc, the "root cause" got written up as "no disk-usage alert configured on that instance." Fine, sure, that's technically true. But the more I think about it the more it falls apart for me. Because it's ALSO true that: the on-call engineer that weekend (not me) didnt proactively pull up the dashboard even though she easily could have; I didn't check that box either, and I have the exact same prod access she does; whoever originally provisioned that instance two years ago didn't size the disk bigger, which would've bought enough headroom that this never happens; and honestly, every single one of the ~15 people at our company with prod AWS access, who at any point in the last two years did not add that alert, is — by the same logic — someone whose inaction would've prevented the outage had they acted differently. If "this would've prevented it and they didn't do it" is what makes something count as a cause, I don't see a non-arbitrary place to stop before you're implicating basically everyone with access. But obviously nobody's going to write "15 engineers, collectively" as the root cause in the doc, and it also feels wrong to say I personally caused the outage just because I too never got around to adding an alert to a box that isn't mine.

When I pushed on this, my manager just said "it's about whose job it was to catch it," but that feels like it's dodging the question rather than answering it — either whose-job-it-was is doing actual causal work, in which case I want to know what that work is exactly, or it's just us picking someone to hold responsible after the fact and calling that pick the "root cause" to make it sound more objective than it is. So which is it? Is there a real, principled basis for singling out one missing action as THE cause instead of the twenty other equally-true missing actions sitting right next to it, or is "root cause analysis" for this kind of missing-action stuff basically just structured scapegoating dressed up as engineering rigor? I'd like an actual argued answer here, not a "depends on context" shrug.
