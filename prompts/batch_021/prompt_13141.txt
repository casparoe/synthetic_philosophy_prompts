"Non-reductive physicalism claims that mental properties are not identical with physical properties, even though every mental event is nothing over and above some physical event, because a mental property is instantiated only in virtue of the instantiation of a physical property that necessitates it (this is the supervenience relation). Kim's causal exclusion argument tries to show that this combination is unstable. It starts from the causal closure of the physical: every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause, a principle usually taken to rule out the existence of any non-physical causes or properties in the first place. Suppose a mental event M, some desire, causes a physical event P*, an arm movement. M has a physical supervenience base P, and by closure, P alone already qualifies as a sufficient cause of P*. So P* has two candidate causes, M and P. Because M and P are not independent of one another the way two separately fired bullets are, Kim denies that this is an ordinary case of causal overdetermination; instead, one of the two must be doing no real causal work, and since closure guarantees P is sufficient on its own, it is M that gets screened off. Mental properties turn out to be epiphenomenal unless they can be identified outright with physical properties.

One influential response is Sydney Shoemaker's subset view of realization. Shoemaker denies that M and P are ever rival causes to begin with. Because a mental property like pain is multiply realizable -- realized by C-fibers in humans, something else in octopuses, perhaps silicon in a sufficiently sophisticated robot -- pain itself must carry all of the causal powers exercised by any of its possible realizers, so a physical realizer's causal powers are only ever a subset of the causal powers belonging to the mental property it realizes. Whenever P causes P*, then, it does so by exercising powers already included among M's powers, so crediting the causing to M steals nothing from P. This lets the non-reductive physicalist reject Kim's exclusion principle without accepting brute overdetermination, since M and P were never competing for the same causal role in the first place. I think the response succeeds: once the powers of one are wholly included in the powers of the other, there is simply nothing left for exclusion to exclude, and Kim's argument loses its grip."
