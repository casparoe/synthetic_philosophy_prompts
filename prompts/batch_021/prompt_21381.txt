So this happened during the just war theory proseminar I TA for (I'm a PhD student in political philosophy, loosely working on ethics of armed conflict) -- we'd just finished the McMahan chapters on liability to lethal force, specifically the bit where he goes after the traditional "moral equality of combatants," and two of the students, Theo and Nadia, got into it for a good twenty minutes after everyone else had basically checked out. I didn't referee it in the moment and now I can't stop turning it over.

Theo opened by running McMahan's line pretty much straight: an unjust combatant is morally liable to be killed because he's contributing to an unjustified lethal threat, and that's true independent of whether he personally believes his cause is just -- the traditional idea that soldiers on both sides have "equal" moral standing is, on this view, basically a legal/institutional convenience that Walzer dresses up as a deep moral truth. Nadia pushed back with the obvious epistemic objection: ordinary soldiers, especially conscripts, have no reliable way of knowing whether their state's war satisfies jus ad bellum -- that's a job for statesmen and diplomats, not an 19-year-old with a rifle, so it seems unfair to ground someone's liability to be killed in facts they had no real access to. Theo came back with the standard McMahan move: liability doesn't require culpability, you can be a totally non-culpable threat -- coerced, deceived, whatever -- and still be liable to have force used against you to stop you, same way it'd be permissible to use force against someone who'd been hypnotized into attacking you. So the soldier can be excused (blameless, shouldn't be punished) while still being a legitimate target in the sense that matters on the battlefield. Nadia's comeback was that this proves too much: once bare, non-culpable causal contribution to an unjust threat is enough for liability, there's no principled way to stop the circle expanding outward to the farmer growing food for the army or the factory worker making the rifles, who are "contributing" too. Theo tried to patch this with a directness/proximity requirement -- liability needs a sufficiently direct and substantial causal contribution, not just any contribution -- and Nadia said "sufficiently direct and substantial" is doing all the work there, and that without a principled way of cashing it out, his fix just quietly reimports the very intuitions the responsibility-based account was supposed to ground rather than assume.

Could you go through this move by move and say who's got the better of it at each step, rather than just picking an overall winner, and flag anything a specialist in this literature would notice that neither of them raised? Also curious (and this is the bit I feel bad about) -- right after, someone brought up Walzer's or maybe Benbaji's convention-style defense of combatant equality and I didn't write down how it was supposed to bear on Nadia's last point. Do you think reaching for something like that would actually give her a stronger version of her objection than the one she made on her own, or is it a different kind of move entirely that doesn't really help her there?
