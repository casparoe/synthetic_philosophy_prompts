Weird week: in my Social Epistemology seminar we just spent two classes on the generalism vs. particularism debate about conspiracy theories (Cassam's stuff about epistemic vices and propaganda vs. Dentith's argument that "conspiracy theory" just means a theory that posits a conspiracy, and some of those are literally true, so you can't reject one just for being a member of the category) — and completely separately, I sit on my university's Student Senate Speaker & Events Board, which decides whether to fund and give space to outside speakers that clubs want to bring to campus. Those two parts of my week are now colliding and I could use help writing something.

A club has asked us for $2,000 and the auditorium to host an alum who wants to give a talk arguing that last spring's IT data breach (which the university confirmed happened and quietly settled a lawsuit over) wasn't an accident, but an inside job tied to a data-sharing deal with an ed-tech vendor, and that the sealed settlement, the vague official statement, and a staffer's since-deleted LinkedIn post are all evidence of a cover up rather than normal breach-response mess. About half the board wants to deny it outright, on the grounds that its basically unfalsifiable (any lack of evidence just becomes more evidence of the cover-up) and we shouldn't lend university money and a room to that structure of reasoning, full stop. The other half, including me, keeps pointing out there IS a real settlement and real unanswered questions, and that rejecting a talk just because it fits the "conspiracy theory" pattern, without engaging what's actually being claimed, is exactly the question-begging move our particularism reading warns about. Our bylaws just say we can deny funding if a talk's "central premise lacks a reasonable evidentiary basis," with no further guidance on who decides that or how. We reconvene in ten days and owe the club an answer.

Could you write the memo I'd bring to that meeting? I need it to actually land somewhere — approve, deny, or approve-with-conditions (and if conditions, name them) — with that ten-day timeline built in, not a wishy washy "both sides have a point." And I really want it to take the losing side's best argument seriously rather than caricaturing it, since both "all conspiracy talk is automatically illegitimate" and "any institutional skepticism toward this stuff is just suppressing whistleblowers" feel like cop-outs to me, and I suspect they'd feel like cop-outs to my professor too.
