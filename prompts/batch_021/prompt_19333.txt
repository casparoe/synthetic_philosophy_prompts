I'm a philosophy PhD student finishing a dissertation chapter on the limits of testimonial knowledge, and my advisor keeps pressing me to treat the aesthetic case on its own terms rather than folding it into the more familiar debate over moral testimony. The question turned concrete for me last weekend: a friend and I were standing in front of an unfamiliar painting in a gallery, and she was ready to call it a masterpiece purely on the strength of the wall label's quotation from a well-known critic, while I found myself oddly unwilling to accept that verdict until I had actually looked hard at the canvas myself — despite having no comparable qualms about taking her word for, say, the year the gallery opened.

I'd like you to write a dialogue, set in that same gallery, between a pessimist about aesthetic testimony — someone drawn to something like Wollheim's acquaintance principle, who holds that a bare critical verdict relayed on a wall label cannot by itself ground a warranted aesthetic judgment — and an optimist, who holds that aesthetic testimony works no differently from testimony about anything else. Have the optimist press the pessimist to say whether the objection is that testimony could never yield aesthetic knowledge even in principle, or rather that it could, but some further norm — autonomy, authenticity, something along those lines — forbids relying on it anyway, since the two versions call for quite different replies. I'd also like the exchange to take up the fact that people who do rely on aesthetic testimony tend to hedge their claims ("I hear it's supposed to be devastating") in a way they don't hedge testimony about mundane facts, with each character offering a competing explanation of why, and to have the optimist press the further point that we routinely decide which films or exhibitions are worth our time on nothing more than a friend's or critic's say-so. Neither side should concede easily or simply restate their opening position, and the conversation is welcome to end without a tidy resolution.
