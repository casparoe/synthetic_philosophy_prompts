I'm taking a formal epistemology seminar this term, and a couple weeks back we covered I.J. Good's old result that the expected value of free evidence is never negative for a Bayesian agent who updates by conditionalization -- basically, looking at more (costless) information before you act can never make your expected payoff worse, only better or the same. The instructor presented it almost as a throwaway, this cute little theorem that's practically trivial once you see the proof, but it's been bugging me since, because it seems like it should generalize way past individual decisions.

Here's what I can't figure out: doesn't this basically settle the whole debate about transparency? If a rational Bayesian literally can't be hurt in expectation by having more information before deciding, shouldn't it follow that institutions -- governments, insurers, financial markets, whatever -- should just default to maximum public disclosure, since more information floating around can only make outcomes better overall, never worse? I brought this up with a friend doing econ and she said something like "that stops being true once there's more than one person in the room," but couldn't really spell out why. Is she right, and if she is, what exactly breaks once you go from one Bayesian deciding alone to a bunch of seperate people interacting with each other? On the face of it, Good's result feels like the kind of thing that should just add up across people, not fall apart.
