In a graduate game theory seminar, we recently spent a session on the centipede game: two players alternately choose either to end the game right away by "taking" the pot that has accumulated so far, or to "pass" and let the pot grow further, handing the next decision to their opponent. Backward induction delivers a famously severe verdict here: since taking is better than passing at the very last possible move, and since this is supposedly common knowledge between the players, the second-to-last mover should take as well, and so on back to the very first move, so that two "rational" players are predicted to end the game immediately and forgo all the joint gains that a few more rounds of mutual passing would have produced. What struck me more than the familiar empirical complaint — that real players tend to keep passing for quite a while — was the subtler puzzle that game theorists sometimes call the "backward induction paradox": the argument seems to require common knowledge of rationality to hold at every single node, including nodes that, by the argument's own logic, should never be reached if the players really are rational. So once a player has actually watched their opponent pass several times in a row, is "take now, because backward induction says so" still obviously the right conclusion, or has the very history that produced this node already undercut the assumption the argument needs?

Write a dialogue between two equally sharp game theorists who find themselves at some middle node of a centipede game, having just watched several rounds of mutual passing unfold. One argues, in the spirit of Robert Aumann's celebrated proof that common knowledge of rationality mathematically forces the backward-induction outcome, that taking right now remains the uniquely rational move no matter what path led to this node. The other argues, in the spirit of the objection that Philip Pettit and Robert Sugden raised under the heading of the backward induction paradox, that the pattern of passing just observed has already undermined the common-knowledge-of-rationality assumption on which the first theorist's confidence depends, so that nothing so clean as "take now" straightforwardly follows any longer. The two should engage directly with each other's reasoning about what, if anything, can still be inferred about the opponent's rationality and likely future play at this point in the game, and each should press the other on what they take to be the weakest link in their opponent's position, rather than simply restating their opening view.
