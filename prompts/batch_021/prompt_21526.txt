Here's the question I wrote:

Explain (a) why ordinary probability theory assumes "logical omniscience" and why that's a problem for a computationally bounded reasoner, (b) what the logical induction criterion actually requires of a sequence of credences over sentences, precisely enough that someone could check whether a given sequence satisfies it, and (c) why a logical inductor's credence in an unresolved conjecture (like the twin prime conjecture) should move around before anyone finds a proof, instead of just sitting at some flat prior until a proof shows up.

And here's my friend's answer:

"Standard probability theory assumes logical omniscience: a rational agent has to assign probability 1 to every logical/mathematical truth and 0 to every falsehood, and its credences have to be closed under logical implication instantly. That's fine for an agent with unlimited computing power, but no real reasoner can actually derive all the consequences of the axioms it accepts -- e.g. nobody currently knows whether the twin prime conjecture is a theorem, even though it's already either true or false as a matter of mathematical fact. So a bounded reasoner needs some principled way to have a graded, non-extreme credence in things like that.

Logical induction handles this by running an evolving sequence of price assignments over sentences, generated by simulating a market of "traders" who buy and sell shares that pay off 1 if a sentence is eventually proven and 0 if it's refuted. The logical induction criterion says the prices have to be efficient enough that no trader implementable in polynomial time can ever come out ahead betting against them -- basically a Dutch-book condition extended across time instead of just applying at one instant. Because the criterion rules out any efficient trader turning a profit, the prices are forced to already reflect every pattern a poly-time algorithm could detect, which is what gives logical inductors their calibration and convergence properties: for any decidable sentence you can actually compute in advance how many days it'll take before the price locks in near 0 or 1, since slower convergence would itself be an exploitable pattern.

That's also why the market doesn't wait for an actual proof before moving on something like the twin prime conjecture: partial results, heuristic density arguments, and analogous solved conjectures are themselves exploitable patterns, so a good trader bids the price up or down long before a full proof exists, and the criterion forces market prices to track that instead of sitting still."
