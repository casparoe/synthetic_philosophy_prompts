I started a job a few months ago as a control-systems engineer on the hardware side of a small quantum computing company. My actual work is closer to cryogenics and RF wiring than to physics, but I've begun sitting in on the theory team's weekly foundations reading group so I have some idea what the machine I help build is supposedly doing — which matters more than it probably should, since I've also started fielding "so particles really do talk to each other faster than light?" questions from friends and recruiters, and I'd rather stop bluffing my way through the answer. Two sessions ago the group went through the 2015 loophole-free Bell tests, including the Delft electron-spin experiment, and the conversation afterward turned into a genuine disagreement between two of the theorists, Ines and Marcus, that I've been turning over ever since, mainly because I can't tell who actually has the better of it.

The exchange went roughly like this. (1) Ines said the loophole-free results settle the matter: nature is nonlocal, full stop — entangled particles' outcomes really do depend on each other instantaneously, however far apart they are. (2) Marcus objected that this overstates what the theorem shows: Bell's argument refutes theories that are simultaneously local, that satisfy "measurement independence" (the hidden variables aren't correlated with the experimenters' choice of settings), and that assign each trial one definite outcome — and you can save locality by giving up either of the other two instead, the way superdeterministic models give up measurement independence and many-worlds models give up single outcomes. (3) Ines replied that superdeterminism isn't a serious option, since it requires a conspiracy between the particle source and the experimenters' "free" choices reaching back into their shared causal past, and that even a committed Everettian still owes an explanation of why the branches on both wings end up correlated exactly the way quantum mechanics predicts across a spacelike separation — which just sounds like nonlocality wearing a costume. (4) Marcus answered that this is precisely the open question, not a win for Ines by default: the Everettian line is that the correlation is fixed once, at the source, by the shared entangled state, and each wing's local, continuous evolution simply plays out a joint fact that was already settled there, so nothing needs to propagate between the wings afterward; whether that earns the word "local" is genuinely disputed among people who work on this, but it's a separate question from whether superdeterminism is credible, and shouldn't be dismissed by association with it. (5) Ines said that regardless, all any of these experiments actually certify is that you can't use entanglement to send a signal faster than light, which is a fact about correlations between measurement records, and that calling this "nonlocality" oversells a much narrower result. (6) Marcus answered that this collapses two things Bell himself insisted on keeping separate later in his career: mere absence of signalling, and "local causality," the much stronger requirement that a complete specification of the local state should screen an outcome off from the distant setting and the distant outcome entirely. The experiments confirm the first and refute the second, and it's that gap — not any faster-than-light message — that is the actual puzzle.
