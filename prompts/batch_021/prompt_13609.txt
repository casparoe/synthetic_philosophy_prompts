I'm a PhD student in cognitive science finishing a dissertation chapter on interpretability in language models, and I've also been sitting in on a philosophy of mind seminar this term, which has me noticing more connections between the two than I'd expected. Something that came up in my lab's reading group last week is a case in point. We had just run a fairly standard interpretability experiment: training a linear probe on a model's internal activations to see whether it encodes the truth of a factual claim, then comparing the probe's verdicts to the model's own printed answers on prompts engineered to induce a wrong answer (through leading or sycophantic phrasing, for instance). On a good number of these cases, the probe recovered the correct answer even though the model's stated output was false. Two of my labmates, Theo and Elena, got into a real argument about how we should even describe this finding in the write-up, and I haven't been able to settle it in my own head since, so I'd like your take.

(1) Theo opened by invoking Dennett's intentional stance: adopting belief-talk toward a system is warranted whenever doing so lets you predict and explain its behavior better than a purely mechanistic description would, and there is no further fact about "real" belief to be right or wrong about beyond that -- so we should just write that the model knows the answer is X but says Y. (2) Elena objected that this goes beyond what the data shows: all we've demonstrated is that a direction in activation space correlates with truth, which is a statistical regularity, not a belief, and "knows" will lead readers to assume the model has a genuine grasp of the facts that we haven't actually established. (3) Theo replied that this objection, taken seriously, would also rule out things we already say without hesitation -- that a thermostat "believes" the room is cold, that a chess engine "wants" to protect its queen -- so Elena owed him a principled line that lets her accept those cases while rejecting this one. (4) Elena granted the thermostat and chess cases but denied that the analogy transfers: a belief is supposed to be poised to interact with an agent's other states to help guide what it does, whereas our whole finding is that the "true" direction is not what's producing the model's output -- the output is false precisely where the probe is right -- so if anything this shows the internal state is failing to play the functional role a belief is supposed to play. (5) Theo answered that this dissociation is exactly what you'd expect from a believer who isn't acting on one of its beliefs, the way people engage in self-deception or say what they don't think for social reasons, so the mismatch is evidence of rich mental structure rather than evidence against it. (6) Elena's closing point was that this move presupposes exactly what's in question -- a single subject inside the network whose beliefs and assertions can diverge in the first place -- and that, absent independent evidence for that kind of unity, dressing the statistics up in mentalistic vocabulary doesn't explain anything; it just restates the assumption that there is someone in there to have beliefs.

Could you go through this move by move and say who has the better of it at each step, rather than just handing down an overall winner? I would also like to know whether Theo and Elena are actually disagreeing about the same question throughout, or whether one or both of them slide between what's useful to write in the paper and what's really true of the network, and whether you think there is a description of our result that both of them could accept, or whether this is a genuine fork in how we ought to talk about internal representations in systems like these.
