In my formal epistemology seminar this week we covered Dutch book arguments for probabilism, and afterward two classmates of mine, Marisol and Devin, kept arguing about it in the hallway while I listened in. I wrote down the exchange once I got home and would like an outside adjudication, since each of them seems right about something at a different point and I can't work out who comes out ahead overall.

(1) Marisol: The Dutch book theorem settles this. If your credences aren't probabilities, there is a set of bets, each one fair by your own lights, that together guarantee a loss no matter what happens. So rationality requires probabilism, full stop.

(2) Devin: That only shows something pragmatic — that incoherent credences make you exploitable in a made-up betting game. Whether your beliefs are thereby epistemically defective is a separate question. Plenty of things expose you to a guaranteed loss without making the exposed attitude irrational: announcing your poker strategy in advance guarantees the table can drain your stack, but nobody thinks the strategy was an epistemic error.

(3) Marisol: The bookie and the cash are just stage-dressing. What the construction really exposes is that you are pricing one and the same sure-thing prospect two different ways depending on how it is described — as "a bet on A plus a bet on not-A" versus "a bet on the tautology." Assigning different values to logically equivalent prospects is an inconsistency in your evaluative attitudes themselves, independent of whether a bookie ever shows up. That is an epistemic failing, not a pragmatic one.

(4) Devin: Granted, for a single-time case like that. But the Dutch books used to justify updating rules — for conditionalization, or for the Reflection Principle — are diachronic: they need a bet now and another bet later. Those only work if the bookie can predict which later bet you will accept, which means predicting your update rule in advance. A sufficiently foresighted agent could just decline the earlier bet once they see that accepting it, given how they are bound to act later, sets them up for a sure loss. Calling that agent irrational for declining seems to just assume that they are obligated to treat their future self's bet as already locked in.
