Retired experimental physicist here, and lately retirement has turned into a bit of a philosophy-of-science kick. I've spent the last few months slowly working through Li and Vitányi's textbook on algorithmic information theory, mostly because a former postdoc of mine who moved into ML research wouldn't stop talking about "Solomonoff induction" as some kind of gold standard for inductive reasoning, and I wanted to see for myself whether it holds up philosophically. I think I've got the basic picture: Kolmogorov complexity K(x) is the length of the shortest program that outputs x on some fixed universal machine, Solomonoff's universal prior weights each hypothesis by roughly 2^-K(x), and induction just becomes Bayesian updating with that prior baked in. Everyone describes this as finally putting Occams razor on rigorous footing, and the invariance theorem is supposed to be doing the heavy lifting there: swap the universal machine and K only shifts by some additive constant that doesn't depend on the string in question.

Here's the bit that's been nagging me. That constant is uniform across all strings, sure, but nothing in the theorem stops it from being enormous relative to the actual complexity difference between the two particular, finite hypotheses I happen to be comparing on any given Tuesday. Real science is never done "in the limit of infinitely long strings" — we're always sizing up some short-ish, finite hypotheses against each other, exactly where the invariance guarantee is at its weakest. So it seems like two people using two different, perfectly legitimate universal machines could rank the very same pair of hypotheses in opposite order of "simplicity," and the theorem gives them no way to adjudicate, since it only promises agreement asymptotically, as strings get long. It reminds me a bit of gauge freedom in physics, except there we have independent physical reasons why observables can't depend on the choice of gauge; I don't see the analogous force here compelling two reasonable machines to agree on any specific short string, rather then just usually agreeing by luck of construction. Is there something structural in the math I'm missing that actually blocks this worry, or is the machine-dependence of complexity for any given finite case basically a known soft spot that people in the field have just learned to live with?
