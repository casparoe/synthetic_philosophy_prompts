A friend and I spent most of Sunday evening arguing about longtermism, and the conversation eventually drifted somewhere I wasn't expecting: whether Leibniz, of all people, deserves to be called history's first population ethicist. Her claim was that his theodicy is really a maximization argument dressed up in theological language — God surveys every possible world contained in the divine understanding, discards whichever combinations of individual substances turn out not to be jointly compossible, and actualizes whichever compossible set contains the greatest possible amount of perfection, or reality. On her reading, this makes Leibniz a proto-utilitarian about possible worlds, and commits him, whether he liked it or not, to something very close to Parfit's Repugnant Conclusion: pack enough additional, barely-worthwhile lives into a compossible world and the total sum of perfection goes up, so his God would apparently have to prefer that crowded, mediocre world to a smaller, more flourishing one.

I pushed back on two fronts, and we never quite settled it. First, Leibniz's own reasons for calling this the best of all possible worlds don't read to me like pure quantity-maximization; he is fairly explicit that what God is after is the greatest variety together with the greatest order, the richest array of phenomena obtainable from the simplest possible laws, which sounds like a qualitative standard of harmony rather than an additive sum of local welfare. Second, and this is what really stalled the conversation, Leibniz's doctrine of the complete individual concept seems to sit badly with the apparatus Parfit needs even to state the Repugnant Conclusion or the Non-Identity Problem. If Julius Caesar's individual concept already contains his crossing the Rubicon, then there is no further person, "Caesar in a world where he stayed in Gaul," living a worse life somewhere else in modal space; there is only a distinct possible individual in a distinct possible world. It isn't obvious to me that Leibniz's God is choosing among different outcomes for the same people at all, in the way that modern population ethics generally presupposes.

What I'd like from you is a reconstruction of how Leibniz, arguing strictly from his own actual commitments — the theodicy's optimizing structure, the compossibility constraint, and the complete-concept doctrine as he defended it against Arnauld's objections — would most plausibly have handled a case like choosing between a policy that produces a smaller, more flourishing future population and one that produces a much larger but far more marginal one. Does the maximizing structure of the theodicy really commit him to something in the neighborhood of the Repugnant Conclusion, or does the order-and-harmony requirement give him principled grounds to resist it? And does the complete-concept doctrine let him dissolve the Non-Identity Problem outright, or does it just relocate the difficulty into a different question about which possible world an infinitely wise and good being would have most reason to actualize? I'd rather have your own best reconstruction of where the internal logic of his system leads than a survey of what secondary sources say about it.
