A friend of mine and I spent a good part of dinner last night arguing about tariffs, and at one point I reached for Hume to make a point I couldn't quite land. My claim was that Hume had already dismantled the mercantilist obsession with running trade surpluses back in the 1750s, in his essay "Of the Balance of Trade," through what's now usually called the price-specie-flow mechanism: any attempt to accumulate specie via a persistently favorable trade balance is self-undermining, since the resulting rise in the domestic money stock pushes up domestic prices, which makes exports less competitive and imports more attractive, draining the specie back out until some rough equilibrium reasserts itself. I even tried, probably not quite accurately, to reconstruct his thought experiment about suddenly wiping out most of Britain's money supply overnight and tracing how prices and trade would readjust. My friend countered that this is actually a different argument from the one people usually have in mind when they say "free trade is good" — namely Ricardo's comparative advantage — and that I'd been sliding between the two without noticing.

I'd like your help working out whether he was right, since neither of us fully sorted it out at the table. Could you compare Hume's specie-flow argument against mercantilism with Ricardo's comparative-advantage case for free trade, treating them as the genuinely distinct arguments they are rather than as interchangeable ways of defending the same policy conclusion? I'm especially interested in what each argument actually licenses: does Hume's mechanism give any reason to think trade between nations is mutually beneficial, or only that a persistent trade imbalance can't be sustained no matter what policy tries to do about it? And does the comparative-advantage argument depend in any way on the monetary dynamics Hume describes, or does it go through just as well in a moneyless, barter version of the story? I'd like enough substance here that I could go back to my friend with an account that actually holds together, rather than a vague assurance that Hume and Ricardo were both "pro-free-trade."
