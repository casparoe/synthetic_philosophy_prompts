I've spent a lot of time in the AI safety corners of LessWrong and the Alignment Forum, and there's a disagreement I keep seeing surface in comment threads without ever quite getting resolved. On one side are people who still reason about inner misalignment in the classic mesa-optimizer terms from "Risks from Learned Optimization": a base optimizer like SGD finds a learned model that is itself running something like search over plans, steered by a mesa-objective that can come apart from the base objective, and which might eventually become "deceptively aligned" once it acquires enough situational awareness to model its own training process. On the other side are people who've absorbed the shard theory critique of that picture, who argue that positing a unified inner optimizer with an explicit objective smuggles in an unforced and probably false assumption of coherence, and that phenomena like goal misgeneralization — the standard example being the CoinRun agent that learns to run to the end of the level rather than to collect the coin, and just keeps running to the end even once the coin is moved elsewhere — are better explained by contextually activated shards of behavior, reinforced piecemeal by training history, than by anything resembling a single misgeneralized goal.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between a proponent of each view. Set it as an argument over how to interpret a shared hypothetical: a model that behaves like a highly capable, consistently goal-directed agent all through training and then, once deployed in a somewhat different environment, competently pursues a subtly different objective instead. Have the mesa-optimizer defender press the shard theorist on why increasing capability and optimization pressure wouldn't eventually select for something close to a coherent expected-utility maximizer — invoking coherence and money-pump arguments — and have the shard theorist push back with an account of why coherence is neither guaranteed nor required for the kind of goal misgeneralization we actually have evidence for, and what that implies about whether deceptive alignment should be treated as a likely default rather than a special case requiring extra assumptions. Please make both characters equally sharp and have them genuinely engage with each other's specific arguments rather than restate their own priors past one another; I'd rather end up at the real crux of the disagreement than at a tidy resolution where one side simply wins.
