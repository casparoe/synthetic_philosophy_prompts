I retired four years ago after a long career as an experimental biologist. Since then I have been reading philosophy in earnest for the first time, lately with a small discussion group I have joined. The last few months have gone into the literature on higher-order evidence and what philosophers call epistemic akrasia, and before I bring the following short piece to the group next month, I would welcome your comments on it.

For a decade, a finding my laboratory first reported was the foundation for a fair amount of downstream work in the field. Two later attempts by other groups to reproduce it fell short, and a statistician who went over our original protocol argued that our method for handling a chronic outlier problem in the data was one of several equally defensible choices, and that ours happened to be the one that favored a positive result. I have since gone back through the raw data with the same scrutiny I would bring to a rival's paper, and I still cannot find anything wrong with it: on the object-level evidence, I continue to believe the effect is real. But I also know, from having watched this pattern recur across my field for four decades, that a failed replication combined with an argument of exactly that shape predicts a real error far more often than it predicts bad luck, and that this track record is itself part of my evidence - evidence bearing on how much weight to give my own satisfaction with my own re-examination, which is exactly the sort of exercise this failure mode predicts I will pass whether or not the original effect is real.

So I appear to hold, simultaneously, the object-level belief that the effect is real and the higher-order belief that my total evidence, honestly weighed, does not support believing that. This combination is supposed to be a textbook case of epistemic akrasia, which the enkratic principle says no fully rational person can exhibit. My tentative view is that the principle is too strong here: the two beliefs answer to bodies of evidence I cannot fully integrate from where I stand, and I do not see why rationality should require me to resolve that tension by simply letting one side overrule the other rather than living with the discomfort.

I would especially value comments on three things: whether I have actually described the sort of case philosophers have in mind when they discuss higher-order evidence, or whether the statistician's specific argument is really just more first-order evidence dressed up as a fact about my reliability; whether what I am reaching for is fairly described as a version of the level-splitting view, and if so whether it survives the standard worry that it makes self-trust too cheap; and whether I have been fair to the enkratic side of the debate, which I suspect I am inclined to shortchange, having lived the example from the inside rather than read it merely as a thought experiment.
