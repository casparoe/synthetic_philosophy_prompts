In my seminar on moral psychology this term, we've been contrasting two rival accounts of moral worth that both reject Kant's requirement that a right action must be done specifically "from duty" in order to be praiseworthy. On Julia Markovits' view, an action has moral worth just when the agent is motivated by the very considerations that in fact make the action right, whether or not she conceives of these as moral considerations at all. On Nomy Arpaly's view, by contrast, moral worth requires only that the agent's motivating desire be appropriately responsive to the action's right-making features, even if the agent explicitly disavows the correct moral verdict about her own conduct—as, Arpaly argues, is true of Huckleberry Finn, who helps Jim escape despite consciously believing this to be wrong. Since we've already spent a full session discussing that case, could you devise a different example of an action that would count as morally worthy on one of these views but not on the other, and briefly explain what exactly generates the divergence between them?
