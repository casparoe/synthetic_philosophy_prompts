Since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have spent much of my free time reading philosophy of religion, and I keep returning to the two classic branches of the cosmological argument: the kalam version and Leibniz's argument from contingency. Both aim at the same conclusion, a necessary, uncaused first cause of everything else that exists, but they reach it very differently: Leibniz relies on a fully general principle of sufficient reason (every existing thing needs an explanation, either in itself or in something else), whereas kalam relies only on the narrower premise that whatever begins to exist has a cause. This difference matters, because Leibniz's version invites the old "taxicab" objection: the principle of sufficient reason is invoked to demand an explanation for every contingent thing, then quietly set aside once we reach the necessary being. Kalam's defenders claim their narrower premise avoids this problem, since an eternal, uncaused God simply falls outside the scope of a principle that only concerns things that begin to exist. I would like your honest assessment of that claim: does kalam's narrower premise really let it escape the taxicab-style charge more cleanly than the contingency argument does, or does it merely relocate the same arbitrariness into the unargued assumption that the universe, unlike God, could not itself be beginningless? Please give a real verdict rather than a neutral summary of both positions.
