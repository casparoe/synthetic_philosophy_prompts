Two years ago I was appointed to the civilian review board that oversees use-of-force complaints against our city's police department -- one of those volunteer-committee seats you accept at a community meeting and don't think much about again until a genuinely hard case lands on your desk. We're deadlocked ahead of Thursday's hearing on exactly such a case, and I'd like your help thinking it through before I have to write it up. An officer shot a man during a stop after the man reached into his jacket pocket; the officer has said consistently, across multiple interviews, that he clearly saw a black handgun, but the object recovered from the scene was a phone. The body-camera footage from that angle and lighting doesn't settle much either way, and nothing in the officer's record or demeanor suggests he's lying about what he thought he saw.

Roughly half the board wants to treat this as an ordinary case of the split-second, imperfect-information judgment calls that our use-of-force policy is designed to excuse. The other half keeps citing the lab research on split-second shoot-or-don't-shoot decisions, which fairly reliably finds that people misidentify harmless objects as weapons more often when the person holding them is Black, and argues this should count against the officer rather than for him, since it suggests his misjudging this particular object wasn't just bad luck. I've read blogs like LessWrong and Overcoming Bias long enough to notice that this exact fork -- whether an effect like that works by coloring what someone perceives in the moment, or instead leaves the perception alone and only biases the split-second inference drawn from an admittedly ambiguous glimpse -- comes up constantly in discussions of bias, and I suspect the two camps on our board are quietly assuming opposite answers to it. Our policy, which only asks whether the officer's belief was "objectively reasonable," doesn't seem to have been written with that distinction in mind at all.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate to the full board before Thursday's hearing? I need it to land on one concrete recommendation -- sustain the complaint, clear the officer, or some calibrated middle path such as mandatory retraining without formal discipline -- argued in terms both camps could actually engage with, and including a fair, serious statement of the strongest argument on whichever side the memo doesn't ultimately favor.
