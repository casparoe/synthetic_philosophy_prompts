Write a dialogue between two philosophers standing in a sculptor's studio, looking at a just-finished clay statue that's about to get squashed back into a shapeless lump. One of them thinks there's actually two coinciding objects here -- the statue and the lump of clay -- and defends this with the usual Leibniz's Law argument: the lump will survive the squashing, the statue won't, so they can't be one and the same thing right now. The other thinks positing two objects made of exactly the same matter, arranged the exact same way, in the same place, is a bad case of multiplying entities beyond necessity, and presses the grounding problem back at them: if the two objects are total physical duplicates of each other right now, what fact makes it true that only one of them would survive the squashing? Have them actually engage with each other's strongest move instead of just repeating their own side, and see whether the one-object view can explain away the modal argument without just denying that the statue would be destroyed.
