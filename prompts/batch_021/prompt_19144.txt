So I do a bunch of Bayesian stuff at work (updating priors on model params, the usual posterior/likelihood business) and I feel pretty solid on the basic mechanics of it. But I went down a rabbit hole recently and ran into this thing called the "problem of old evidence" in philosophy of science, and its bugging me. As I understand it: if some evidence E was already known before a theory H was even proposed (classic case being Mercury's orbit before general relativity), then P(E) is already sitting at 1 in your model, so naively E can't raise P(H) at all -- yet obviously scientists treated the Mercury thing as a massive point in favor of GR. Can you explain why this counts as a genuinely hard problem for Bayesians, rather than just "well, you should've assigned a lower prior to E to begin with, you just didn't know better yet" -- and walk me through why that obvious fix runs into trouble? Bonus if you can get into how this connects (or doesn't) with the idea that Bayesian agents are supposed to be logically omniscient.
