After forty years running an experimental physics lab, I retired last year and have finally had time to read seriously in moral philosophy, something my career never left room for. Lately I've become particularly interested in Henry Rosemont Jr. and Roger Ames's account of Confucian role ethics. On their reading, the tradition has no use for the bounded, pre-social self assumed by most Western ethical theories; a person simply is their evolving nexus of concrete roles and relationships—the wu lun, or five cardinal relationships, together with kindred ones like teacher and student—cultivated through ritual practice (li) and paradigmatically expressed in filial piety (xiao). This has struck rather close to home, since retirement has quietly dissolved most of the roles that used to structure my obligations: I no longer supervise students, referee for journals, or sit on grant panels. Please describe what you take to be the most important implications of Rosemont and Ames's relational, role-constituted picture of personhood for how someone in my position should now think about their own standing, identity, and remaining duties. I'm after what actually follows from their view itself, not generic reassurance about staying busy in retirement.
