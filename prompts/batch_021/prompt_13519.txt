I spent my career as an experimental condensed-matter physicist, which let me use quantum mechanics every working day without ever having to settle what it means -- a luxury, I now realize, most of us allow ourselves. Since retiring I've been trying to make up for that, largely by working through Bell's own essays in "Speakable and Unspeakable in Quantum Mechanics" with a small reading group made up of former colleagues. Last month's session, nominally on the EPR paper, turned into a disagreement between two of the regulars that none of us managed to settle, and it has stuck with me since.

Alan, still an active experimentalist, opened by saying that Bell's theorem is routinely overstated: (1) strictly speaking, all it rules out is a deterministic local hidden-variable theory, so if you simply decline to assume that measurement outcomes are predetermined -- i.e., you drop "realism" in the EPR sense -- locality survives for free, since there is no pre-existing value left for anything, local or not, to act on. Nora, who trained in philosophy of science before moving into policy work, replied that (2) this gets Bell's own mature view backwards: in a later essay, "The Theory of Local Beables," he reformulated the locality condition -- calling it "local causality" -- precisely so that it no longer presupposes determinism; it is a screening-off condition that any theory, deterministic or genuinely stochastic, must meet to count as local, and quantum mechanics violates it under any interpretation, so dropping predetermined values does not buy locality back. Alan pushed further: (3) doesn't "local causality" still assume there is some complete, observer-independent specification of local physical facts in the two backward light cones for the screening-off condition to be a condition on -- so that if you deny outcomes are well-defined independent of some wider context (relational, or many-worlds-style), there is no longer any determinate fact for the theorem to apply to? Nora answered that (4) this escape route is real -- you needn't posit any specific hidden variable, since the condition can be imposed on whatever local facts do obtain, even "none beyond the settings" -- but it is a far more radical move than simply giving up predetermined values, since it requires denying that a measurement yields a single outcome at all, not merely that the outcome was unpredictable beforehand.

I would be grateful if you could go through this move by move and say who has the better of it at each step, rather than handing down an overall winner -- both of them struck me as making genuine points, not just repeating themselves. I am especially interested in your own verdict on where Alan and Nora end up in (3) and (4): does Bell's "local causality" condition really manage to be free of any assumption worth calling "realism," or is Alan right that some residual realist commitment -- weaker than full determinism, but a commitment nonetheless -- is still built in?
