I'm currently taking a seminar on Plato's Laws, and I want to explore a puzzle raised by the Athenian Stranger's discussion of drinking parties in Books I and II. Since he holds an expansive view of courage, on which virtue requires resisting pleasure and appetite as much as pain and fear, and Spartan-style education only drills the latter, the Stranger proposes wine as a remedy: drunk under the supervision of a sober symposiarch, it can serve as a safe, artificial stimulant of desire on which citizens practice self-mastery, much as soldiers train for danger by facing it under controlled conditions. Megillus is unconvinced, and the whole proposal sits oddly next to the Republic, where intoxication is treated simply as a cause of cowardice and intemperance, with no pedagogical upside at all. Write an essay that takes a position on the following question: since wine, unlike a merely frightening drill, impairs the very faculty that is supposed to be doing the learning, is the Stranger's argument coherent, or does the need for an external sober supervisor show that what regulated drunkenness cultivates is mere habituation rather than genuine rational self-mastery? Weigh the strongest considerations on each side and defend a definite verdict.
