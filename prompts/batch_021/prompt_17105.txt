I'm a software engineer at a small robotics startup that builds robotic arms for warehouse picking; our planning stack recently moved from a hand-coded action model to an LLM-based planner that just reads a running text description of the world state and proposes the next action. Every other Friday a few of us run an informal paper-reading group, and last week's session was background reading before we get to a recent paper on LLM-based agents: McCarthy and Hayes's original paper introducing the frame problem, Reiter's successor-state-axiom solution to it, and Dennett's "Cognitive Wheels" piece with its robots R1, R1D1, and R2D1. Two of my colleagues, Priya and Marcus, spent most of the session arguing about whether any of this is still actually relevant to what we're building, and since I'd only learned this history that same afternoon, I couldn't referee it myself.

(1) Priya opened by saying the frame problem is "basically a solved historical curiosity" for us specifically: the version McCarthy and Hayes worried about was a representational problem, where you needed a separate frame axiom for every action-fluent pair just to say what an action doesn't change, and Reiter's successor-state axioms fixed that in 1991 by folding the effects and non-effects into one axiom per fluent. She added that our planner doesn't use an axiomatized world model at all, so there's no frame axiom to write in the first place — the problem doesn't get solved so much as it stops applying.

(2) Marcus said this runs together two problems Dennett was careful to keep apart: the narrow bookkeeping problem Reiter solved, and the harder "epistemological" frame problem, which is about how any bounded reasoner picks out which of the enormous number of potentially relevant facts to actually check before acting, rather than either missing something disastrous or grinding through everything. He pointed out that handing Dennett's robot a complete, logically correct set of axioms didn't help at all: R1D1 methodically deduced that moving the wagon wouldn't change the color of the walls, among countless other true but useless implications, and got blown up while still deducing. He said our planner has the same failure in new clothing — it doesn't miscompute frame axioms, it just periodically "forgets" which earlier changes to the world still hold over a long task and acts on a stale picture, which is the relevance problem wearing statistics instead of logic.

(3) Priya answered that Marcus's standard proves too much: if solving the frame problem means having a general, provably bounded procedure for relevance that works in any possible situation, no system has ever met that bar, humans included, so the label stops doing any real work. She also pushed back on the "forgets what's still true" framing, saying that calling it a mysterious mechanism we haven't found yet is just a promise that future interpretability work will explain it, which is a bet, not evidence that a decades-old philosophical problem is still open.

(4) Marcus replied that he wasn't invoking that strong a standard — his claim was comparative: humans have some efficient, mostly automatic way of narrowing attention that fails gracefully (nobody double-checks that the sky didn't turn green before crossing the street), while the planner's failures are ungraceful and hard to predict, which is itself evidence of a difference worth explaining. He added that Priya's appeal to future interpretability cuts both ways: if it turns out the planner's trick is just a large pile of learned heuristics with no principled account of relevance behind it, that wouldn't dissolve Dennett's worry, it would confirm it, since Dennett's actual target was always solutions that work by ad hoc patching rather than by any general principle.
