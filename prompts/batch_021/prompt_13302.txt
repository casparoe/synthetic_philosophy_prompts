Back in the spring, when OpenAI's new image-generation tool made it possible to turn any photograph into something that looked like a still from a Studio Ghibli film, my sister (who has made her living as an illustrator for about fifteen years) and I had a long, fairly heated argument about it over the phone. Her position was that a machine reproducing a person's entire visual signature, for free, in seconds, and without ever asking, is simply theft dressed up as a party trick; someone in the replies under one of my posts even dug up that old clip of Miyazaki reacting to an AI animation demo years earlier and saying something to the effect that he found it an insult to life itself. I didn't have a good comeback at the time, but the argument stayed with me, and a while later, reading around on the history of patents, I came across Thomas Jefferson's 1813 letter to a man named Isaac McPherson. Jefferson, who had personally examined patent applications as a member of the first U.S. patent board back in the 1790s, wrote it after being asked for his opinion in a dispute over a mechanical invention.

The letter surprised me. Jefferson argues that an idea is not the kind of thing that can naturally be owned at all, because unlike a plot of land or a bushel of wheat, my having and using an idea doesn't stop you from having and using it too -- he compares it to one candle lighting another without losing any of its own flame. On his account, a patent or a copyright isn't a right in the way that property in land is a right; it's a limited monopoly society chooses to grant to inventors and authors as a purely practical bargain, on the theory that doing so produces more useful inventions and books than it costs in temporary exclusivity. Nature, he says, made ideas free to everyone, and any fencing-off of them is a late and artificial human arrangement, justified only insofar as it does more good than harm.

What I haven't been able to work out is what Jefferson would actually say about something like the Ghibli situation, following his own reasoning rather than just his general reputation. A "style" seems like about as pure an example of an unownable idea as exists -- nobody has ever managed to patent a way of painting -- so his first instinct might well be that nothing was stolen, only imitated, exactly as he thought imitation was supposed to work. But his whole case for allowing any exclusivity at all was that it has to earn its keep, producing more benefit than harm on balance, and I'm not convinced his arithmetic comes out the same way once one candle can light ten million others in an afternoon, for nobody's profit but the company selling the matches, while the original candle-maker's shop quietly goes dark. Could you work through this the way you think Jefferson himself would, rather than telling me what a modern commentator would say in his name -- and point out where, if anywhere, you think his own reasoning would start to strain against a technology he had no way of imagining?
