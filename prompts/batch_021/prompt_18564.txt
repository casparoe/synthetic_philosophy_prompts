I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and next week I'm interviewing a philosopher whose new book argues that animal-welfare policy -- including edge cases like insects and AI systems -- should follow a precautionary rule: any being with a realistic, evidence-backed chance of sentience deserves protection proportionate to that chance and to the cost of being wrong, without waiting for proof. Her stock defense against the obvious follow-up -- why not plants, or a thermostat? -- is that the rule requires positive markers, not merely the absence of disproof. Draft six interview questions that push past that defense: one on why a marker such as nociceptors plus flexible, motivation-sensitive behaviour counts as evidence of felt experience rather than unconscious processing; one forcing a concrete trade-off -- octopus farming versus insect farming -- instead of an answer in principle; one on what she does when markers from rival theories of consciousness disagree about the same species; and a final question on what finding about a species already on her list would lower its status, since critics say the rule can only add candidates, never subtract them. For each, flag her likely deflection and how I should follow up.
