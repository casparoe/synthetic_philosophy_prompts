I'm a philosophy PhD student working on the epistemic foundations of game theory, and I want to get past the standard gloss on the centipede game — 'backward induction recommends immediate defection, but real players cooperate, so rationality must be bounded' — to the sharper puzzle that Pettit and Sugden's 'paradox of backward induction' identifies: the inference used to justify defecting at the very last node presupposes common belief that the opponent will keep playing rationally, yet reaching an off-path node (after some earlier 'irrational' move) looks like exactly the kind of evidence that should undermine that belief, which threatens to make the backward-induction argument self-undermining rather than self-vindicating. Aumann's 1995 result is often cited as dissolving this worry, since it proves that common knowledge of rationality is consistent with, and indeed forces, the backward-induction path — but Binmore and Stalnaker have each replied that this 'victory' is bought only by building into the model a notion of knowledge that cannot be revised in light of surprising evidence, which is exactly the assumption the paradox calls into question. Could you explain, with some precision, exactly where in the formal apparatus this fork between knowledge as a fixed partition and revisable belief does its work, and say whether the disagreement between Aumann and his critics strikes you as substantive — a real dispute about rational belief revision — or ultimately just a choice between competing idealizations?
