For the last several months I have been reading through essays and forum posts on suffering-focused ethics, written mostly by fellow amateurs in the effective-altruism and rationalist communities rather than by academic philosophers, and there is one old objection to negative utilitarianism that I cannot get out of my head. As I understand it, the idea that morality should be primarily about minimizing suffering rather than maximizing happiness goes back to a throwaway remark of Popper's, and a critic pointed out not long afterward (I want to say his name was Smart, though I could be misremembering) that this seems to license something monstrous: if there were a button that would instantly and painlessly end all sentient life forever, pressing it would guarantee that no further suffering ever occurs again, and since a suffering-focused view is not supposed to weigh the happiness we would thereby lose very heavily, pressing the button comes out as permissible, maybe even obligatory. From what I can tell, people who still defend suffering-focused views today have a few ways of trying to escape this: distinguishing a "weak" version of the view from a "strong" one, building in some kind of lexical threshold so that only truly extreme suffering gets absolute priority over happiness, or abandoning the pleasure/pain framework altogether in favor of something like "tranquilism," on which the good in an experience is just the absence of craving rather than a positive quantity in its own right. I have never managed to work out for myself whether any of these moves actually solves the problem or just relabels it.

What would really help me is a dialogue between two people who take each other completely seriously: one a suffering-focused ethicist defending the most sophisticated version of the priority-of-suffering view available, the other a classical utilitarian who treats happiness and suffering as commensurable and, roughly, symmetrical. I would like the classical utilitarian to press the button scenario as hard as it can be pressed, forcing the other side to either bite the bullet openly or spell out exactly which move blocks the conclusion and why that move is not ad hoc; and at some point I would like the suffering-focused ethicist to turn the tables with an equally hard case against symmetrical utilitarianism, so that the pressure runs in both directions rather than only one. Please write both characters as equally sharp and genuinely responsive to whatever the other has just said, with neither one reduced to a mouthpiece for objections nor a pushover who folds too easily, and do not feel that the disagreement needs to be resolved by the end, since as far as I can tell it is not resolved in the literature either.
