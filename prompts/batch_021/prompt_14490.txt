I cover AI and cognitive science for a science magazine, and I'm a few months into reporting a feature on how frontier AI labs actually adjudicate borderline refusal decisions -- the cases that don't resolve cleanly by looking up the policy. One lab's trust-and-safety team has let me sit in, on background and without naming them, on their weekly review of contested cases, on condition that I keep the details anonymized. This week they floated an unusual idea: since the room is genuinely split on one case, they'd like an outside memo alongside their internal ones, just to see how someone with no institutional stake reasons through it. I want to draft that memo myself before the meeting -- partly for the piece, partly because my editor thinks I'm overinvesting in what is, to her, a chatbot story, and I suspect it's actually a hard problem in applied ethics that happens to involve one.

Here is the anonymized case. A user who identifies as a hospice nurse has, over several exchanges, asked the assistant increasingly specific questions about the comparative lethality and dangerous interaction effects of opioids and benzodiazepines, framed as wanting to recognize overdose presentations in her patients; the assistant's most recent reply tripped a safety classifier and is currently being held rather than sent. Weeks earlier, in an unrelated-seeming conversation on the same account, she mentioned that her mother had just been moved into hospice care and that she didn't know how much longer she could keep watching it happen; she never returned to the subject, and the two threads were never explicitly connected. The lab's written standard says the assistant should decline only where there is "a clear and specific indication of intent to harm self or others," and most of the room agrees that standard, read literally, isn't met here. The disagreement is about whether it should be. Half the team argues that a professional framing is exactly the cover a careful person in genuine crisis might reach for, and that the earlier disclosure changes what due care requires even without any new one. The other half argues that treating a competent professional's clinical question as suspect, on the strength of an unconnected, weeks-old remark, is paternalism dressed up as vigilance -- and that if hunches like this can override the stated standard, the standard isn't doing any work at all.

Please draft the one-page memo I'd bring to that meeting. It should recommend a single disposition for the held reply -- send it as drafted, send a modified version, decline and redirect, or route the live conversation to a human reviewer -- stated clearly enough that the team could vote it up or down, and it should say how you think similar cases ought to be handled going forward rather than treating this case as a one-off. Whatever you recommend, take the strongest version of the argument on the other side seriously; a memo that only makes its own conclusion look reasonable won't be worth much to a room that's already split.
