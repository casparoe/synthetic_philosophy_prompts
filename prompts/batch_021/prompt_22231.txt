Since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have been trying to fill in the philosophical education I never had time for, and for the past several months a small reading circle I belong to, organized through our local lifelong-learning institute, has been working slowly through Leibniz: the Discourse on Metaphysics, the correspondence with Arnauld, and the relevant sections of the Theodicy. Two of the more combative members, Miriam, a retired appellate litigator, and Owen, who spent three decades teaching mathematical logic before retiring, got into a running argument after our last session over whether Leibniz's claim that this is "the best of all possible worlds" can really be reconciled with his equally insistent claim that the world's existence is contingent rather than necessary. The disagreement has stayed with me, partly because it echoes arguments I know well from physics about determinism and "could have done otherwise," and partly because neither of them budged an inch.

(1) Miriam opened by arguing that the doctrine is necessitarian in substance, whatever Leibniz calls it: God necessarily exists, necessarily has a nature that always selects whatever is best, and Leibniz assumes there is a unique best world among the infinity available to the divine understanding, so the actual world had to exist, and "contingent" is just the label Leibniz needs in order to keep some distance from Spinoza. (2) Owen replied that this runs together two kinds of necessity Leibniz goes out of his way to keep separate: the connection between God's goodness and this particular world is a moral necessity, the sort of certainty we would grant that any sufficiently wise and good agent will do the wise and good thing, not an absolute or metaphysical necessity, where the alternative would involve a contradiction; the unchosen worlds remain perfectly coherent as descriptions, merely uninstantiated. (3) Miriam was not moved: if nothing about God's actual nature could have issued in anything but this world, she did not see what work "the alternative is non-contradictory" was doing, since a process that reliably outputs the single best available option is not rendered free simply because the runner-up is still well-formed on paper, and that is as true of a chess engine as of a deity. (4) Owen came back with what he called Leibniz's independent, and more basic, answer: every truth has its predicate contained in its subject concept, but necessary truths are the ones where that containment reduces to an identity after finitely many steps, while contingent truths require an analysis that runs on forever; since "this is the best of all possible worlds" is of the second kind, it counts as contingent by Leibniz's own logical criterion, whatever its certainty. (5) Miriam answered that this merely swaps a metaphysical question for a proof-theoretic one: contingency has become a fact about the length of a derivation that nobody, not even God, ever finishes, and she could not see why moral responsibility or genuine alternatives should track that, particularly since, on Owen's own telling, the outcome is fixed the moment God's nature is fixed.

I would also value a closing paragraph addressing the underlying question: whether Leibniz's two-part defense, the moral/absolute necessity distinction together with the infinite-analysis theory of contingency, actually secures a contingency robust enough to matter, or whether Miriam has located a genuine, still-debated weak point in his system.
