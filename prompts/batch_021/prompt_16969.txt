Grading finals for my Frege seminar this week and want a second pair of eyes on one of the answers before I write anything in the margins.

The question was: "In 1994 a diary Frege kept for about a month in the spring of 1924 came to light, full of vehemently antisemitic and anti-democratic opinions. Michael Dummett, who probably did more than anyone to make Frege central to Anglophone philosophy, has said that learning about it genuinely shook him. Does any of this bear on how we should assess Frege's actual philosophical achievements -- the logicism, the case against psychologism? Argue for your answer, engaging with at least one consideration on each side."

And here's what the student handed in:

"Frege kept this diary for about a month in the spring of 1924, and it's ugly reading -- he wants Jews stripped of political rights, he despises the Weimar constitution and parliamentary politics generally, and he wants some kind of strongman to take charge. Dummett, who did more than anyone to bring Frege into Anglophone philosophy, has admitted he was rattled when this came out, after building a career on treating Frege as a model of rigor.

The obvious response is that none of this touches Frege's philosophy. His achievements are the invention of modern quantificational logic, the logicist project of deriving arithmetic from logic alone, and his attack on psychologism -- the idea that the laws of logic are just generalizations about how humans happen to think, so that they could vary from person to person or culture to culture. Thinking that a philosopher's personal opinions make his theorems better or worse is the genetic fallacy: where a belief comes from doesn't bear on whether it's true. Basic Law V is inconsistent no matter who asserts it, and the proof that every number has a successor works the same regardless of what Frege thought of the Weimar Republic.

Still, I don't think this fully lets Frege off the hook. Anti-psychologism says logical laws hold for every thinker, everywhere, regardless of who they are -- that's the whole point of the "third realm" of objective thoughts that isn't relative to any particular mind. So there's a real inconsistency in Frege's position: someone committed to that kind of universalism cannot, without contradiction, also think some people's standing should depend on their ancestry. The diary doesn't undermine any specific proof, but it shows Frege failed to live up to his own deepest commitment.

Worth keeping in mind too that Frege wrote all this as a young man, well before he'd worked out his mature logicist system in the Grundgesetze -- so it might say more about the political culture of the German Empire than about anything distinctively Fregean."
