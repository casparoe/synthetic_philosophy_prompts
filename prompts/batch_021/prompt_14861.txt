I've been putting together a session for my formal epistemology seminar on decision-making under imperfect recall, and the absent-minded driver problem is giving me a bit of a headache in terms of how confidently to present it. Quick refresher in case its been a while since you thought about this one: in Piccione and Rubinstein's setup, a driver starts down a highway and hits intersection X, where EXIT pays off 0 and CONTINUE sends him toward a second, visually identical intersection Y; at Y, EXIT pays 4 and CONTINUE (sailing past both exits) pays only 1. The whole point is that the driver can't tell X and Y apart and has no memory of whether he's already been through one of them, so he has to set a single probability of continuing that applies "whichever intersection this happens to be." Worked out from the planning stage before he sets off, continuing with probability 2/3 maximizes his expected payoff, which seems straightforward enough -- but Piccione and Rubinstein present this as generating a real tension between that "planning" answer and what looks rational from inside the intersection itself. Aumann, Hart and Perry then wrote a whole reply insisting there's no genuine paradox at all once you define optimality properly from the action side, treating the driver-at-X and driver-at-Y as two selves who need to coordinate.

I go back and forth on this every time I teach it, so I'd like your take, written up properly. Write me an essay on whether the absent-minded driver problem exposes a genuine inconsistency in ordinary decision theory once you allow for imperfect recall (something that should actually worry us, maybe with knock-on implications for how we think about self-locating belief a la Sleeping Beauty), or whether Aumann, Hart and Perry are basically right that its a pseudo-paradox that evaporates under careful analysis. Weigh the considerations on both sides but actually land somewhere -- I don't want a "well, it depends how you define optimality" cop-out, I want a defended verdict.
