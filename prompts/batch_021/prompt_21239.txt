Spent 35 years doing experimental physics (mostly metrology) before retiring, and these days I'm having a lot of fun reading philosophy on the side. Lately I've been getting into imprecise probabilities -- representing your credence as a whole set of probability functions instead of one sharp number, for when the evidence is too thin to pin down a single value. But isnt this basically what we already do with error bars in the lab? You've got a combined uncertainty built out of type A and type B components, and if you're unsure which distribution in that range is the "real" one, you just slap a hyperprior over it, integrate it out, and you're back to one sharp posterior -- standard Bayesian updating like always. So why do people treat imprecise credences as some big seperate departure needing its own decision theory (all this dominance and "E-admissibility" business), rather than just ordinary Bayesianism with extra bookkeeping for uncertainty about your uncertainty? Feels like philosophers are dressing up a routine error-propagation problem as a deep new puzzle -- or am I missing something?
