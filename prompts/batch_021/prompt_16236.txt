I am a novelist currently rereading Tolstoy's *What Is Art?*, partly to test my own ambitions against it, and I keep running into what looks like an internal strain in the argument. Tolstoy makes a work's infectiousness depend on three conditions: the individuality, or particularity, of the feeling conveyed; the clarity of its expression; and the sincerity with which the artist actually experienced that feeling. Yet he also insists that genuinely good art -- as opposed to the "counterfeits" produced for the leisured classes -- must be intelligible even to an unschooled peasant, and on precisely these grounds he disowns most of his own pre-conversion fiction, along with most of Shakespeare. I can't tell whether the individuality condition and the universal-accessibility requirement are actually working against each other, or whether Tolstoy has some non-obvious way of reconciling them. Could you set out how he means the two to fit together, and tell me candidly whether the tension I'm sensing is genuine or whether I'm missing the move that dissolves it?
