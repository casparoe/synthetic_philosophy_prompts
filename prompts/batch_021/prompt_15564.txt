I am a philosophy of science PhD student working on probabilistic causality, and I have run into a puzzle about Simpson's paradox that the standard textbook treatment does not fully resolve. In the canonical cases -- the UC Berkeley graduate admissions case is the standard example -- the moral is that we should trust the disaggregated, department-by-department figures over the misleading pooled association, since averaging across departments of very different competitiveness is precisely what manufactures the apparent bias against women. In the epidemiological "birth weight paradox," however, the advice runs the other way: among low-birth-weight infants specifically, babies born to smoking mothers show lower mortality than babies born to non-smoking mothers, and here we are told to distrust the birth-weight-stratified figures and trust the pooled, unadjusted association instead. Both are cases in which a third variable reverses an association once we condition on it, yet the correct methodological verdict comes out exactly opposite. I gather this is supposed to track the distinction between confounders and colliders in a causal graph, but I do not have a real gut-level feel for why conditioning on a collider like birth weight does not merely fail to remove bias but actively fabricates it out of nothing. Could you walk me through, as concretely as possible and ideally with a simple causal story I can picture, what is structurally different about the collider case, such that stratifying on it turns out to be exactly the wrong move?
