"Explain what a rigid designator is and why Kripke thinks ordinary names are rigid designators. Then, using one example, explain the 'necessary a posteriori,' and using a different example, explain the 'contingent a priori.' Finally, say what these two cases together are supposed to show about the traditional idea that necessary truths = a priori truths and contingent truths = a posteriori truths."

Their answer:

"A rigid designator is a term that picks out the same object in every possible world where that object exists, unlike a non-rigid designator such as a definite description, which can pick out different objects in different worlds. 'Hesperus' is rigid because it just names Venus directly, but 'the brightest object visible in the evening sky' is non-rigid, since in some other world Mars or a satellite could've been the brightest thing there. Kripke thinks ordinary names work like this, tags directly on things rather than disguised descriptions, which is part of why the description theory of names fails.

For the necessary a posteriori, take 'Hesperus is Phosphorus.' Both names rigidly designate Venus, so the identity is true in every possible world where Venus exists, hence necessary. It's a posteriori because it took real astronomical work for ancient observers to figure out that the evening star and the morning star were the same object, not something you could work out from the armchair. This is also why identities discovered by science, like water is H2O, count as necessary: chemistry tells us the underlying composition, and since the same laws of nature hold across all possible worlds, the identity has to hold everywhere, not just here.

For the contingent a priori, take Kripke's example of the standard meter stick S in Paris. 'One meter' was defined as whatever length S has at t0, so 'S is one meter long at t0' can be known a priori, no measuring involved, just from knowing how the definition worked. It's contingent because the definition was a stipulation, and stipulations are always contingent - we could just as easily have grabbed a different stick, or a different length, and called that one meter instead.

Together these two cases show that necessity and apriority come apart, against the traditional rationalist picture where all and only necessary truths are a priori and all and only contingent truths are a posteriori. The necessary a posteriori shows some necessary truths are only knowable empirically, and the contingent a priori shows some contingent truths are knowable with no empirical work at all."
