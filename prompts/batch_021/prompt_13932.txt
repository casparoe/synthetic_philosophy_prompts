Leah's opening move: the shallow pond case shows you're on the hook to keep giving until it starts costing you something of real moral weight, full stop, and that duty doesn't shrink just because other people are also failing to pitch in. if there's ten bystanders around the pond and you're the only one who jumps in, you don't get to swim out halfway and say "well, my fair share was 10%, the rest is on them."

Owen: generalize that and you get a theory that's harshest on exactly the people already doing the most, since their duty balloons in direct proportion to how many free-riders there are. that's a bad property for a moral view to have — it basically punishes compliance and rewards everyone who successfully ignores the pond.

Leah: it's not "punishing" anyone, the kid doesn't stop needing rescuing just because the crowd sucks. that's the whole reason Singer picked a case with bystanders in it, to shut down the "not my job anymore" move.

Owen: except the pond is a bad model for *this* disagreement specifically, because it's all-or-nothing — the kid lives or drowns, there's no 60%-rescued kid. money isn't like that, each dollar buys a bit more good on the margin, so "fair share" is at least a coherent idea for aid in a way it just isn't for a one-shot rescue. you can't lift an intuition off a lump-sum case and assume it survives the trip to a divisible one.
