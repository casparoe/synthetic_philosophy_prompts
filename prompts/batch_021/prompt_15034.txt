Last year I decided to actually track down and read Alan Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" from start to finish, rather than just relying on the potted, secondhand description of the "Turing test" that gets recycled in every article about AI. I have no background in philosophy or computer science; I just like going back to primary sources when something interests me enough. What struck me most wasn't the famous setup with the interrogator and the two hidden contestants, but how the paper actually ends. After working through the more familiar objections he lists — the theological one, the argument from consciousness, Lady Lovelace's objection, and several others — the very last objection Turing takes on is what he calls "the argument from extrasensory perception." He treats the statistical case for telepathy as basically settled, which is startling coming from someone otherwise so careful, and he worries that a human contestant with a gift for telepathy could, say, name the suit of a hidden card far more often than chance, giving the game away for reasons that have nothing to do with intelligence. His fix is oddly literal: he proposes running the whole test inside what he calls a "telepathy-proof room."

I mentioned this to a friend, expecting them to treat it as a throwaway curiosity, and instead we ended up in a real disagreement about what it means for the rest of the paper. Their view is that it changes nothing important: Turing had already committed himself to defining "thinking" as whatever cannot be told apart over a text-only channel, so sealing off an exotic leak like telepathy is no different in kind from making sure the interrogator cannot hear anyone's voice — it is housekeeping, not a concession. I find myself thinking it is more damaging than that. If Turing has to keep sealing off channels he cannot even explain, purely because he found some statistics impressive, then the test was never directly picking out "thought" at all, only whatever is left over once every known and unknown non-verbal channel has been closed off. On that reading, the test can never really be finished, since some new channel could always turn up later and force another room to be built around it.

We went back and forth for a while without either of us conceding anything, and I do not think either of us managed to state the other side's case as well as it deserves. Could you write a dialogue between two people who hold these two positions: one who thinks the telepathy-proof room is a harmless bit of experimental housekeeping fully in keeping with Turing's original method, and one who thinks it quietly gives away that the imitation game was never a positive test for thought, only an ever more elaborate way of ruling things out? I would like the two of them to be equally sharp, genuinely responding to the substance of whatever the other has just said rather than restating their own position, and I would rather the dialogue not tip its hand at the end about which of them is right. Feel free to set the scene however comes naturally to you — I only care about the quality of the exchange itself.
