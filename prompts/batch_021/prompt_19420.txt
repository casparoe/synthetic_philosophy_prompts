So I'm outlining a novel set around a rewilding project (wolves or lynx going back into a managed valley), and one of my characters is a young ecologist who's picked up a lot of Effective Altruism-adjacent philosophy on the side. I want her to make a genuinely sharp case to her more traditional colleagues for why "just let nature take its course" isn't obviously the compassionate default, given how much suffering is apparently built into the way wild populations reproduce and die. I've absorbed the general vibe of the wild animal suffering literature (Horta, I think, is the name that keeps coming up) but I want to get the actual mechanics right rather then have her spout platitudes about "nature is cruel" that any of her colleagues could poke holes in.

The move I keep running into is this: point to r-selected species, frogs, fish, insects, that kind of thing, that produce enormous numbers of offspring, the vast majority of which die very soon after birth, often unpleasantly, and use that as the empirical backbone for the claim that suffering outweighs wellbeing in the wild on net. But then there's a counter-move from the more skeptical characters, which is that intervening in predator numbers doesn't obviously reduce total suffering, because of density-dependent regulation: knock out the wolves and the deer don't just get to live happily ever after, the population overshoots and crashes from starvation or disease instead, maybe in a way that's worse than just getting eaten. Could you lay out, clearly enough that I could put real arguments in both of their mouths instead of hand-wavy ones, why that second move, the "we can't actually know whether this helps" objection, is supposed to be a serious epistemic problem and not just squeamishness about interfering with nature? I specifically want to understand what would actually have to be true about how these population dynamics work for someone to be justified in believing an intervention reduces suffering rather than just moving it around the system.
