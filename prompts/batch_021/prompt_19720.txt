I am putting together a session for my graduate seminar on the metaphysics of space and time, and I keep going back and forth on how to frame the relationship between two arguments that are often mentioned in the same breath but rarely pulled apart carefully. The first is Leibniz's shift argument against Newtonian absolute space, developed in his correspondence with Clarke, where he invites us to consider shifting the entire material universe some distance in space (or displacing the whole history of events earlier or later in time) and argues that the resulting scenario is not merely unverifiable but metaphysically empty, given the Principle of Sufficient Reason and the Identity of Indiscernibles. The second is Earman and Norton's hole argument, which shows that if one is a substantivalist about the points of the spacetime manifold, then applying an active diffeomorphism that is the identity outside some "hole" region but smoothly differs within it generates a distinct model of the very same field equations, agreeing with the original everywhere outside the hole yet disagreeing inside it, which threatens to saddle substantivalism with a radical and physically unmotivated indeterminism.

A colleague of mine insists that the hole argument is simply Leibniz's shift argument "wearing the clothes of differential geometry," while I suspect there are real disanalogies that matter philosophically and not just technically. I'd like you to help me adjudicate this. Please compare the two arguments directly, addressing at least the following: whether the indiscernibility each argument trades on is best understood as merely epistemic (two scenarios we cannot tell apart) or as genuinely modal (a single possibility misdescribed as two), and whether this differs between the two cases; whether the move from Leibniz's static or kinematic shifts to Norton and Earman's active diffeomorphisms marks a difference in argumentative kind rather than mere technical sophistication, especially given that the hole argument seems to generate a much more localized and dynamically constrained kind of indeterminism than anything available to Leibniz; and whether "sophisticated substantivalism" (accepting that diffeomorphic models represent the same possibility while still denying that spacetime points are reducible to their relations) is a strategy Leibniz himself could in principle have availed himself of, or whether it only becomes intelligible once one has at hand the modern distinction between a bare manifold and a metric field. I'd be grateful if you would close with your own judgment as to which of the two arguments poses the more serious problem for substantivalism about space, and why.
