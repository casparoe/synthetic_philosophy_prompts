I spent about thirty-five years running an insect ecology lab before I retired, and like just about every field biologist I know, I leaned on t-tests and ANOVA's for decades without ever really asking myself what a p-value means -- I'll fully admit I probably did some of what people now call p-hacking before anyone had bothered to give it that name. Since retiring I've been sitting in on a philosophy of science group that meets at the university extension, mostly retirees like me plus a couple of grad students. A few weeks back the session was on Bayesian confirmation theory and it turned into a real arguement between two of the regulars, Elaine (who used to do biostatistics consulting) and Dominic (a philosophy grad student), and I've been chewing on it ever since because I honestly can't tell who came out ahead.

(1) Elaine opened by blaming the replication crisis largely on frequentist testing itself: a p-value only controls the long-run false-positive rate if the sample size and stopping point were fixed in advance, but real labs peek at incoming data and stop once it looks significant, and if you're patient enough that can push the false-positive rate arbitrarily close to certainty even when the null is exactly true. Bayesians, she said, dodge this completely, because the posterior after any batch of data depends only on the data you actually collected, not on the stopping rule in your head, so peeking whenever you like doesn't distort anything. (2) Dominic granted the math but said it makes Bayesian updating sound like a free lunch, which it isn't: the researcher degrees of freedom Elaine wants to pin on stopping rules don't vanish, they just move house, into the choice of prior, the choice of model, which comparisons get reported. Someone chasing a flashy result can shop around for a prior that flatters the effect, and if a referee balks, just say the first prior "wasn't well thought through" -- prior-hacking, he said, is exactly as invisible on the page as optional stopping ever was. (3) Elaine granted that priors can be abused but denied the two cases are symmetric: the stopping-rule result guarantees that a Bayesian with one fixed, honestly-chosen prior gets a calibrated read of the evidence at every single peek, no matter how often they check, while a frequentist doing the same thing is provably not calibrated at all -- the false-positive rate marches toward one. So even a somewhat careless Bayesian isn't caught in the trap a frequentist automatically is. (4) Dominic's comeback was that "calibrated" was carrying a lot of weight in that sentence -- the theorem gives you a posterior that's internally coherent given your prior, which isn't the same thing as the field-wide reliability everyone outside the room actually cares about. If enough researchers each use their own generous-but-defensible prior, he said, the field-wide rate of false "discoveries" can end up just as bad as it was under p-values, which is why he thinks the crisis was mostly about incentives, publication bias, and the lack of preregistration, not about which formula happens to be under the hood.

I've gone back and forth on this all week without settling it myself. Could you adjudicate move by move -- 1 through 4 as I've numbered them above -- rather than just declaring an overall winner?
