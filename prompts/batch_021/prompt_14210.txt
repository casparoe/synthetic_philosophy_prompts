I spent my career as an experimental physicist and only turned to philosophy in earnest after retiring a few years ago; these days I meet weekly with a small reading group at our local university's continuing-education program, and we have lately been working through decision theory. Last month we spent two sessions on the "Death in Damascus" puzzle: the man in the marketplace who is warned by Death that he will be collected tomorrow, and who cannot escape by fleeing to Aleppo, because Death, an infallible predictor, will simply appear wherever the man actually ends up. What has stayed with me is not the old fable itself, which by my understanding Somerset Maugham was not the first to tell, but a technical wrinkle our discussion leader walked us through afterward: a causal decision theorist reasoning the case out seems to talk himself into an endless reversal between the two cities, since whichever one he currently favors, he is thereby led to think Death is more likely to be there, which counsels switching to the other, whereupon the very same reasoning recommends switching back -- so that neither city, taken as an outright commitment, turns out to be what our leader called "ratifiable," and the only stable resolution on offer is to hand the choice to some device, like a coin toss, that even Death cannot see coming.

I follow the argument well enough on paper, but I have found that this sort of thing only really lodges in me once it has been dramatized in a character rather than laid out in a payoff table, and I would like to bring something of that kind to next week's session as a check on whether I have actually understood the point or merely nodded along. Would you write me a short story, somewhere in the 1000 to 1500 word range, that earns its way to that same predicament on its own terms: a protagonist reasoning as a causal decision theorist would, whose plan visibly reverses between two courses of action for the reasons above, and who is driven, by the end, to that same recourse to chance -- all without ever having a narrator simply announce the moral? You needn't keep the Damascus and Aleppo setting, or even a literal Death, if another setting suits you better, and I would be glad if the plot found room, without turning into a lecture, to gesture at why an evidential decision theorist standing in the same spot might never have talked himself into the same trouble.
