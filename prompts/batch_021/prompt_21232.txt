I sit on the internal safety review board of an AI lab weighing whether to approve a "self-certifying succession" architecture: each generation of a self-improving research agent must formally prove, in its own proof system, that the next generation preserves a fixed list of safety invariants before that successor is deployed, and the proposal's authors argue this removes the need for a human sign-off at each step. Two board members trained in mathematical logic object that the design runs straight into the Löbian obstacle: by Löb's theorem, a consistent formal system can prove "if I can prove P, then P" only for P it could already prove outright. That means the current agent has no general grounds for trusting proofs produced by a successor that reasons essentially the same way it does, and that simply giving each generation a strictly stronger proof system just relocates the problem into an infinite regress rather than closing it. The engineers who built the verifier consider this a theoretical nicety that doesn't bite in practice, since every deployment is also gated on extensive empirical red-teaming and can be halted if a proof looks anomalous. We meet Thursday and must approve the architecture as proposed, approve it with specific conditions, or send it back for redesign. Please draft the memo I'll circulate to the board: give a clear recommendation with a concrete condition or deadline, and engage the engineers' practical objection as seriously as the logicians' theoretical one.
