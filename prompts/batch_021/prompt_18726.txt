I've been meditating pretty regularly for about three years now, and last winter I fell into this deep rabbit hole reading about anatta, the Buddhist 'no-self' teaching. Around the same time I was also getting into a bunch of pop neuroscience stuff, books and podcasts about how the brain builds up this feeling of being one single unified 'me' out of a bunch of separate processes that aren't actually run by anybody in there. It kept striking me that these two things, one from 2500 years ago and one basically from an MRI machine, seemed to be arriving at the exact same answer.

So heres the question thats been bugging me: if modern neuroscience has basically independently confirmed what the Buddhists already figured out, doesn't that make most of the actual philosophical content of Buddhism -- the two truths stuff, the arguments between different schools about what no-self even means, all the karma and rebirth theory that's supposedly there to explain how morality can still work without a permanent self -- kind of redundant? Like, interesting history lesson, but if I can just get the real answer from a neuroscience textbook, why would I bother digging into something like the Abhidharma at all? I brought this up with the teacher at my sangha and she said something like 'you're treating two totally different kinds of claims as if they're one claim,' but got pulled away before she could unpack it, so I've just been chewing on it since. Is she onto something real there, or was that kind of a dodge on her part?
