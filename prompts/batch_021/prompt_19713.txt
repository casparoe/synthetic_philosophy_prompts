I don't have any background in philosophy or economics, but over the past year or so I've gotten pretty absorbed in the "longtermism" end of the internet — podcasts, a couple of popular books on effective altruism, the odd forum thread — and the part that has actually stuck with me, more than the material on AI risk or pandemics, is population ethics. It feels like one of the rare philosophical puzzles with real teeth, since it's supposedly forced on you by premises you'd have signed up for anyway, rather than resting on some far-fetched thought experiment. A couple of weekends ago it came up after dinner at a friend's place, and two people there, Marisol and Owen, spent a good half hour going back and forth on it while the rest of us just listened and picked at leftovers. I jotted down the gist afterward because I couldn't stop turning it over, and I still can't decide who came out ahead, so I'm hoping you can help me referee it properly, move by move, rather than just handing down a final score.

Marisol opened by saying that any view which sums welfare across everyone who ever lives has to be rejected outright, because it implies what she called "the repugnant conclusion": a vastly larger population living lives barely worth living can always outweigh a smaller population living wonderful ones, provided you pack in enough people. Owen said she couldn't dismiss it that cheaply: if you accept that adding extra people with decent lives to a population, without touching anyone already there, never makes things worse, and you also accept that redistributing welfare toward equality without lowering the total never makes things worse either, then a sequence of small, individually plausible steps drags you, by your own logic, all the way down to that same repugnant-looking population — so refusing the conclusion at the end just means one of the small steps at the start was actually false. Better, he said, to admit that our intuitions about enormous, unfamiliar populations aren't trustworthy, and accept it. Marisol granted she couldn't see where to block the chain, but said there was a cheaper way out than swallowing the whole thing: adopt a "critical level" version of the theory, where a life only adds positive value once it clears some minimum threshold, so that lives which are merely barely worth living count for a little less than nothing and can never swamp anything by sheer numbers. Owen's comeback was that this trades one repugnant result for an uglier one, sometimes called "the sadistic conclusion" — on a critical-level view there is always some much smaller number of genuinely miserable lives it would be better to bring into existence than a huge number of mildly good but middling ones, and he thinks a few real horrors are a worse trade than a lot of gentle disappointments, not a better one. Marisol shot back that Owen doesn't get to run that move twice: if he's entitled to tell her that intuitions about vast, unfamiliar populations can't be trusted, she's entitled to say the same about intuitions concerning a handful of merely hypothetical suffering people who, on either view, are never actually born. Cornered, Owen tried switching to a view that maximizes average rather than total welfare, on the grounds that this dodges both conclusions in one move. Marisol replied that averaging has embarrassments of its own: it can rank a perfectly good, happy new life as making things worse purely because it falls below the existing mean, and it makes whether today's choices count as improvements hinge on the welfare of people who died thousands of years ago, who have nothing to do with anyone actually affected by those choices. Owen said those were merely theoretical embarrassments, unlike the other two, and the argument fizzled out over dessert with neither side budging.

Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each stage, rather than just handing down an overall verdict? I'm especially curious whether Marisol's jab about Owen not being allowed to pick and choose which intuitions to distrust actually lands, given that she leans on a structurally similar move herself when she waves away his chain-of-small-steps argument, and whether there's some consideration neither of them raised that a more careful treatment of the topic would put on the table.
