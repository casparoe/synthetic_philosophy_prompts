I'm in the middle of negotiating the sale of a small company I co-founded, and one piece of the deal structure has been nagging at me. Rather than paying the full price at closing, the acquirer wants to defer roughly a third of it into an earn-out due in two years, contingent on our product line hitting revenue targets, with the main levers behind those numbers—staffing, pricing, which sales channels get priority—controlled entirely by their team once the deal closes. A friend who works in private equity tells me it would be naive to count on that money: once they own us outright, actually hitting those targets costs them real effort and resources that no longer buy them anything they don't already have, so the coldly rational move on their end is to quietly under-resource the unit and let the earn-out lapse, whatever anyone is promising now. But when I raised this with the acquirer's own head of corporate development, he said something that has stuck with me: their pipeline of future acquisitions depends on being known as a buyer who pays out earn-outs in full even on the ones they could get away with shorting, because word travels fast among founders, and that reputation is worth more to them than any single payment. I want to believe him, partly because it would make the deal easier to accept, which makes me suspicious of my own read on the situation. Which view should carry more weight here, and how should the answer change how hard I push for cash up front instead?
