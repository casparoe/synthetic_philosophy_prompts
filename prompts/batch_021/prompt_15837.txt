I serve on the clinical ethics committee at my hospital, and we have been asked to settle a disagreement in the oncology service before it hardens into inconsistent bedside practice. Several attendings have adopted a habit, when disclosing a poor prognosis (say, a five-to-ten percent five-year survival rate), of pairing the statistic with something like "some patients do beat these numbers, and going into treatment with a fighting spirit seems to help the odds." Their reasoning is that a population statistic doesn't tell any particular patient whether they are in the surviving fraction, that outlook demonstrably affects adherence and quality of life and, on some evidence, outcomes themselves, and that withholding this kind of encouragement is itself a disclosure choice rather than some neutral default.

Other attendings regard the practice as a paternalistic dressing-up of wishful thinking: patients are owed a sober, evidence-calibrated picture, not a rhetorical nudge toward optimism, however well-meant, and "fighting spirit" language has a way of leaving patients who decline anyway feeling they simply didn't try hard enough. Our informed consent policy requires disclosure to be "accurate and balanced," a phrase both sides in this dispute confidently claim for themselves. One committee member joked that we are really just re-running the old argument between William James and W.K. Clifford over a bedside chart; I suspect there's something to that, but I'm not sure the comparison holds up once you look closely at what each of them actually required before belief without full evidence becomes permissible.

Please draft the committee's policy memo on prognosis communication for the oncology service, to be circulated before next month's service meeting. It should give attendings a concrete, workable answer about what may and may not be said alongside statistical prognoses, and under what conditions, rather than settling for either "always stick to the numbers" or "hope matters too, so say something encouraging." It should also take the strongest version of whichever side your recommendation doesn't fully satisfy seriously enough that an attending on that side would recognize their actual objection in your reasoning, rather than a weaker stand-in for it.
