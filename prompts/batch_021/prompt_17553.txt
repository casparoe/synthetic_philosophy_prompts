For an article on how philosophers try to formalize rational self-trust across time, I keep running into Bas van Fraassen's Reflection Principle, which holds that an agent's present credence in a proposition, conditional on the supposition that their credence in it will later equal some value x, must itself equal x. Compare this principle with the more general requirement that rational agents update their beliefs only by conditionalizing on whatever evidence they acquire in the interim. In which ways does Reflection go beyond conditionalization? Can it actually be derived from conditionalization plus reasonable assumptions about how an agent expects to learn, or does it impose an independent constraint on rational belief? Use van Fraassen's own "Ulysses and the Sirens" style case—in which an agent correctly predicts that their future opinion will be corrupted rather than improved—to explain why the unrestricted principle cannot simply be identified with conditionalization, and say what, if anything, survives of Reflection once it is qualified to handle cases like this.
