I'm a software engineer who works on multimodal machine learning -- models that have to build some shared representation of what an object looks like and what it feels like from two very different streams of training data. That professional preoccupation is the entire reason I ended up reading about Molyneux's Problem: the seventeenth-century question, posed to Locke by his correspondent William Molyneux, of whether a person blind from birth who can already tell a cube from a sphere by touch would be able to distinguish them by sight alone the instant she gained vision, before touching either one. It is also, indirectly, how I ended up two years ago filling the seat on my university's IRB reserved for someone with a quantitative background from outside the health sciences, and why I have now been asked to write the discussion memo for a proposal that has split the board roughly down the middle ahead of next Tuesday's meeting.

The proposal comes from our hospital's pediatric ophthalmology group, which partners with a nonprofit that funds sight-restoring surgery for children who have been functionally blind since infancy because of untreated congenital cataracts. The researchers want to attach a short behavioral test to their already-approved outcomes study: in the first minutes after each child's bandages come off, and before the child is permitted to reach out and touch anything, a researcher would hold up two objects the child has handled blindfolded many times that week -- a block and a ball, say -- and ask the child to identify, by sight alone, which is which. It amounts to about as direct a real-world staging of Molyneux's question as one could ethically attempt, and the small number of genuine historical cases in which something like it has happened -- by accident of clinical circumstance rather than by research design -- seem, from what I can tell, to have leaned toward the skeptical answer that Locke and Berkeley both gave, at least on the newly sighted person's first attempts. Roughly half the board sees a rare chance to gather real data on a question philosophers have argued about on paper for three centuries, at what looks like negligible added medical risk; the other half sees a plan to use a child's single most disorienting and emotionally loaded moment -- compounded by making the child delay the very thing, touch, that would resolve the confusion -- in order to answer a question that does nothing for that particular child, at the exact moment when getting genuinely informed consent from the family is hardest.

Could you draft that memo for me? I need it to arrive at one concrete recommendation -- approve the testing component as written, reject it, or approve it with specific modifications, whether to the consent process, the length of the permitted delay, or an option for families to accept the surgery while declining the test -- and to support that recommendation with reasoning the research team and the more skeptical board members could each actually engage with, rather than reasoning that simply invokes "minimal risk" or "respect for persons" without saying what those principles actually demand in a case this specific.
