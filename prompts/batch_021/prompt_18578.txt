Last night a friend and I spent nearly two hours arguing about whether a rational agent should always want more information, and neither of us really won. She anchored her whole position on I. J. Good's 1967 theorem on the value of information, treating it as settling the matter; I was left with the feeling that something was being oversold, without being able to say exactly what. I wrote up the following afterward to sharpen my objection, and I'd like your comments before I bring it back to her.

Good's theorem is usually summarized like this: for a Bayesian decision-maker, the expected value of a cost-free, relevant signal, observed before acting, is never negative, and is generically positive unless the signal wouldn't change your optimal action anyway. The proof idea is simple once you see it: the "value of the decision problem," as a function of your current probabilities, is a convex function (it's a maximum over actions of something linear in the probabilities), and posteriors average back to the prior across the distribution of possible signals, so by Jensen's inequality, the expected value of deciding after seeing the signal is at least the value of deciding on the prior alone. On this basis, my friend wants to conclude something close to: a rational agent should never prefer ignorance to free knowledge.

I think this overreaches the theorem in two ways.

First, Good himself, later in his career, distinguished between an idealized "Type I" rationality, which maximizes expected utility given your current probabilities instantaneously, and a "Type II" rationality, which accounts for the cost of the deliberation and computation needed to actually process a signal and update on it. Once that cost is real, as it is for any bounded agent, declining to think something all the way through can be the better policy even when the signal itself is free. The theorem is silent about this, because it assumes updating is instantaneous and costless, not just that acquisition is.

Second, and I think more importantly, the theorem is a single-agent result, and the informal slogan doesn't survive the move to strategic or market settings. Take Hirshleifer's classic example: suppose some risk (a harvest, say) is going to be publicly and costlessly resolved by an announcement, but only after risk-averse parties have had a chance to write insurance contracts against it. Delay the announcement, and there's a mutually beneficial risk-pooling arrangement everyone would sign up for in advance. Move the announcement earlier, before contracting, and that arrangement disappears entirely, because there's no longer any uncertainty left to pool. So the very same piece of free information, about the very same event, can make everyone worse off in expectation, purely by virtue of when it becomes available relative to other agents' opportunities to contract. That's not the boring point that information can lead you astray in a particular realization, the way a screening test can come back a false positive; it's a systematic reversal of the sign of the expected value, driven entirely by the multi-agent setting.

What I can't decide is how best to state the moral. Is Hirshleifer's example a genuine counterexample to the "more information is always weakly good" slogan, full stop, or is it better described as showing that Good's theorem simply doesn't apply once the decision problem itself, here, the set of contracts available, is allowed to depend on the timing of the information? I'd also welcome pushback on whether I'm representing Good's Type I/Type II distinction correctly, since I'm working from memory, and on whether there's a cleaner or more standard example than Hirshleifer's for making the same point.
