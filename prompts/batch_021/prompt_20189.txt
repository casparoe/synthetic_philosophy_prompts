I'm a psychology senior enrolled this term in a philosophy of science seminar that has spent several weeks on the replication crisis and the underlying disagreement between frequentist and Bayesian approaches to statistical inference. The readings have mostly kept things at the level of abstract principles, but something that happened with my own honors thesis this semester has made the issue feel a lot less abstract, and I haven't been able to resolve it to my own satisfaction.

My pre-registered analysis plan specified forty participants per condition for a priming study. Once I reached that number, the key comparison came out at p = .09: in the predicted direction, but short of the conventional .05 cutoff. My advisor, who has seen this happen many times before, told me to simply collect twenty more participants per condition "since we're so close." I did, and the p-value for the combined sample came out to .043. She was pleased and wants the thesis written up around this as a clean, significant result. When I mentioned this sequence of events to a labmate who is unusually into methodology, he reacted strongly: he said the .043 doesn't mean what I think it means anymore, and that my situation is close to a textbook case of the practices people have in mind when they talk about the replication crisis. A different labmate, who has been reading about Bayesian statistics on her own, said none of this would be a problem if I had just reported a Bayes factor instead of a p-value, since Bayesian updating is supposed to be indifferent to when or why a researcher decided to stop collecting data.

I don't know which labmate has the better argument, or whether they're even really disagreeing about the same thing, and I'd rather understand why than just be handed a rule to follow, since this will not be the last dataset I analyze. Should I write up the .043 as my advisor wants, treat the result as inconclusive until an independent, properly pre-registered replication comes in, or go back and recompute the evidence in Bayesian terms as my second labmate suggests? And underneath all of that: is one of my labmates simply right and the other wrong, or is there something legitimate behind both of their worries?
