In my world religions elective, we just covered the argument for anatta ("no-self") from the Milindapanha, where the monk Nagasena leads King Milinda through the famous chariot simile. Milinda has to admit that the chariot isn't the wheels, isn't the axle, isn't the frame, and isn't simply all the parts heaped together either — the word "chariot" just conveniently names the parts functioning together in a certain way. Nagasena then runs the same argument on the king himself, checking "Milinda" against each of the five aggregates — body, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness — one at a time, and finding no separate self sitting behind or underneath any of them.

I can't stop thinking about how much more unsettling this gets once you try running the argument on yourself instead of just following it as a logic puzzle about someone else. Could you write me a short story (a page or two, any genre or setting) in which a character is forced to actually go looking for their own self this way — checking it off against each candidate part in turn, the way Nagasena checks the chariot — and comes up with nothing left over? I'd like the story to earn that conclusion dramatically, through what happens to the character, rather than having someone simply state it, and to end on the specific mix of vertigo and relief the argument seems to be reaching for, rather than settling into either straightforward horror or a tidy uplifting moral.
