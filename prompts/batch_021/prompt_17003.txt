Aristotle says money's value is basically just a matter of convention/law (nomos) -- that's literally why the Greek word for currency, nomisma, is related to nomos, since money isn't valuable "by nature" the way sheep or grain are. But then his famous argument against usury is that charging interest is unnatural specifically because money is barren/sterile and cant naturally "breed" more money the way livestock or crops do (hence the pun on tokos, which means both "interest" and "offspring"). Argue that these two bits of Aristotle's economic thinking don't actually fit together -- the usury argument only makes sense if money already has some natural function that interest is violating, which is exactly what the "money is just nomos" line denies it has.
