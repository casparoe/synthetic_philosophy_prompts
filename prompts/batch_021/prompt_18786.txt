A few of us on my team started an informal reading group a couple of months ago, working through AI safety material during slow weeks between sprints. Last week we got to MIRI's "Embedded Agency" write-up by Scott Garrabrant and Abram Demski, and it has stuck with me more than almost anything else we've covered so far. The core move is contrasting an idealized agent like AIXI — which sits outside its environment, trading clean observations and actions across a fixed interface, with effectively unlimited scratch space to think before it ever has to act — against an "embedded" agent that is physically made of the same stuff as the world it is trying to model and act within.

What got me is how closely this tracks failure modes I've actually run into at work: a monitoring daemon that can never hold a complete, up-to-the-millisecond model of the very system it runs inside, because it draws on that system's own resource budget; a service that has to answer before it has finished gathering information, because waiting has a cost too; a team that ships a new version of itself, so to speak, and has to trust that version will behave, because fully verifying it beforehand would require already being the smarter, more complete version doing the verifying. Embedded agency generalizes exactly this kind of bind. An embedded agent cannot fit a fully detailed model of the world, itself included, inside itself; it cannot finish deliberating before it must act, since deliberation draws on the same time and resources as everything else it does; and it cannot fully audit a successor — a future, more capable version of itself, or a subroutine it spins off — before handing over control.

I would like to see these ideas dramatized rather than explained yet again, so could you write me a short story built around one or more of these binds? I do not want a generic "AI wakes up and resents its creators" story; the tension should come specifically from embeddedness itself — a mind that cannot step outside the system it is part of to get a God's-eye view of its own workings, that has to act on a self-model it already knows is too small to be accurate, or that has to hand control to something it cannot fully verify in advance. Set it wherever best serves the idea — inside the agent's own reasoning, from the outside as researchers watch it behave strangely, as a control-room transcript, or something else entirely — but I would like someone who actually knows this material to be able to point at specific beats and recognize exactly which piece — self-reference, resource limits, trusting a successor, a rogue subprocess — is driving events, rather than everything dissolving into a vague "the AI got confused." Something in the 800–1200 word range would be ideal.
