I have spent a long time immersed in the rationalist blogosphere's enthusiasm for Bayesian updating as the gold standard of rational belief revision, but I recently ran into a complication that I think receives too little attention outside specialist circles. Richard Jeffrey's generalization of conditionalization to uncertain evidence — often called Jeffrey conditionalization or probability kinematics, in which a partition's probabilities shift by some amount short of certainty while the conditional probabilities on each partition cell are held fixed via the "rigidity" condition — is not, in general, commutative. That is, unlike strict conditionalization on evidence one becomes certain of, where the order in which evidence arrives never affects the final credence, updating first on one piece of uncertain evidence and then on a second can leave you with a different final credence than updating in the reverse order. Please describe the most important implications of this order-dependence for how I should reason in ordinary life when uncertain evidence trickles in over time — for instance, a sequence of ambiguous texts and secondhand comments that each only partially shift my confidence about whether a friend is upset with me. Should the order in which such evidence arrives be allowed to shape my final view? And if there is no principled way to correct for the effect, does this expose a genuine limit on Jeffrey conditionalization's claim to be the correct normative model of belief revision, or is order-sensitivity actually a defensible feature of rational updating on imperfect evidence?
