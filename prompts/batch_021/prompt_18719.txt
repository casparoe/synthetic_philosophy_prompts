This term I have been leading a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of modality, and we moved directly from a unit on Leibniz's theory of possible worlds (centered on the Discourse on Metaphysics and the Theodicy) into David Lewis's On the Plurality of Worlds. In her response paper on that transition, one of my strongest students argued, in effect, that Lewis's genuine modal realism just is Leibniz's picture of possible worlds with the theology surgically removed. Both philosophers, on her reading, analyze modal claims by quantifying over a plurality of worlds; Lewis's only real innovation, she suggested, was to let all of those worlds be concretely realized rather than remain ideas in the mind of God, since he had no theological apparatus left over to keep the non-actual ones merely possible.

I suspect this elides some real structural differences, but before I write comments on her paper I would like to pressure-test my own reasoning. Could you work through, in some detail, how far the analogy between the two pictures actually holds and exactly where it gives out? I am especially interested in two things: first, what work Leibniz's notions of compossibility and the principle of the best are doing in individuating and selecting among worlds, given that Lewis's recombination-based plenitude has no need for either a compatibility constraint of that kind or an evaluative ranking; and second, whether Leibniz's conception of actuality as a genuine achievement conferred on a single world by God's choice, and Lewis's indexical analysis of actuality - an idea he had already floated in "Anselm and Actuality" and later developed at length in the book - are different enough that calling Lewis's view "secularized Leibniz" obscures more than it clarifies. Please also flag any respects in which you think her reading is onto something real, rather than simply confirming my hunch that it isn't.
