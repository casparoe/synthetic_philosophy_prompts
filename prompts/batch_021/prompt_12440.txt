I organize my university's public "Constitution Day" lecture each fall, and this year's invited speaker has just published a book-length defense of a quite specific epistocratic proposal: a standing council whose members are seated by competitive examination in economics, law, and constitutional history, and which holds an absolute veto over legislation passed by an otherwise universally elected assembly. She is a careful arguer who has clearly pre-empted the crudest objections already — a whole chapter insists that the examination itself would be designed and periodically revised by a rotating, politically insulated academic board, precisely so that no sitting government could rig the qualifying test in its own favor. I will be moderating the public Q&A after her talk, in front of an audience running from political theory PhD students to undergraduates and interested townspeople, so the questions need to reward the specialists in the room without losing everyone else.

Could you draft eight to ten questions for that session? I would like at least one question apiece on: the regress problem — since specifying and revising the competence criteria is itself a contestable political act, who has standing to settle that non-circularly, without either quietly presupposing the democratic authority her proposal denies or simply enthroning the epistocrats' own judgment as the uncontested standard; the charge, associated with critics like Estlund, that publicly ranking citizens by political competence is objectionably invidious even granting that the ranking is accurate, which is a different complaint from a mere feasibility worry; the sociological fact that any examination correlating with formal education will tend to reproduce exclusions of the kind literacy tests achieved in the Jim Crow South, whatever the examiners' intentions; the awkward point that the Condorcet jury theorem she cites in favor of a small, highly competent council is the very same theorem classically used to justify extending the franchise as widely as possible, and that its conclusion reverses entirely once average competence dips below chance on the more polarized questions; and Landemore's use of the Hong-Page diversity result to argue that a cognitively varied but individually less able electorate can out-perform a homogeneous panel of "the best." For each of these, please add a short follow-up pitched at the deflection she is most likely to reach for — in her published articles it is usually some version of "that's an implementation detail, not an objection to the ideal" — and close with one further question asking what specific empirical or historical finding would actually change her mind.
