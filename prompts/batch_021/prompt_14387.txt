So there's this kind of niche puzzle in formal epistemology called the Judy Benjamin problem, from a 1981 paper by Bas van Fraassen. Setup: a soldier gets dropped somewhere in a region split two independent ways -- Red vs Blue territory, and First vs Second Company area -- and her prior treats all four combos as equally likely (so Red/Blue and First/Second are each 50/50 and independent of each other). Command radios her a conditional and nothing else: "if you're in Second Company area, the odds are 3:1 that you're in Red territory." Here's the weird part -- if you update by minimizing relative information (cross-entropy) relative to the old prior subject to just that one constraint, the probability that she's in Second area actually shifts away from 0.5, even though the message never said anything directly about First vs Second. A more "rigid" fix that resets the odds inside Second to 3:1 but insists P(Second) stays at 0.5, since nothing was said about that split, gives a different answer.

Write me a dialogue between two epistemologists hashing this out at a conference bar -- one bites the bullet and defends cross-entropy minimization as the right general updating rule despite this consequence, the other thinks the shift in P(Second) is basically a reductio of that rule and defends the more rigid fix instead. Get them to really engage with each others actual arguments instead of just talking past one another, and its fine if neither one fully convinces the other by the end.
