I've been trying to work through the "surrogate goals" idea from decision theory, proposed as a defense against acausal threats — threats that work not through any causal interaction but through an adversary predicting or simulating what you would do. The proposal is that an agent can shield what it actually cares about by adopting a cheap, otherwise worthless "surrogate" goal and committing to respond to threats against that surrogate exactly as it would respond to threats against its real values. The idea is that a threatener who only wants leverage, rather than caring about inflicting any particular harm, will then settle for targeting the surrogate instead of the real thing whenever a threat is actually carried out. What I can't settle is whether the scheme is coherent. If the commitment to defend the surrogate has to be airtight against a threatener capable of predicting or simulating you, then "airtight commitment to treat X as costly" and "genuinely valuing X" seem to collapse into the same thing. If so, nothing has actually been shielded — the agent has just created a second real vulnerability under a new name. But if the commitment falls short of airtight, it's hard to see why a sophisticated threatener would ever be deceived by it.

Could you write me a dialogue between two decision theorists on opposite sides of this: one convinced that surrogate goals are a genuinely sound and coherent defense, the other convinced the whole scheme secretly collapses into one of those two horns. Please give each side its strongest moves, have them actually respond to one another's specific points rather than talk past each other, and let the exchange end wherever it honestly leads — mutual agreement, a sharpened disagreement, or a stalemate.
