I am a PhD student in philosophy working primarily on nineteenth-century British ethics, and a recent graduate seminar on Sidgwick's *The Methods of Ethics* has left me preoccupied with a connection I hadn't previously appreciated. We spent a long session on the notorious "dualism of practical reason": Sidgwick's admission that rational egoism and universal hedonism both present themselves as self-evident axioms of practical reason, yet cannot be shown always to recommend the same action, so that the book closes not with a clean utilitarian victory but with a confession that this dualism threatens to reduce practical reason to chaos. Afterward, a classmate mentioned, almost as an aside, that this same Sidgwick spent nearly two decades as the founding president of the Society for Psychical Research, meticulously investigating mediums, telepathic experiments, and apparitions of the dying, all the while remaining privately unconvinced by nearly everything he examined. This struck me as more than a curious biographical footnote, since Sidgwick himself apparently connected the two projects: he hoped that solid empirical evidence of personal survival after death, and perhaps of some moral governance of the universe, might be the only way to close the gap the dualism leaves open, given that a purely secular ethics seemed to him unable to guarantee that duty and self-interest ultimately coincide.

I would like help thinking through how this compares with William James's roughly contemporaneous involvement in psychical research: as a founder of the American Society for Psychical Research, a longtime investigator of the medium Leonora Piper, and, briefly, president of the British society as well. On the surface, the two men appear to be doing much the same unusual thing, treating séances and telepathy claims as legitimate scientific data. But I suspect their motivations, and what would have counted as vindication for each, differed substantially given how differently their broader philosophical projects were built: Sidgwick's axiom-based rationalist ethics with its unresolved dualism, against James's pragmatism, radical empiricism, and general comfort with letting evidence accumulate piecemeal rather than demanding a tidy metaphysical payoff. Could you compare Sidgwick's and James's motivations for, and implicit standards of evidence in, their psychical research, and say which of the two had the more philosophically coherent reason for undertaking this kind of inquiry at all, given what each was otherwise committed to?
