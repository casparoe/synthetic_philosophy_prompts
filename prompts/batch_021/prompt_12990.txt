I'm a novelist at work on a collection of short stories, each one built around a single classic thought experiment in philosophy, meant for readers who have never taken a philosophy course. I would like your help with the entry on the nature of space. In the Principia, Newton offers two illustrations for why he believed space itself must be a real, independently existing thing rather than merely a system of relations among bodies: the spinning bucket, whose water climbs the sides even once it has stopped moving relative to the bucket, and the less-discussed thought experiment of two globes joined by a cord, revolving in a universe that otherwise contains nothing else at all, where the tension in the cord still betrays the rotation. His relationist critics, from Leibniz through Mach, held that motion only ever makes sense relative to other bodies, and so owed some account of what, if anything, the globes could be said to be rotating relative to. Please write a story that dramatizes this dispute through a character's concrete situation rather than through a lecture disguised as dialogue. I would like the globes-in-an-empty-universe case, in particular, to be taken seriously enough that a reader finishes the story feeling why the question is genuinely hard to settle, rather than simply being told that it is.
