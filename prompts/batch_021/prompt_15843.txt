So in my African Philosophy seminar we've spent like two weeks on the Menkiti vs. Gyekye debate over personhood, and I brought up something from my own family as an example: my great-uncle cut off contact with basically everyone after a falling out over a family business deal back in the 90s -- doesn't call, skips every wedding and funeral, never chips in for anything -- and my grandma has this line about him, "he stopped being people to us a long time ago, he's hardly a person now." I mentioned it in my study group as a real-life instance of the "relational personhood" stuff we keep reading about, and it kicked off a whole argument between Ade and Marcus that I honestly can't settle myself.

Ade's opening move was that my grandma's line is actually a pretty faithful application of Menkiti's radical communitarianism: on his view personhood isn't a fixed fact you're born with, it's achieved and sustained by discharging your obligations within a web of relations, and Menkiti says explicitly that someone can fail to attain full personhood or attain it only partially -- so someone who's opted out of those obligations for thirty straight years has, by Menkiti's own logic, actually regressed, not just fallen out of favor. Marcus said that's stretching the theory past what its built for: Menkiti's actual examples are about kids maturing into personhood through initiation and moral development, not grown men losing personhood points for skipping Christmas, and if "diminished personhood" also covers voluntary withdrawal, you've basically handed the community a license to downgrade anyone it's currently annoyed with -- which is exactly why Gyekye's "moderate" communitarianism insists on a minimal core of personhood, tied to the okra (the soul, in Akan thought), that's metaphysically complete prior to communal approval and can't be revoked by it. Ade's comeback was that Marcus can't just restrict the theory to childhood like that: if achieving personhood through discharging obligations is the whole mechanism in Menkiti's view, confining it to childhood is arbitrary, so either the "achievement" picture bites the bullet on adults too, or it isn't doing the normative work Menkiti clearly wants it to do. Marcus's reply was, fine, say it extends to adults -- that's exactly the reductio: it just shows Menkiti's picture needs Gyekye's locked layer underneath it, because otherwise my grandma's verdict becomes a live philosophical conclusion instead of just a harsh thing grandmothers say. Ade wasn't done though: if Gyekye's locked layer is doing all the work of blocking the "not a person" conclusion, Ade said, then "moderate" communitarianism is barely distinguishable from ordinary individual-rights liberalism wearing an African name, which seems like a weird result for a theory that's supposed to be a genuine alternative to it.
