I'm teaching a graduate seminar on medieval political philosophy this term, and last week we spent a long session picking apart Aquinas's actual position on tyrannicide -- De Regimine Principum, the relevant bit of his commentary on the Sentences, and the question on sedition in the Summa. What struck the class is how much more cautious he actually is compared to the "Aquinas said its fine to kill bad kings" version that gets passed around in intro classes -- he's pretty wary of private individuals taking it on themselves to kill a tyrant, seems to want any deposing done by whatever public authority set the ruler up in the first place, and keeps circling back to the worry that a botched or unauthorized tyrannicide could produce more disorder and suffering than the tyranny it's replacing.

That's the background for a question one of my students asked that I couldn't answer well on the spot, and it's been bugging me since. Say a tyrant today gets taken out by a drone strike ordered by some other country's government, maybe with a nod of approval from the UN Security Council -- so not a lone assassin, but also not any authority that the tyrannized population itself set up or answers to. Does that count as the kind of "public authority" action Aquinas thinks can licitly depose a tyrant, given that his own examples all seem to assume some authority internal to the same community (a council, the barons, a liege lord within the same political order)? Or is a foreign power acting on a population's behalf just a structurally different case that his framework doesn't really have the resources to adjudicate one way or another? I'd love an answer that actually works through how his argument hangs together rather than just citing the "yes tyrannicide can be ok" headline.
