Last week a friend and I got into a long argument about Jeremy Bentham's unpublished manuscripts on sexual morality, in which he argued—decades before this was a remotely respectable thing to say in print, and well before Mill's harm principle existed—that the law had no legitimate business criminalizing sodomy or other "irregular" sexual acts between consenting adults, on the grounds that such acts, practiced privately, produce no real injury to anyone. My friend took this as a striking historical vindication of hedonistic utilitarianism: run the felicific calculus honestly, without smuggling in anything like a modern right to privacy, and you land on a strikingly liberal conclusion about sexual conduct.

I'm not convinced, and I'd like help making the opposing case. It seems to me that Bentham can only reach his tolerant conclusion by quietly declining to count a particular kind of pain: the disgust and "antipathy" that the surrounding population feels toward the conduct in question. Nothing in the calculus itself tells you that this antipathy shouldn't count, or should count for less than the pleasure of the minority engaged in the disfavored act—if enough people are sufficiently revolted, the sums could easily favor suppression instead. I know that Bentham elsewhere treats "sympathy and antipathy" as a spurious rival to the principle of utility rather than a legitimate input to it, but that move looks question-begging here, since the antipathy he needs to disqualify is, as a psychological matter, just ordinary pain, felt by ordinary people, with no obvious mark distinguishing it from any other pain that's allowed to enter the ledger.

Could you construct the strongest version of this argument on my behalf: that Bentham's toleration of consensual "irregular" sexual conduct is not something his hedonistic premises deliver on their own, but instead depends on a substantive, independently motivated restriction on which pains are allowed to count—a restriction that a thoroughgoing felicific calculus has no internal resources to justify? I'd rather see the argument pushed as far as it can go before you tell me where you think it breaks down.
