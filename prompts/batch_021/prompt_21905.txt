I've spent the past few months reporting a feature on how philanthropic funders are trying to bring more rigor to decisions about existential-risk research, and something a source told me over coffee last week has stayed with me. She sits on a small technical advisory committee at a foundation that funds work aimed at reducing catastrophic risks from advanced AI. For every proposal, each of the committee's five members privately estimates the probability that the research direction under review will meaningfully reduce that risk within a decade, and those numbers feed into a standard expected-value formula the foundation uses to rank proposals against their cost. For one recent proposal, the five estimates ranged from around 5 percent to 65 percent. She was confident this wasn't simply a matter of some members being better informed than others: everyone had read the same background material, and when she pressed the outliers to defend themselves, nobody budged or conceded error, because each was weighing the same thin, genuinely inconclusive evidence in a defensible way. She also doubts that polling a dozen more advisors would have narrowed that spread; if anything, she suspects it would just have produced more reasonable-sounding numbers scattered across the same wide range.

Since the foundation's rules call for a single number, the committee averaged the five estimates and let the proposal move forward, but my source admitted it felt like a small sleight of hand: the average implies a shared, settled view that plainly doesn't exist, and it quietly discards the fact that this estimate was contested in a way that, say, five estimates clustered tightly around 30 percent would not have been. She's now turning over whether the committee should instead have deferred to the most pessimistic member, reported a range rather than a point estimate, or handled it some other way entirely, and she asked me, half-joking, whether she'd simply flunked a statistics test or whether her unease was tracking something real. I'd like to work out whether she's onto something. Is there a principled objection to folding that kind of disagreement into a single number, or is this just the ordinary, unavoidable texture of deciding under uncertainty? And if there's a genuine problem here, what would handling it well actually look like for a committee like hers?
