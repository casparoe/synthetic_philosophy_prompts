I spent my working life as a geophysicist, much of it devoted to mapping the ocean floor, and so I lived through the tail end of one of the great reversals in my field: continental drift went from a fringe, faintly embarrassing idea to the unquestioned foundation of plate tectonics over the course of a few decades. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy of science, and something about that history nags at me. Wegener was vindicated on the big picture — the continents really do move — but the specific mechanism he proposed, involving tidal and rotational forces, was rightly dismantled on physical grounds by contemporaries such as Harold Jeffreys; a workable mechanism, in the form of seafloor spreading and mantle convection, only arrived decades later. So when I hear today's cosmologists speak with real confidence about the dark matter and dark energy supposed to make up some ninety-five percent of everything there is, I no longer know how to calibrate my own trust. Is my hesitation just a vague, unearned skepticism dressed up as wisdom, or is there a principled way of telling, in advance, which parts of a well-supported theory are likely to survive its eventual overhaul and which are likely to be swept away with it?
