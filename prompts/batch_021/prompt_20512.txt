So this happened at work and it's been bugging me for a week. I'm an engineering manager, and I run our team's "architecture review board" -- three senior engineers (let's say Renee, Tobias, and Wei) who have to sign off on any design doc that touches a public-facing API before it ships. The charter for this board is basically an old wiki page nobody's updated in years, and it says, more or less verbatim: "A proposal is approved once it is confirmed to be (a) backward compatible and (b) accompanied by a tested rollback plan. No further review is needed once both are confirmed." I've been writing software for about a decade and never took a philosophy class in my life, but I can't shake the feeling that something genuinely weird happened here, not just a personality clash.

Here's what went down last sprint. All three reviewers answered the two yes/no questions on a proposal, and it came out: Renee said compatible=yes, rollback=yes; Tobias said compatible=yes, rollback=no; Wei said compatible=no, rollback=yes. Tally the two questions separately and compatibility passes 2-1 (Renee, Tobias) and rollback also passes 2-1 (Renee, Wei) -- so per the charter, both boxes are checked and the proposal auto-approves. But look instead at each reviewer's own personal verdict: Renee was the only one who actually said "approve"; Tobias and Wei each independently said "reject," for completely unrelated reasons, so on a straight headcount the proposal looses 2-1. We went with the charter and shipped it as approved, and now the wider team is split about whether that was even defensible. One camp says it's absurd to call something "approved" when two out of three actual reviewers rejected it, no asterisk needed. The other camp says the headcount method is actually worse, because Tobias and Wei don't even object to the same thing -- Tobias's problem is the rollback plan, Wei's is compatibility -- so their "2-1 majority" isn't a shared verdict on anything, whereas the box-by-box method at least corresponds to a real majority on each real question.

I have to bring a recommendation doc to Friday's retro. Could you draft it for me? I want an actual pick for which procedure the board formally adopts from now on (not a wishy-washy both-sides thing), the reasoning laid out plainly enough that I can defend it out loud to the room, a real engagement with the strongest version of whichever side you don't pick instead of one dismissive sentence, and a call on what we do about the proposal that's already live -- leave it, revert it, or something in between.
