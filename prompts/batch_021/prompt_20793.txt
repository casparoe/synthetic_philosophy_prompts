For my Metaphysics of Causation seminar (small elective, maybe 12 of us) we spent basically two whole sessions this week on causation by omission, and I'm still turning it over. The professor's go-to example was that my basil plant dies because I forgot to water it, but on a plain counterfactual analysis literally everyone else who also didn't water it counts as a cause too — my downstairs neighbour, some guy in Antarctica, anyone else on the planet who owns a watering can, since it's true of all of them that if they had watered it, it wouldn't have died. That's obviously absurd, but apparently how you fix it is where people in the literature actually split, and I want to get this straight before my response paper is due Friday (also got into a whole argument about it with my roommate, who isn't even in the class, so now I need to win that too).

As I understand it, Lewis's move (I think its the essay called "Void and Object"?) is to just bite the bullet: keep the ordinary counterfactual-dependence analysis, admit that yes technically the neighbour and the guy in Antarctica are causes too, and explain why we never mention them by appeal to pragmatics/explanatory relevance — basically the same trick he uses elsewhere for the cause-vs-background-condition distinction. Sarah McGrath's answer, in that "Causation by Omission" paper, goes a different way: she wants the norms built right into the causal relation itself, so an omission only counts as a genuine cause when it deviates from some default (a duty, a statistical norm, proper function, whatever) — meaning the neighbour's non-watering isn't a cause at all, not just a cause nobody bothers citing.

So here's what I actually want from you: don't just summarize both positions, actually compare them. Does putting the normativity into the metaphysics (McGrath) buy you anything real over doing it at the level of pragmatics/explanation (Lewis), or is she just smuggling in the same kind of judgment he does and relocating it? And is there a cost to her view in cases where there's no obvious norm or default to appeal to at all — does causation just go silent there? I want an actual verdict on which strategy handles the puzzle better, not a "both have their merits" cop-out.
