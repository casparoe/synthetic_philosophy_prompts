It's the last question on the take-home final (worth 20 points, 5 per part), from the unit where we spent two weeks contrasting Lewis's counterfactual analysis of causation with process/conserved-quantity theories, including the so-called profligacy problem for causation by omission -- whether an absence can really be a cause of anything. Here's the question, word for word:

"Priya agreed to feed her neighbor's goldfish, Bubbles, while the neighbor was away for a week. On day four, Priya forgot. Bubbles starved to death before the neighbor got back.
(a) Using the counterfactual theory of causation, explain why Priya's forgetting counts as a cause of Bubbles's death.
(b) Explain the profligacy problem this generates for the counterfactual theory, illustrating with your own example.
(c) Present one strategy from the literature for restricting which omissions count as causes (or for denying that omissions are causes at all), and state one significant difficulty facing that strategy.
(d) Does the metaphysical verdict you reach in (c) settle whether Priya is morally responsible for Bubbles's death? Briefly explain why or why not."

And here's the student's answer, copied exactly as submitted:

"(a) On the counterfactual theory of causation (Lewis), an event c is a cause of an event e just in case e counterfactually depends on c: had c not occurred, e would not have occurred. Here, if Priya had fed Bubbles, Bubbles would not have died when she did. Since Bubbles did die, and would not have if Priya had acted, Priya's omission counts as a cause of Bubbles's death, even though 'not feeding' isn't a positive event or action.

(b) The trouble is that the same reasoning applies to literally anyone else who also didn't feed Bubbles. If I had flown out and fed her myself, she wouldn't have died either -- so by the same logic, my not doing that is also 'a cause' of her death. But it seems absurd to say that every person on Earth who didn't feed Bubbles is equally implicated in causing her death. This is the profligacy problem: the counterfactual theory generates far too many causes for any given omission.

(c) One response, due to David Lewis, is to insist that causation strictly requires an actual physical process connecting distinct events -- some transfer of energy or a conserved physical quantity from cause to effect. Since there's no such process linking, say, my inaction on the other side of the world to Bubbles's death, Lewis concludes that omissions are never causes in the strict sense; at best they're 'quasi-causes', picked out by a seperate and weaker counterfactual relation that we loosely call causation in everyday speech. This blocks the profligacy problem by denying that any of the candidates -- Priya included -- really cause anything.
The trouble with this response is that it proves too much: we routinely and successfully explain events by citing absences (the plant died for lack of water, the fire went out for lack of oxygen), and it's hard to believe all of these explanations are simply mistaken about there being a causal relation there. If Lewis is right, a huge swathe of ordinary and scientific causal explanation turns out to be systematically false, which is a steep cost to pay just to avoid profligacy.

(d) Yes, I think it does settle it. Since (c) shows that, strictly speaking, Priya's omission isn't a genuine cause of Bubbles's death any more than anyone else's omission was, and moral responsibility should track genuine causal contribution, it follows that Priya isn't specially responsible for the death either. So the metaphysics does settle the responsibility question, in a fairly deflationary way: nobody in particular is responsible for what happened to Bubbles, or if anyone is, it isn't in virtue of some special causal relation that Priya bears to her death and the rest of us don't."
