So my department's Thursday reading group on Moore blew up into an actual argument last week and I could use a neutral referee. We'd just finished the "Ideal" chapter of Principia Ethica -- personal affection and beauty as the two great goods, plus the organic unities stuff, i.e. a whole can be worth more than the sum of its parts. (1) Sam brought up Moore's thought experiment with two uninhabited planets, one ravishingly beautiful and one just a heap of filth, neither ever to be seen by anyone -- Moore says the beautiful one is still the better world, and Sam took this as Moore's strongest case that beauty has worth even with zero consciousness attached to it. (2) Nadia said that sits badly with what Moore says a few pages later about love: affection is only really valuable, organic-unity-wise, when the admiration actually fits, i.e. when the person really has the good qualities being responded to -- feeling minus a worthy object isn't worth much on his view. So apparently consciousness without a fitting object is nearly worthless to Moore, but an object with zero consciousness attached (his lonely planet) isn't, which she called a flat inconsistency. (3) Sam said no: the planet case is only a comparative claim, beautiful-but-unwitnessed beats ugly-but-unwitnessed, not a claim that unwitnessed beauty equals appreciated beauty. So there's a small "floor" value sitting in the object alone, plus a much bigger bonus once its correctly appreciated, and that same floor-plus-bonus structure is available on the affection side too, no inconsistency needed. (4) Nadia answered that this is too generous to him: Moore's remarks on evils treat admiration misdirected at something rotten as close to a positive evil, not merely a smaller good, which wrecks the tidy floor Sam wants for affection. They just kept going in circles after that, so I gave up. Can you go through this move by move and say who's right at each step, and whether there's something both of them keep missing that Moore himself actually has to answer for?
