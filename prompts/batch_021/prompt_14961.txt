So for my philosophy of mind seminar we've spent the last couple of weeks on a unit my professor calls "realism about interpretability" - basically the question of whether it even makes sense to say neural nets "have representations" in the same serious sense philosophers usually mean when they talk about mental representation, or whether that's just folk-psychology talk we're projecting onto black boxes to make them feel more familiar. I think I actually found the move that settles the debate though: a bunch of the interpretability papers we read train a simple linear probe on a model's hidden activations and show you can decode stuff like sentiment, part of speech, or even whether the model "believes" a claim is true or false, at like 90-95% accuracy, just from a linear readout of the activations. If the information is sitting right there in a form a dead-simple linear classifier can pull out, that seems like about the strongest evidence you could ask for that the network is genuinely representing that property internally, rather than the info just being incidentally correlated with something else going on in the activations.

My roommate, who's doing an ML PhD, says I'm being way too quick and that probing results like this are basically inconclusive on there own, which honestly just sounds like moving the goalposts so people who are already skeptical of interpretability never have to update their view. Given that we can now reliably decode all these concepts with a simple linear probe, shouldn't the burden of proof have shifted decisively onto people like my roommate who deny that networks have real internal representations, rather than the other way around? And if hitting 90%+ probe accuracy still isn't enough to count as "representing" a concept, what would it even take - doesnt that just make the whole realism-about-interpretability debate unfalsifiable?
