A friend of mine who's way more into philosophy of mind than I am was trying to convince me last week that the whole cognitive penetration of perception debate is basically settled at this point and that Fodor's modularity picture lost. His two "gotcha" examples were (1) the Adelson checkershadow illusion, where two squares are literally the same shade of grey but you can't help seeing them as different once the shadow context is there, even after you've checked it yourself with a color picker, and (2) predictive processing / Bayesian brain models of vision, where your priors and expectations get baked directly into the perceptual inference itself. He says both cases show that your beliefs about the world are changing what you actually see, not just how you judge or report it, so cognitive penetrability is basically confirmed now and Fodor's old "observation reconsidered" arguement for encapsulation doesn't really hold up anymore.

I don't know this literature well enough to push back myself, but something about it felt a little too easy, like maybe he's using "penetration" more loosely than philosophers actually mean it, or lumping together things that shouldn't be lumped together. I've got an intro phil of mind course covering exactly this next week, so I'd rather show up already knowing whether his argument actually does the work he thinks it does instead of just nodding along in seminar. Does either example really prove what he's claiming it proves?
