I have no formal training in game theory, but I've spent a good deal of time working through the centipede game on my own, and something about the standard backward-induction argument strikes me as circular rather than merely counterintuitive. It concludes that common knowledge of rationality forces both players to take at the very first opportunity, yet to reach that conclusion it must also specify what a rational player should believe if the other player passes instead -- a situation which, by the argument's own conclusion, can never actually arise. It looks to me like the argument is quietly answering a question it has no standing to answer, since common knowledge of rationality was supposed to rule that very situation out from the start. Please argue that this is a genuine defect in the backward-induction argument and not just an artifact of how the paradox is usually popularized: specifically, that no amount of common knowledge of rationality can make immediate defection the uniquely rational way to play the centipede game, because the case for that conclusion depends on beliefs about nodes that, by its own logic, rational players will never reach.
