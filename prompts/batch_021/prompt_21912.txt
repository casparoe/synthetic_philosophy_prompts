Ok so I've fallen down a signaling rabbit hole again (happens every few months, blame overcomingbias) and there's one thing about the handicap principle that I don't think I've ever seen properly settled. Zahavi's original pitch is that a signal has to be costly -- like, actually wasteful -- to be trustworthy, since a free signal could just as easily get produced by a low-quality faker. But Grafen's ESS models from the 90s, the ones that supposedly rescued the handicap principle mathematically, only seem to require that the marginal cost of upping your signal fall as your quality rises -- not that anyone at equilibrium is actually burning resources. And then Maynard Smith and Harper go and carve out "index" signals that are honest for free, no handicap required, just because they're physically tethered to the trait. So which is it: does costly signaling actually require cost to be paid, or is the "cost" mostly counterfactual -- i.e. it just has to be true that cheating would cost more than a low-quality signaler could afford, while honest signalers at equilibrium pay next to nothing? Write an essay that actually engages with Zahavi vs. Grafen vs. the index-signal objection here, weighs them properly, and doesn't wimp out with "it depends" -- pick a reading of what cost has to mean for the handicap principle to do the honesty-guaranteeing work everyone thinks it does, and defend it.
