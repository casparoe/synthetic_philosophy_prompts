My high school's philosophy elective just finished a unit on hypocrisy and the standing to blame, and a few nights later something from it happened for real at student council: our president, Sam, publicly called out a member, Alex, for skipping a mandatory meeting without notice. Two classmates from that elective, Priya and Marcus, ended up arguing about it afterward, because Sam had skipped a meeting himself the month before and never said a word about it.

Priya said Sam had no standing to criticize Alex, since holding someone to a rule you've broken yourself, without ever mentioning it, is the textbook case of hypocrisy undermining a critic's standing. Marcus said that's beside the point: Sam's hypocrisy has no bearing on whether skipping the meeting was wrong, and accused Priya of committing a tu quoque -- attacking the messenger instead of the message. Priya replied that she wasn't disputing the message at all, only denying that Sam was the right messenger to deliver it. Marcus pushed back that if any unacknowledged past lapse permanently disqualified someone from criticizing that same behavior, nobody could ever criticize anything, since almost everyone has skipped some commitment at some point. Priya answered that the cases still differ: Sam never owned up to his own absence, while owning up to a lapse before criticizing others isn't hypocritical in the same way. Marcus's comeback was that this concedes her real point -- if an apology is what fixes things, then the actual issue was never hypocrisy as such, just whether Sam had been honest about his own record.

Go through this round by round and say who has the stronger argument at each step, including anything you think both of them are missing, and end with a final verdict on whether Sam had standing to call Alex out.
