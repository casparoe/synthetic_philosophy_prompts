This year marks the centenary of Frege's death, and as the department's resident Frege scholar I've been asked to give the opening remarks at the day-long workshop we're hosting on his philosophy of logic and language. The audience splits roughly down the middle: historians of analytic philosophy, who already know about the political diary Frege kept in the spring of 1924 and about Michael Dummett's own dismay on discovering it, versus graduate students in logic and philosophy of language, who know Frege only through Sinn and Bedeutung and the Grundgesetze, and who have never heard of any of this. I don't want to reduce the diary to a tactful aside before the 'real' philosophy begins, but neither should it hijack a day otherwise devoted to technical work. Please draft about five minutes of opening remarks that bring the newcomers up to speed on just enough of the story, stake out a genuine position — rather than simply surveying both sides — on whether the diary bears on our assessment of his logicism and his insistence that thoughts inhabit an objective realm untouched by the psychology of whoever thinks them, and leave the room in the right frame of mind for a day of talks on sense, reference, and the foundations of arithmetic.
