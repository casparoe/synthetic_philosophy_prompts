I spent the better part of four decades in radio astronomy, including some years contributing to one of the SETI survey programs, and only in the last couple of years, in retirement, have I turned from journals to philosophy -- a stranger and more unsettling pleasure than I expected. Lately I have become rather fixated on one particular proposed resolution to the Fermi paradox: the "aestivation hypothesis" put forward by Anders Sandberg, Stuart Armstrong, and Milan Ćirković, in a paper whose title borrows a line from Lovecraft about that which lies eternal. Their argument, as I understand it, runs roughly as follows: because Landauer's principle ties the minimum energy cost of erasing a bit of information to the ambient temperature, a civilization patient enough to wait would get far more computation out of a given store of mass-energy by deferring almost all of its activity until the universe has cooled well below its present temperature, rather than by acting now, while the cosmos is still comparatively hot and wasteful to compute in. On this view, a sufficiently advanced and sufficiently patient civilization's rational course is near-total dormancy in the present epoch, with real activity delayed until an unimaginably distant and colder future -- so that the great silence we observe would not be the absence of anyone out there, but the sound of everyone sleeping on purpose.

What I would like from you is a short story built on that premise. I am less interested in a tale that explains or defends the physics -- I can get that well enough from the paper itself -- than in one that sits with its psychological and ethical strangeness: what kind of mind, or civilization, chooses to defer nearly all of its experience for a payoff so remote that nothing presently existing could ever witness it, and what such a choice would do to whoever makes it, or to whoever stumbles across evidence of it. You might write it from inside the dormant civilization itself, in whatever fragmentary way such a mind could be said to experience anything at all; or from the vantage of a younger, comparatively impatient species, not unlike our own, that detects some faint anomaly suggesting it is not as alone as it seems and must decide what, if anything, to do with that knowledge. Either way, I would rather have a story that takes the underlying wager seriously as an existential and ethical proposition than one that treats it merely as set dressing for a first-contact plot.
