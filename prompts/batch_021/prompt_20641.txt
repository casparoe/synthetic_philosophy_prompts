"Explain Leibniz's claim that the actual world is 'the best of all possible worlds.' Your answer should address: (a) what Leibniz means by a 'possible world' and the role that compossibility plays in distinguishing one possible world from another [6 pts]; (b) the criterion by which God ranks possible worlds in perfection [7 pts]; and (c) how Leibniz can maintain that God necessarily chooses the best world while still holding that the world's existence is contingent rather than necessary [7 pts]."

And here's the answer I'm trying to grade:

"For Leibniz, a possible world is not just any random collection of things we can imagine; it's a maximal set of individual substances whose complete concepts are compossible, meaning they can all be true of the same world at once. Since each substance's complete concept already contains everything that will ever happen to it, two substances are compossible only if there's no contradiction in their both existing together. This is why Leibniz can say there's a possible world in which Julius Caesar doesn't cross the Rubicon: it's simply a different, but internally consistent, complete concept of 'Caesar' paired with the rest of that world's substances, not compossible with our world's other substances.

God, surveying all these possible worlds in his understanding, picks the one to actualize by weighing how much perfection each one contains. For Leibniz perfection is basically the balance of happiness and unhappiness of all the creatures involved — the best world is the one that comes out with the greatest net sum of happiness once you add up every rational creature's lot across all of history, which is why Leibniz can say this world is best even though it has evil and suffering in it: the suffering is outweighed elsewhere in the ledger.

The hardest part is squaring 'God necessarily chooses the best' with 'the world is contingent.' Leibniz's move is to say God's choice has only moral necessity, not absolute necessity: God, being supremely wise and good, will certainly select the best, but nothing in the concept of 'world' forces this the way a contradiction would. And since God's choice is what fixes which world is actual, and God could only ever rationally settle on the best one, the other 'possible' worlds Leibniz talks about are possible in name only — once you factor in that a perfectly wise God is doing the choosing, there was really only ever one option on the table, so calling the alternatives 'possible' is more a manner of speaking than a substantive commitment on Leibniz's part."
