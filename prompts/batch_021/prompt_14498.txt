I got into a great argument with a colleague from poli sci at a faculty dinner last week (there was wine involved, so forgive me if the numbers below are a bit mangled). She's writing a piece for one of the law reviews arguing that we should replace deliberating criminal juries with a large anonymous poll of randomly selected citizens, majority vote decides. Her whole case rests on Condorcet's jury theorem: as long as each juror is even a little better than a coin flip at figuring out whether the defendant is actually guilty, a panel of a few hundred independent voters reaches the correct verdict with something like 99% certainty, comfortably beating the twelve people we currently lock in a room to argue each other into or out of thier initial impressions. On her view deliberation isn't doing any real epistemic work; it's just persuasion and social pressure dressed up as reasoning, so swapping it out for more independent voters is a straightforward accuracy win with no real tradeoff.

I found this pretty convincing at the table, and I'm tempted to build a week of my social epistemology seminar around her draft as the provocation. Before I commit to that though, can you walk me through whether Condorcet's theorem actually get her all the way to that conclusion? Is there a real gap between what the theorem proves and what she's doing with it, or is this basically right, so the burden of proof now really is on people who still want to defend deliberation?
