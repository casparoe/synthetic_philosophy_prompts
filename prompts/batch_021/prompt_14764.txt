**Question 3.** The Kalai–Smorodinsky (1975) solution is often presented as "the" monotonic alternative to Nash's (1950) bargaining solution.

(a) State the axioms that Nash's solution and the KS solution have in common, and state precisely the axiom on which the two solutions diverge, for each. Explain in your own words what the diverging axiom requires.

(b) Let the disagreement point be d = (0,0) and let the feasible set S be the convex hull of (0,0), (6,0), (5,1), and (0,3). Find the KS solution and the Nash solution for (S,d), and explain geometrically why they come out different.

(c) A classmate claims: "Kalai's (1977) egalitarian solution is really just a special case of the KS solution, for problems where the ideal point is symmetric relative to the disagreement point." Is this accurate? Explain how the egalitarian solution is defined, and how it relates, in general, to the KS solution.

And here is the student's answer:

---

**(a)** Both solutions satisfy Pareto-efficiency (no other feasible pair makes one player better off without making the other worse off), Symmetry (if permuting the players' labels leaves the problem unchanged, the solution must give them equal payoffs), and Scale Invariance (applying a positive affine transformation to either player's utility scale shouldn't change the physical outcome picked out, since a von Neumann–Morgenstern utility function is only meaningful up to such transformations anyway). They part ways over a fourth axiom. Nash additionally imposes Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA): if the feasible set shrinks but still contains the old solution point, the solution shouldn't move. Kalai and Smorodinsky instead impose Individual Monotonicity: if the feasible set expands in a way that is weakly more generous to player 2 at every level of player 1's payoff, then player 2's payoff at the solution shouldn't fall. It was precisely the observation that Nash's solution can violate this last, intuitively very mild-sounding requirement — a set can expand entirely "in favor" of a player and that player's Nash payoff can still drop — that motivated Kalai and Smorodinsky's alternative.

**(b)** The ideal point is a = (6,3): player 1's payoff ceiling is 6 (at (6,0)) and player 2's is 3 (at (0,3)). The KS solution sits where the ray from d = (0,0) through a = (6,3) — slope 1/2 — crosses the Pareto frontier. That frontier has two pieces, (6,0)–(5,1) and (5,1)–(0,3). Trying the first piece forces u1 = 4, which falls outside that segment's range of u1 between 5 and 6, so the ray must cross the second piece instead; solving there gives the KS solution (10/3, 5/3). For the Nash solution we maximize u1 × u2 along the frontier: on (6,0)–(5,1) the product is increasing throughout, so its best point on that piece is the kink (5,1), worth 5; on (5,1)–(0,3) the product 5 + 5s − 10s^2 (with u1 = 5 − 5s, u2 = 1 + 2s) is maximized at s = 1/4, giving (15/4, 3/2), worth 45/8 — better than 5. So the Nash solution is (15/4, 3/2), noticeably different from the KS point. Intuitively, Nash's solution only cares about where the product is maximized, while KS's solution is pinned to the direction of the ideal point, so it moves whenever the ideal point moves even if the frontier near the eventual answer hasn't changed shape at all.

**(c)** I think the classmate's claim actually understates the connection. The Kalai–Smorodinsky solution is standardly characterized as the Pareto-efficient point at which the two players' gains over the disagreement point, u1 − d1 and u2 − d2, are equalized — it splits the difference between what each side could hope for. The egalitarian solution, due to Kalai (1977), is built around exactly the same idea: pick the efficient point with u1 − d1 = u2 − d2. So whenever the two players' ideal payoffs above disagreement happen to match, the two solutions must coincide, and even off that special case they stay close in spirit, differing mainly in bookkeeping rather than in substance. In problem (b), for instance, since the egalitarian solution also looks for the point with u1 = u2 on the frontier, you'd expect broadly the same kind of split as the KS answer above, just normalized differently. What really separates egalitarianism from KS is not this shared "equal gains" idea but the fact that the egalitarian solution gives up Scale Invariance: "equal gains" is only a meaningful target once you've fixed a cardinal scale for each player, so independently rescaling one player's utility (say, doubling player 1's numbers while leaving player 2's alone) changes which point counts as equalizing gains. KS, by construction, is immune to this, which is why most treatments present it as the more defensible of the two "equal-treatment" solutions within the standard, ordinally-invariant approach to bargaining.
