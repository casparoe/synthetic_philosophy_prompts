I've spent a long time reading around the rationalist blogosphere, where talk of "calibration training," Brier scores, and generally putting numbers on your beliefs is treated as background common sense. That habit eventually pulled me into the more academic end of formal epistemology, specifically the literature on "accuracy-first" or "epistemic utility" approaches to justifying probabilism—the requirement that rational credences obey the probability axioms. I already knew the classic pragmatic case for this, the Dutch Book argument: if your credences are incoherent, a clever bookie can construct a set of bets that you'll accept one by one but that guarantees you a net loss. What I hadn't appreciated until recently is that there's supposed to be an entirely separate, "purely epistemic" argument for the same conclusion, originally due to the philosopher James Joyce (no relation to the novelist), which never mentions bets or money at all—it just says that incoherent credences are always dominated in accuracy by some coherent alternative, in every possible world.

I can follow the rough shape of that claim, but I can't reconstruct for myself why it's true, or why—as I gather from some further reading—it depends so delicately on measuring accuracy with a "strictly proper" scoring rule (like the quadratic, or Brier, rule) rather than some other seemingly reasonable way of scoring distance from the truth, like the plain absolute-value rule. Apparently the whole argument breaks down under an improper rule, and the reason has something to do with a property called "immodesty": very roughly, the idea that a rational credence function should never look at itself, by its own lights, and expect some other credence function to be more accurate. Could you walk me through the intuition for why coherence guarantees accuracy-dominance under proper scoring rules—ideally with a feel for the geometry involved, rather than the full proof—and why that guarantee specifically requires propriety? And separately, I'd be curious whether restricting the argument to proper scoring rules in the first place is a neutral methodological choice, or whether (as I've seen suggested in passing, in connection with "separateness of propositions"-style objections to epistemic consequentialism) it quietly smuggles probabilism in as a premise rather than deriving it as an honest conclusion.
