I've been working through F. H. Bradley's 1893 book *Appearance and Reality* for a seminar on the origins of analytic metaphysics, and I've gotten stuck on the paragraph in Book I, Chapter III ("Relation and Quality") that's standardly cited as the source of what's now called "Bradley's regress." Every secondary summary I've found gestures at "an infinite regress of relations" and moves on, but none of them walk through the actual sentence-by-sentence movement of Bradley's own argument, and the prose itself is dense enough that I keep losing the thread of what's doing the real work. Here is the passage:

"But how the relation can stand to the qualities is, on the other side, unintelligible. If it is nothing to the qualities, then they are not related at all; and, if so, as we saw, they have ceased to be qualities, and their relation is a nonentity. But if it is to be something to them, then clearly we now shall require a new connecting relation. For the relation hardly can be the mere adjective of one or both of its terms; or, at least, as such it seems indefensible. And, being something itself, if it does not itself bear a relation to the terms, in what intelligible way will it succeed in being anything to them? But here again we are hurried off into the eddy of a hopeless process, since we are forced to go on finding new relations without end. The links are united by a link, and this bond of union is a link which also has two ends; and these require each a fresh link to connect them with the old."

Could you reconstruct the argument here premise by premise, making explicit in particular the assumption that forces the move from "the relation is something to its terms" to "therefore we need a further relation," rather than just letting the original relation do its relating and stopping there? I'd also like to understand on what grounds Bradley treats the resulting regress as vicious and as evidence that relations are "unintelligible," rather than as a harmless infinite structure of the sort mathematicians would later grow comfortable with elsewhere. Finally, this passage is often flagged as the ancestor of the "unity of the proposition" problem that occupied Russell a decade later, in his discussion of how something like "A differs from B" manages to be a genuine unity rather than a mere list of A, difference, and B. Is Russell's puzzle really the same regress transposed from things-in-the-world onto propositions, or does something about the argument change once what's being unified is a meaning rather than a fact?
