Since retiring from a career in the natural sciences, I've been working through Maimonides in some depth, drawn to him partly because he explains a major religious institution in functional-historical terms, much the way a biologist might explain a vestigial trait that has outlived the pressures which produced it. In the Guide for the Perplexed (III:32), he argues that God never desired animal sacrifice as such; it was instituted only as a concession to the Israelites' idolatrous surroundings, a transitional device to wean a people who could not yet be expected to worship through prayer and contemplation alone. Yet in the Mishneh Torah he codifies the sacrificial laws in exhaustive legal detail and states, as plain normative fact, that the Messiah will rebuild the Temple and restore that very service, with no hint that the restoration is itself only provisional.

Today there are groups in Jerusalem, the Temple Institute foremost among them, who take this restoration literally and are actively preparing for it: reconstructing vessels and priestly garments, training kohanim in the practical details of the service, and even trying to breed a ritually qualified red heifer. Given Maimonides' own rationale for why sacrifice was ever commanded, and given that prayer-based worship has by now been the norm among Jews for far longer than sacrifice ever was, how do you think he would have regarded such a literalist restoration project? Would he see it as simply fulfilling the law and the messianic prophecy as he himself codified them, or does the reasoning he gives in the Guide actually undercut the whole enterprise? I'd also be curious whether you read this as a case where his legal and philosophical writings were deliberately pitched at different audiences with different messages, and, if so, which side he would ultimately have come down on.
