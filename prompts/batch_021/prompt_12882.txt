In my seminar on the metaphysics of relations this term we've been working through F. H. Bradley's regress argument and the "unity of the proposition" problem that Russell tied to it in his early work. I follow the bare mechanics well enough: if a relation like "is wiser than" genuinely connects two terms, it seems to need a further relation tying it to each of those terms, and that further relation needs another to tie it to its terms in turn, and so on without end -- which Bradley took as evidence that relations, and so the whole pluralistic picture of a world of things standing in relations to one another, are not ultimately real. My professor keeps insisting that Bradley meant this regress to be genuinely "vicious" rather than a harmless technical curiosity -- the sort of thing that should make you doubt whether an ordinary true sentence like "Socrates is wise" names one unified fact at all, rather than just Socrates and wisdom sitting side by side on a list -- and half of us in section still can't quite say why it should feel that way rather than merely look that way on paper.

Could you write me a short story that takes this problem as its actual engine rather than its topic? I don't want a story where a philosopher-character sits someone down and explains the regress to them over coffee; I want the plot's own setting and rules -- an institution, a job, a journey, whatever you think will carry it -- to be constructed so that a character runs headlong into the actual problem of needing an ever-further connector to hold two things together, the way a good personal-identity story can make you feel the puzzle of teleportation rather than just have someone state it. I'll leave the genre, length, and ending up to you (something Kafka- or Borges-adjacent in mood seems apt, but don't feel bound by that), except that I'd like it to close with the reader sensing why an infinite regress in the glue of a fact is supposed to be vertiginous, and not just logically odd.
