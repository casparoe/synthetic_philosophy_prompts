For a feature on how old philosophical puzzles keep resurfacing in the formal study of artificial agents, I would like help working through one particular case. In work on self-modifying AI, the 'Löbian obstacle' names the finding that an agent which only trusts conclusions it can formally prove cannot, in general, prove in advance that its own future reasoning — or that of a successor it builds — will be sound, a limitation that follows from Löb's theorem and is closely related to Gödel's second incompleteness theorem. This bears a striking resemblance to Agrippa's trilemma, the ancient skeptical argument that any attempt to justify a belief-forming faculty must terminate in infinite regress, circularity, or an unargued dogmatic stopping point. Compare the two: is the Löbian obstacle best understood as a rigorous, modern instance of Agrippa's trilemma applied to a reasoner's trust in itself, or is the resemblance only superficial? And when researchers try to work around the obstacle — for instance, by having each generation of a self-modifying agent trust only a strictly weaker proof system than its own — which horn of the trilemma are they effectively choosing, and does that choice genuinely dissolve the ancient problem, or merely relocate it?
