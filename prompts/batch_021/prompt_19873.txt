The standard first move is to cash out a disposition conditionally: "the glass is fragile" just means "it would break if struck." But then my professor walked us through a bunch of cases built specifically to break that analysis. There's finks, from C.B. Martin's wire example: a wire that's genuinely dead (disconnected, no current) but wired up to a device that senses contact and switches the current on fast enough that the wire shocks you anyway, so "it would shock you if touched" comes out true even though a second earlier it wasn't live at all - the disposition itself gets flipped on or off right at the moment its being tested. Then, seperately, there's masks (I think this is the antidote case from Bird's paper?), where the disposition stays completely intact the whole time but something blocks it from ever manifesting, like swallowing a poison and its antidote at once, so the stuff never stops being poisonous, it just never gets the chance to actually kill you. My professor mentioned almost as an aside that people run this exact machinery on free will too, asking whether "she could've done otherwise" is the kind of thing a sufficiently sneaky neuroscientist could fink or mask on you without you ever finding out, and that offhand comment is honestly what's been keeping me up more than the readings themselves.

Bonus points if it leaves the reader with that same unsettled feeling my seminar left me with, about whether abilities and powers are ever really sitting "inside" something, versus just being an artifact of when and how we happen to go poking at it.
