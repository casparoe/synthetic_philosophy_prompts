This is my first year serving as area chair for the theory track of a machine learning conference, and one submission has me stuck on how to write up the decision. The paper offers a single low-parameter mechanism for both "grokking" (networks that memorize quickly but generalize only abruptly, much later) and double descent (test error that rises, then falls again, as capacity passes the point where a model can just interpolate the training data) -- two effects that were already well documented, and that the paper visibly fits after the fact rather than deriving in advance. Reviewer 1 wants to reject: we already assign those published curves credence near 1, so reproducing them tests nothing and can't count as confirmation, however elegant the fit; a theory has to risk a prediction before the data is in. Reviewer 2 wants to accept: that standard would make it mysterious why unifying two unrelated phenomena with almost no free parameters ever counts as evidence in any science, and what should matter is the paucity of tunable knobs, not whether the curves happened to already be public. One genuinely untested prediction sits in section 5; Reviewer 1 calls it decorative, Reviewer 2 a good-faith gesture there wasn't time to run. I have to send the meta-review. Could you draft it -- accept, reject, or accept conditional on something specific -- in a way that actually engages Reviewer 2's unification argument rather than just asserting that old data can't confirm anything?
