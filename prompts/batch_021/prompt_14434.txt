so for National Ethics Bowl this year my team got assigned this case where a guy at a party was the only one who noticed his friend had passed out and didn't check on him or call 911, and the friend ended up dying. half my team was like "well obviously his not-calling caused the death, thats not even a question" but our coach (who did a phil minor) said that's actually a huge can of worms philosophers call "causation by omission," and now I can't stop thinking about it. apparently some philosophers think an omission can be a real cause just like an action, but others think that's a mistake, since a genuine cause has to be some actual process happening — like a transfer of energy or motion — and "nothing happening" can't transfer anything, so technically the not-calling is only morally relevant, not literally causally responsible for the death. theres also this annoying asymmetry that makes it worse: if I forget to water my neighbor's plant while she's on vacation, it feels obviously true that my not-watering killed it, but if some totally random stranger on the other side of the world also didn't water it (and also could've saved it, hypothetically), we don't say their not-watering caused anything — even though both of us were equally "necessary" for the plant dying in the counterfactual sense.

can you write me a dialogue between two philosophers debating this, one who defends the "omissions can be genuine causes, full stop" side and one who defends the "only positive events/processes can ever be real causes, omissions just get tacked on for moral reasons" side? i want them to actually use both the party case and the plant case instead of staying abstract, respond directly to each others' specific arguments instead of talking past one another, and ideally get into whether "duty" or "what was normally expected" is secretly doing all the work of deciding which omissions we even bother calling causes in the first place.
