For my philosophy elective, we've been comparing Avicenna's "flying man" argument with Descartes' cogito. Avicenna asks us to imagine someone created instantaneously, fully rational, but suspended in a void, his sight veiled and his limbs held apart so that none of them touch each other or anything else, leaving him with no bodily sensation at all. Such a person, he claims, would still affirm his own existence, even though he could affirm none of his limbs or organs. Avicenna takes this to show that the self is grasped immediately -- in what specialists now call knowledge "by presence" (huduri) -- rather than through any awareness of the body. Our teacher pointed out that interpreters still disagree about how much this really proves: some read it as a genuine proof that the soul is an immaterial substance distinct from the body, while others read it more cautiously, as showing only that self-awareness has this peculiar, non-representational immediacy, without settling any question about substances.

Reasoning from Avicenna's own premises, rather than from later dualist or physicalist positions, what do you think he would conclude about whether a large language model could have the kind of self-awareness his flying man is meant to illustrate? Please work through both readings above, note whether they actually diverge on the AI case, and say whether a "flying man" scenario even makes sense for something like an LLM -- is there a coherent way to strip away its "senses" as Avicenna strips away the flying man's, or does the analogy collapse before we get that far?
