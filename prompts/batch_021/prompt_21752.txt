I'm doing a PhD in philosophy of physics, and our foundations-of-QM reading group (working through Wallace's Emergent Multiverse this term) went completely off the rails last week into quantum suicide. Two people in the group, Owen and Lina, got into a real disagreement about it that never got resolved cuz we ran out of room-booking time, and it's been bugging me since.

Owen's move was basically the classic quantum-immortality argument: if many-worlds is literally true, then every time you pull a trigger wired to some quantum event that kills you in half the branches, you're guaranteed that in every branch where "you" have any further experience at all, that experience is of surviving, since there's no observer left in the other branches to register anything. So he said your subjective credence that you'll wake up should be 1 no matter how lopsided the amplitudes are, and if you ran the experiment a bunch of times and kept waking up, that should even count as evidence for Everett over collapse. Lina said this confuses "some successor of mine will have experiences" with "I should rationally anticipate and care about that outcome," and pointed out that even hardcore Everettians who take branching completely seriously (she brought up Vaidman) still say don't pull the trigger, because the right way to reason about your future is to weight successors by the Born-rule measure -- the same move Wallace needs to justify using the Born rule for ordinary quantum bets in the first place. Owen shot back that this just smuggles ordinary probabilistic caution into a case that's supposed to be pure branching, not chance: he *knows*, with certainty, some version of him survives, so why should a low-measure branch count as "almost none of him" rather than just "a real him with fewer copies"? Until someone gives a non-circular reason why measure should govern a single agent's prudential choices, he said, quantum suicide is reckless toward the people who love the death-branch versions of him, but not irrational for him. Lina's comeback was that this proves too much, since by Owen's logic he should also be indifferent between aging normally and surviving as a vegetative husk in some branch, as long as a successor technically "has experiences" -- which is exactly the sort of thing the measure is suppose to rule out. Owen wasn't having it, and said maybe that just shows measure can't do the job she wants it to do either.

Could you go through this move by move and tell me who's actually got the better of it at each stage, rather than just "both raise good points"? Also curious what, if anything, you think Owen and Lina are each still missing.
