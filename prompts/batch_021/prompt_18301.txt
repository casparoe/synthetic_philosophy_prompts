so I've been re-reading old Overcoming Bias posts on disagreement and something's been bugging me. Aumann's agreement theorem gets trotted out constantly as a reason two honest, rational people can't just agree to disagree, but the whole result only goes through if they share a common prior -- and I've never seen a good defense of why two people with totally different life histories shouldve started out with the same prior in the first place. Hanson's old move (I think it's from that "uncommon priors require origin disputes" paper) is that if you and someone you already trust as rational end up with different priors, you'd have to think the process that produced your prior is more trustworthy than whatever produced theirs -- which seems like it proves way too much, forcing identical priors on stuff that feels more like a starting axiom than a belief you got from evidence.

Can you write me a dialogue between two rationalist-forum regulars hashing this out? One defends something like Hanson's line -- uncommon priors are basically illegitimate absent a reason to trust your own prior-source over the other person's, so lasting disagreement between two people who already trust each other's rationality is still evidence somebody's being sneaky or self-favoring. The other thinks the common prior assumption is doing way more work than people admit, and that the whole Bayesian setup can't even get off the ground without letting some priors just be brute starting points that aren't answerable to "evidence" in the relevant sense. Make them actually engage with each other's strongest moves rather than talk past each other -- I don't want either one to come off as the obvious pushover in the exchange.
