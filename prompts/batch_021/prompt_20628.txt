Here's the rough shape of it: the standard "gotcha" against the no-private-language conclusion is to point at Robinson Crusoe -- alone on his island, inventing his own private ostensive definitions for his own sensations, so clearly a private language is possible and Wittgenstein must be wrong. My move is that this fails because it trades on an ambiguity in "private." Crusoe's language is only *contingently* private -- nobody happens to be around to learn it, but nothing in principle stops another person, dropped on the island later, from picking up his usage the normal way, by watching what he does and the circumstances he uses a word in. The language Wittgenstein is actually attacking is *necessarily* private: the thing a word supposedly names -- a bare sensation with no behavioural "surroundings" at all -- couldn't be picked up by anyone else even in principle, because there's no fact about whether Crusoe's using "S" correctly even for him to check later. His memory of "the same sensation as before" isn't an independent check, it's just more of the same thing that was supposed to be checked in the first place. So Crusoe's diary is irrelevant to the argument, not a counterexample to it. My friend from the seminar says this is basically a cop-out -- that I've just defined "necessarily private" so that nothing could ever satisfy it, which makes the whole thing unfalsifiable -- and now I'm second-guessing myself.
