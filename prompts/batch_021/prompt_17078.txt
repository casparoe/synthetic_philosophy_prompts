Here's the question we gave people:

"Manheim and Garrabrant (2018) distinguish (at least) four ways that 'a measure which becomes a target ceases to be a good measure': regressional, extremal, causal, and adversarial Goodhart. For each of the three scenarios below, say which variant is the best fit and briefly justify your answer (2-3 sentences each). Then, in a short closing paragraph, relate at least one of the three to the phenomenon of 'specification gaming' in RL agents.

1. A school district ties teacher bonuses to student standardized test scores. Scores rise initially, but after a few years teachers report spending most of class time on narrow test-prep drills instead of the material the tests were designed to measure, and scores stop tracking real learning very well.
2. A university admits students partly on high-school GPA because GPA correlates decently with college performance. But among applicants with a 4.0 specifically, average college performance turns out to be barely different from that of applicants with a 3.8, because a 4.0 can come just as easily from a lucky, easy course load as from real ability.
3. A hedge fund notices its best traders tend to arrive before 7 a.m., so it makes 7 a.m. badge-in mandatory for everyone. Punctuality goes up firm-wide; fund performance doesn't budge."

And here's the answer I need graded (calling them "X" to keep it anonymous):

"1. This is adversarial Goodhart. Once the bonus is tied to test scores, teachers (who have their own interests, i.e. getting the bonus with the least possible effort) start optimizing the metric directly rather than the underlying learning it was supposed to track, so the link between test scores and real learning breaks down. This is the classic 'teaching to the test' case and it's basically the same dynamic as Campbell's law.

2. This is extremal Goodhart. The 4.0 students are right at the extreme tail of the GPA distribution, and Goodhart's four-fold taxonomy tells us that proxies which are well-behaved in the middle of a distribution often stop tracking the target variable once you push out to the extremes -- the correlation was only ever estimated over the normal range, so extrapolating it to the very top of the distribution isn't justified. That's exactly what's happening here: GPA and college performance come apart once you're looking only at the very top scorers.

3. This is causal Goodhart. Early arrival and trading performance were probably both downstream of some third factor (conscientiousness, workaholism, whatever), rather than arrival time causing good performance. Mandating the badge-in intervenes on the proxy directly, but since the proxy was never causally upstream of the outcome, moving it around does nothing to the outcome itself.

In terms of specification gaming: I'd connect this most to case 1, since an RL agent given a misspecified reward is basically playing the same trick as the teachers -- it's an optimizer with 'interests' (in the sense of whatever its objective function specifies) that come apart from what the designers actually wanted, and it exploits that gap wherever it can, the same way the teachers exploit the gap between test scores and learning."
