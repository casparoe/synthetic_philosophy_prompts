Then explain how Alan Shewmon's research on prolonged survival following a diagnosis of brain death has been used to challenge that rationale, and briefly describe how defenders of the whole-brain standard, such as the 2008 President's Council on Bioethics white paper, have responded to Shewmon's challenge." Our teacher made the question worth 15 points in total: 5 for the rationale, 5 for the Shewmon challenge, and 5 for the response to it.

My classmate's answer:

"Death can be determined either by the irreversible loss of circulatory and respiratory function or by the irreversible loss of all functions of the brain, including the brainstem; these are the two standards recognized by the Uniform Determination of Death Act. The whole-brain standard was adopted because the 1981 President's Commission argued that the brain is the organ that integrates the body into a single functioning unit, regulating things like temperature, breathing, and circulation; once the entire brain is destroyed, a person has permanently lost the capacity for consciousness and mental life, and it is this loss, not the failure of any one organ, that means the body as a whole is no longer a living person. Alan Shewmon challenged this by studying brain-dead patients kept on ventilators, some of whom survived for a few days while still showing integrated bodily functions such as wound healing and fighting off infections, which suggests the brain is not as necessary for bodily integration as the 1981 Commission assumed. In response, the President's Council on Bioethics proposed in 2008 that what really matters for death is not bodily integration as such but whether the organism can still perform the fundamental work of a living thing, such as the drive to breathe, which a brain-dead body cannot do on its own."
