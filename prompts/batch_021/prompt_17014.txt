I've spent a good part of this year working through the old LessWrong and Arbital material on decision theory, and there is one terminological knot I have not been able to untangle on my own: the relationship between Wei Dai's updateless decision theory (UDT) and the functional decision theory (FDT) that Eliezer Yudkowsky and Nate Soares later formalized. Dai's original 2009 posts frame everything in terms of policy selection: instead of updating on your observations and then reasoning about what to do next, you commit in advance to the best overall mapping from possible observations to actions, evaluated against your prior, so that you are never tempted to deviate from — or regret — the plan your earlier, unupdated self would have wanted, as in counterfactual mugging or Parfit's hitchhiker. The FDT paper, by contrast, is pitched in terms of "subjunctive dependence": you treat your decision as the logical output of a fixed algorithm, and ask what happens everywhere that same algorithm's output shows up, including inside other agents' predictions or simulations of you.

I would like your help thinking through whether this is a substantive difference or merely a change of framing. Please compare UDT and FDT as theories of instrumental rationality, focusing on whether "subjunctive dependence between algorithmic nodes" does any genuine technical work that "policy selection under a fixed prior" could not already do on its own, or whether FDT is best read as a restatement of UDT dressed up with more vivid machinery for intuition-pumping. Where possible, illustrate your answer with a concrete decision problem — a scenario in which a predictor is itself simulating the agent would be a natural test case, though any other example from the literature works too — on which the two frameworks might actually recommend different actions, or explain carefully why no such case exists. Finally, I would be curious to hear which of the two theories you consider the more satisfying foundational account of rational choice, and why.
