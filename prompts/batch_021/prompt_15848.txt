I've been down a bit of a rabbit hole this week for a piece I'm poking at (probably for the ideas section) on why democracies seem so bad at self-correcting lately, and I ended up re-reading that odd little Simone Weil essay from 1943, "On the Abolition of All Political Parties" -- the one where she argues, dead seriously, that organized parties should just be gotten rid of entirely and elections kept, with candidates standing purely as individuals. Her whole case rests on three claims about what a party actually *is*: a machine for manufacturing collective passion, an apparatus for pressuring members' minds into line so independent judgement erodes, and an entity whose real, mostly unspoken goal is its own unlimited growth. That third trait, she says, is what makes every party totalitarian in tendency even when it's too small or too moderate to ever act on it.

I mentioned all this to two friends, Dan and Farah, who both write about politics for a living, and they ended up in a whole back-and-forth over drinks that I just sat back and watched, since I honestly wasn't sure who was winning. Roughly how it went:
(1) Dan said Weil basically nailed the American two-party system decades early -- primaries reward the most zealous slice of each base, crossing the aisle gets you primaried out, and both parties are visibly organized around fundraising and seat-share for their own sake, with loyalty tests standing in for actual argument.
(2) Farah said that's anachronistic: Weil was writing about rigid, doctrinal mass parties in a Europe that had just watched fascist and communist parties claw their way to absolute power, not sprawling coalitions that routinely nominate people who disagree with each other on huge questions, so it's a stretch to hang her diagnosis on something so structurally different.
(3) Dan replied that the mechanism she's describing doesn't require a shared doctrine at all, only mind-pressure plus a growth imperative, and both of those are plainly present in a big-tent party no matter how much internal disagreement it tolerates on policy.
(4) Farah said fine, grant the diagnosis -- but Weils actual cure, abolish the parties and keep the elections, doesn't survive contact with reality, because the coordination vacuum just gets filled by personality cults, name recognition, and media money, which is worse for accountability, not better.
(5) Dan said that's a real worry about her proposal, sure, but it doesn't touch her diagnosis of what's wrong with parties as such.
(6) Farah said a diagnosis nobody can act on without making things worse isn't much of a diagnosis.

Neither of them budged, and I don't trust my own read on this enough to referee it myself. Could you go through moves 1 through 6 and say, for each one, who's got the stronger of it and why, rather than just declaring an overall winner -- and flag anything you think both of them are missing? Especially curious whether Dan's right in move 3 that the mechanism doesn't need a shared doctrine to bite, and whether Farah's right in move 6 that a diagnosis is only as good as the cure it points to. End with a one-line bottom-line verdict in the form \verdict{Dan}, \verdict{Farah}, or \verdict{Draw}.
