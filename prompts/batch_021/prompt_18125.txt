A few nights ago, a friend and I got into a long, increasingly animated argument over dinner about whether Nāgārjuna's use of the tetralemma (catuṣkoṭi) is actually logically respectable or just impressive-sounding mysticism. We'd both just sat in on a reading group session on the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, working through the chapter on nirvāṇa, where Nāgārjuna runs through all four options — that nirvāṇa exists, that it doesn't, that it both exists and doesn't, that it neither exists nor doesn't — and rejects every one of them. My friend, who has a background in formal logic, kept insisting that this is a straightforward violation of excluded middle and non-contradiction dressed up in exotic vocabulary, and that any appeal to "two truths" is just a rescue maneuver invented after the fact to paper over the inconsistency. I pushed back that there's supposed to be a more careful logical story here, involving exactly what is being negated and how, but by the end of the night I realized I couldn't actually reconstruct that story with any precision, so we left it unresolved.

What I'd like is a rigorous but accessible reconstruction of how the more classically-minded (i.e., non-paraconsistent) interpretations of the tetralemma are supposed to work. In particular, could you unpack: (a) the distinction between prasajya-pratiṣedha and paryudāsa-pratiṣedha negation, and why it's supposed to matter which kind is doing the work in each of the four koṭis; (b) how fixing the target of negation as svabhāva (inherent or intrinsic existence) rather than existence as such changes what each horn is actually denying; and (c) how the saṃvṛti/paramārtha (conventional/ultimate truth) distinction is supposed to let Nāgārjuna affirm dependently originated, conventional existence while denying all four modes of inherently existent being, without this just amounting to equivocating on the word "exists." I'd also be curious for your own assessment of whether this really dissolves the apparent contradiction, or whether Jay Garfield and Graham Priest's paraconsistent, dialetheist reading — on which Madhyamaka is read as embracing genuine, true contradictions at the very limits of conceptual thought — actually has the upper hand over these more conservative reconstructions.
