A friend and I spent most of dinner last night arguing about how historical explanation actually works, and neither of us walked away convinced. It started innocuously, as a disagreement about why the July Crisis of 1914 spiraled into a general war, but it quickly turned into a bigger argument about the logic of historical explanation as such. My friend, who has read more Hempel than I have, kept insisting that any historical explanation worth the name is ultimately a covering-law explanation: even a narrative that just walks through the assassination in Sarajevo, the mobilization schedules, the alliance obligations, and the ultimatums is explanatory only insofar as it tacitly invokes general regularities about how crises of this kind escalate, and that absent those regularities it is at best what Hempel called an "explanation sketch," not a finished explanation. I argued for the narrativist line most associated with Danto: that the understanding we get from a well-constructed historical narrative comes from seeing how the particulars hang together as an intelligible sequence, not from suppressed general laws, and that this sort of narrative colligation is a legitimate, autonomous mode of explanation in its own right, rather than a placeholder for one we simply haven't finished stating.

We went back and forth for almost an hour without either of us budging, so I'd like to see the disagreement played out properly by two people who actually know the terrain, rather than the somewhat improvised version my friend and I managed. Could you write a dialogue between a covering-law theorist and a narrativist philosopher of history who have been asked to co-write the explanatory chapter of an edited volume on the outbreak of World War I, and who discover, once they sit down to draft the opening section, that they cannot agree on what an explanation of the July Crisis should even look like? I want the covering-law theorist to press hard on the claim that narrative is only ever explanatory to the extent that it gestures at implicit general regularities, and I want the narrativist to have a substantive answer rather than just insisting that narratives feel satisfying — ideally engaging with the wrinkle that some of the descriptions under which we pick out an event (for instance, calling the assassination in Sarajevo "the spark that lit a war that would reshape the century") are only available in hindsight, which seems to complicate the idea that a general law could have connected the event, so described, to what came after.

Please don't let either side win by default: I want genuine back-and-forth, with each pressing the other on the real weak point of their view, rather than two prepared speeches that happen to be sitting next to each other. And it doesn't need to resolve neatly — if the two of them just end up locating a sharper version of their disagreement, or discover that some part of it is merely terminological, that's a perfectly good place to stop.
