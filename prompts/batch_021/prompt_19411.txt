Been reading about Kronecker for a piece on mathematicians who couldn't stomach infinity, and he's such a good character: he basically tried to sink Cantor's career because he thought reasoning about completed infinite sets was near meaningless, and he insisted math should only deal with stuff you can build from the whole numbers in finitely many steps. Got me wondering about Zorn's Lemma, which came along decades after he died and is everywhere in algebra and number theory now (his own turf) -- proving a vector space has a basis, a ring has a maximal ideal, a field has an algebraic closure, none of it actually constructed, just showing a maximal example has to be lurking somewhere. If you explained Zorn's Lemma to Kronecker and pointed out its doing load-bearing work in exactly the concrete algebra he cared about, not just Cantor's set theory that he could wave off as abstract nonsense, do you think he'd have dismissed it just as hard? Or does dressing it up as a maximal-element principle instead of transfinite numbers make it harder for a committed finitist to reject cleanly? Curious how far you think his finitism would actually bend once his own turf was at stake.
