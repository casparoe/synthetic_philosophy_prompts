Okay so this has been on my mind the past couple weeks because of something that happened at work. I've been building an LLM agent for my team that plans and executes multi-step tasks using tool calls (closing out tickets, reorganizing shared files, that sort of thing), and it keeps doing this maddening thing where it "finishes" the step it was told to do but has no clue what else that action quietly changes -- like it merged two duplicate tickets and didn't realize that also silently unassigned everyone who'd been cc'd on the original, which broke three other workflows downstream. My teammate, who did a cog-sci minor, said "congrats, you just rediscovered the frame problem," and that sent me down a rabbit hole reading about the old McCarthy and Hayes paper and Dennett's thing with the robot and the bomb on the wagon, which I vaguely remember skimming in an intro phil-of-mind class years ago.

Here's the part I can't get past though. From what I can tell, the reason classic AI systems choked on this is they needed explicit "frame axioms" spelling out everything that DOESN'T change after an action, and that blows up combinatorially -- theres just alot of facts to track. But that's not how LLM agents work at all, they just take a giant context window and lean on learned statistical priors to guess what's probably still true versus what's changed, basically the same shortcut humans use instead of consciously reasoning through every fact one by one. So doesn't that mean bigger context plus better retrieval basically reduces the whole frame problem down to a boring, solvable engineering question (how much can you fit in context, how good is your retrieval), rather than the deep philosophical puzzle Dennett and apparently Fodor made it out to be? My teammate swears I'm still missing the actual point of the problem, but if the model does converge on mostly-right guesses about what's relevant almost all the time, isn't that basically "solving" it in whatever sense actually mattered? Someone set me straight here.
