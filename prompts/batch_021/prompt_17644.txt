I spent almost thirty years running an immunology lab, and for a good chunk of that time I also sat on my university's research ethics board — the committee that decides whether a proposed study is allowed to go ahead. Most of us on that board were scientists, not philosophers, and whenever a case got genuinely thorny (deception in a psychology study, say, or consent from people who arguably can't give it) we'd more or less just defer to the one moral philosopher on the panel and vote however she told us to. Now that I'm retired and going to a little philosophy reading group with some friends, I've learned there's a whole literature on exactly that move, filed under "moral testimony" — and that it splits people into "optimists," who think taking someone's word for a moral claim is epistemically no different from taking a doctor's word for a diagnosis, and "pessimists," who think theres something specifically off about believing (or acting on) a purely moral verdict second-hand, even from an impeccable source, in a way that doesn't hold for ordinary factual or even aesthetic testimony. We argued about it for a whole evening and got nowhere.

Could you write me a dialogue between a sharp defender of each camp? Maybe set it just after a vote like the ones on my old board, where everyone deferred to the resident ethicist on a hard case, and have the optimist and the pessimist hash out afterward whether that was a perfectly respectable way for the rest of us to reach the right verdict, or whether something was missing even so. I don't want either side to be a pushover: the pessimist should have to do more than assert that moral deference feels icky — they should explain why it's supposedly worse than deferring to, say, a wine critic or a structural engineer, and grapple with the idea (I think it's due to a philosopher named Hills, but don't hold me to that) that testimony can hand you moral knowledge without handing you moral understanding. And the optimist should have to actually engage with the pessimist's best cases rather than just insisting all testimony is basically alike — including the worry that there isn't even a reliable way to pick out a "moral expert" the way you can point to a reliable mechanic. I want real back and forth, with each one responding to what the other just said, not two people talking past each other.
