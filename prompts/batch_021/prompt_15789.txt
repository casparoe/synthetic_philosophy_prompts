I've been teaching myself some probability out of general curiosity -- no coursework, just books and old forum threads -- and a few weeks ago I got completely stuck on Bertrand's chord paradox and haven't been able to let it go since. The setup: draw a "random" chord of a circle and ask for the probability that it's longer than the side of the equilateral triangle inscribed in that circle. If you generate the chord by picking two independent random points on the circumference and joining them, the answer works out to 1/3. If instead you fix a random direction for a radius, pick a point uniformly along that radius, and draw the chord through that point perpendicular to the radius, the answer is 1/2. And if you pick the chord's midpoint uniformly at random inside the disk, the answer is 1/4. Each recipe treats some different quantity as uniformly distributed, and each looks like a perfectly reasonable way of cashing out "choose a chord at random" -- yet they can't all be answering the same question the same way.

My first instinct was that there's no real paradox here, just a hidden equivocation, on the model of "what's the probability a randomly chosen person is over six feet tall" giving different answers depending on whether you sample people or, say, households. On that reading, the principle of indifference isn't refuted by any of this -- it's just been fed three different, perfectly well-specified problems dressed up in the same vague sentence. But then I read about Jaynes's 1973 response to the paradox. He argued that the problem becomes genuinely well-posed once you demand that the answer be invariant under rotating, rescaling, and translating the whole setup -- picture straws rained down onto the circle from far away -- and that this requirement singles out the "random radius" method and its answer of 1/2, a conclusion he then went and checked experimentally. That seemed like a much stronger claim than "you were equivocating": it sounds like he thinks indifference reasoning can be rescued and made rigorous by an added invariance requirement, rather than merely being cleared of a charge by pointing out an ambiguity.

So here is what I would like help thinking through, on an intuitive rather than a fully formal level. First, why do the three methods actually conflict rather than just quietly answering three different questions -- what, if anything, is left of the "paradox" once you accept that they are simply different random variables? Second, is Jaynes's invariance argument really a vindication of the principle of indifference, giving it a principled way to pick out the "right" uniform distribution, or does it quietly substitute a different, more specific question -- about straws physically tossed from a distance -- for Bertrand's original, more abstract one, in which case has he actually solved the puzzle or just changed the subject? And third, is there a general moral here about whether "assign equal probability to each possibility when you have no reason to favor one over another" is ever a meaningful instruction on its own, without already presupposing some privileged way of carving up the space of possibilities?
