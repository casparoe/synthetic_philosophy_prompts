I'm at work on a novel whose emotional core is the relationship between Ilse, a philosophy-of-economics professor, and Teodor, her most gifted and most self-lacerating student — a former derivatives trader who left his firm after a trade went catastrophically and publicly wrong. In the scene I'm currently drafting, Teodor confesses that even now, faced with a straightforwardly favorable bet, he cannot bring himself to accept it, and rather than simply feel the flinch, he tries to intellectualize it: he tells Ilse that decision theorists coined the term "risk-weighted expected utility theory" so that the probability-weighting distortion Kahneman and Tversky catalogued in prospect theory could be renamed into something that sounds like a rational theory of choice, rather than admitted as the cognitive bias it actually is — that his caution, in other words, is just loss aversion wearing a philosopher's coat, and the fancier name is a courtesy economists extend to their own instincts.

I want Ilse's reply to be the intellectual and emotional hinge of the scene, so I need to get the philosophy exactly right before I draft it again. Could you lay out, with real precision, how risk-weighted expected utility theory — in the sense developed by decision theorists such as Lara Buchak — is actually structured, and how it truly relates to the probability-weighting function of prospect theory? I want Ilse's explanation to engage the substance rather than wave at it, so that a specialist reading the finished scene wouldn't wince — and if it turns out that Teodor's framing already gets something right, or wrong in a more interesting way than I've assumed, I would rather rebuild the scene around that than around a distinction I have only half understood.
