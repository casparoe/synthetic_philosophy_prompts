So this has been bugging me for months now. My brother-in-law, who smokes and drinks more than literally anyone I know, sat me down last thanksgiving and gave me this whole speech about how I need to get my act together health-wise because of my weight. I said something like "that's a bit rich coming from you" and he just shrugged and said "doesn't make me wrong though" -- which, ok, fair, but it still felt like he had no right to be the one saying it to my face. That throwaway line sent me down a pretty deep internet rabbit hole about something philosophers apparently call "standing to blame," which is basically the question of whether being a hypocrite actually takes away your right to call someone out, even when what you're saying is 100% true.

Turns out there's a whole bunch of competing explanations for why (or whether) hypocrisy does this, and honestly they don't agree with each other much at all. From what I've managed to piece together there's: an "equal standing" idea, where the hypocrite is basically claiming a secret exemption for themselves that they refuse to extend to you, which breaks the "we're both bound by the same rule" thing that blaming someone is supposed to rest on; a "free rider" idea, where the problem is just that they want everyone else to eat the cost of following the rule while they personally dodge it; something I think came from a guy named Cohen, where hypocrisy never actually touches whether the criticism itself is true, only whether that particular person had any business being the one to say it -- so the standing question and the truth question are totally separate; a skeptical "no such thing as standing" view, which says every "who are you to talk" complaint secretly reduces to something else entirely (tone, the blamer being a total stranger to the situation, whatever) and isn't really about hypocrisy at all; a "unified" theory that lumps hypocrisy in with meddling in stuff that isn't your business and being complicit in the actual wrongdoing, treating all three as basically the same underlying defect; and finally a newer pushback view where hypocrisy is just a gross character flaw in the blamer but doesn't actually make them loose their standing, so "you're a hypocrite!" is more of an insult than an actual rebuttal to the blame. Let's just call these six "equal standing," "free rider," "true-but-not-their-place," "no such thing," "unified," and "just a vice" so its easier to refer back to them.
