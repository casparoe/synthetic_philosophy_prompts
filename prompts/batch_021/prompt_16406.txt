So this is going to sound like a weird tangent but stick with me. I got sideswiped while merging a couple months back and my insurer and the other drivers insurer are still fighting over "proximate cause" -- basically who actually caused the crash, since I signaled late but he was also speeding. My brother-in-law's in law school and, trying to explain the "but-for" test to me, sent me a philosophy paper on causation that quotes this bit from Hume's Enquiry (Section VII, the necessary connexion part), and it's been stuck in my head since:

"We may define a cause to be an object followed by another, and where all the objects, similar to the first, are followed by objects similar to the second. Or, in other words, where, if the first object had not been, the second never had existed."

What I don't get is why Hume treats that second sentence as just restating the first one ("or, in other words") when they don't seem equivalent at all -- one's about regularities/constant conjunction, the other's a counterfactual claim. Couldn't there be cases where one holds and not the other? Is Hume being sloppy, or is there a way to read this where they actually do collapse into each other? And is this basically the fork in the road that later splits into "counterfactual" theories of causation (Lewis and co.) versus the "interventionist" ones (Woodward, Pearl), or is that a totally separate disagreement that just gets layered on top of this one? Would love an actual close reading, not just "yeah Hume's kind of ambiguous there."
