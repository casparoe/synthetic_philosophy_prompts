Ok this is a bit of a niche one but I've been chewing on it since a meetup discussion went down a Frege rabbit hole (started from someone bringing up sense vs reference, as these things go). Someone mentioned that Frege kept a diary in the last year or so of his life that only surfaced decades after he died, and that it's full of pretty ugly stuff — virulent antisemitism, contempt for the Weimar parliament, pining for a strongman to sort the country out, basically the whole reactionary package. Apparently Dummett, who more or less singlehandedly revived Frege as a major figure in 20th century philosophy, was genuinely rattled when he read it, to the point of saying something like he had to admit his hero was a nasty piece of work politically.

What I keep getting stuck on is the general question hiding underneath that anecdote. Frege is *the* guy who insisted logical laws and "thoughts" (Gedanke, in his jargon) are objective and completely independent of the psychology or biography of whoever happens to be thinking them — that's basically the whole point of his attack on psychologism in the Grundlagen. So is there something almost self-undermining about reaching for that same move to wave away the diary — "his politics are irrelevant, judge the Begriffsschrift purely on its logical merits" — or does that just quietly assume the very separability thesis that's actually in question? I'm not fishing for Frege gossip so much as the underlying conceptual map: what are the actual positions on when, if ever, an author's personal moral or political character is supposed to bear on how we evaluate or interpret their theoretical work, versus that just being a dressed-up genetic fallacy? And is there a principled reason formal disciplines like logic or math would be more insulated from this than, say, ethics or political theory, or do we just want that to be true because we like Frege's logic and don't want to lose it? Would love a somewhat systematic rundown of the positions here, cause i can't quite carve it up cleanly on my own.
