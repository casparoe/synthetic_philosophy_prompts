Question. A three-member zoning board must decide whether to grant a permit for a proposed development. Board policy specifies that the permit is to be granted if and only if (i) the environmental review is favorable and (ii) the project meets the applicable safety code. The three members reach the following individual judgments:

Member 1: (i) favorable; (ii) meets code; therefore grant.
Member 2: (i) favorable; (ii) fails code; therefore deny.
Member 3: (i) unfavorable; (ii) meets code; therefore deny.

(a) [6 points] Determine the board's decision under (i) a premise-based procedure, which takes separate majority votes on (i) and (ii) and then applies the stated policy, and (ii) a conclusion-based procedure, which takes a majority vote directly on whether to grant the permit.
(b) [8 points] Explain what this scenario illustrates about the limits of majority voting, situating it relative to the relevant literature.
(c) [6 points] Is the premise-based procedure the correct general solution to the problem raised in (b)? Justify your answer.

Answer as submitted.

(a) Tallying each column separately for the premise-based procedure: on (i), Members 1 and 2 judge the review favorable, giving a majority for "favorable"; on (ii), Members 1 and 3 judge the project code-compliant, giving a majority for "meets code." Applying the board's rule to these two majority premises, both conditions hold, so the premise-based procedure grants the permit. Under the conclusion-based procedure, we instead tally the bottom-line votes directly: only Member 1 votes to grant, while Members 2 and 3 vote to deny, so the majority verdict is to deny. The two procedures disagree — premise-based grants, conclusion-based denies — even though each member's own judgments are individually consistent with the board's rule.

(b) This is a textbook illustration of the discursive dilemma, first identified by List and Pettit, who showed that voting proposition-by-proposition can produce a result that contradicts what a direct vote on the conclusion would produce, even though no individual voter is being irrational. What is really going on is structurally the same problem Condorcet identified for majority voting over candidates: there is a majority for "favorable," a majority for "meets code," and a majority for "deny" — three majority verdicts that cannot all be realized by any single consistent overall judgment, just as Condorcet's cycles show that pairwise majorities can fail to add up to a coherent ranking. List and Pettit's impossibility theorem essentially generalizes this Condorcet-style cycling from preferences over candidates to sets of logically connected propositions, which is why their result is regarded as the counterpart to Arrow's theorem for judgment aggregation.

(c) Because the premise-based procedure is the one that respects the board's own stated rule and thereby avoids the paradox, it is the correct general solution: List and Pettit's own recommendation is to aggregate on the premises and let the conclusion follow logically, since this guarantees a collectively rational outcome and ensures the board's decision tracks the reasons that were actually voted on, rather than resting on an unexplained bottom-line tally.
