I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and I had spent the afternoon reporting on a museum's new exhibition on the forensic science of art authentication -- pigment analysis, canvas dating, and so on -- which leans heavily on the old case of Han van Meegeren, the Dutch painter whose fake Vermeers fooled the leading experts of his day so completely that one ended up in Hermann Göring's collection, and who avoided a treason conviction after the war only by proving, under guard, that he could paint a brand-new "Vermeer" from scratch. Afterward, over drinks, two friends who had tagged along -- Nadia, who restores Old Master paintings for a regional museum, and Owen, who only reads about and collects them -- fell into a disagreement I haven't been able to settle myself and would like your help adjudicating.

(1) Nadia's opening claim was that an undetected forgery is not merely fraudulent but actually worth less as art, full stop: once you know a painting is fake, trained eyes genuinely begin to see it differently -- passages that once looked confident now look labored -- and she insists that is not hindsight bias but connoisseurs correctly perceiving that the forger never solved the problems the real artist solved. (2) Owen replied that this gets the order of explanation backwards: if a blind viewer cannot tell the two apart, their aesthetic properties are identical, full stop, and any "laboredness" Nadia now sees is contamination from knowing the backstory -- exactly the bias connoisseurship is supposed to guard against, not exhibit. (3) Nadia pushed back that connoisseurship just is a historically trained way of seeing, and part of what a trained eye responds to in a painting is its place in a solved artistic problem; a forger is solving a different problem, imitation, and that difference is perceptible in principle even when missed in practice, which is exactly why forgeries do eventually get caught by eye and not only by chemistry. (4) Owen countered that this proves too much, since plenty of legitimate work -- a study produced by a master's own workshop, or an artist reprising their own earlier breakthrough -- is equally uninvolved in solving a new problem, yet nobody treats a Rembrandt studio piece as aesthetically worthless the way we treat a fake; so something else, probably just the intent to deceive or the true causal history, is doing the real work. (5) Nadia's answer was that deception is not a separate, merely moral add-on: engaging with a painting as art rather than décor already means treating it as the record of a particular mind's achievement, so when that history is faked, the resulting experience is like mistaking a con man's warmth for friendship -- real as an experience, but not what it purports to be, which is an aesthetic failure and not only a legal one. (6) Owen's last word was that this quietly retreats from where Nadia started: she began by saying the forgery lacks the relevant aesthetic properties, and has ended up saying it has them, but under false pretenses -- a much weaker claim, and one fully compatible with his original point that, considered in itself, the forgery is exactly as beautiful as the original.

Neither of them budged by the time we left, and I confess I keep going back and forth myself. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each step, not just who wins overall? I would be especially interested in whether Owen is right that move (5) quietly abandons the ground Nadia staked out in (1) and (3), and in what, if anything, you think both of them are missing about why originals seem to matter to us in the first place.
