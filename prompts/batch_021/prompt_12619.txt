I'm on the board of a small wildlife rescue charity and we've got a real disagreement brewing over our fox mange program. For the last few years we've run feeding stations that dose food with anti-parasitic meds to treat sarcoptic mange in the local urban fox population -- nasty disease, mites basically eating them alive, and left untreated it's usually a slow death from secondary infection or hypothermia once they've lost their fur. Half the board wants to expand it to more sites next year. The other half wants to scrap it and put the money into native-species habitat work instead, and their best argument isn't cost, its that we're not actually reducing suffering at all, just deferring it -- the cubs we save from mange this year mostly die of something else (roads, starvation, mange again) within a year or two anyway, since the local population's basically at carrying capacity regardless of what we do. Nobody's framed this as a "should we even be intervening in nature" question but thats clearly what's underneath it. Can you write the memo I'll bring to next month's meeting -- an actual recommendation on what to do with the program (keep/expand/cut/redesign), with a rough budget and timeframe, that genuinely grapples with the deferred-suffering objection instead of just asserting that compassion trumps it?
