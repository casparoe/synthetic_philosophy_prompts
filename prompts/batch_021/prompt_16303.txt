Having spent my career as an evolutionary biologist, I first met game theory through Maynard Smith rather than through economics, and it is only in retirement that I have begun reading the decision-theoretic literature on rational choice for its own sake. The puzzle occupying me most right now is the trouble backward induction runs into in the centipede game. Step by step the reasoning looks airtight: at the final move the player should take rather than pass, since taking yields more; anticipating this, the player before should take as well, rather than pass into a move that will only be met with defection; and so the argument unravels all the way back to the very first move, concluding that two players who are rational, and know each other to be rational, must forgo the far larger payoff that even modest cooperation would have secured. Yet the argument seems to undercut its own premises: it depends entirely on rationality being common knowledge between the players, while the mere fact of reaching almost any later move already looks like evidence against that very assumption having held. I would like you to write a short dialogue between two equally capable interlocutors who divide over how to handle this: one holds that backward induction remains binding at every node regardless of how the game has actually unfolded so far, while the other holds that arriving at a move backward induction said should never be reached gives a rational player good reason to revise their assessment of their opponent and to go on cooperating. Have them press each other on the strongest form of each position rather than talking past one another, and keep the exchange anchored in the structure of the paradox itself rather than in loose appeals to trust or fairness.
