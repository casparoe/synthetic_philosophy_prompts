About a month ago our forecasting team ran into a disagreement that has since eaten up more of my evenings than I'd like to admit. We were tracking an upcoming referendum, and going in, our model treated whether it would pass and whether turnout would be high as independent and equally likely, so each of the four combinations (pass-and-high, pass-and-low, fail-and-high, fail-and-low) sat at a flat 25%. A source we trust then got word to us that they couldn't say whether the referendum would pass, but that if it did pass, they'd put the odds on high turnout at 3-to-1. Half the team wanted to simply re-split the "pass" branch 3:1 while leaving the "fail" branch, and the overall pass/fail split, exactly as they were; the other half ran the update through our usual minimum-cross-entropy tool, which returned a distribution in which even the marginal probability of passing shifted, despite the source's message never comparing passing to failing at all. When I went looking into why two seemingly reasonable methods could disagree about a case this simple, I discovered we'd stumbled into a toy version of something philosophers already had a name for: the "Judy Benjamin problem," which van Fraassen apparently posed against maximum-entropy-style updating back in the early 1980s, and which still doesn't seem to have a settled resolution.

I'd like to actually understand this rather than just default to whichever answer feels more intuitive to me. Could you write me an essay that first explains clearly why learning a bare conditional probability like this resists being handled by ordinary Bayesian conditionalization, or even by Jeffrey conditionalization? It should then take seriously the strongest considerations for and against the leading candidate update rules — at minimum the minimum-cross-entropy answer and the "hold fixed whatever the message didn't mention" intuition, plus any other principled proposal from the literature you think deserves a hearing. I don't want a survey that shrugs and concludes reasonable people disagree: please actually defend a verdict about what the rational update is, for Judy Benjamin's original case and for mine. A conditional verdict is fine (e.g., "rule X is correct when such-and-such holds of the source, otherwise rule Y"), but if you think the matter is genuinely underdetermined by the bare setup as given, say precisely what additional fact would settle it and what you'd do in its absence. I want to bring an actual position back to my team, not a literature review.
