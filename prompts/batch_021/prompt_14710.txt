I am a PhD student in environmental philosophy, and I have been asked to give the eulogy at my aunt's funeral this coming Saturday. My mother says the family settled on me because I was the one relative who kept up with the same journals my aunt did; we spent a fair number of phone calls over the last few years arguing amicably about exactly the question I now have to write around. My aunt Ingrid spent thirty-two years as a field biologist and wildlife rehabilitator, and for most of that career she held to what I would call the standard naturalist's creed: suffering in the wild is regrettable, but it belongs to a larger system that is, on the whole, good, or at least none of our business to correct. In the last decade of her life, though, she worked her way through much of the wild-animal-suffering literature -- Ng on welfare biology, Horta's case against the "idyllic view" of nature, Tomasik's essays, Johannsen's recent book -- and it genuinely altered her thinking. She came to believe that the sheer scale of suffering built into predation, parasitism, and disease, and above all into the reproductive strategy of most wild vertebrates (what ecologists call an r-selected strategy, in which the great majority of offspring die within their first year), meant nature could not be regarded as fundamentally benign, and that conservation's usual policy of non-interference was itself a substantive moral stance rather than a neutral default. In her last years she argued, at some real cost to her standing among colleagues, for things like immunocontraception programs and expanded wildlife-hospital capacity, and was more than once accused of sentimentality by people who thought she had confused animal welfare with conservation.

The funeral is going to put two audiences in the same room who do not usually have to talk to one another: relatives and old family friends who will expect, and be comforted by, the familiar language of nature's balance and the circle of life, and former colleagues from the state wildlife agency and the university ecology department who know exactly which side of the compassionate-conservation-versus-land-ethic dispute she ended up on, and who will notice immediately if I quietly reverse it for the occasion. She left no instructions for her own funeral, but I knew her well enough to be confident that she would not have wanted to be sent off with a view of nature she spent her last years arguing against, and I have no wish to turn her funeral into a conference paper either.

Could you write this eulogy for me? I am picturing something in the range of six or seven minutes, delivered to a room that is mostly not philosophers. I would like her actual, harder-won view of nature to do real work in the speech -- shaping what kind of comfort can honestly be offered, and what kind cannot -- rather than being softened away into the platitudes she rejected, or delivered as straight argument.
