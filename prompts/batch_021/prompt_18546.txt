I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on a dissertation chapter about legal moralism, and for a jurisprudence reading group this term I've been going back through the Hart–Devlin exchange on the enforcement of morals. It's almost always taught through the lens of the Wolfenden Report and the decriminalization of private homosexual acts, but since that particular fight has long since been won in most of the jurisdictions we discuss, I've become more interested in whether Devlin's "disintegration thesis" and Hart's revised harm principle still generate real disagreement once you run them against a harder, more contemporary-feeling case: the English case of R v Brown, decided by the House of Lords in 1993, in which a group of men were convicted for consensual sadomasochistic acts, conducted entirely in private and stumbled upon only incidentally by the police, on the reasoning that consent is no defence to a charge of causing bodily harm once the activity itself is judged sufficiently degrading or dangerous.

Could you write me a dialogue between two contemporary legal philosophers -- one defending Hart's position, one defending Devlin's -- who have been asked to advise on how a case like Brown ought to be decided, and who spend the conversation trying to talk each other out of their view? I want the Devlinian to make Devlin's actual best case: the disintegration thesis itself, the "intolerance, indignation and disgust" test for distinguishing a genuine threat to the shared moral fabric from mere squeamishness, and the analogy to the law of treason, where the state protects society as such rather than any specific victim. And I want the Hartian to press the real objections -- that the disintegration thesis is either an untested empirical claim Devlin never actually supports, or a piece of definitional sleight of hand that makes any shift in positive morality count as disintegration by fiat -- while also being forced to answer for their own side's weak point: why a harm principle that has already made room for paternalism is entitled to rule out harm inflicted on a fully consenting self, of just the kind at issue in Brown, so easily. I'd like each of them to give specific, substantive comebacks rather than restating their opening position, with neither side allowed to simply win by the other going quiet, and it's completely fine -- probably more honest, given the actual history -- if they don't reach agreement by the end.
