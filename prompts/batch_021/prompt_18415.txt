Ok so I have zero formal philosophy background (CS degree, write backend code for a living), but we've got a #random-philosophy channel at work that somehow gets more traffic than half our actual engineering channels, and last week it produced an argument between two coworkers, Marco and Ines, that I can't stop chewing on. It started from a thread about the lottery paradox and the preface paradox. Quick refresher in case it's not top of mind: the lottery paradox is the fair 1000-ticket lottery where for any single ticket your 99.9% sure it loses, so you seem justified believing that of every ticket, except you also know for certain some ticket wins, so you end up "justifiably" believing something false overall. The preface paradox is the author who's genuinely justified in every individual claim in her book (good sources, careful reasoning, whatever) but is honest enough to write in the preface that a book this size definately contains at least one mistake somewhere -- so she believes every sentence AND believes the book as a whole has an error in it.

Here's roughly how the Slack argument went:
(1) Marco's opening shot was that these are literally the same paradox in two outfits, and the only consistent move is to accept that justified belief just isn't closed under conjunction, period, in both cases.
(2) Ines pushed back that they're actually pretty different: your belief about any one lottery ticket rests on nothing but bare, symmetric statistics -- the identical "evidence" you'd cite for any other ticket -- and there's good reason to think that kind of purely statistical fact never really justifies believing something about a specific case, only having a credence about it. The book is different because each claim is backed by its own specific research, so maybe the lottery "paradox" just shows you were never justified about individual tickets to begin with, while the preface is a genuine case of justified-but-jointly-inconsistent belief.
(3) Marco replied that this proves too much, since we flatly believe tons of stuff based on nothing but base rates -- reliability numbers, a doctor telling a patient they probably don't have some rare condition -- and nobody calls that irrational, so you can't disqualify the lottery beliefs just for being "merely statistical" without torching a ton of ordinary reasoning too.
(4) Ines said fine, statistics can justify belief sometimes, but the deeper problem with the lottery is that ticket-beliefs are completely interchangeable: nothing about ticket 452 specifically connects to whether it wins, you'd say the exact same thing about ticket 111, while your belief in chapter 4 isn't interchangeable with your belief in chapter 9 -- they stand or fall on totally unrelated grounds.
(5) Marco came back with: we also have excellent inductive evidence, from tons of other books, that books researched this carefully at this length still contain errors at roughly this rate -- so by Ines's own logic, that generalization should undercut the "safety" of each individual chapter-belief too, not just excuse it, meaning the book isn't so special after all.
(6) Ines's last word was that there's still an asymmetry that matters: in the lottery, since every ticket-belief is equally arbitrary, if something has to be rejected it's obviously all of them, and nothing principled is lost doing that uniformly. In the book there's no comparable "weakest" claim to single out -- each one is justified on its own distinct merits -- so you can't just reject some of them, which is exactly why the book forces a real closure failure while the lottery might just dissolve instead.
