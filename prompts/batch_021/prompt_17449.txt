Since retiring from a career in physical chemistry and only recently turning to philosophy, I have been working through Hume's essays on money and trade, and I was struck by how closely "Of the Balance of Trade" resembles Le Chatelier's principle applied to nations: perturb the money supply of one country relative to its trading partners, and the resulting shift in prices and competitiveness will push specie back out until the disturbance is undone, much as a chemical system shifts to counteract whatever displaces it from equilibrium. Hume treated this self-correction as close to a law of nature, but his argument is built entirely around metallic currency moving between nations on a specie standard. He never had to reckon with pure fiat money, floating exchange rates, or a country running a large, persistent trade deficit with a single partner for decades, as the United States has with China, even as China has spent much of that period actively managing its own currency. I would like your best reconstruction of how Hume would argue about this, staying with his own premises about money, prices, and industry rather than importing modern trade theory wholesale. Would he say the mechanism still operates once exchange-rate and capital-flow adjustment are substituted for specie flows, so that Chinese currency management is simply a modern disguise for the bullion-hoarding he mocked in the mercantilists? Or would he have to concede that a reserve currency able to absorb deficits indefinitely represents a genuine obstruction of the self-correcting process he took to be practically automatic?
