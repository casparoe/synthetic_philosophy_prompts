My uncle Ray is retiring in a few weeks after 33 years as a trauma surgeon, and somehow I got voted the one to give the toast at his send-off (there's cake and a slideshow, it's happening in the hospital cafeteria right after his last shift, so I genuinely don't know how formal this is supposed to be). Here's the thing that's been stuck in my head though: a couple years back at Thanksgiving, Ray got going about some philosopher - Williams, I think - and this example of a truck driver who runs over a kid through no fault of his own, breaks fail or whatever, and how the driver still ends up feeling wrecked about it in a way a random bystander watching wouldn't, even though everybody agrees he didn't do anything wrong. I went down a whole Stanford Encyclopedia rabbit hole after that dinner and found out there's a whole area of philosophy about this called "moral luck," with something like four different flavors of luck that supposedly go into whether we end up calling someone's life or character good. It kind of messed with my head, honestly, especially the part where if you take it all the way seriously, there's barely anything left about a person that wasn't just luck of one kind or another - not even the good judgment part, since nobody chooses to be born with steady hands and a calm head either.

Anyway, here's the actual problem. Ray told my cousin (who told me) that he does not want the toast to do the standard thing where someone stands up and says "33 years and he never lost a patient who didn't deserve to go," like it's a stat that proves something about him, because he knows for a fact that at least once the only reason a guy walked out of there was where exactly the injury happened to land that particular night, not anything Ray did differently than he would've on a night that went the other way. He wants to be honored for the actual care and judgment he brought every single time, not the scoreboard, because he says the scoreboard was never fully his to take credit for in the first place. Which I actually think is a pretty admirable thing to want. Except half the room is hospital people who will absolutely clock it if the toast either does the cheesy hero thing anyway or overcorrects into something that sounds like "well actually none of it was really up to you," and the other half is family who just want to hear their uncle was a hero and will get a little weird if it sounds like I'm taking that away from him.

Could you actually write the toast for me - the real words I'd say out loud, something like 4 to 5 minutes worth? I want it to hold onto the distinction Ray genuinely cares about, the part of his career that was really his versus the part that wasn't, without it turning into a philosophy lecture, and it still has to be warm and get at least a couple of laughs, since it's a retirement party and not a wake. If it's useful you can even work in that unsettling bit about where exactly you draw the line on what counts as "his" - but honestly I don't know how to think about that part myself, so feel free to just leave it out if it doesn't fit.
