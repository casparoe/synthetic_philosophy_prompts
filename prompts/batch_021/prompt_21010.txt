I'm a retired astrophysicist who spent about thirty-five years staring at stellar spectra, and now that I've got time on my hands I've fallen down a philosophy rabbit hole -- mostly logic and philosophy of mind stuff. Last month I read Hao Wang's book on Gödel back to back with Rebecca Goldstein's biography, and I got kind of stuck on the fact that Gödel apparently believed quite sincerely in a personal afterlife and thought he had something like an argument for it, seperate from his much more famous modal ontological proof of God. None of my old physicist friends are any help with this stuff and my philosophy reading group only meets once a month, so I tried writing up my own reconstruction of the "afterlife argument" and I'd really like a second pair of eyes on it before I embarrass myself with it there.

Here's roughly how I've pieced it together, stripped down to the bones: (1) the world, including the arc of a human life, is fundamentally rational -- nothing happens "for no reason" in some deep sense, basically Leibniz's principle of sufficient reason; (2) a rational world would not produce beings that spend a lifetime developing capacities, knowledge and character that then simply vanish, unused, the moment the body dies -- that would be an enormous and pointless waste, which contradicts (1); (3) but that's exactly what a single earthly life looks like for almost everybody, we barely get started before we die; so (4) there must be some further mode of existence after bodily death in which a persons development continues or gets "cashed out." My own hunch is that this is really a teleological/rationalist argument dressed up as an afterlife proof, and that premise (2) is doing all the work while basically just restating the conclusion (that pointless waste is impossible) rather than earning it.

So a few things I'd love your take on: first, whether I've actually got the shape of Gödel's reasoning right, historically speaking -- I have a vague memory of something in Carnap's diaries from the Vienna Circle days suggesting Gödel held views like this decades before the letter to his mother, but I could easily be misremembering; second, whether the argument is any good on its own philosophical merits, independent of whether it's accurate to Gödel; third, how it stacks up against his formal ontological proof of God -- are these two arguments really doing the same kind of work, or is one much weaker than the other; and honestly, fourth, whether this whole line of inquiry is even worth pursuing further or if I'm just chasing a curiosity that goes nowhere. Please be blunt about the weak spots.
