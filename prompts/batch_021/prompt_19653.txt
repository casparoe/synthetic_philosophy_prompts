I have no formal training in philosophy, but over the past year I have worked my way through David Lewis's papers on objective chance in my spare time, after wondering what forecasters and pollsters actually mean when they attach a single number to something as unrepeatable as tomorrow's weather or an election. I run a small interview podcast for other self-taught people, and next week's guest has built her career defending Lewis's Principal Principle: the claim that rational credence should always defer to objective chance. I do not want her simply restating the principle, so please draft eight to ten interview questions, each with a natural follow-up keyed to the reply she is likely to give. Cover at least the following: the worry that defining "admissible evidence" already smuggles in an assumption about which evidence is allowed to override chance, which would make the principle circular; Lewis's own "big bad bug," his worry that a Humean about chance cannot coherently defer to a chance that the actual future course of history might retroactively undermine; whether the standard fix of conditionalizing on a complete theory of chance, rather than on admissible evidence, really solves that problem or just relocates it; and whether a non-Humean about chance could accept the Principal Principle while simply sidestepping the undermining worry, which would suggest the trouble lies with her Humeanism rather than with the principle itself.
