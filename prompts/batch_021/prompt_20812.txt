As a PhD student working on the ethics of armed conflict, I keep returning to the fault line that Jeff McMahan's revisionist account has opened beneath the traditional doctrine of the moral equality of combatants (MEC). On Michael Walzer's independence thesis, jus in bello permissions are detached from jus ad bellum, so that soldiers on opposing sides face one another as moral equals with an equal right to kill, regardless of whose cause is just. McMahan denies this: on his liability-based account, the permission to kill tracks moral responsibility for contributing to an unjust threat, so that combatants who fight without a just cause typically lack the permission to kill those who justly resist them, however scrupulously they observe in bello constraints. Write an essay adjudicating this dispute. Engage substantively with at least two of the following defenses of MEC, and assess whether McMahan's responses to them succeed: the epistemic argument that ordinary soldiers cannot reliably determine which side's cause is just; Yitzhak Benbaji's contractarian defense of combatant equality as a mutually beneficial convention that limits the savagery of war; and Seth Lazar's treatment of whether associative duties to one's compatriots could ground a permission to fight even in an unjust war. Address, too, what it costs the revisionist position that McMahan himself concedes the law of armed conflict may need to diverge from the "deep morality" of war that his account describes. Reach and defend a definite verdict: should the moral equality of combatants be abandoned, retained, or reconstructed, and on what grounds?
