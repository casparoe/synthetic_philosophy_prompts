Hey, I'm putting together a short bit for my "Philosophy of Love and Sex" seminar and could use another pair of eyes before I inflict it on the students. Here's the gist of the argument so far:

In Marriage and Morals, Russell's real target isn't "sex is fun, actually" — its jealousy. His claim is that jealousy isn't some fixed biological given but something whose intensity is set largely by custom and economics. He leans on the comparative anthropology of his day (Trobriand material of the Malinowski sort, Westermarck's history of marriage) to argue that in societies where a spouse isn't treated as property and paternity isn't tied to inheritance, jealousy is comparitively muted, whereas bourgeois Christian Europe, obsessed with legitimate heirs, inflames it. Because jealousy is this plastic, he thinks a more rational upbringing plus more honest customs could shrink it substantially, and that licenses more flexible arrangements for couples without children yet. Marriages meant for raising kids get a stricter standard from him though — he's explicit that he isn't proposing one regime of sexual freedom for everybody, contrary to how he sometimes gets caricatured now.

The obvious modern objection is evolutionary psych: mate-guarding, paternity uncertainty, and cross-culturally robust jealousy patterns (Buss and co.) all get read as evidence that jealousy has a solid adaptive basis rather than being a free-floating social artifact, which seems to puncture Russell's "just raise people differently" optimism pretty badly.

I try to gesture at a reply on Russell's behalf but I don't think I pull it off convincingly yet.

Could you take a look and tell me: (1) am I actually representing Russell's argument here, or have I flattened/modernized it too much? (2) is there a real reply available to the evo-psych objection on his behalf, or does his view just not survive contact with it? (3) honestly, is this a good angle for a lecture, or a bit of a tired one at this point?
