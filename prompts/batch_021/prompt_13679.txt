I'm writing a dissertation chapter on the structure of practical reason, and I keep getting stuck on an unresolved impasse between Ruth Chang's treatment of hard choices and John Broome's rival diagnosis of the same phenomenon. Chang holds that when two options are hard to rank relative to a covering consideration -- her standard illustrations involve choices between markedly different careers, a stable legal career against a riskier creative one, say -- the options can stand in a fourth basic evaluative relation, "parity," that is irreducible to being better than, worse than, or equally good. Her case for this rests on what is sometimes called the small-improvement argument: if the two options were equally good, then slightly sweetening one of them should make it determinately better than the other, yet in genuine hard cases it does not, even though the options are clearly not incomparable in the way that items from wholly unrelated evaluative domains are. In "Is Incommensurability Vagueness?", Broome argues that exactly this pattern is what we should expect from ordinary vagueness in the comparative "better than," so nothing beyond the standard trichotomy, suitably blurred at the edges, needs to be posited. Write an essay adjudicating this dispute on its philosophical merits. Explain precisely what work the small-improvement argument needs to do in order to rule out both equality and mere vagueness as explanations, assess whether it succeeds, and close with a definite verdict on whether parity deserves the status of a sui generis fourth relation.
