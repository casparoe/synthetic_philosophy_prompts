Ok so this might sound like a weird flex but I think I found a knockdown argument against moral dilemmas being real, from my ethics class. We covered deontic logic this week: obligations "agglomerate" (if you ought to do A and you ought to do B, then you ought to do A-and-B), and obligation requires ability -- you can't be obligated to do something impossible, aka "ought implies can." So if a genuine dilemma existed, where you're really obligated to do A and really obligated to do not-A, agglomeration means you'd be obligated to do the impossible (A-and-not-A), which violates ought-implies-can. Doesn't that just prove dilemmas can't be real, and that all the stuff Bernard Williams says about "moral residue" (guilt that lingers even after you did the right thing) is really just him mistaking a bad feeling for an actual leftover obligation? My professor seemed kind of into it when I brought it up in section but didn't really shoot it down, so now im wondering if im missing something or if this actually settles it.
