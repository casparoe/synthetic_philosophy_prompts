Been lurking on LW/AF since basically the original Irving/Christiano/Amodei debate paper came out, and I finally wrote down an argument that's been nagging at me about the whole scalable-oversight-via-debate agenda. Gonna clean it up into a shortform at some point but wanted eyes on it first, mainly to know if I'm just reinventing a well-known objection or if there's an actual gap here. The pitch for debate leans on a complexity-theory intuition: a weak judge can extract the truth from two much stronger debaters because pointing out a flaw is supposed to be way cheaper than constructing a convincing lie, kind of like how a poly-time verifier gets convinced of a PSPACE statement via an interactive proof even though it could never compute the statement itself. My worry is that this analogy quietly smuggles in exactly the assumption AI safety needs to be true: that there's always a short, locatable flaw in any wrong argument, and the honest debater can always find and point to it in the time allotted. The "obfuscated arguments" objection (Christiano and Barnes, if i'm remembering the name right) is basically that a wide/deep enough tree of locally-plausible-but-individually-unverifiable steps can hide the flaw so well that neither judge nor honest debater can locate it, even though it technically "exists" - and the exact regime where i'd expect a deceptive superhuman model to operate is precisely the regime where it has every incentive to build that kind of argument.

There's a newer "doubly efficient debate" result that's supposed to patch this using interactive-proof-style tricks (recursively bisecting the disagreement, sumcheck-ish) to keep honest strategies efficient even against an obfuscating opponent. But skimming it, the guarantee seems to lean on the disagreement having some bounded, recursively-bisectable structure, like a computation graph, and I don't see why an actual disagreement about, i.e., whether some policy backfires in ten years has that kind of structure at all. Separately, every debate-vs-consultancy experiment I've seen tests whether a more persuasive debater gets a judge to the right answer on trivia or reading-comprehension questions - domains where truth is cheap to check after the fact anyway - so they feel orthogonal to the hard case debate was supposed to help with in the first place.

Does this hold together, or is there an existing rebuttal to the "the theory only covers formally structured disagreements" point that I'm missing? Also, separately: is the persuasiveness-optimization worry (debaters get trained to win, not to be honest) basically the same problem as obfuscation wearing a different hat, or a genuinely distinct failure mode? Would love comments on either, or on whether this is even a coherent enough line of argument to be worth turning into a real post.
