Random work thing but I could use some help thinking it through properly before Friday. I'm one of three "employee reps" on my company's internal appeals panel -- when HR disciplines someone and they contest it, this panel (us three plus one person from HR and one from Legal) reviews the case and sends a written recommendation up to the VP who actually decides. Normally we just rubber-stamp whatever HR already wanted, but this time I got asked to draft the memo myself and its a genuinely hard one, not the usual stuff.

Here's the situation. About a dozen engineers organized a one-day walkout over a new contract to build surveillance/content-moderation tooling for a foreign government's interior ministry -- kind of like the controversy a few years back over Google's Project Maven contract, or Amazon selling Rekognition to police departments. They organized it openly on our internal Slack, which technically breaks two policies: the "acceptable use" rule that internal tools can't be used for organizing unrelated to your actual job, and a "no unauthorized work stoppages" line buried in the handbook. But they weren't sneaky about any of it -- they used their real names, told their managers beforehand, and basically said "we know this could get us fired and we're doing it anyway because we think building this is wrong." HR wants to fire the two people who organized it and give everyone else who joined a formal written warning. Legal seperately flagged that under the NLRA, "concerted activity for mutual aid and protection" is protected for pretty much all private-sector employees whether or not they're unionized, and it's genuinely unclear whether a protest like this counts, so firing people might backfire legally no matter what the handbook says.

Honestly I keep flip-flopping. Part of me thinks this is just the Thoreau/MLK model of civil disobedience -- open, non-violent, consequences accepted up front -- and coming down hard on it mostly just teaches people to be sneaky next time instead of raising concerns openly. But another part of me thinks "civil disobedience" is a concept built for citizens versus a government, and it might just not transfer to an employee versus a company, in which case this is basically insubordination with extra steps and HR had it right from the start. Could you write the actual memo I'd circulate to the panel -- a specific recommendation (uphold both firings, reduce it to something lesser, or clear the organizers entirely), a rough timeline for what happens next, and reasoning that genuinely engages the "policy has to mean something or none of this works" side instead of just steamrolling past it to reach the answer I probably want?
