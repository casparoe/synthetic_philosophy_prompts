I've been sitting in on a graduate reading group on philosophy of science, and after a session that covered David Lewis's "New Work for a Theory of Universals" alongside chapters from Armstrong's *What Is a Law of Nature?*, two of the regulars, Renata and Daniel, kept arguing about laws of nature long after everyone else had packed up. I couldn't referee it well in the moment, so I'd like a more careful, move-by-move verdict than I could give on the spot.

Renata, defending Lewis's Humean best-system account, opened by pressing what's sometimes called the inference problem against Armstrong's necessitarian alternative: (1) even granting a primitive relation of necessitation N(F,G) between the universals F and G, nothing about positing N(F,G) rules out, even as a bare metaphysical possibility, some F that fails to be G, and since Armstrong gives no story about why N-ing guarantees the corresponding regularity rather than merely correlating with it, the relation is explanatorily idle -- it "necessitates" in name only. (2) Daniel replied that this proves too much, and turned Lewis against himself: Humeans need their own unexplained primitive just to get the best-system machinery off the ground, namely a class of perfectly natural properties, needed to rule out the gerrymandered systematizations that would otherwise tie for simplicity and strength; and Lewis's own Ramseyan Humility argument concedes that if fundamental properties are individuated only by the nomic roles the best system assigns them, we could never know which intrinsic quality occupies which role. So, Daniel said, Humeans bottom out in a "something we know not what" too, and Renata can't press the brute-primitive complaint against Armstrong without it boomeranging onto her own view. (3) Renata answered that the two primitives aren't doing comparable work: naturalness is invoked only to individuate a privileged systematization of a pattern that's already fixed, and never purports to explain why the pattern holds, whereas N(F,G) is introduced specifically to explain and necessitate the regularity and then, on Daniel's own admission, can fail to do that job. Being unexplained is one thing, she said; failing at the very task you were posited for is another. (4) Daniel didn't back down: he said Renata was holding necessitation to a standard nobody applies to the Humean mosaic itself, since on her view the fundamental pattern of particular fact simply is what it is, unexplained, and laws are just an economical redescription of it after the fact -- which was exactly Armstrong's original complaint against regularity theories, that they can't explain their own instances. If unexplained brutality sinks necessitation, he said, it should equally sink the idea that summarizing brute facts amounts to a law governing them.

Could you go through this move by move and say who has the better of it at each stage, rather than just calling an overall winner -- including whether move (4) actually lands against move (3), or trades on a conflation between two different senses of "explain"? I'd also be curious whether you think their shared framing of the debate, Humean regularities versus Armstrongian necessitation and nothing else, is leaving some live alternative position out of the picture entirely.
