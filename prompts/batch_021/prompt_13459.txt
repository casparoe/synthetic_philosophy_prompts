In my decision theory seminar, we spent a week on Parfit's Hitchhiker: dying of thirst in the desert, you're found by a driver who will take you to town only if she predicts, with near-perfect reliability, that you'll pay her $100 once you arrive; by the time you're standing in town, the ride is already secured and paying gets you nothing further. Two people in the seminar kept arguing about it after class, and I couldn't settle who had the better of it.

Naomi said the case simply refutes causal decision theory: since the disposition to pay is what gets you rescued, while the disposition not to pay leaves you to die, any theory telling you not to pay once you're safe has thereby been shown wrong as a theory of rational action -- we should just call paying rational, rather than reach for talk of "rational irrationality." Devon replied that this quietly changes the subject: whether it was rational to become disposed to pay, before you knew you'd be picked up, is a different question from whether paying is rational once you're standing in town with nothing causal left to gain, and a good theory can answer these two questions differently without contradicting itself. Naomi answered that this just splits "rational" into two words tracking which moment you ask at, conveniently letting the theory call the disposition good and the act bad without ever being wrong. Devon said that isn't gerrymandering but the same disposition/act distinction already accepted for precommitment and deterrent threats, and that Naomi's own alternative -- judging the act by the policy it instantiates -- merely relocates the problem, since it still owes us a story about why facts about the best policy should guide you at a moment when, by your own lights, deviating makes no causal difference.

Could you go through this move by move, say who has the better argument at each stage, and flag anything you think both of them are missing?
