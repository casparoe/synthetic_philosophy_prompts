A friend and I spent much of last night arguing about Frank Ramsey's "Epilogue," the short, unusually good-humored paper he read to the Cambridge Apostles in 1925 and which was later printed as the closing piece of The Foundations of Mathematics — not quite five years before his own early death. Neither of us persuaded the other, so I have written up my side of the dispute as a short essay below, and I would value your comments on it before I answer her.

Russell's "A Free Man's Worship" treats the sheer size and indifference of the universe as a genuine threat to human significance, one to be met with tragic defiance rather than argued away. Ramsey's "Epilogue" is usually read as simply refusing to be impressed by size: the stars, he says, "cannot think or love," and he feels no humility before their vastness, since thought and love strike him as the properties that make something matter, not bulk. On this standard reading, Ramsey is making a substantive counter-claim — humans outscore galaxies on the dimension that actually counts.

I want to defend a stronger reading. Ramsey's real move, I think, is not to win a contest of importance against the cosmos but to deny that any such contest is being run at all. When he writes that his "picture of the world is drawn in perspective, and not like a model to scale," with the stars shrunk to "threepenny bits," he is not reporting a further fact about relative sizes; he is refusing the demand, implicit in Russell, that a life be assessed from some vantage point standing outside every perspective. On this reading, cosmic insignificance was never a coherent worry, because mattering is not a property a thing has or lacks independently of some perspective that assigns it, and no perspective-independent tribunal exists to consult.

My friend's objection, borrowed from Nagel's later discussion of absurdity, is that this only postpones the trouble: if mattering is always mattering-to-someone, the pessimist can ask why our ordinary human perspective, rather than the detached one we are equally capable of adopting, should be the one that governs how we live — and we do not seem able to simply decline that detached standpoint the way Ramsey's breezy tone suggests we can.

My own answer is that the detached standpoint does not actually supply the missing verdict either: viewed from nowhere in particular, nothing is significant or insignificant, including the universe's size, so adopting it hands the pessimist no fact to be gloomy about — it only empties the question, leaving our ordinary perspective as the sole place where "does this matter" can be sensibly asked at all. My friend suspects this reply just restates my original claim rather than meeting her objection.

I would appreciate it if you could go through this point by point: whether I have Ramsey's, Russell's, and Nagel's positions right; whether my "no perspective-independent fact" reading is really the best reconstruction of Ramsey's argument, as against my friend's flatter, more literal one; and, as the exchange stands, who is getting the better of it and why.
