I spent nearly forty years as a condensed-matter physicist before I retired a couple of years back, and since then I've gotten properly hooked on philosophy — there's a little discussion group that meets at the library once a month, mostly retired teachers and engineers, and we've been working our way through philosophy of science. Last month it was reduction and emergence: we did Nagel's old bridge-laws picture of reduction, then Fodor's multiple-realizability argument against it (the standard "pain can be realized in a human brain, an octopus, or in principle silicon, so psychology can't reduce to physics" move). Everybody got that one fine, its not exactly a hard sell at this point.

What's been nagging at me since is a case from my own corner of physics that nobody in the group had really chewed on: phase transitions. The free energy of a statistical-mechanical system with a finite number N of particles is, mathematically, a perfectly boring analytic function — no kinks, no discontinuities anywhere. A genuine phase transition (water suddenly boiling, a magnet losing its magnetization at the Curie point) only shows up as an actual mathematical singularity once you take N to infinity, the so-called thermodynamic limit. So strictly speaking, no actual finite chunk of matter — which is all that exists, presumably — ever undergoes a "real" phase transition at the microphysical level; you need a fiction (infinitely many particles) to make the phenomenon appear at all, even on paper. I gather Batterman has built a good chunk of his career arguing this is a case of real, ontologically serious emergence, not just some epistemic limitation of ours. But I also came across what I think is a direct reply to exactly this, a paper with a title that's a cute pun on Anderson's "More is Different" — "Less is Different," by Butterfield I believe — arguing that reduction and emergence are actually reconcilable here once you think about the finite-N behaviour as asymptotically approaching the infinite-N behaviour. I havent fully worked through that paper myself, so I might be garbling the details a bit.

Anyway — could you write me an essay that actually gets into this specific case, rather than just rehashing the generic multiple-realizability stuff I already have down, and weighs whether the thermodynamic-limit business in phase transitions is good grounds for believing in strong, ontologically robust emergence, or whether it's better understood as a mathematical idealization that a sensible reductionist can happily live with? I don't want a wishy-washy "both sides have a point" ending — pick a side and defend it, the way you'd have to if someone in the reading group pushed back on you.
