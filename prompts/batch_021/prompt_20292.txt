I'm a PhD student in philosophy working mainly on non-classical logics, and this term I'm auditing a graduate seminar on Madhyamaka housed in our Buddhist studies program. We spent an entire session arguing about how literally to take Nāgārjuna's use of the catuṣkoṭi, the tetralemma of "is," "is not," "both," and "neither." Graham Priest and Jay Garfield's well-known reading takes passages such as Mūlamadhyamakakārikā (MMK) 22.11, which denies that "empty," "non-empty," "both," or "neither" may be asserted of the Tathāgata, together with the "emptiness of emptiness" doctrine and the claim that saṃsāra and nirvāṇa are ultimately indistinguishable, as evidence that Nāgārjuna is a genuine dialetheist who accepts true contradictions at the limits of thought. Other commentators, such as Tillemans and Westerhoff, resist this conclusion, preferring to treat the fourfold negations as scope-restricted denials of svabhāva-laden predicates, or as therapeutic devices addressed to a particular audience, so that no law of classical logic actually needs to give way.

What the seminar never resolved is how this bears on two further complications. First, MMK 18.8 runs the tetralemma the other way, apparently affirming all four horns at once rather than negating them (something close to: everything is real, and is not real, both real and not real, and neither real nor not real), and it isn't obvious that an affirming tetralemma and a negating tetralemma are doing the same logical work or should receive a uniform semantic treatment. Second, Nāgārjuna's own signature method through most of the text is a prasaṅga, or reductio: he refutes svabhāva-based views, as in the opening rejection of the four possible ways anything could arise (MMK 1.1), by deriving consequences that his opponents themselves would reject as absurd. That method seems to presuppose that landing in a contradiction is a mark of falsity, which sits awkwardly with the idea that he elsewhere treats certain contradictions as literally true. There is also the "no thesis" defense of Vigrahavyāvartanī 29, which threatens to be self-undermining in just the way a dialetheist might expect but a classical logician should not.

Could you write a dialogue between two philosophers of comparable sophistication, one defending a broadly Priest/Garfield-style paraconsistent reading of Nāgārjuna and the other defending a classical-logic-preserving, contextualist reading in the spirit of Tillemans or Westerhoff, in which they work through these two complications together? I would like each side to engage with the other's strongest moves rather than talk past each other, and I would rather you not force a tidy resolution at the end, since this is exactly the point at which my seminar got stuck.
