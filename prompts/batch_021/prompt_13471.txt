I have a background in computer science rather than philosophy, but I've been stuck on the Gödelian anti-mechanist argument ever since I first met it, in passing, in Hofstadter's *Gödel, Escher, Bach*, and then again more seriously a few years later in Penrose's *Shadows of the Mind*. The basic move, which goes back to J. R. Lucas's 1961 paper, is that for any consistent formal system F strong enough to encode arithmetic, Gödel's first incompleteness theorem hands us a sentence G(F) that F itself cannot prove, yet which we, reasoning from outside F, can apparently see to be true; from this, Lucas and later Penrose conclude that no fixed formal system — hence no Turing machine, hence no "mere mechanism" — can be the whole story of what a human mathematician is doing.

The standard objection I keep running into is that the argument only gets off the ground if we already know that F is consistent, and Gödel's second incompleteness theorem says a consistent F can never establish its own consistency from within, so it looks like Lucas is simply helping himself to the one piece of self-knowledge the theorem says we can't have. What I haven't been able to settle for myself is whether Penrose's more careful version of the argument in *Shadows of the Mind* — which replaces bare consistency with the stronger, more specific claim that F is knowably sound, and tries to squeeze a contradiction out of the assumption that some single fixed F is both knowably sound and fully captures human mathematical competence — actually escapes this objection, or whether it just reproduces the same problem one level up: a thoroughgoing mechanist can apparently still say, "I grant that I am some fixed F; I simply deny that F's soundness is ever something I could come to know," and it is not obvious that Penrose's contradiction has any grip on that position.

Could you write me an essay that lays out Penrose's soundness-based argument precisely enough to make clear where it is supposed to improve on Lucas's original version, weighs the strongest case for and against the claim that it survives the "we can't know we're sound" objection, and then actually reaches a verdict on whether the Lucas–Penrose argument, in its best available form, succeeds in showing that minds cannot be mechanisms — rather than settling for a conclusion along the lines of "reasonable people disagree"?
