Random one, but it's been bugging me since I was reporting a feature on the AI-safety/longtermist scene a few weeks back, and a couple of my friends couldn't fully talk me down from it either. At the conference afterparty, a researcher from a tiny two-person outfit cornered me with an actual spreadsheet open on his laptop, walking me through why I should redirect basically all my charitable giving to his project instead of the mosquito-net charity I normally support. The pitch: his team's work has maybe a 1-in-100-million shot of being the thing that heads off a specific future catastrophe, but if it did pan out, it would preserve something like 10^30 future lives (his number, not mine), since it'd basically secure humanity's whole cosmic future. Then he pre-empted my obvious objection before I even said it — "fine, assume I'm probably a crank, dock my odds of being right by another factor of a thousand if you want, it still doesn't matter, because the number on the other side is so big it eats any reasonable amount of doubt."

The annoying part is his arithmetic isn't wrong as far as I can tell — a tiny probability times an astronomically large payoff still comes out ahead of any ordinary charity, math-wise, and we don't skip out on flood insurance or smoke detectors just because the bad event they cover is individually unlikely. But it also seems like the exact same move would justify handing my wallet to literally anyone who names a big enough number, no matter how made-up, which cant just be a coincidence. I've got a follow-up call with the guy next week and would love to go in with something better than "idk, it just feels off." Is there an actual, principled flaw in this kind of argument, or am I just refusing to bite a bullet I ought to bite given how much I claim to care about future generations?
