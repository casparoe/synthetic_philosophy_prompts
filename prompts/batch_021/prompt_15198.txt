I sit on my state's Pretrial Risk Assessment Oversight Board, which certifies the actuarial tools that counties may use to help set bail and release conditions. Two years ago the board certified a recidivism-risk instrument built by an outside vendor; last month, partly in response to a local newspaper's investigation, we received the independent statistical audit we had commissioned, and its findings have proven far more contentious than anyone on the board expected. The audit confirms that the tool is well calibrated: among defendants assigned the same score, Black and white defendants are rearrested within two years at essentially the same rate, so a "7" means the same thing regardless of race. But it also confirms that, because Black defendants in our counties have a higher base rate of rearrest than white defendants, the tool necessarily produces a higher false-positive rate for Black defendants (wrongly flagging as high-risk people who would not in fact have been rearrested) and a correspondingly higher false-negative rate for white defendants (wrongly clearing as low-risk people who go on to be rearrested). Our consultants were emphatic that this isn't a defect specific to this vendor's model: it is a general mathematical consequence of calibrating a score across groups with different base rates, and no rescoring of this particular instrument can remove the asymmetry without giving up calibration. The dispute echoes arguments that have played out publicly around comparable tools elsewhere, but our board has to settle it for our own instrument, on our own timeline.

The board has split into three camps. One wants to mandate equalized false-positive and false-negative rates going forward, accepting that the score will then mean different things -- in effect, different thresholds -- for defendants of different races. A second, backed by several judges and the vendor's own methodologists, wants calibration kept non-negotiable, on the grounds that judges need a score whose meaning doesn't depend on the defendant's race, and that explicit race-conditioned thresholds invite a serious equal-protection challenge. A third wants to decertify the instrument entirely and return to unstructured judicial discretion, despite the board's own earlier finding that discretion had produced racial disparities that were at least as large, and considerably harder to detect, than the tool's. I need to circulate a recommendation memo before the board's public vote next month. Please draft it: take a clear position among these three options, or lay out a specific alternative resolution of your own (for instance, restricting the stage of the process at which the score may be used); give the board a concrete implementation timetable; and engage honestly with the strongest argument for whichever option your memo asks the board to give up, rather than dismissing it.
