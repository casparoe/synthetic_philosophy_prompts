I'm a retired paleontologist who's gotten completely hooked on philosophy since retiring. I sit on the acquisitions committee for a small university natural history museum, and this month we've got a genuinely annoying decision: $40k in discretionary endowment money, and two competing uses for it. Option one is a rare, nearly-complete Eocene mammal skeleton our two research staff want for phylogenetic work -- real but modest payoff, maybe two or three solid papers. Option two is funding a hands-on "dig pit" exhibit for the public gallery, which the education staff say will boost visitor numbers and school bookings for years.

Half the committee wants to score both options on a shared rubric -- dollars per expected citation vs dollars per expected visitor -- normalize it, weight it, and just fund whichever comes out higher. Since reading Ruth Chang on "hard choices" I've started to suspect that's fake precision, since research value and public-education value might be different kinds of good rather then two amounts of the same thing. But "its all subjective, go with your gut" doesn't sit right either.

Could you draft the short memo I'll bring to committee next week: a recommendation for the $40k (all to one option, or some split, and on what basis), with reasoning that takes the rubric approach seriously rather than just waving it away?
