I am a novelist working on a book structured around Borges's preoccupation with recurrence and the dissolution of the self, and in preparing for it I have returned to his essay "A New Refutation of Time." I can follow the opening moves of the essay well enough: he borrows Berkeley's case against the independent existence of matter, then Hume's further step of dissolving the self into a mere succession of impressions with no substance behind them. What I cannot reconstruct is the next inferential step -- why the loss of a persisting subject and a persisting substance is supposed to license denying succession itself, rather than simply leaving us with an ownerless, impersonal sequence of perceptions that time could still be true of. I am equally unsure what to make of the essay's closing reversal, where Borges appears to grant that the argument holds and yet insists he remains as subject to time as ever. Could you walk through the logic that is supposed to carry us from the denial of matter and self all the way to the denial of time, and offer a reading of what that final concession is doing -- whether it amounts to a retraction, a distinction between argument and lived conviction, or something else again?
