I write about philosophy and the sciences of mind for a general-interest magazine, and next week, as part of a short series on how chance shapes moral judgment, I'm recording an interview with a moral philosopher whose signature position is a strict defense of what the literature calls the control principle: the claim that an agent's praise- or blameworthiness can only track what was genuinely within her control at the time of action, so that the phenomena Bernard Williams and Thomas Nagel dubbed "moral luck" — differences in how we judge people depending on how things turned out, what circumstances they happened to face, or even what temperament they were born with — are ultimately illusions that dissolve once actions and characters are individuated correctly. I've read enough of her published exchanges to know her preferred move whenever a hard case comes up: she says the differential judgments moral-luck theorists point to are "a fact about the psychology of blame, not about its metaphysics" — that we simply feel differently toward the reckless driver who kills a child and the equally reckless driver who gets home safely, without there being any real difference in what either one deserves.

Could you draft nine or ten interview questions that make that deflection progressively harder to sustain, so the conversation doesn't just retread the drunk-driver and Gauguin cases that show up in every summary of the debate? I want at least one question built on Nagel's category of circumstantial luck — his point that someone who was simply never tested, say by living under an occupying regime, can't straightforwardly be credited with a virtue she never had occasion to fail — used to ask why the control principle doesn't excuse the untested exactly as readily as it excuses the merely unlucky. I want at least one question built on Williams's notion of agent-regret, asking how a purely psychological error theory explains why that sentiment seems appropriate specifically to the agent and not to a bystander with identical information about the accident. And I want at least one question that pushes her individuation strategy down to constitutive luck itself: if blameworthiness tracks the quality of the will rather than the result, but the temperament and capacities that make a will good or bad were themselves never chosen, hasn't the control principle simply relocated the luck problem by one level rather than solved it? For each of these three, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give — my guess is she'll try to distinguish assessing the will from assessing the whole person, and I'd like that follow-up ready rather than improvised live. Close the sequence with a question that puts the dilemma to her directly: pursued all the way down, doesn't a control principle this strict end up proving too much, collapsing into the sort of thoroughgoing "hard luck" skepticism about responsibility associated with philosophers like Galen Strawson and Neil Levy, rather than rescuing responsibility from luck at all?
