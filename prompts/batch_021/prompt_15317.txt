Gödel's reasoning was not an appeal to scripture but to his Leibnizian conviction that the world is fundamentally rational, that nothing happens without sufficient reason, and that a mind elaborately built up over decades, only just becoming capable of grasping mathematics, ethics, and beauty, could not simply be annihilated the moment it matures without the whole arrangement being pointless. He took the argument seriously enough to defend it in earnest letters to his own mother, despite being, by every other measure, the last person inclined toward wishful thinking.
