So in my philosophy of physics seminar we just spent two weeks on Boltzmann's H-theorem and the objections Loschmidt and Zermelo threw at it -- Loschmidt's point that the underlying dynamics are time-reversible so entropy can't have a preferred direction built into it, and then Zermelo's reccurrence argument that any bounded mechanical system has to eventually come back arbitrarily close to where it started, so entropy can't just keep rising forever the way the H-theorem seems to say. What I didn't realize until our professor mentioned it almost as a throwaway line is that Boltzmann's own comeback to Zermelo -- basically, that the universe as a whole probably sits in equilibrium almost all the time, and we just happen to be living inside one of the rare, enormous fluctuations away from that -- is the direct ancestor of what cosmologists now call the Boltzmann brain problem. Except the modern version is apparently way worse for the idea than Boltzmann seems to have realized: people like Dyson, Kleban and Susskind pointed out that if all you actually need is a fluctuation big enough to produce your immediate surroundings, it's astronomically more likely to get one lone brain with false memories flickering into existence out of a vacuum than an entire coherent low-entropy galaxy, and this now gets used as a real argument against certain eternal-inflation multiverse models, tangled up with the "measure problem" of how you even assign probabilities to observers spread across infinite space and time.

I can't stop wondering what Boltzmann would actually say if someone sat him down and explained the full modern version to him: de Sitter vacuums fluctuating forever, the measure problem, the whole apparatus. Would he basically say "yes, obviously, that's just my fluctuation idea worked out properly," or does something about how he was already thinking about probability and unobservable entities back then (remember he was still fighting Mach and Ostwald over whether atoms even exist) suggest he'd be uneasy with how confidently physicists now treat an infinite ensemble of observers as something you can just assign probabilities over? Can you actually reconstruct his likely position instead of just saying he'd find it "fascinating" -- I want to know specifically where you think he'd agree with the modern argument and where his own commitments would make him push back on it.
