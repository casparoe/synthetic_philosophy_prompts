Ranked-choice voting is expanding to more American cities, and I'm working on a piece about whether the reform lives up to its billing. The more I read into the theory behind it, the more I suspect its central selling point doesn't survive contact with the math: since the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem proves that any voting rule for three or more candidates is either a dictatorship or manipulable by some voter, ranked-choice voting must be exactly as exposed to strategic voting as plain plurality, which would make its reputation for "resisting" tactical voters little more than marketing. If that's right, is there any principled reason left for a city to bear the cost of switching, given that the two systems are equally gameable?
