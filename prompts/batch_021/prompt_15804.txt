So I fell down a rabbit hole a few weeks back reading about AI alignment stuff and kept bumping into this idea people call the "Lobian obstacle" (I know it's actually spelled with an umlaut, Löb, but I can never be bothered typing that). From what I can tell it goes like this: Lob's theorem says that for a formal system T, if T can prove "if T proves P, then P" for some sentence P, then T can actually prove P outright, full stop, no matter what P is. People in the AI safety world (I read through the old MIRI "tiling agents" paper, then a pile of LessWrong posts after that) use this to argue that a self-modifying agent can never fully certify, using its own proof system, that a successor running that same proof system is trustworthy -- trying to prove "whatever my successor proves is true" for every P basically forces the parent to already prove every P itself, which no consistent system can do. Apparently there's a bunch of proposed workarounds (probabilistic reflection, infinite towers of successively stronger systems a la Feferman, stuff like that) but I honestly can't tell if those dissolve the problem or just relocate it, and I really can't tell if this is a deep fact about self trust in general, including for us squishy humans, or just an artifact of demanding proof-theoretic certainty that nobody actually needs anyway.

Lena argues the "pessimist" side: that the Lobian obstacle is a genuine and pretty general limit on self-trust -- no consistent reasoner that's expressive enough to represent its own proof predicate can ever have fully justified confidence in its own future reasoning, and the probabilistic or hierarchical fixes don't really escape this, they just move the spot where you have to take something on faith instead of proof. Marcus argues the "deflationist" side: that Lob's theorem only rules out one very specific and unreasonably strong form of self-trust (an unconditional reflection schema covering literally every sentence at once), that real reasoners get by fine on weaker, local, or statistical self-trust all the time, and that treating this as some tragic epistemic limit is a category error, like being upset a hammer can't also be a screwdriver.
