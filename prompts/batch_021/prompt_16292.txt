Here is the student's answer, verbatim:

"Gödel's first incompleteness theorem shows that for any consistent formal system F rich enough to represent arithmetic, there is a sentence G_F, built by a diagonal construction, such that F can prove neither G_F nor its negation, and G_F is true just in case F is consistent. Gödel himself, in his 1951 Gibbs lecture, drew the moral that the human mind must therefore infinitely outstrip any Turing machine, since a mathematician can always step outside a given formal system and recognize the truth of its Gödel sentence. Lucas (1961) and later Penrose (1989, 1994) turned this into a full argument against mechanism: suppose the mind, or the totality of a mathematician's reasoning powers, is equivalent to some formal system F. Granting that F is consistent, we can see that G_F is true, yet F itself cannot prove G_F. So there is something we can do that F cannot; and since F was an arbitrary stand-in for 'the mind as a machine,' no machine can capture the mind's mathematical powers.

The standard rebuttal, associated with Putnam and Benacerraf, is that this move is circular. Lucas's own argument requires that no sound formal system can certify its own soundness, which is just Gödel's second incompleteness theorem applied to F: if the mind is F, the mind can never establish that F, i.e. itself, is sound, so it is never entitled to assert that G_F is true rather than merely conjecture it. The objection is compelling because it shows that Lucas's confidence in his own consistency is exactly the kind of thing his opponent is entitled to deny him, and no amount of introspection substitutes for a proof. On balance, I think this is close to decisive against the argument in its original form, and I am not aware of a response that keeps the argument's ambitions intact."
