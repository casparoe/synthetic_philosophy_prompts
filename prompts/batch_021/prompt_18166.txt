Over dinner last week, a friend and I spent nearly two hours arguing about Kurt Gödel, and neither of us budged. He thinks Gödel's private conviction that he would survive death deserves to be taken seriously as philosophy rather than dismissed as a curiosity: the reasoning Hao Wang recorded from their conversations, and the letters Gödel wrote to his mother Marianne working out why a "rationally constructed" world would be a disappointment if death were simply the end, come from the same mind that gave us the incompleteness theorems and a formal ontological proof of God's existence, so it deserves the benefit of the doubt. I argued that a man who spent his final years convinced that doctors, and eventually even his own wife, were trying to poison him, and who ultimately starved rather than eat, has forfeited some claim on that authority, and that we only dress up a genius's private hopes as arguments because he was a genius. Neither of us could settle it, so I'd rather put the question to someone who actually works on the material.

I produce a podcast on the history of logic, and I've booked a historian of philosophy who has spent years working through Gödel's unpublished notebooks and correspondence, for a segment on his private theology. Please draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having her simply narrate what Gödel believed. I want at least one question that presses on the actual logical shape of what's sometimes called his "disappointment" argument, and whether it is an argument at all or a hope wearing argument's clothes; one asking her to relate that informal reasoning to the ontological proof he showed Dana Scott in 1970, and whether the two are doing the same philosophical work or quite different work; one on how his avowed sympathy for Leibniz's monadology shapes the specific, rather unorthodox picture of survival he seems to have had in mind, as opposed to a generic religious afterlife; one that puts the genetic-fallacy defense to her directly -- that his paranoid delusions are irrelevant to whether his reasoning is sound -- and asks what, if anything, should change in our assessment of him once we know how he died; and one on what, if anything, the philosophical notebooks Gödel kept in shorthand, only partially transcribed until recently, add to or complicate the picture Wang gives us secondhand. For each question, include a brief follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, so the interview keeps pressing rather than moving on to the next item.
