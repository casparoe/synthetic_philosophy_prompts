I'm writing my IB Extended Essay on Henry Sidgwick's utilitarianism, and while reading around The Methods of Ethics for background I went down a bit of a research rabbit hole into his life. It turns out that alongside being one of the most rigorous and self-critical moral philosophers of the Victorian era, Sidgwick was also a founding member and the first president of the Society for Psychical Research, and for roughly the last quarter-century of his life he personally sat in on séances and tested mediums, in pursuit of scientific evidence for telepathy and for survival after death.

When I mentioned this to my extended essay supervisor, she called it a "quirky footnote" that has nothing to do with his actual ethical theory and told me not to waste word count on it. Still, it strikes me as a strange thing for someone so famous for careful, skeptical argumentation to have devoted decades to, and I can't quite shake two related suspicions: first, that pouring that much of his career into something which looks, on the face of it, a lot like credulous ghost-hunting should lower our confidence in him as a rigorous thinker generally; and second, that whatever was going on there was a separate, compartmentalized project that just happened to occupy the same mind as the ethics, rather than something that actually connects to it. Am I right to read the psychical research that way, or am I missing something about either the kind of investigator Sidgwick actually was, or about how the two projects fit together?
