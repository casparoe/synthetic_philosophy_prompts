I'm a software engineer at an AI company, and a few months ago I got rotated onto our internal model-release review board -- a standing group of engineers, a couple of safety researchers, and one policy staffer who has to sign off before any new model version goes out the door. I don't have any formal philosophy training, but I've read enough alignment write-ups over the last couple of years -- the original ARC report on eliciting latent knowledge, and the line of follow-up work that tries to find "truth-like" directions in a model's activations without relying on human labels, something like contrast-consistent search -- that I can follow the technical arguments even when I can't always name the underlying philosophical issue myself.

Here's the situation the board is stuck on. Our interpretability team built a probe on the model's internal activations that's meant to catch cases where the model "knows" something different from what it says out loud -- essentially an automated honesty detector. It was trained and validated exclusively on questions where we already had independent ground truth: held-out factual questions, plus cases where we deliberately inserted a false belief and could check the probe against it directly. On that test suite it performs well, flagging a meaningful fraction of cases where the internal signal and the stated answer diverge, including several where the output looked completely fine to human reviewers. Roughly half the board wants to treat a clean result on this probe as close to a solved instance of what people call ELK, and to gate future releases on it. The other half of us worry the probe is just a more sophisticated "human simulator" one level removed -- that it may have learned to fire on whatever surface pattern would eventually strike a human, or another model, as suspicious, rather than actually reading off the network's latent beliefs -- and that strong performance on cases where we already know the answer is close to uninformative about the cases the whole exercise exists for, where we don't. Three meetings in, nobody has managed to state that disagreement precisely enough for the board to actually vote on it.

I have to bring a short written recommendation to Friday's meeting, and I'd like your help drafting it. Please write the memo: it should take an actual position -- gate releases on the probe's verdict, don't, or something conditional in between, spelled out concretely rather than left vague -- and defend it with reasons the other camp could actually respond to rather than talking past. In particular, it should engage directly with the human-simulator worry instead of dismissing it, say something specific about whether and why good performance on ground-truth-known cases should transfer to the ground-truth-unknown cases we actually care about, and close by naming the concrete evidence, from the next release cycle or two, that would change the recommendation in either direction.
