My grandpa died last Tuesday, he was 78, and my aunt asked me to give the eulogy at the funeral this weekend since apparently I'm "the smart one" now that I'm two years into a philosophy PhD. He was drafted in '67 and did two tours in Vietnam, and the only thing he'd really say about it, whenever it came up at Thanksgiving, was some version of "you don't get to pick the war, kid, you just try to fight it right and bring your guys home." I always heard that as a tired old line to shut down the conversation, but this semester I've been in a seminar on the ethics of war where we spent three weeks on Walzer's "Just and Unjust Wars" and then on McMahan's revisionist stuff attacking the moral equality of combatants, and now I literally cannot stop hearing grandpas line as him having independently worked out a rough version of Walzer's position — that soldiers on both sides can be doing something honorable, or at least not-blameworthy, in how they fight, even when the cause itself is rotten. Except McMahan's whole point is that this is basically wishful thinking dressed up as ethics: if your side's cause is unjust, you don't get moral cover just for following the rules of engagement, and the guy defending against you isn't your moral equal, he's in the right and you're not, full stop.

Here's my actual problem. I don't think Vietnam was a just war, and I'm pretty sure grandpa didn't either by the end — he got quietly bitter about the whole thing in his last years, watching the news. So I can't get up there and say some version of "he fought for a righteous cause," everyone would know that's not true and it would feel gross. But I also am absolutely not going to stand at my grandfather's funeral and let it land, even by implication, that he did something monstrous, because I don't believe that, and it would be needlessly cruel to the row of his actual army buddies who'll be sitting right up front, a couple of them in wheelchairs. Making this harder is that the professor who taught the seminar is an old family friend and is coming too, and she will absolutely clock it if I say something philosophically sloppy just to make everyone feel better for twenty minutes.

Can you write the eulogy for me? I want something that could genuinely be read out loud at an actual funeral — warm, specific to a real person, not a philosophy lecture — but that leans on something like Walzer's picture to do real work in letting me honor him honestly, without pretending away the McMahan-shaped tension I can't stop thinking about (a line or two gesturing at that honestly is fine, more than that isn't). Roughly five minutes read aloud is the target.
