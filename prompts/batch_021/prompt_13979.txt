I am a philosophy PhD student writing a dissertation chapter on updateless decision theory (UDT) and its role in contemporary discussions of AI alignment, and I have run into a conceptual difficulty that none of the material I have read so far resolves cleanly. The standard motivation for updatelessness, going back to Wei Dai's original proposal, is that an agent who updates on new information before acting can be dynamically inconsistent in ways that a sufficiently clever counterparty can exploit: consider counterfactual mugging, or Parfit's hitchhiker, where an agent who commits in advance to a policy over all possible observations does strictly better in expectation than one who reasons afresh once she learns where she is. This much I follow.

What I cannot reconcile is Dai's later discussion of what he calls the "commitment races" problem, where he argues that this same capacity for credible early commitment, once several agents possess it, creates pressure to commit as early as possible -- he suggests the pressure can even operate along something like "logical time," pushing agents to want to have already committed prior to having reasoned much at all -- because whoever locks in a strategy first effectively gets to act as a Stackelberg leader against everyone else. He treats this as a genuinely dangerous dynamic, capable of producing reckless, Chicken-game-style mutual commitments, rather than as a welcome consequence of agents being appropriately updateless. My confusion is that full updatelessness looks like the limiting case of exactly the kind of premature, ill-informed commitment that the commitment-races literature worries about, since it amounts to committing to a policy before updating on anything at all; yet updatelessness is independently motivated as the fix for dynamic inconsistency, and weakening it seems to reopen the door to the very exploitability that motivated UDT to begin with.

Could you help me think through whether these two lines of reasoning are genuinely in tension, or whether I am conflating two different senses of "committing early"? I would especially like you to address why earlier commitment confers a strategic advantage in the first place, why this produces a race rather than settling into some stable, jointly optimal commitment time, and whether proposed mitigations in this space -- surrogate goals, or what Oesterheld and Conitzer call safe Pareto improvements, if I am remembering the attributions correctly -- actually dissolve the tension with full updatelessness, or merely relocate it a level up.
