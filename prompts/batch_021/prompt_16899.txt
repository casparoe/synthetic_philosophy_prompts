"The thesis in question—sometimes called Stalnaker's Thesis, sometimes Adams' Thesis—says that for any propositions A and C, P(A > C) = P(C | A) whenever P(A) > 0. The idea is that your confidence in a conditional just is your conditional credence in the consequent given the antecedent, which fits the Ramsey-test picture of how we evaluate conditionals: suppose A, and see how confident you'd be in C.

Lewis's proof shows this can't hold in full generality. Take any A and C such that A is compatible with both C and not-C, i.e. P(A&C) > 0 and P(A&~C) > 0. Conditionalize on C: since C is now certain, the Thesis applied to this updated probability function forces P(A>C | C) = 1. Conditionalize on ~C instead: now the Thesis forces P(A>C | ~C) = 0. Averaging these by the law of total probability, P(A>C) = P(C)·1 + P(~C)·0 = P(C). But the (unconditionalized) Thesis also says P(A>C) = P(C|A). Putting the two together, P(C|A) = P(C): A and C come out probabilistically independent. Since this has to hold for any A and C meeting that weak compatibility condition, and it's trivial to find correlated propositions in any reasonably rich probability space, the Thesis collapses into absurdity. 'Trivial' here doesn't mean the result is unimportant; it means the only probability spaces where no contradiction arises are ones so impoverished that nothing is ever genuinely uncertain relative to anything else.

Because the Thesis is bound up with Stalnaker's semantics for the conditional—'A > C' is true at w iff C holds at the closest A-world to w—Lewis's proof is standardly read as showing that this whole possible-worlds approach to indicative 'if' cannot be right: no assignment of nearest worlds can be found that makes the probabilities come out correctly across the board. That's a big part of why so much subsequent work (Adams, Gibbard, Edgington) treats indicative conditionals as not truth-apt at all, giving them assertability conditions instead of truth conditions. Once the Equation can't survive as a fully general thesis, the natural conclusion is that conditionals were never in the business of expressing propositions to begin with—our intuition that they're truth-evaluable was simply mistaken."
