This semester, for an elective on classical Chinese philosophy, I've been reading the Zhuangzi in the Burton Watson translation, and I'd like to explore it beyond the butterfly dream, which comes up in almost every summary of the text. Two other passages have stayed with me: the scene where Zhuangzi finds a skull by the roadside, questions it about how it came to be there, and that night dreams that the skull describes the strange contentment of being dead and free from the troubles of the living, refusing outright when Zhuangzi offers, in the dream, to have its body and life restored; and the scene near his own death, where he waves off his disciples' plans for an elaborate burial by noting that heaven and earth will serve as his coffin just fine, and that it makes no real difference whether his corpse is eaten by crows and kites above ground or by insects below. Could you write a short story of around 1,000 words, set in a contemporary world of your choosing, that conveys the outlook behind these two passages through plot and character, rather than by having a character explain the philosophy out loud? To keep it from feeling too tidy, please build in some genuine resistance to that outlook somewhere in the story — a character, possibly the protagonist, who isn't fully convinced.
