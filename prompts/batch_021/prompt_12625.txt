I'm about a third of the way into a novel whose big set-piece chapter, genuinely the emotional heart of the whole book, is built around a woman named Ana, blind since birth from dense congenital cataracts, getting her sight surgically restored in her early thirties, and I want the scene where she's shown a wooden ball and a wooden cube — things she already knows perfectly well by touch — to actually hold up if a philosopher of mind happens to read it, not just come off as a nice bit of magical-realist prose. Somewhere in the research for this I fell into Molyneux's problem, which is apparently exactly this set-up (Molyneux wrote to Locke about it back in 1688), and I've got the headline version down: Locke says no, she won't recognize the cube by sight alone; Leibniz says yes. But every summary I find just states that as a fact and moves on to the next guy, and then Berkely gets tacked on as agreeing with Locke's "no," which throws me, because the little I've read of Berkeley's theory of vision makes it sound way weirder and more radical than Locke's — something about sight and touch not even sharing the same kind of space, not just that Ana hasn't learned to pair them up yet. Could you actually walk me through what's doing the philosophical work for each of the three — what specifically licenses Leibniz's confidence that reasoning alone could close the gap where Locke and Berkeley insist it can't, and whether Berkeley's reason for saying no is a genuinely stronger or different claim than Locke's, or just the same worry dressed up?

There's a second half to this I'd love your take on too. I want to keep Ana's case honestly congenital rather than borrow the arc of Oliver Sacks's patient Virgil (the guy from "To See and Not See"), since Virgil actually had a bit of infant vision before he lost it, and I gather that detail does real work in how his case usually gets read — I don't want to accidentally write Virgil's story with a different name on it. Separately, a friend who used to work in vision science mentioned at a dinner party, kind of in passing, that someone has actually run a version of this experiment for real, on kids who'd had congenital cataracts removed late, somewhere in Asia she thought, and that what they found was stranger than a clean yes or no either way. I don't remember anything past that. If you know the study she means, I'd like to hear what it actually found, and, more for my purposes, whether it settles anything between Locke, Leibniz and Berkeley at all, or whether it's really just answering a different question than the one those three were arguing about — so I don't accidentally have a character wave it around later like it proves one of the old philosophers "won."
