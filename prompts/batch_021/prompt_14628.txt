I am several drafts into a novel whose central conceit turns on a slow, almost imperceptible transformation: over some sixty years, my protagonist's body and brain are replaced, component by component and neuron by neuron, with synthetic substitutes, until nothing of the original biological matter remains. Structurally, this is simply the sorites paradox in science-fiction dress: a long chain of individually negligible changes that somehow adds up to a categorical difference, with no single substitution that seems to mark the boundary between her and whatever she becomes. In researching the novel's internal logic, I was drawn into the literature on whether vagueness of this kind is genuinely out in the world — an objective, if fuzzy, matter of fact about exactly when she ceases to exist — or whether it only ever reflects the imprecision of our language and concepts applied to an underlying reality that is itself perfectly determinate. That reading eventually led me to Gareth Evans's famously compressed argument that there cannot be indeterminate identity, and hence no vague objects, at all — only vague ways of representing an objectively precise world. Short as it is, I gather that argument has occasioned a surprising amount of technical dispute in the decades since it appeared.

I would like you to write an essay, pitched at a philosophically literate but non-specialist reader, addressing whether vagueness should be understood as ontic — a real feature of objects and their identity conditions — or as merely semantic or epistemic — a feature of our representations, concepts, or knowledge. Please go beyond the standard heap-of-sand exposition of the sorites paradox: engage specifically with Evans's argument against vague identity and with at least one serious line of response to it, before weighing the two general positions against one another. I would rather not receive a diplomatic survey that leaves the matter open; please commit to, and argue for, a definite verdict on the ontic-versus-representational question, even if that verdict has to be qualified or restricted to cases structurally like my protagonist's rather than issued as a blanket ruling.
