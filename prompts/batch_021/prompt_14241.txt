A friend and I spent an entire dinner arguing about Henry Sidgwick, and neither of us gave ground. He's the utilitarian philosopher who spent decades investigating mediums and thought-transference claims, first privately and then as founding president of the Society for Psychical Research. She thinks it was a lapse: the same philosopher who, in The Methods of Ethics, refused to trust any moral intuition until it had passed a demanding test of clarity and independent, convergent agreement, spent all that time chasing evidence in séance rooms that he would never have tolerated in an ethics seminar. I think the opposite: that the psychical research was the rational continuation of that book, not a departure from it, because Sidgwick closes the Methods by admitting that the "dualism of practical reason" between egoism and utilitarianism cannot be resolved without some further fact about a moral order to the universe — and that, unlike Kant, he refused simply to postulate that fact on faith. Write the strongest possible version of my argument. Engage specifically with what that closing chapter actually commits him to, explain why his own standards of evidence, applied consistently, left him little rational choice but to go looking for the further fact empirically rather than assume it, and then meet the hardest objection to my view head-on: that decades of essentially null results should themselves have counted, by his own lights, as evidence against the very possibility he was hoping to find.
