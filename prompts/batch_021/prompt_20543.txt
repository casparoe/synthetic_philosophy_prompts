As a software engineer, I tend to ask whether a set of rules is internally consistent before I worry about whether I like them, and that is essentially my question about the Christian doctrine of penal substitution: the idea that Christ was punished in the sinner's place. Critics call the doctrine incoherent because, unlike a debt, punishment supposedly cannot be transferred from a guilty party to an innocent substitute — only the wrongdoer, they say, can justly be punished. David Lewis took the objection seriously enough to write a short paper, "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?", testing it against everyday secular cases of substitution, to see whether our existing moral and legal thinking already leaves room for something like it.

I'd like help running that same test on six candidate cases. For each, the question is whether it is a genuine instance of transferring the punishment itself, as opposed to merely transferring a cost, a liability, or blame, and, if so, whether that transfer looks morally acceptable or morally troubling.

1. Paying off someone else's court-imposed fine.
2. A volunteer who serves someone else's prison sentence, with the court's approval.
3. Vicarious liability in tort law, where an employer pays damages for an employee's negligence.
4. The historical "whipping boy," a servant raised alongside a prince and caned in the prince's place for the prince's misdeeds.
5. The Yom Kippur scapegoat, on which the community's sins are ritually placed before it is driven into the wilderness.
6. A junior engineer who volunteers to take the blame, and the formal write-up, for a production outage actually caused by a teammate, with their manager's knowledge.

Please sort these six cases into three groups — genuine and morally acceptable transfers of punishment, genuine but morally troubling transfers of punishment, and cases that are not really transfers of punishment at all — and give your answer as a JSON object of the form:

```json
{
  "genuine_and_acceptable": [<case numbers>],
  "genuine_but_troubling": [<case numbers>],
  "not_real_transfer": [<case numbers>]
}
```

Please also include a one-sentence rationale for each case, keyed by case number, in an accompanying "rationale" object.
