A friend and I spent a long evening disputing Augustine's treatment of the officious lie in Contra Mendacium — the lie told purely to benefit someone and harm no one, which he still counts as a real sin, though a lesser one than a malicious lie. She argued that this makes his prohibition on lying practically empty. Since a lie, for Augustine, is asserting what one believes to be false with the intent to deceive, a caregiver can achieve exactly the same effect through silence, redirection, or a statement that is true but incomplete — and if those are permitted, the absolute rule isn't ruling out anything that matters. I argued that asserting and withholding remain genuinely different acts even when their upshot for the listener is identical. We never settled it, and now I have to put the question to use: I sit on the ethics committee for our hospital's memory-care unit, where staff currently improvise on exactly this point. Some tell patients with advanced dementia that a deceased spouse is "at work" or "will visit later" rather than re-delivering news of the death every time it's asked for; others simply redirect the conversation; families disagree sharply about which practice should become official policy. Please draft the short written opinion I'll bring to next week's meeting: it should recommend one specific, trainable policy rather than splitting the difference, give a timeframe for introducing it, and take seriously the strongest argument for the practice it recommends against.
