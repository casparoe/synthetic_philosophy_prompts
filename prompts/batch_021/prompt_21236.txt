Three weeks ago my mother suffered a catastrophic intracranial hemorrhage, and after two days of testing she was declared dead by neurological criteria — what most people call "brain dead." Signing the organ-donation paperwork afterward left me with more questions than the hospital's explanation had answered, and the reading I've done since has only made me less confident that either of our two legal standards for death is doing the philosophical work it's supposed to do. I've sketched the argument below as notes toward an essay and would welcome comments before I draft it properly, especially on whether I'm representing the literature fairly and whether the conclusion in point 5 actually follows from what precedes it.

1. The Uniform Determination of Death Act offers two independent tests — irreversible loss of circulatory function, or irreversible loss of all brain function — and treats them as two ways of detecting the same underlying fact. But "irreversible" in donation-after-circulatory-death protocols just means a short, stipulated waiting period of a few minutes without a heartbeat, not a demonstrated impossibility of resuscitation. Some conventionalism, it seems, is built in before we even get to the brain.
2. The standard defense of the whole-brain criterion, associated with James Bernat, is that the brain is the organism's integrator, so its total loss ends the organism as a functioning whole. Alan Shewmon's case reports of "chronic brain death" — patients who went on healing wounds, fighting infections, growing, and in at least one case gestating a fetus for months after a rigorous brain-death diagnosis — seem to me to refute this directly: something was still integrating these bodies, and it wasn't a functioning brain.
3. The President's Council on Bioethics's 2008 white paper tried to save the standard with a subtler idea: a living organism must still be doing the fundamental work of self-preservation, of which unassisted breathing is the emblematic sign. That survives Shewmon's cases, but it seems to prove too little, since I can't see how to distinguish a brain-dead body's dependence on a ventilator from a living patient's dependence on one after a high spinal cord injury without already assuming the conclusion.
4. If neither rationale is fully stable, the more honest position may be the one associated with Truog and Miller: stop insisting that donors are dead in any deep biological sense at the moment organs are taken, and ground the ethics of transplantation directly in consent and in the fact that further treatment could no longer serve the patient's interests.
5. Tentative conclusion: both of our legal thresholds for death are best understood as socially useful conventions rather than metaphysical discoveries, and admitting that would mostly just mean having a harder, more honest conversation with families like mine.

Where do you think this reasoning is weakest, and is the move in point 4 as low-cost as I'm making it sound?
