The question given to students was:

Your answer should (a) state what the theorem claims, (b) explain each of the theorem's four conditions in your own words, (c) give one concrete example of a familiar ranked-voting method violating one of these conditions, and (d) state precisely what the theorem does and does not establish about the possibility of designing a fair voting system."

Here is the student's answer in full:

"Arrow's Impossibility Theorem says that once there are three or more candidates and voters simply rank them, no method for turning everyone's individual rankings into one group ranking can satisfy four reasonable-looking fairness conditions at the same time.

Unrestricted domain: the method must produce a group ranking no matter what combination of individual rankings the voters happen to submit; we aren't allowed to just exclude 'weird' preference patterns in advance.

Pareto efficiency: the group ranking has to be Pareto efficient. If some other ranking would satisfy at least one voter's preferences better without satisfying any other voter's preferences worse, the rule is obligated to switch to that ranking instead.

Non-dictatorship: there is no voter whose personal ranking automatically becomes the group's ranking regardless of what everyone else prefers.

Independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA): whether the group ranks X above Y should depend only on how individual voters rank X against Y, not on where some third candidate Z happens to fall in anyone's ballot.

Example: with three candidates A, B, and C, the Borda count can rank A above B when all three names are on the ballot, and then flip to ranking B above A once the also-ran C is dropped and the same ballots are re-tallied on just A and B, even though not a single voter changed their mind about A versus B. That is a textbook IIA violation.

So what does the theorem actually establish? It shows that however cleverly you design a ranked voting rule, you cannot make elections fair: some voters will always have their rankings effectively overridden. That is why economists describe a perfectly fair election as mathematically impossible."
