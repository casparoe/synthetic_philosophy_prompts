I've been down a rabbit hole (pun very much intended) for a piece I'm writing on the weirder philosophical corners of general relativity. A philosopher-of-physics friend walked me through Einstein's "hole argument" over drinks last week and it's been stuck in my head since: if your gravitational field equations are generally covariant, you can take some little region of spacetime with no matter in it, apply a diffeomorphism that's the identity everywhere outside that region but smoothly drags the points around inside it, and end up with a mathematically distinct solution that still agrees with absolutely everything outside the hole. If you think spacetime points are real individual things in their own right, independent of the metric (substantivalism), that seems to mean the theory literally can't tell you whats happening inside the hole, even given complete information everywhere else - a totally gratuitous indeterminism. Apparently something like this is what convinced Einstein himself, for a good two years around 1913-15 (don't quote me on the exact dates), that generally covariant equations had to be wrong, before he talked himself out of it with what's usually called the "point-coincidence" argument.

I'd love a short story built around this. It can be straight up historical fiction about Einstein's own crisis of confidence in that period, if you want to take a crack at dramatizing it from the inside - or it can be totally invented, a physicist or grad student or even an AI working through the problem, in whatever setting you like. The one thing I really want is for the story's actual dramatic tension to come from the argument itself: the vertigo of realizing your best theory might be predicting nothing at all about some region of the world, the substantivalist vs. relationist stakes riding on how you resolve it, maybe even a version of the resolution (or a refusal to resolve it). I don't want something that just gestures vaguely at "physics is trippy" - I want a story where you can tell the author actually understands what's logically at stake, the way the best Ted Chiang or Greg Egan stories earn their emotional beats through the science itself rather than around it. Doesn't need to be long, a page or two of actual prose is plenty.
