I recently started working for an animal-welfare NGO campaigning against plans for the world's first commercial octopus farm, a campaign that gained real momentum once the UK's Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022 formally added octopuses and other cephalopods to the legal category of sentient beings. Reading the scientific literature on octopus cognition for work has sent me back, oddly, to Descartes, whose bête-machine (beast-machine) doctrine still shapes much of the popular intuition about which creatures "really" have minds. As I understand him, Descartes rested his case on two distinct tests: a language test, since only genuine reason could produce flexible, topic-general replies to whatever is said, unlike a parrot trained to repeat a few set phrases, and a test from the versatility of action, since a cleverly built machine might outperform us at one narrow task but would inevitably fail outside it, for want of the "universal instrument" that reason alone supplies. He was also explicit that impressive behavior proves nothing by itself, citing bees and swallows as creatures whose intricate, well-adapted behavior is pure instinct rather than thought. Given that framework, and given that octopus cognition is distributed across semi-autonomous arms rather than gathered in one place — which sits awkwardly next to his separate argument that a thinking soul must be indivisible — do you think Descartes would simply have filed the octopus away as an unusually elaborate automaton, or does its cognitive profile put real strain on his own criteria? I'd like your best reconstruction of his actual reasoning, rather than just a general verdict on octopus sentience.
