I've been putting together a seminar on personal identity and keep getting stuck on the stretch in Parfit's Reasons and Persons where he leans on Buddhist no-self to soften people up for what he calls (I think?) the Extreme Claim -- roughly, that once you accept the reductionist view of persons (a person just is a long causal series of interrelated mental states and events, no seperate persisting subject underneath all that), the boundary between you-now and you-later, and even between you and other people, starts looking less morally load bearing, so the kind of full-blooded partiality toward your own future that we take for granted is less rationally required than we assume. He basically says the Buddha got there first and would've signed off on the whole package. One of my students, who's actually read a decent chunk of the Abhidharma material -- Vasubandhu's no-self chapter, the old "chariot is just a label for its parts" move, that sort of thing -- pushed back hard in discussion: she argued that anatta was built to dissolve craving and suffering through insight and practice, not to hand you an impartial, quasi-utilitarian ethic for free, and that Parfit is quietly borrowing the Buddhist metaphysics while skipping over the part of the Buddhist framework that actually does the normative work.

I think she's onto something real but nobody in the room, me included, could actually spell out the argument on the spot, so I'm curious what you'll do with it. Can you argue, as sharply as you can, that Parfit's inference from the reductionist/no-self metaphysics to his ethical conclusions about diminished partiality and the reduced significance of personal boundaries doesn't actually go through -- that the metaphysical claim (no further fact about identity, just overlapping psychological continuity and connectedness) underdetermines the normative claim, and that whatever does the bridging work in the Buddhist case isn't something Parfit gets to help himself to without a lot more argument. Don't just restate both positions at me -- I want the actual structure of the gap laid out.
