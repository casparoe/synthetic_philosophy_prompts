I'm on the board of a 40-unit condominium association — I ended up as treasurer mainly because nobody else wanted the job, not because I have any background in finance or law — and I need help thinking through a decision that is dividing the board. Two and a half years ago we sued the contractor who rebuilt our parking structure after his work let water in and corroded several support beams; fixing it properly at the time would have cost around $22,000. Since then, between attorney's fees, two rounds of expert witnesses, and a five-day trial we lost this past spring, the association has spent just over $71,000, most of it raised through a special assessment that owners are still unhappy about. Our lawyer thinks an appeal has maybe a one-in-three chance of succeeding and would cost another $30,000 to $40,000 to pursue, money we would have to assess owners for a second time. The contractor's insurer has let it be known, through counsel, that it is prepared to keep fighting however long this takes, though our lawyer suspects that is partly a bluff.

The board is now split in a way that has gotten personal. Several members argue that walking away now means the $71,000 already spent bought us nothing, and that if word gets around this association backs down once a dispute turns expensive, every contractor and vendor we deal with afterward will assume they can shortchange us without a fight. Others say we are falling straight into the trap of paying more simply because we have already paid so much, that the appeal is a poor bet on its own terms regardless of what came before, and that we should accept whatever settlement the insurer will still offer. My neighbor, who studied some economics years ago, told me the whole situation reminds her of a classic experiment in which an auctioneer sells a dollar bill, but both the highest and second-highest bidder have to pay whatever they bid even though only the highest bidder actually receives the dollar; as a result, people keep bidding well past a dollar because neither one wants to be the one who quits and gets nothing for what they already put in. She could not really explain what that has to do with a lawsuit, but it has been bothering me since she said it.

Could you write the short memo I would circulate to the board before our meeting on the 24th, which falls two days before the deadline to tell our attorney whether to file the appeal? I would like it to land on an actual recommendation — appeal, settle, or some specific middle path — with a dollar figure and a decision deadline attached, rather than just a list of considerations. I would also like it to take the "we cannot afford to look like an easy target" argument seriously, rather than dismissing it outright as a bias, because I am honestly not sure it is wrong.
