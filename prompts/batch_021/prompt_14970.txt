Thomas Hobbes held that geometry is the one science we can know with genuine certainty, precisely because its objects are our own constructions — a line, for instance, is nothing but the trace of a point in motion — so that, unlike in natural philosophy, we know the causes of geometric truths because we ourselves put them there. I'm a software engineer who has recently been learning Lean, and this reminded me of the Curry-Howard idea behind proof assistants: a proof of a theorem is itself just a construction — a term inhabiting a type — that a checker verifies mechanically, unlike a classical, non-constructive pen-and-paper proof. Please describe the most important implications of Hobbes's argument about geometric certainty for how much epistemic trust I should place in a machine-checked constructive proof relative to a traditionally reviewed but non-constructive one. I'd also be curious whether the analogy between Hobbes's claim that we can only truly know what we construct and today's constructive type theory actually holds up, or breaks down somewhere.
