I cover science and ideas for a living, and I'm currently reporting a feature on the stranger corners of twentieth-century mathematical logic. Nothing in my notes has needled me quite as much as Skolem's paradox. The mechanics are simple enough to state: if the usual axioms of set theory (ZFC) are consistent, the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem guarantees that they have a model whose domain is merely countable, yet that very same theory proves, via Cantor's theorem, that the power set of the natural numbers is uncountable. A set theorist I interviewed for the piece waved this away as just a fact about the non-absoluteness of "countability," but when I went back and read Skolem's own writing on the subject, from his original 1922 paper, he seemed to draw a far less comfortable conclusion than my source did — treating the paradox as evidence that notions like "set" and "uncountable" cannot be pinned down in any absolute sense by an axiom system at all, a kind of relativity built into the very foundations of mathematics.

That detour eventually led me to Hilary Putnam's model-theoretic argument against metaphysical realism, laid out most fully in "Reason, Truth and History" and the earlier paper "Models and Reality," which more than one commentator has described as a direct descendant of Skolem's worry. Putnam, too, leans on the fact that a single, even ideally complete theory can be satisfied by more than one model to argue that no body of theory or evidence could ever single out one intended interpretation of its own terms — and he uses this to attack the very idea of a mind-independent fact of the matter about reference and truth. I'd like your help working out how much weight that lineage can really bear. Could you compare Skolem's own reaction to his paradox with Putnam's later argument: how much of Putnam's anti-realist conclusion is already implicit in Skolem's relativism about set theory, and where, if anywhere, does Putnam need extra philosophical assumptions that Skolem's own reasoning never required? I would also value your judgment on which of the two conclusions is harder to resist, and why.
