I'm about a third of the way into a novel about a woman in her thirties who quits her soul-crushing corporate job to become a full-time "content creator" around pottery, which used to be just the thing she did in her garage to unwind after work. Now every bowl she throws has to be filmed, captioned and turned into a reel, and shes started using one of those gamified habit-tracker apps that gives her streaks and little badges for "creative output" so she doesnt fall behind her posting schedule. While I was drafting her crisis-of-meaning scene I went down a rabbit hole and reread Moritz Schlick's old essay on the meaning of life, the one where he argues meaningful activity is basically play, stuff done entirely for its own sake with no eye on some further payoff, and that most of adult existence is cursed by exactly the opposite: everything done as a means to some further end, on and on, so we never actually just inhabit the activity itself the way a kid does when they're absorbed in a game.

Obviously Schlick never saw an iPhone, let alone a habit-tracker with dopamine-optimised streak counters or an economy where people monetize their hobbies for algorithm approval. But given how carefully he laid out what separates genuine play from mere instrumental grinding, I'm curious what you reckon he'd actually say about my character's situation, and about gamified productivity apps more generally. Would he say the streaks and badges are just a cynical costume dressing up means-end drudgery in the outward look of play, without it ever becoming play in his actual sense? Or is there a way, on his own terms, that throwing pots could stay genuine play even once part of the motive is rent money and follower counts? I want her arc to resolve into something philosophically honest rather than a generic "she logs off and finds herself" ending, so I'd love your actual read on how his specific criteria would cash out here, not just a general take on whether social media is corrosive.
