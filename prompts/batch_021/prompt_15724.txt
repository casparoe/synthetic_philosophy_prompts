I spent thirty-odd years as a condensed-matter physicist, and since retiring I have joined a small philosophy-of-science reading group that meets monthly at the local library. We have spent the last couple of sessions on reduction and emergence, using phase transitions and the renormalization group as the test case, and last week two of the regulars — Elena, who trained originally in physics before moving into philosophy of science, and Marcus, who is still an active theoretical chemist — got into a running argument over coffee afterward that I have not been able to settle in my own mind. I would appreciate a more philosophically trained perspective on it.

Elena opened by claiming that phase transitions are about the best evidence physics offers for genuine emergence: a transition, in the strict sense of a non-analyticity in the free energy, only appears once one takes the thermodynamic limit and lets the number of particles go to infinity, and since no actual magnet or kettle of water contains infinitely many particles, the sharp transition belongs to an idealized system rather than to any real, finite one, so it cannot simply be read off the microphysics of the systems we actually study. Marcus replied that this mistakes a mathematical idealization for a metaphysical discovery: every finite system's partition function is perfectly analytic, and as the particle number grows the curve merely bends more and more sharply, in a way wholly fixed by the microscopic Hamiltonian, so the limit adds nothing beyond convenience — physicists take such limits throughout the discipline purely for tractability, without concluding that some new layer of reality switches on at infinite N. Elena came back by invoking renormalization itself: what makes critical phenomena striking, she said, is that wildly different microscopic systems — a lattice of interacting spins (the Ising model, say) and a fluid near its liquid-gas critical point — flow, under the renormalization group, to the very same fixed point and share exactly the same critical exponents, and if that shared behaviour can be explained without the microscopic details mattering, then the microphysics has stopped doing the explanatory work, which is all she thinks emergentists have ever meant by an autonomous level. Marcus's rejoinder was that explanatory irrelevance of certain details is not the same as ontological independence: good reductive explanations are precisely in the business of showing which microscopic details do not matter to a given outcome, and the renormalization group spells out exactly which details wash out and why, in fully microphysical terms, so if anything this vindicates reduction rather than undermining it.

They went on like this for a good twenty minutes without either one giving ground, and I found that I could follow every technical step while still having no idea who was actually winning the underlying philosophical argument. Could you go through the exchange move by move, saying who has the better of it at each stage rather than just who wins overall, and flag anything you think both of them are simply taking for granted about what a thermodynamic-limit idealization would have to show in order to count as genuine metaphysical emergence, rather than a mere mathematical convenience?
