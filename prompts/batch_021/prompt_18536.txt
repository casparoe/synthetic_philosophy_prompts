At my school's ethics bowl practice, two of my teammates, Maya and Jonah, got into a long argument about whether genuine moral dilemmas -- cases where every option leaves you doing something wrong -- are actually possible. I've been turning it over since and would like an outside read on who had the better of it at each stage.

(1) Maya argued that dilemmas are always resolvable in principle: given a fully consistent set of values with the right priority ranking, there is always one uniquely correct action, so feeling torn just means you have not finished ranking the relevant considerations.

(2) Jonah pushed back with a case of his own: he had independently promised two different friends, weeks apart, that he would be the one driving them to their college interviews, which turned out to be scheduled at the same time in different cities. He said no amount of ranking breaks that tie, because the promises are equally weighty, so the conflict is in the situation itself and not in his ignorance of some hidden priority.

(3) Maya answered that equal weight just makes it a tie rather than a dilemma: a tie is a case where either option is permissible, the way it does not matter which of two identical parking spots you pull into, whereas a genuine dilemma requires that both options remain forbidden, which an ordinary tie does not give you.

(4) Jonah replied that this ignores what happens afterward: with the parking spots, you owe nobody anything once you have chosen, but whichever friend he lets down, he still thinks he owes that friend a real apology and some way of making it up to them -- and that leftover obligation is exactly what shows both "oughts" survived the choice, rather than one simply disappearing once he acted.

(5) Maya's response was that this proves too much: if we take that leftover guilt at face value, we are saying Jonah was obligated to do both things at once, which by ordinary logic means he was obligated to do their conjunction -- but he could not possibly do both, so either that "ought" claim was never quite right to begin with, or we have to abandon the very natural idea that you cannot be obligated to do the impossible. She said she was not willing to give up the second thing just to preserve the drama of the first.

Could you go through the exchange move by move, say who has the better argument at each step rather than just declaring an overall winner, and flag anything important you think both of them are missing?
