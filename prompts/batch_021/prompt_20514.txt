I came across Eliezer Yudkowsky's 'torture vs. dust specks' dilemma online -- would you rather one person be tortured for fifty years, or an incomprehensibly vast number of people (he uses 3^^^3, in Knuth's up-arrow notation) each get a single dust speck in their eye? -- and I want to use it as the topic of a paper for my ethics elective. My teacher won't accept anything that just rehearses 'utilitarians add it up, deontologists say some things can't be traded off,' so the essay needs to grapple with a specific complication from the academic literature on aggregation.

Alastair Norcross defends aggregation against contractualists like T. M. Scanlon by building a spectrum of intermediate harms running from a headache to death, and arguing that comparing only the adjacent cases on that spectrum commits us -- on pain of an arbitrary cutoff -- to accepting that enough headaches (or dust specks) outweigh a death (or fifty years of torture). Larry Temkin argues that this same kind of spectrum reasoning, combined with the equally intuitive claim that no amount of trivial harm is ever as bad as torture, generates cycles that violate the transitivity of 'all things considered worse than.'

Write the essay. Take a definite position on torture vs. dust specks -- 'it depends on the numbers' only counts if you specify what it depends on -- and state explicitly what your verdict commits you to giving up: transitivity, one of the spectrum argument's premises, or strict lexical priority for severe harms.
