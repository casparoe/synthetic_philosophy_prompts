I sit on the small grants committee of a nonprofit that funds early-career artists, and we are weighing a new fellowship design: the full $40,000 award is paid up front, the moment a finalist tells us in an interview that they genuinely intend to spend the coming year on the one ambitious, risky project they pitched rather than several safer, more presentable ones. There are no check-ins during the year and no clawback if they quietly drift toward safer work instead -- most of the committee likes this because it treats finalists like adults and fits our trust-based mission. But years ago I read about a philosopher's puzzle involving a toxin: a stranger pays you generously just for genuinely intending, tonight, to drink a foul but harmless liquid tomorrow, and you keep the money whether or not you go through with it, since by tomorrow there is nothing left to gain from actually drinking it. Our fellowship seems to have exactly that shape -- once the money has cleared, the fellow has no more reason to push through the hard project than to drift toward safer work -- so I am no longer sure what the interview is really testing, or whether a clear-eyed applicant could honestly pass it. Please draft the memo I will bring to our April board meeting: a recommendation on whether to adopt this design, revise it, or drop it, with reasoning that could change the mind of a committee member who thinks I am overthinking a straightforward act of good faith.
