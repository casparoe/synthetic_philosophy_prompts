A friend and I spent much of last night arguing about so-called "hard choices": cases like deciding between two career paths, where neither option seems better than the other, worse than it, or truly its equal. My friend defended Joseph Raz's view that such values are genuinely incommensurable, so that reason simply runs out and the choice is left to the agent's will. I argued instead for Ruth Chang's proposal that the options are "on a par": related by a fourth, sui generis value relation, distinct from being better, worse, or equal, that still amounts to a real form of comparability rather than the absence of one. My main reason was that a hard choice doesn't feel arbitrary the way a comparison between two utterly incommensurable things would -- say, whether the number seven is better, worse, or equal to a particular shade of blue -- since we can cite real considerations for and against each career, which suggests the options are being weighed against each other rather than simply left uncompared. My friend wasn't moved, insisting that the very failure to rank the options already shows there is nothing more to say. We never resolved it. Please compare Chang's parity thesis with Raz's incommensurability thesis as accounts of hard choices, being precise about what each view is actually committed to and exactly where they diverge -- it is not enough to note that both simply deny the options are better, worse, or equal. Then say which of the two offers the more defensible account of what is going on when we agonize over a hard choice, and justify your verdict.
