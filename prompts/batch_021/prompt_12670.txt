I'm drafting a column on the limits of Bayesian rationality and want to pressure-test a claim I keep encountering in decision-theory and rationalist circles: that I. J. Good's 1967 proof that the expected value of free information is never negative settles, once and for all, whether a rational agent should always be willing to gather more evidence before deciding. Skeptics point to Hirshleifer's finding that publicly disclosing information can unravel mutually beneficial insurance and risk-sharing arrangements, to strategic settings in which credible ignorance itself carries bargaining value, and to the vulnerability of merely boundedly rational agents to bias, overload, or paralysis from additional data that an ideal Bayesian would not suffer. Write an essay that states Good's theorem and its key assumptions precisely, explains exactly which assumption each of these purported counterexamples violates (or shows that none of them actually undermine it), and defends a specific, well-justified verdict on whether "never refuse free, decision-relevant evidence" is a norm that idealized Bayesian reasoners, humans, and AI systems facing high-stakes choices should actually follow.
