I am several drafts into a novel whose second act turns on a fictional game theorist, modeled loosely on Thomas Schelling, who is coaxed out of retirement to advise a set of increasingly frantic negotiations among rival governments and the AI labs whose breakthroughs keep upending the talks. While rereading *The Strategy of Conflict* and *Arms and Influence* to get the character's voice right, I was struck by how directly Schelling's own ideas about credible commitment, the strategic value of tying one's own hands, and what he called "the threat that leaves something to chance" seem to anticipate a worry that today's AI safety researchers have taken to calling the commitment races problem: the concern that as agents — whether they are AIs, the labs that build them, or the states that host those labs — become able to make faster and more verifiable binding commitments, each is pressured to lock in a favorable bargaining position as early as possible, before it has gathered adequate information or coordinated with anyone else, simply because committing first is what wins inside Schelling's own logic of bargaining. An AI-safety researcher friend, who first explained the idea to me over dinner, put the danger this way: if this dynamic plays out at machine speed, among parties that cannot fully verify one another's reasoning, the result will not be the kind of stable deterrence Schelling described in his Cold War writing, but a scramble of premature, badly calibrated threats that no party can safely retract.

Schelling died in 2016, well before this framing entered AI safety discourse, so I would like your help thinking through, in enough depth that I can build real dialogue out of it, how he might plausibly have responded. Would he have recognized the commitment races problem as a faithful, if unsettling, extrapolation of his own account of why commitment confers bargaining power, or would he have resisted it using distinctions he cared about a great deal in his Cold War work, such as the roles of delay, communication, and mutual verification in keeping brinkmanship from tipping into catastrophe? I am especially curious whether you think he would locate the new danger specifically in the speed and opacity of machine commitments rather than in the underlying logic of commitment itself, and what kind of safeguard, in the spirit of the hotlines and verification regimes he once wrote about, he might have proposed for bargaining among AIs, or among the institutions that build and deploy them.
