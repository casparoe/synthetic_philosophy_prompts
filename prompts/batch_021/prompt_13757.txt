Two friends from my old ethics reading group got into this argument at a barbecue and it's been bugging me since. The setup they were arguing over: a rescue crew can reach exactly one of two flooded areas before the road washes out, not both -- one has a single person trapped on a roof, the other has five people trapped together a mile downstream, and there's no way to split resources or come back for the second group in time. Leah said the choice is obvious: you go for the five, because five people dying is a worse outcome than one person dying, full stop, and treating the numbers as morally irrelevant here is just squeamishness dressed up as principle. Sam pushed back with an argument he'd picked up from the philosopher John Taurek: he said there's no single person who actually experiences "the loss of five" -- each of the five, taken seperately, stands to lose exactly what the lone guy on the roof stands to lose, one life and no more -- so adding up their losses into one bigger number is an extra move that needs its own defense, not something you get automatically just because more bodies happen to be on that side of the river. He said that absent any other difference between the two groups, the fair thing is to flip a coin rather than let headcount silently decide who the crew goes for.

Leah's comeback was that this can't be the right principle, because Sam surely wouldn't flip a coin between saving that one guy and saving fifty people, or a hundred -- and if numbers do matter at that scale, he owes her a reason why five doesn't already count, instead of biting the bullet on the small case and dodging the big one. She also pointed out that triage protocols and other real emergency policies weigh headcounts all the time, so it'd be strange if morality specifically banned that logic in the one dramatic case. Sam admitted the huge-numbers version is the hardest case for him, but said her hundred-person example doesn't actually rescue the aggregating move -- it just runs the same intuition pump at a bigger size, and our gut reactions to extreme, cinematic scenarios might not be a reliable guide to what's actually true. He added that there might be totally separate, non-aggregative reasons for caring about huge disparities (something about what everyone could reasonably agree to beforehand) without ever conceding that five lives simply outweigh one the way Leah wants. Neither of them budged, and I don't know this literature well enough to referee it myself. Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who's got the better of the argument at each stage, not just who wins overall, and flag anything you think both of them are missing?
