I'm a retired physicist who's only gotten seriously into philosophy of math the last couple years, so bear with me here. I've been reading about Hilbert, and that famous line of his from 1930, "wir mussen wissen, wir werden wissen" -- he genuinely believed every well-posed math problem has a definite answer out there, provable or refutable, no exceptions, no "ignorabimus." He lived long enough to see Godel's incompleteness results land in 1931 and by most accounts it rattled him, but he died in '43, two decades before Cohen's forcing proof showed that his own problem #1 from the 1900 list, the continuum hypothesis, is actually independant of the standard axioms (ZFC) -- can't be proved or disproved either way, not just "too hard for now." What do you think Hilbert would've made of that particular result if he'd lived to see it? Would he have bitten the bullet and said CH just has no determinate truth value, or insisted there's still a fact of the matter and we just haven't found the right new axioms yet, kind of like what some set theorists still argue today?
