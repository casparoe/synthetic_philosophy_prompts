Ok so this is a weird lead-in but bear with me. My dad coded during a minor procedure last month, he was gone for something like three or four minutes before they got his heart going again. Hes totally fine physically now, but he keeps bringing up this thing he "saw" while he was out, a warm light, feeling really calm, like he was waiting for something, and it's thrown the whole family a bit, me included. I went down a rabbit hole afterward reading about near-death experiences and landed on A.J. Ayer, the logical positivist who spent basically his whole career arguing that talk of surviving your own bodily death doesn't even really hang together philosophically. Turns out he had a famous one himself in 1988 (choked on some smoked salmon in hospital, heart stopped for a few minutes), wrote up what he experienced -- a weird red light, some kind of officials in charge of space and time who'd apparently botched their job -- and afterward said his conviction that death is just the end of him had only been slightly shaken. He didn't convert to anything, kept insisting he was still an atheist right up until he died the next year.

I have to write something for my philosophy of religion seminar and I want to use this, because that "only slightly" bit has genuinely been bugging me since I read it. On one hand it looks like a really disciplined, well-calibrated reaction from a guy who actually walked the walk. On the other hand, it seems suspiciously convenient that the one person whose whole reputation rested on this stuff barely had to revise anything. I don't want a summary, I basically know the outline already -- what I want is an essay that digs into Ayer's actual reasons for thinking survival of bodily death was philosophically suspect in the first place. I think that reasoning shifts somewhat between his earlier "unverifiable claims about a disembodied soul are meaningless" line and his later stuff about personal identity and what would even have to be true for it to count as the same person surviving, so please don't flatten those into one argument. Then work out, using Ayer's own standards, what an experience like his could and couldn't count as evidence of anything -- and don't let him off easy, factor in the obvious "his brain was oxygen-starved for a few minutes" explanation that his doctor and other critics threw at him afterward. Finally, actually commit to an answer: given all that, was barely budging the philosophically correct move for someone in his position, too little, or honestly too much? I don't want a "there are good points on both sides, who's to say" type ending -- I want an actual verdict and the reasoning behind it.
