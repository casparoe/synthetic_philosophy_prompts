I'm a software engineer, and a coworker who's deep into the rationalist/LessWrong scene dragged me into a multi-hour argument last week about anthropic reasoning. It started from Bostrom's simulation argument, but we ended up stuck on something further upstream: the disagreement between the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) and the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA), and specifically Bostrom's "presumptuous philosopher" thought experiment. As I understand the setup, two cosmological theories are compatible with all the evidence gathered so far but disagree wildly, by many orders of magnitude, about how many observers exist in the universe; physicists are about to run a decisive experiment to settle which theory is correct, when a philosopher interrupts to announce that they can already give the answer from their armchair, since SIA implies that the theory predicting far more observers is thereby far more probable, just given the bare fact that they exist. My coworker treated this as an open-and-shut refutation of SIA — obviously nobody should be able to out-reason an actual experiment that way — and moved straight to SSA as the safe default. That felt too quick to me, especially once he admitted SSA has its own notoriously strange consequence in the Doomsday Argument.

I don't have any formal training in philosophy, so before I side with my coworker, I'd like to see the other side argued as forcefully as possible by someone who actually knows the literature. Could you construct the best case you can that SIA is the correct principle for updating on self-locating evidence, and that the presumptuous philosopher case doesn't actually succeed as an objection to it? I'd rather you not lean mainly on the standard point that SIA conveniently dissolves the Doomsday Argument, since I already understand that trade-off exists — I want an argument that engages directly with what's supposedly wrong with letting the philosopher reason that way. In particular, please address this worry head-on: if we're not willing to let the bare fact of existing tell us which cosmology is true, there presumably has to be some principled distinction between the ordinary, apparently unproblematic uses of self-locating evidence and the apparently absurd use the philosopher is making of it here — so where exactly does that line fall, and does SIA still come out looking correct once it's drawn?
