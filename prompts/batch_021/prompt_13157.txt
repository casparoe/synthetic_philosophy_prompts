Three months ago, a DNA-testing site matched me with a biological twin sister neither of us knew existed; we had been separated as infants and raised by unrelated families on different continents. Beyond the surface-level coincidences that everyone fixates on, what has really stayed with me is how often we converge, entirely independently, on the same answer to decisions that don't have an obviously "correct" solution — how to divide an unexpected inheritance, how hard to push back against a difficult relative — despite never having discussed the matter beforehand.

I mentioned this to a friend from my old graduate program (my own background is in mathematical logic), and she brought up Douglas Hofstadter's old notion of "superrationality" as a name for what seemed to be happening between us. That sent me down a rabbit hole: from there to David Lewis's well-known argument that a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma against a sufficiently similar "twin" opponent has the formal structure of a Newcomb problem — an analogy Lewis himself deployed on behalf of defecting despite the correlation, not on behalf of cooperating — and from there to the more recent literature using Löb's theorem to get computer programs that can inspect one another's source code to cooperate with genuine logical certainty, rather than mere statistical correlation.

What I'd like from you is an argument, not a survey of the debate. Please argue directly for the claim that the sheer statistical correlation between my sister's choices and mine — however striking, and however well-confirmed it becomes with further evidence — could never by itself make it rational for either of us to cooperate in a genuine one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma against the other, and that only something playing the structural role of the mutual, verifiable transparency exploited in the provability-based cooperation results could do that. Please engage head-on with the most natural objection: that this looks like it proves too much, since it seems to rule out relying on someone else's cooperation on the basis of any amount of purely evidential correlation, however overwhelming — which sits uncomfortably close to denying that correlated-but-causally-unconnected events can ever be relevant to rational choice at all. In resolving that tension, please explain why treating my sister's predicted choice as a reason to cooperate would amount to the same kind of illegitimate "managing the news" that causal decision theorists have long levied against evidential decision theory, and say whether anything short of full mutual transparency could still change your verdict.
