A close friend from my rationalist reading group is getting married next month, and both she and her fiancé are the sort of committed effective altruists who have spent a decade taking Peter Singer's drowning-child argument at full strength: they have told plenty of skeptical relatives that a drowning child in a shallow pond a world away has exactly the same claim on you as one drowning at your feet, and that letting proximity or personal history tip the scales is a kind of moral illusion. I have been asked to give the toast, and it strikes me that a wedding vow is about the most extreme act of moral partiality on offer: a lifelong promise to place one particular person's welfare above every stranger's. Roughly half the room will recognize the pond, enjoy a well-aimed jab at the demandingness objection, and may even expect a nod to Bernard Williams's complaint that a husband who needs an impartial principle to permit him to save his drowning wife rather than a stranger has had "one thought too many"; the other half have never heard of Peter Singer and just want a nice wedding toast. Please write me a five-minute toast that uses this tension between the ethics of the pond and the ethics of the vow as its actual engine, and that resolves it warmly in the couple's favor, without either softening Singer's argument into something milder than it really is or letting the toast collapse into a philosophy lecture.
