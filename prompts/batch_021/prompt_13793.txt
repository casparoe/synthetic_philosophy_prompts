Ok so I've been kind of obsessed with Bayesian probability stuff lately (fell into it through a rationalist blog rabbit hole, then started reading random formal epistemology pages), and I keep running into this "Dutch book argument" thing that's presented as basically THE proof that rational people's beliefs have to obey the probability axioms. The idea as I understand it: if your degrees of belief don't add up right (like your confidence in rain plus your confidence in no-rain isn't 1, or whatever), a clever enough bookie can offer you a set of bets that you'd each accept as individually fair, but that together guarantee you loose money no matter what actually happens. And since guaranteed loss is obviously bad, having beliefs like that makes you irrational.

The more I sit with it though, the more cracks I notice. Doesn't the whole setup assume money is worth the same to you no matter how much you've already got (no diminishing returns), which is definitely not how real people value money? And even if I can't be Dutch-booked, all that seems to show is that I'm being consistent, not that my beliefs are remotely sane -- I could be 99.9% confident the earth is flat and still be totally "coherent" as long as everything else lines up with that one weird belief. There's also apparently a fancier version of the argument, about updating your beliefs correctly when new evidence comes in (something to do with "conditionalizing," I think), where if you don't update the right way you're exploitable across time instead of just in a single moment -- but that one feels even shakier to me, like it's already assuming you've committed to some fixed updating rule for the bookie to exploit, which seems to dodge the actual question of whether that's the rule you're rationally required to follow in the first place.

Anyway, I'd love an actual essay on this, not just a summary. Give me the strongest case for why the Dutch book argument(s) really do prove something important about rationality, then the strongest objections (feel free to use the ones above if they actually hold up, plus anything else relevant), and then don't just leave it at "well, there are good points on both sides" -- actually tell me where you land: do these arguments succeed in showing that rational credences must be probabilistically coherent, and does the "updating over time" version hold up as well? Pick a real verdict and defend it.
