"The knowledge norm of assertion, defended by Timothy Williamson, says its proper to assert p only if you know p. Part of the motivation is that sentences like 'it's raining, but i don't know that its raining' sound totally unacceptable to say out loud -- this is a version of Moore's paradox, and its actually a logical contradiction, which is exactly what you'd expect if assertion requires knowledge and not just truth or belief.

The lottery case is the main argument against a justified belief norm. say you have one ticket in a fair 1,000 ticket lottery. you're extremely justified in believing your ticket lost, since the odds are 999/1000, but most people still think its improper to just flatly say 'your ticket lost' before the results are posted -- you'd have to hedge and say 'it probably lost.' since your belief there is justified but could still turn out false, this shows justified belief isnt enough for proper assertion, so you need actual knowledge instead.

as for whether the knowledge norm is really different from the truth norm: i don't think it is, not in any deep way. knowledge requires truth, so anything you know is true, and anything true is something you know (once you rule out weird stuff like lying or misremembering) -- so the two norms are basically going to agree about which assertions are ok in every case, they're just two ways of phrasing the same requirement."
