Over a forty-year career as a physical chemist studying reaction mechanisms, I took it for granted that when a flame touches cotton, the burning that follows is not mere accident or custom, but a necessary consequence of the fire's own nature. Since retiring I have been reading medieval Islamic philosophy, and I have found al-Ghazālī's treatment of exactly this example, in the seventeenth discussion of The Incoherence of the Philosophers, harder to dismiss than I expected: with real logical rigor, he argues that fire is an inanimate thing incapable of genuine agency, that the true cause of the cotton's burning is God, and that the observed conjunction of fire and burning is merely a habitual custom (ʿāda) that God could suspend at any moment without contradiction. Averroes's reply in The Incoherence of the Incoherence — that abolishing necessary connections between causes and effects would abolish the very possibility of demonstrative science, and that al-Ghazālī has mistaken what is merely conceivable for what is really possible in nature — strikes me as forceful, but perhaps too quick. Rather than read another summary of the dispute, I would like to watch the two of them contend with it directly. Could you write a dialogue between al-Ghazālī and Averroes, occasioned by a piece of cotton catching fire in front of them, in which each presses the other on their strongest point and they try to pin down exactly what kind of necessity, if any, is genuinely in dispute? Neither should be granted an easy exit, and Averroes's distinction between logical and natural necessity in particular deserves a substantive answer, not a shrug.
