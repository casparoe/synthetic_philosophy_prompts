I'm a software engineer, and I recently built an active-learning pipeline at work where the standard justification for "always query for more labels when they're free" boils down to something I've since learned is called Good's theorem: a Bayesian decision-maker's expected utility can never go down, and can only go up, from getting a free look at some extra evidence before acting, provided they update on it correctly and aren't otherwise forced into a worse plan. I found that pretty reassuring as a general principle -- until a friend who prices insurance policies for a living pushed back, telling me about something economists call the "Hirshleifer effect," where the arrival of public information can supposedly leave everyone worse off by destroying the market for insuring against the very uncertainty that the information resolves. She was adamant that this is a genuine counterexample to the "more information is always at least weakly good" intuition, and I haven't been able to work out for myself whether she's right, or whether her example and Good's theorem are really just answers to two different questions that only look like they're in tension.

I'd like an essay that settles this for me rather than just listing considerations on both sides. Start by pinning down exactly what Good's theorem does and doesn't guarantee -- in particular, which of its background assumptions (e.g., a single decision-maker facing a fixed decision problem, optimal use of the evidence once received, and a utility function defined only over final outcomes and actions rather than over the process of learning itself) is doing the real work in the proof. Then use that analysis to adjudicate two cases where free information seems to leave a rational party worse off: the Hirshleifer-style case of information destroying an insurance market, and the more personal case of someone who would rather not learn the result of a medical or genetic test ahead of some deadline, because the waiting period itself has value (or disvalue) apart from whatever they'd do with the result. Weigh the strongest version of the claim that these are genuine counterexamples against the strongest defense of the theorem's robustness, and don't hedge at the end -- tell me whether Good's theorem is best read as a fully general vindication of "always seek out more free information," or as a considerably narrower result than people often treat it as, and if it's the latter, state plainly the conditions under which a rational agent should actually prefer to stay ignorant.
