I have spent the last three years working on a historical novel built around a real but minor figure: a young woman who ran courier routes for a resistance network in occupied France and was caught and killed only months before the liberation. Almost everything I know about her comes from two biographical sources that disagree with each other in a way I have found strangely hard to think my way through, and the whole emotional architecture of the novel depends on which one I end up building from.

The earlier source is a memoir-biography written in the 1950s by a former comrade who had clearly adored her; it invents at least a couple of scenes and conversations that cannot have happened exactly as described, gets several dates demonstrably wrong, and is shaped throughout by an evident wish to make her a martyr. And yet, when I check it against everything else I have been able to establish about the network, her recruiters, and the choices she made under pressure, it yields a psychologically coherent person whose recklessness, loyalty, and blind spots all hang together. The other source is a rigorous academic biography published in the last decade, painstakingly footnoted against archival and police records, which corrects the earlier book's errors of fact one by one — but reconstructs, out of those corrected facts, someone colder and more calculating, whose choices look at least as consistent with ambition as with conviction. Neither source is simply right; each is demonstrably false in some of its particulars.

What I cannot work out is whether it even makes sense to ask which of the two is closer to how she actually was. My own instinct is that the first book, for all its invented dialogue and wrong dates, is somehow nearer the truth of the person, while the second is nearer the truth of the record — but I notice I cannot turn that instinct into anything more rigorous than a preference for the more dramatically satisfying account, which is exactly the sort of self-deception I ought to be suspicious of, given my profession. I described the dilemma to a friend from my writing group, who said the answer was obvious: use whichever version makes the better novel. That may well be sound practical advice, but it dodges rather than settles the question actually keeping me up at night, which is whether "closer to the truth" is a coherent way to rank two accounts once both have been shown to be false in places, or whether, once falsity has entered the picture at all, every such ranking collapses into taste wearing the costume of accuracy. Have philosophers actually worked out what it would take for one false account of something to count as nearer the truth than another false account of the same thing, and if so, does that work offer any real traction on a case like mine — or does it instead explain why my sense that one of these two books is "closer" cannot, in the end, be defended?
