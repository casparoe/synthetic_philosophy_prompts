Here's the question (worth 20 points):

"A weather forecaster says 'there is a 30% chance of rain tomorrow.' Explain how a frequentist, a subjectivist, and a propensity theorist would each interpret this claim. Is there a special difficulty facing one of these views that the others avoid, and if so, how might a defender of that view respond?"

And here's the student's answer I've been assigned to grade:

"All three interpretations try to say what it means to attach a number like 0.3 to an event. Frequentism (von Mises, Reichenbach) says the probability of an outcome is the limit of its relative frequency in an indefinitely long series of repetitions of the same experiment or process -- flip a coin a million times and count the fraction of heads. The trouble with 'there is a 30% chance of rain tomorrow' is that tomorrow only happens once, so there is no series of trials to take a frequency over. Von Mises and Reichenbach dealt with this by placing tomorrow inside a reference class of similar cases -- e.g. all days with the same barometric pressure, humidity, and cloud cover on record -- and letting the 30% be the long-run frequency of rain across that class. So frequentism can still make sense of single-case claims, it just has to do it indirectly, by embedding the case in a suitable class.

Subjectivism (Ramsey, de Finetti, Savage) sidesteps this completely, since it just identifies probability with the forecaster's degree of belief, constrained only by coherence -- your credences have to obey the probability axioms on pain of accepting a Dutch book, a set of bets that lose you money for sure. Because credences are attitudes of a single person at a single time, there's no difficulty at all in having a credence about a one-off event like tomorrow's weather; that's exactly the kind of case subjective probability is suited for.

The propensity theory (Popper) treats probability as an objective, physical propensity or tendency of a chance set-up to produce an outcome, a real disposition of the world rather than a fact about a series or a state of mind. Because the propensity belongs to the individual set-up -- tonight's atmospheric conditions -- rather than to any class of cases, single-case propensities were exactly what the theory was built to give us, with no series, hypothetical or actual, required.

So frequentism can handle single cases, but only by finding a reference class, and the choice of class is never fully forced by the data -- this is the reference class problem, and it's part of why many people find subjectivism or propensities more natural for one-off events like the weather."
