So I went down a total rabbit hole a while back reading about Kurt Gödel's proof for the existence of God - the one he apparently didnt want published while he was alive because he was worried people would think he was seriously arguing for God rather than just doing it as a logic exercise. From what I can piece together he defines God as a being that has literally every "positive" property there is, and then argues that if its even possible for a being like that to exist, then it has to necessarily exist, because of some modal logic system called S5 where "possibly necessary" basically collapses into just "necessary." I also read there's a famous problem with this called "modal collapse" - apparently if you just accept his axioms as written, you end up proving that everything true is necessarily true, which sounds like a disaster for the whole idea that things could've turned out differently, though some people have tried to patch the axioms to dodge that.

Anyway this got me thinking about Aquinas, since I vaguely remember from a religion class forever ago that even though the guy obviously believed in God, he wasn't a fan of Anselm's original ontological argument and thought you needed after-the-fact type arguments (the "five ways" thing) instead of pure armchair logic to get you there. I honestly don't remember the actual reasoning behind why he rejected it, just that he did and went a totally different route.

So here's what I'm curious about: if you somehow got Aquinas to sit down and actually look at Gödel's version specifically, what do you think his honest verdict would be? Would his old objections to Anselm still land on this fancier modal logic version, or does Gödel's whole setup (the positive properties thing, treating necessary existence as one of these positive properties, etc.) dodge around them and force Aquinas to come up with a totally different angle of attack? I don't want the generic "here's Anselm, here's Kant's objection" rundown, I've read that a bunch of times already - I want you to really dig into Gödel's specific machinery and how Aquinas's actual reasoning would map onto it (or fail to), and land on a real answer instead of just listing possibilities.
