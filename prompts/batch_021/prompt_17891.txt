Somewhat incongruously, given that my own research is in philosophy of mind, I have spent this semester serving on my university's committee for recognizing new student organizations—ordinarily a duty that amounts to approving budgets for chess clubs and intramural leagues. This term, however, we received a charter application from a group describing itself as a peer-support and discussion network for students in consensual non-monogamous relationships (open relationships, polyamory, and similar arrangements). Nothing about the group's proposed activities raises any conduct-code concern: its events would be off campus, no minors are involved, and no one has come forward claiming to have been pressured or hurt by anything the group does or plans to do.

Two members of the committee nonetheless want to deny the charter, and it's their reasoning I can't quite get a grip on. Their claim isn't that the club would harm anyone. It's that formally recognizing it—with the funding, room bookings, and institutional standing that recognition confers—would put the university's name behind a way of living that a good many of our students, alumni, and donors consider degrading, and that this damages something real about the kind of community the university is. As one of them put it, more or less: "we don't need a victim for there to be a wrong here, since part of what makes a community a community is that it stands for something, and standing for something means declining to back everything." (He also mentioned, almost in passing, that a donor had pulled a pledged gift over a similar dispute at a peer institution, which seems to me like a genuinely different sort of consideration, though it arrived in the same breath as the first.) The rest of the committee responds that as long as the students involved are consenting adults and nobody else is affected, whether their private arrangements strike other people as immoral is simply not the university's business.

I am the deciding vote, and, to my own mild embarrassment, I don't have a crisp way of adjudicating between these two positions: every time I try to explain why an appeal to the institution's "moral character" should or shouldn't carry weight here, I either end up assuming what I most need to establish or just restating an intuition I can't fully defend. The whole dispute has the feel of a debate that must have been conducted somewhere before in a more rigorous form, though I can't place it, and I'd rather reason it through on its merits than go hunting for the reference. So: is there a principled basis on which a community—academic or political—can claim standing to withhold its recognition or resources from a practice simply because the practice offends widely and sincerely held moral sentiment, even granting that no identifiable person is harmed by it? Or is the appeal to the institution's moral character ultimately just collective disapproval dressed up in more respectable-sounding language?
