"Anscombe's objection to Oxford giving Truman the degree was that the atomic bombs killed such enormous numbers of civilians that the deaths were wildly out of proportion to whatever military benefit came from forcing Japan's surrender, so she brands the decision murder rather than a legitimate, if harsh, act of war. That puts her complaint in the same basic family as standard just-war worries about proportionality, rather than a simple utilitarian weighing of lives lost against lives saved by ending the war sooner.

She backs this up with the distinction, central to the doctrine of double effect, between what an agent intends and what an agent merely foresees. If a commander bombs a legitimate military target knowing some civilians will die as a side effect, those deaths are foreseen but not intended, and can be permissible if proportionate to the military good achieved. But Anscombe argues the atomic bombs were aimed at whole civilian populations directly, as the very mechanism by which Japan would be terrorized into surrendering, so the civilian deaths weren't a side effect at all but the intended means to the end. That's what makes it murder on her view, not just a tragic but excusable side effect of otherwise legitimate warfare.

Her argument doesn't depend on her being a pacifist, since she explicitly rejects pacifism elsewhere and thinks killing enemy combatants is permissible in a just war. This is also where she coins her famous term 'consequentialism,' right here in this pamphlet, to name the mistake of thinking any act, however terrible, can be justified as long as the results are good enough."
