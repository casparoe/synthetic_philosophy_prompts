I've recently been reading through the excerpts that circulate from Kurt Gödel's 1961 letters to his mother, Marianne -- written, apparently, in response to her asking him directly whether he believed they would ever see one another again after death. His answer is a fairly careful argument rather than a consoling platitude: if the world is rationally constructed and has genuine meaning, he says, then some kind of afterlife has to exist, since it would make no sense for such a world to endow a being with as wide a field of possibilities for development as a human has, only to extinguish it once barely a thousandth of that potential has been realized. These letters seem to make the rounds every so often in rationalist and transhumanist corners of the internet, presumably because the shape of the argument -- a rationally ordered universe cannot be permitted to waste a mind's accumulated potential -- sounds so close to the case some of those same communities make for cryonics or mind uploading, just with the universe doing the work a preservation company or an emulation project would otherwise have to do.

What complicates this, though, is that Gödel is equally well documented as an opponent of mechanism about the mind. In his 1951 Gibbs lecture he argued that either the human mind outstrips the powers of any finite computing machine, or there exist mathematical problems that are absolutely unsolvable by any mind whatsoever, and by his own later remarks -- recorded by his confidant, the logician Hao Wang -- he clearly leaned toward the first horn, alongside a broadly Leibnizian picture on which minds are simple, non-composite substances rather than the kind of thing a formal or computational description could capture.

Putting these two strands of his thought together: how do you think Gödel himself would have reacted to being told that his 'thousandth of its potential' problem might be solved not by God but by technology -- specifically, by whole-brain emulation (mind uploading), or by cryonic preservation pending future revival? Would a successful upload count, on his own principles, as delivering anything like the reunion his mother was asking about, or would he say it merely preserves or copies the wrong thing -- a functional pattern standing in for whatever it actually is, on his view, that a mind is? And does cryonics, which doesn't presuppose that minds are computational at all, stand in a different relationship to his argument than uploading does? I have a couple of intuitions about how this should go, but they feel too quick, so I'd like to see the reasoning spelled out properly, rather than a one-line verdict.
