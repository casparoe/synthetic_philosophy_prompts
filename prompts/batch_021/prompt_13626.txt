I spent thirty-odd years as a population geneticist, mostly modeling selection in structured plant and insect populations, and it is only since retiring that I have had the leisure to read the philosophy of biology properly rather than picking it up secondhand at conferences. Like most people of my scientific generation I absorbed "The Selfish Gene" as a young researcher and assumed the units-of-selection question had essentially been settled in favor of the gene. Working through Sober and Wilson's "Unto Others" and then Okasha's "Evolution and the Levels of Selection" this past year has disabused me of that: the live debate, as far as I can tell, is no longer "genes versus groups" in Dawkins's sense at all, but a much more technical dispute about what the mathematics of the Price equation actually shows.

As I understand it, once a population is divided into groups, the total change in a trait's frequency under selection can be partitioned, via the Price equation, into a between-group covariance term and a within-group covariance term -- and yet that same total change can apparently be recovered just as well from a purely "individualist" model, in which each organism's fitness is simply a function of its own type and its local group context, with no separate group-level term appearing anywhere. Kerr and Godfrey-Smith's 2002 result on the formal equivalence of individualist and multilevel descriptions of selection in structured populations seems to say exactly this. To someone trained in the natural sciences, where a demonstrated formal equivalence between two descriptions -- a change of coordinates, a gauge transformation -- is normally taken to show that the two descriptions concern one and the same underlying reality, and that arguing over which is "more correct" is an empty exercise, this looks like strong grounds for concluding that there is no observer-independent fact about the level at which selection is acting in such cases: that the individual/group distinction is bookkeeping, not biology. Sober and Wilson resist exactly this move, insisting that any treatment which folds the between-group covariance into an individual-level fitness function commits what they call an averaging fallacy, one that quietly smuggles a real group-level causal effect back in under an individualist label.

I would like you to construct the strongest possible case for the deflationary reading -- that the formal equivalence really does show there is no fact of the matter about which level selection is acting on, full stop -- and I would like it to engage directly with the averaging-fallacy objection rather than talking past it, since simply asserting equivalence without answering that charge would not satisfy anyone who has actually read Sober and Wilson. A former colleague of mine, who regards the entire levels-of-selection literature as a philosophers' relabeling exercise, is curious whether the argument can actually be made to work rather than merely asserted -- and so, rather more open-mindedly, am I.
