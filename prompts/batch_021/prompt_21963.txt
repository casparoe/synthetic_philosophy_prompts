I've been sitting in on a philosophy of mind seminar, and we recently moved past the textbook multiple-realizability argument against mind-brain type-identity theory: the familiar case that pain cannot be identical to any single physical state because it is realized by C-fiber firing in humans, by something else entirely in octopuses, and by something else again in a hypothetical silicon-based system, so mental states must instead be individuated functionally rather than physically. What complicated the picture for me was a couple of papers the professor assigned afterward, arguing that most of the classic examples don't actually establish what functionalists need them to establish. As I understood it (this was from a paper I believe by Bechtel and Mundale, plus a later one by Shapiro, who apparently developed the point into a full book with Polger), the objection turns on two related moves. First, a good deal of the apparent physical diversity behind a single mental-state type is an artifact of comparing a coarsely individuated psychological kind to a finely individuated physical kind; once both descriptions are fixed at the grain that working neuroscientists actually use -- for instance, when they identify a homologous visual system across very different species by its structural and functional role rather than its exact wiring -- much of the physical diversity evaporates. Second, even where two realizers are genuinely physically distinct, Shapiro wants to say this counts as "multiple realization" in the sense that threatens reduction only if the two realizers differ in some way that is causally relevant to how the function gets performed, rather than differing in some incidental respect; his examples tend to involve things like mousetraps or corkscrews built from different materials that trap or uncork in exactly the same way.

I'd like an intuitive, gut-level walkthrough of why these two moves are supposed to be so damaging to the standard argument, rather than a list of necessary and sufficient conditions -- ideally applied to the classic pain example (or another case of your choosing) so I can see concretely how a seemingly clear-cut instance of multiple realizability gets dissolved once the grain is fixed and the causal-relevance test is applied. I'd also appreciate your sense of how much functionalism actually has riding on this: if clean, non-question-begging cases of multiple realizability among actual biological systems turn out to be much rarer than the textbooks suggest, does functionalism lose the argument that was originally supposed to give it the edge over type-identity theory, or can a defender fall back on merely possible realizers -- silicon-based aliens, artificial systems, and so on -- without simply begging the question against the reductionist in some other way?
