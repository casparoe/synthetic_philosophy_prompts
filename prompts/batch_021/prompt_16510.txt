Every year I train a new graduate teaching assistant to help grade essays for my introduction to philosophy course, which enrolls around two hundred students, and over time I have developed what I thought was a reliable calibration procedure. Before letting a TA grade independently, I have her work through a shared packet of about twenty essays drawn from previous semesters: I show her the grades and comments I originally gave, and we go back and forth until her marks track mine closely across the whole set. This year's TA calibrated unusually well—by our third pass through the packet her grades matched mine almost exactly—so I felt comfortable letting her grade the first two essay assignments largely on her own.

About a month in, spot-checking a batch of her grading, I noticed a troubling pattern. Essays that made a single point sharply and defended it with care were being marked down relative to what I would have given them, while essays that ranged more widely over the material—gesturing at several arguments without quite developing any of them—were scoring noticeably higher than I would have awarded. Going back to the original calibration packet to see what had happened, I realized, to my dismay, that by pure accident every strong essay in it had also been comparatively long, and every weak one comparatively short; nothing in the packet ever separated being well-argued from being merely expansive. My written rubric has always explicitly rewarded precision and penalized padding, and this TA is, if anything, unusually diligent and consistent—she simply turned out to be diligently and consistently applying a different rule from the one I intended.

I mentioned this over lunch to a colleague, who shrugged it off as a case for a clearer rubric or closer supervision, but that does not sit right with me, since the rubric was already explicit on this very point and the TA can recite it back accurately. What strikes me is that nothing about her performance on the calibration packet could have distinguished a TA who had learned to reward precision from one who had learned to reward length, since across that whole packet the two happened to coincide; the difference only became visible once real essays came apart from one another in a way none of the practice cases had. I would value your take on what exactly has gone wrong here, whether it points to some more general lesson about training and testing that I am not quite putting my finger on, and how I ought to redesign the calibration process next year so that this kind of failure surfaces before I hand over the gradebook rather than a month into term.
