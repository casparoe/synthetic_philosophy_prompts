Been mulling something over for a piece on moral psychology and want to see it properly argued out, not just gestured at. There's this puzzle that people who work on "moral testimony" argue about: why does it feel off to just adopt a moral belief wholesale from someone you trust, zero independent thought required — like if my vegan friend tells me factory farming is wrong and I just believe it because she said so — when doing the exact same thing with a doctor's diagnosis or a mechanic's verdict on my car feels totally fine? Same structure, trusted source, no personal verification, but a totally different gut reaction. Apparently the field splits into "optimists," who think moral testimony is epistemically just as good as ordinary factual testimony, and "pessimists," who think something's missing even when the testimony-based belief happens to be true.

Write me an essay that actually works through whether that asymmetry is real or a mirage. Cover the best pessimist arguments (stuff about "moral understanding" vs. just having a true belief, and about moral worth requiring you grasp the reasons yourself) and the best optimist comebacks (that we're just as leery of aesthetic or emotional testimony so its not special to morality, and that deferring can be fine for guiding action even when it doesn't earn you moral credit). Don't hedge at the end — actually tell me whether there's a principled asymmetry between moral testimony and other kinds, and defend it.
