I teach Chinese and comparative philosophy, and I've spent much of my career defending Confucian role ethics: the view, developed by Roger Ames and Henry Rosemont, that a person is not a discrete, autonomous self who merely occupies roles, but is constituted by them -- son, elder sister, teacher, friend. My younger brother is getting married next month and has asked me, half in jest, to give the toast. Mencius's canonical five relationships (3A:4) each carry their own governing virtue -- affection between parent and child, trust between friends -- but husband and wife are marked instead by bie 別, 'distinction': a functional division of labor that became the classical root of the gendered inner/outer (nei/wai) split long criticized by Confucian feminist philosophers. I want the toast to do real role-ethics work -- to locate what's being celebrated in the relation being formed, and in the whole web of relations it reshapes, rather than in two individuals 'finding themselves' in each other -- without turning into a seminar, and without quietly reviving the older, hierarchical answer to what a husband and a wife are each for. About a third of the guests are philosophers who will catch every move; the rest just want a good toast. Please write it so that it runs three to four minutes when read aloud.
