I'm a junior, and this year I was elected the student representative on my high school's Publications Board: two students, two faculty members, and our journalism adviser, who review formal complaints about the school newspaper and can recommend sanctions or policy changes to the principal. We happened to read excerpts from Mill's *On Liberty* in my philosophy elective last month, including the passage about how the very same opinion -- that corn-dealers starve the poor -- is fine to print in a newspaper but can rightly be punished when shouted at an angry mob gathered outside one particular corn-dealer's house, and I can't stop thinking about how directly it bears on a case that's now in front of the Board.

Here's the situation. In October, our opinion section ran a piece, published under the writer's real name, that accurately named the four seniors whose leaked party footage got the whole football program's banquet cancelled by the administration; it was blunt and mocking but contained nothing false or threatening. Within a day, an anonymous meme account with a huge student following had reposted a photo of the article with the four faces circled, and by the end of the week those four were getting flooded with mocking comments and, in one case, a fabricated rumor that had nothing to do with the actual party; one of them has since stopped coming to school events altogether. The same writer has now submitted a follow-up piece for our next issue, similarly worded, naming two more students who "still haven't apologized." Two Board members want to run it as submitted, since every fact in it is true, it concerns a matter of real school-policy interest, and the paper can't be held responsible for how an unaffiliated meme account and its followers chose to behave; the other two think that, given exactly what happened last time, running it now is close enough to knowingly handing a crowd its next target that it can't be waved through as ordinary opinion writing, and that we need an actual policy for cases like this rather than a fresh vote every time.

I have to circulate a short memo before we vote this Thursday, and I'd like your help drafting it. It should give a specific recommendation on the follow-up piece plus a concrete, reusable policy for cases like it going forward -- not just "use good judgment" -- and it should seriously engage the strongest argument on whichever side it doesn't take rather than dismissing it in a sentence. I'd also like it to grapple with something that's been bothering me: Mill wrote the harm principle for a society of adults managing their own affairs, and said outright that it wasn't meant to cover children or people who still need to be taken care of by others. Since both the writer and the students the follow-up piece names are minors the school is responsible for, it doesn't seem right to just borrow Mill's conclusions wholesale and call it a day -- but I'm not sure what follows instead, and that's the part I most need help thinking through.
