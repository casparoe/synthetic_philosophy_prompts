So this semester I'm taking a seminar on value theory where we just spent like three weeks on incommensurability -- Ruth Chang's stuff on "hard choices," the idea that two options can be "on a par" instead of just better, worse, or equal, the small-improvement argument, all of that. Our professor has this whole rant about how averaging two scores across a rubric silently converts a hard choice into a tie, which stuck with me because it's suddenly become weirdly relevant to my actual life: I sit on the committee that awards my university's Founders' Fellowship, a single no-strings $40k grant given every year to one graduating senior for a self-directed year-long project, and we're down to two finalists with the committee properly deadlocked over them. Finalist A is a CS/stats double major proposing a tightly scoped, technically rigorous interpretability project with a clear methodology and a real if modest chance of a genuinely useful result. Finalist B is a comparative lit major who wants to spend the year embeded with an aging refugee community, transcribing oral histories before the last first-generation survivors pass away -- everyone agrees this matters, but in a totally different and much harder to pin down way. Half the committee wants to just run both proposals through the same numerical rubric we use every other year (feasibility, impact, originality, need); the other half says that's a category error dressed up as objectivity, since it quietly assumes there's a common scale for these two things in the first place.

I have to circulate a short memo before Monday's final vote recommending what we actually do -- award to one finalist outright, split the money, defer part of it, whatever -- and why. I don't want it to just gesture at "some choices are hard" and then let the rubric-averaging happen anyway by default, which I think is what will happen if nobody writes something more substantive. Could you draft that memo for me? It should land on one concrete recommendation with reasoning the losing side could actually follow, and it needs to seriously engage the strongest version of the objection that these two proposals just aren't comparable and any rubric forcing them onto one scale is fake precision -- which is honestly the position I find hardest to argue against and suspect is closest to right, even though "they're incomparable, sorry" can't be the whole memo since we still have to actually pick someone.
