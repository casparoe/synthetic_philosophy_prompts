Long before I ever encountered the term in a philosophy course (I have no formal training in the subject), I had already been puzzling on my own over what writers such as Philip Goff and David Chalmers now call the "combination problem" for panpsychism: if the most basic constituents of reality each carry some primitive form of experience, how do countless tiny subjects of experience ever add up into a single unified subject, such as a human mind, rather than remaining a mere crowd of separate experiencers? Reading further back into the history of the idea, I arrived at Leibniz's monads and noticed that he seems to have circled something very like this puzzle centuries earlier: his monads are causally sealed off from one another, having, in his own phrase, "no windows"; a body is, strictly speaking, only an aggregate of monads and not a genuine unity; and an organism's felt unity is supposed to come from a single "dominant" monad that represents the rest of the aggregate rather than being composed out of them. Since the combination problem in its current form was not formulated until long after Leibniz's death, I would like your best reconstruction of how he would have handled it: does his apparatus of pre-established harmony and dominant monads actually amount to a solution, or does it merely relocate the very mystery it is meant to dissolve, namely how one monad's private perspective could count as unifying a multitude with which it has no causal contact? Please argue from Leibniz's own actual commitments rather than offering a generic summary of the Monadology.
