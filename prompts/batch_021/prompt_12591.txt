I'm putting together a seminar on Hume's economic essays for my early modern philosophy course (normally we just stick to the Enquiry and the Treatise, but I'm mixing things up this year), and there's a passage from "Of Money" that I keep chewing on and want a second opinion on before I put it in front of students, since half of them will just nod along even if I get the explanation wrong. It comes partway through the essay, right after he's been talking about how nations where money is "flowing in" seem more industrious than nations where its stagnant. Here's the bit:

"To account, then, for this phenomenon, we must consider, that though the high price of commodities be a necessary consequence of the increase of gold and silver, yet it follows not immediately upon that increase; but some time is required before the money circulates through the whole state, and makes its effect be felt on all ranks of people. At first, no alteration is perceived; by degrees the price rises, first of one commodity, then of another, till the whole at last reaches a just proportion with the new quantity of specie which is in the kingdom. In my opinion, it is only in this interval or intermediate situation, between the acquisition of money and rise of prices, that the increasing quantity of gold and silver is favourable to industry."

Here's what's bugging me. If the benefit to industry only shows up during that in-between "interval," before prices catch up, doesn't that commit Hume to something pretty odd -- that a nation ought to want its money supply perpetually increasing, rather than increased once and then settled at a higher level, since otherwise you eventually fall out of the favourable interval and you're back where you started, just with bigger numbers on everything? And if that's right, how does it sit with the stuff a few essays later in "Of the Balance of Trade," where he's scathing about the mercantilist idea that a country can go on indefinitely accumulating specie, precisely because (on his own account) the trade balance self-corrects and stops any one nation from gaining money forever? I ran this by a colleague in econ and she just said "that's Hume being Hume" and went back to her coffee, so that was not helpful. Can you walk through the passage carefully and tell me either what qualification I'm missing that dissolves the tension, or whether this is a genuine soft spot in his account?
