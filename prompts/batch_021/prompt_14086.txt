I have no formal training in philosophy, but for the past year or so I have spent a good part of my evenings working through the old paradoxes in translation, mainly because I enjoy tracing an idea back to before anyone had the vocabulary we now take for granted. The sorites, or heap, paradox is the one that has really stuck with me, and following it back far enough eventually led me to Chrysippus and the Stoics. From what I can piece together out of secondary discussions of Cicero's Academica and the other surviving testimonia, Chrysippus's advice to the Stoic sage, once the grain-by-grain questioning got difficult enough, was neither to name some exact number of grains as the true boundary of "heap" nor to insist that no such boundary exists at all. It was, instead, simply to stop answering — to withhold assent once a proposition could no longer be judged by what the Stoics called a "kataleptic" impression, one clear enough to compel agreement, even though a real answer was presumably sitting there somewhere all along.

That response has been nagging at me, because it seems to straddle a line that, as far as I understand it, is still very much alive in present-day work on vagueness. On one side there is epistemicism, mainly associated with Timothy Williamson, on which a word like "heap" really does have an exact cutoff fixed by our collective patterns of use — we just can't ever know or state where it falls, because our grip on meaning is too coarse to track a boundary that precise. On the other side there is supervaluationism, on which there is no fact of the matter at the borderline at all: a sentence is true only if it comes out true under every acceptable way of making "heap" more precise, false only if it comes out false under all of them, and otherwise neither — even though something like the law of excluded middle can still be rescued at that "super" level. I would like your actual, committed opinion, not an even-handed summary of both sides, on which camp Chrysippus would end up defending if the entire modern debate, formal apparatus included, were somehow explained to him in full.

Please don't let him off the hook by declaring the question undecidable. His "stop and stay silent" policy grew directly out of a specific Stoic theory of knowledge and assent, not out of a stray intuition about piles of grain, so use that connection to actually argue for where he would come down — or, if you think neither modern label really fits, argue for that and tell me what third position is doing the work instead. I would also be interested, if you think it's warranted, in whether he would see the entire epistemicist/supervaluationist split as resting on a confusion from his own vantage point, rather than as a genuine disagreement worth choosing sides on.
