I'm on my school's Ethics Bowl team, and this year's case packet includes one asking whether drone pilots who operate thousands of miles from any battlefield should be held to the same moral standards as soldiers fighting on the ground. While we were prepping, two of my teammates, Caleb and Nadia, ended up arguing about something that isn't technically part of the case: whether ordinary soldiers on opposite sides of a war are morally equal to one another as long as both sides fight within the rules, a question our coach had us read about through short excerpts from Michael Walzer on one side and Jeff McMahan on the other. Neither of them backed down for the entire car ride home, and since I couldn't settle it myself, I'd like your help going through it properly.

(1) Caleb opened by defending the traditional view: a soldier who obeys the laws governing the conduct of war (jus in bello) -- not targeting civilians, using proportionate force, and so on -- isn't a murderer and has just as much right to kill enemy combatants as anyone fighting on the other side, regardless of whose cause (jus ad bellum) actually turns out to be just, because rank-and-file soldiers are almost never in a position to judge the justice of their own war, and treating both sides as morally equal is precisely what makes any shared rules of war possible in the first place. (2) Nadia pushed back with McMahan's analogy to ordinary self-defense: if someone attacks you without any justification and you shoot back, the attacker doesn't get to describe his return fire as "self-defense" just because you're now shooting at him too -- his original threat was unjustified, so he alone is liable to force, and the same asymmetry, she said, should hold between soldiers fighting for an unjust cause and the soldiers resisting them, whatever the laws of war say for separate, practical reasons. (3) Caleb answered that this saddles an ordinary conscript, lied to by his own government and given no real choice about fighting, with exactly the same liability as a volunteer who signs up knowing full well that his cause is unjust, which collapses a distinction between the leaders who chose the war and the foot soldiers who didn't. (4) Nadia replied that liability to defensive force has never required full culpability, only being a responsible cause of an unjustified threat, so a soldier can still be liable even if he's largely excused from blame for fighting, in the same way you may still permissibly defend yourself against an attacker who's been deceived into attacking you. (5) Caleb's last word was that this proves too much: if merely being a non-culpable causal contributor to an unjust threat is enough for liability, it isn't clear why the same reasoning wouldn't also make civilians who pay war taxes or build the weapons just as liable to attack, and once Nadia's side has to draw a principled line that excludes those civilians but not the ordinary soldier, it is leaning on exactly the kind of contestable judgment call that the traditional view was trying to avoid by treating combatants as equals to begin with.
