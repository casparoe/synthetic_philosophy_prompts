So I've been down a rabbit hole on the situationist critique of virtue ethics (Doris, Harman, all that) for a seminar paper, and every version of the argument I read does the same thing: cite Milgram, cite the "Good Samaritan" study where seminary students in a hurry step over a guy slumped in an alley, cite the dime-in-the-phone-booth helping experiment, and then conclude that there's no such thing as a robust, cross-situationally stable character trait, so Aristotelian virtue ethics is basically resting on folk psychology thats been debunked. My professor mentioned offhand that there's an old rejoinder to this that doesn't even dispute the studies themselves - it just points out that the notoriously weak correlations between "has trait X" and "behaves consistently across situations" that situationists lean on aren't actually special to moral traits like honesty or compassion. Apparently you get roughly the same weak correlations (something like the 0.2-0.4 range - this is the old Mischel "personality coefficient" finding, though don't quote me on the exact number) for pretty much any personality trait psychologists have tried to measure this way, including ones nobody doubts are real, like extraversion.

I want you to actually build that argument out properly rather than just gesture at it like my professor did. Make the case that the situationist inference from "low cross-situational consistency" to "virtue-relevant character traits don't exist" is a classic "proves too much" move and should be rejected, or at least heavily qualified, even once you grant every single experimental finding situationists cite. And don't just stop there - engage with the obvious situationist comeback, which is to bite the bullet and say fine, maybe most personality traits, not just moral ones, are mostly illusory too. Does that comeback actually save the situationist critique of virtue specifically, or does it end up costing situationism more than its worth?
