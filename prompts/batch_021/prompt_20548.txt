I'm a retired geophysicist (spent almost 40 years doing gravity and seismic survey work) and now that I've got time on my hands I've been working through some classic American philosophy with a few old colleagues -- we started an informal reading group over Zoom, mostly to keep our brains from turning to mush in retirement. This month we did William James's Pragmatism lectures, and next month we're supposedly doing some Peirce, but skimming ahead I think I already see the problem: they're both basically saying the same thing about truth, which is that an idea is "true" if believing it works out for you, if it pays off in practice. Coming out of a hard-science background this rubs me the wrong way a bit, since I always took "true" to mean a claim matches the facts whether or not its convenient for anyone to believe it -- so doesn't this pragmatist line just collapse into a kind of relativism, where truth is whatever happens to be useful to *you*, and two people could hold flatly contradictory "truths" as long as each one is getting what they want out of it?

One guy in our group did a philosophy minor decades ago and insists Peirce would've been annoyed to be lumped in with James like that, and said Peirce even coined some awkward new word for his own version specifically to distance himself from where James took it. But when I look at Peirce's stuff about the "final opinion" that a community of investigators would eventually converge on if inquiry went on long enough, it still sounds to me like just a fancier, more long-winded way of saying "true is what works" -- just with a committee added instead of one guy deciding for himself. So am I missing some real, substantive difference between what Peirce and James were each up to with the word "truth," or is all this "convergence" talk just window dressing on the same basically subjectivist idea underneath?
