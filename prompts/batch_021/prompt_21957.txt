I've served on my company's incident review board for about a year now. We're a mid-sized payments company, and the board's job is to sign off on postmortems for anything that breaches an SLA, following the "blameless" philosophy that's standard in the SRE world: focus on systems and processes rather than individuals. Usually this is routine, but a recent incident has split the board down the middle, and I've been asked to bring a decision memo to Friday's meeting, where we're supposed to finalize and publish the postmortem.

Here's the case. At 2:14 a.m., an anomaly detector paged our on-call engineer, Dana, about elevated latency on a partner API. Dana checked the canonical health dashboard for the payment-authorization service: error rate and latency both nominal, everything green. Reasonably concluding this was a false alarm from an overly twitchy detector, she acknowledged the page and stood down without escalating, exactly as the runbook instructs. It later emerged that a bad deploy earlier that day had silently broken the pipeline feeding the dashboard, so what Dana was actually looking at was a frozen snapshot from six hours earlier, not live data. That snapshot happened to show a healthy service, and — for entirely unrelated reasons — the service genuinely was healthy at that exact moment too; the real degradation didn't start until 2:35 a.m., when the partner API failed outright. By then Dana was asleep and no one else was watching, so we absorbed a forty-minute outage that customers flagged before we did. Half the board wants the postmortem to say Dana made a reasonable, defensible call on the information available, with the stale dashboard logged as the sole root cause. The other half argues that her "all clear" wasn't actually connected to the service's real state in any meaningful way — she got the right answer for a reason that had nothing to do with the truth — and wants a mandatory secondary verification step added to the on-call runbook, this incident logged as a contributing judgment factor rather than a pure tooling bug, and a note added to Dana's next review flagging it as a call worth coaching on.

I described the whole mess to a friend who studied philosophy, and she said it sounds exactly like an instance of something called "the Gettier problem" — apparently a long-standing puzzle in epistemology about why justified true belief doesn't automatically add up to knowledge. That's not a phrase I expected to need when I agreed to sit on this board, but it does seem to be the actual crux of what's dividing us. Could you draft the decision memo? It should give a concrete finding on how this incident gets classified in the postmortem, specify any action items with owners and a rough timeframe, and — rather than dodging the disagreement or just cataloguing theories of knowledge — explain, in terms the board can actually act on, why you're drawing the line where you are, engaging honestly with the strongest argument on the side you don't recommend.
