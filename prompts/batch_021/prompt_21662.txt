Okay so this went from a fun philosophy-class memory to an actual thing I have to write policy about. I'm on my fraternity's Standards Board, and two weekends ago at our spring lake formal, one of our own guys got caught in a current near the dock late at night and started actually struggling — not the "haha help me" kind, the real kind. Three brothers were standing right there on the dock and it took a good 20-30 seconds before anyone actually went in after him. When we talked to them afterward, each one basically said some version of "I figured someone else would go in," or admitted he hesitated because there'd been drinking and he didnt want to be the one who "made it a whole thing." The kid is totally fine, just scared and waterlogged, but it very easily could not have been fine, and now the Board has to decide (a) what happens to the three guys who hesitated, and (b) whether we need to actually write a "duty to intervene" rule into our chapter's code of conduct, since right now the only relevant policy we have is a medical-amnesty clause that protects you for calling for help, and it says literally nothing about bystanders who just don't act.

I keep coming back to the Peter Singer drowning-child argument from my intro ethics class — the shallow pond thing, where he says if you can save a kid at the cost of ruining your clothes you're obligated to, and that this is basically what our "duty" to help far-away strangers amounts to too, since distance shouldn't matter morally. What's messing with me is that our case seems like the easy version of his argument (a guy you actually know instead of a stranger's kid, some minor risk instead of just mud), so on paper it should be a slam dunk that they were obligated to act right away. But there's stuff Singer doesn't really deal with: does having two other people also standing there actually lower any one person's individual obligation, or does that diffusion-of-responsibility thing psychologists talk about just describe a bias rather than justify anything? Does it matter at all that nothing bad ended up happening, or are we effectively judging a decision and should ignore how lucky the outcome was? And I vaguely remember googling this after and finding that most US states don't impose any legal duty to rescue at all — I think Vermont's one of the rare exceptions, don't quote me — so is it even reasonable for our internal code to hold members to a stricter standard then the actual law does?

Could you draft the actual memo I need to bring to Wednesday's meeting? It should say clearly what happens to the three brothers (a real recommendation, not "the Board should discuss further") and whether we adopt a formal duty-to-intervene standard going forward, and it needs to seriously engage with the strongest reason not to come down hard on them rather than just brushing past it.
