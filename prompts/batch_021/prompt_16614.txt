I'm a few years into a PhD in philosophy of physics, and a chunk of my cohort runs a biweekly quantum foundations reading group (mostly physics grad students, plus me and one other philosophy person). We'd just finished the "local causality" material from Bell's papers and some of Maudlin's nonlocality book, and afterward two people in the group, Owen (physics) and Nadia (philosophy), ended up arguing for like an hour about what Bell's theorem actually forces on us. I stayed out of it at the time since I honestly found both sides persuasive in the moment, but it's been bugging me since, especially since I'm supposed to be writing a dissertation chapter that takes some kind of stance on this. Roughly reconstructed, it went:

(1) Owen: after the loophole-free experiments, and now that Aspect/Clauser/Zeilinger have a Nobel for it, it's just settled physics that nature is nonlocal -- no local hidden-variable theory can reproduce the correlations we actually measure, full stop.

(2) Nadia: too fast. The derivation doesn't only assume locality, it also assumes "measurement independence," that the hidden state of the particles is statistically independent of whatever setting the experimenter ends up choosing. Deny that instead -- superdeterminism -- and you keep locality and still match the data.

(3) Owen: that's not a real escape hatch, its a way of refusing to play the game. If the "random" setting can be conspiring with the particle's hidden state, then no experiment about anything proves anything, since any inconvenient correlation can always be blamed on a conspiracy between your randomizer and the system you're studying. That's not physics, that's just giving up on the idea that experiments test anything.

(4) Nadia: granted it sounds crazy, but "conspiracy" is doing alot of unearned work in that sentence. In a fully deterministic universe everything shares a causal past, so some correlation between distant things is inevitable; the real question is whether it's cheap, unexplained fine-tuning or something that falls out of simple underlying dynamics. And we already accept one enormous, extremely special correlated boundary condition without much fuss -- the low entropy at the big bang -- because it buys us the entire thermodynamic arrow of time for a low price. Why is that fine and a deterministic account of the settings isn't even worth considering?

(5) Owen: because the entropy case is a generic, one-parameter condition, not a bit-for-bit correlation between one specific quantum-random-number-generator output in a lab and one specific particle pair created maybe light-years, or (in the quasar versions of these experiments) billions of years, away. That's a wildly more specific and targeted correlation than a smooth thermodynamic gradient, and "it's logically possible" isn't the same as "it's a serious physical hypothesis."

(6) Nadia: fair intuition, but not yet an argument -- nobody's actually shown that a simple, lawlike deterministic theory couldn't produce that correlation without anyone "targeting" anything, any more than the second law is secretly targeted. And separately, she said, even granting locality is violated, she's not sure what that even means physically, given that the no-signalling theorem guarantees you can never use an entangled pair to send an actual bit faster than light. So whatever the theorem forces on us might not be the kind of causal "influence" that the word nonlocality suggests it should be.

That's basically where they left it, both a little annoyed. Could you go through the exchange move by move -- (1) through (6) -- and say who has the better of it at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner? Feel free to just number your verdicts to match. And is there some bigger point that both of them are missing which would actually move this forward, or is it genuinely just a case of two live, competing options with no knock-down answer between them?
