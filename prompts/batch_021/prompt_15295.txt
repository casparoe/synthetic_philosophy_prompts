Random context: I'm doing a philosophy MA and this semester's ethics seminar has kind of wrecked my ability to have opinions about anything. We spent two weeks on Kant's claim that an action only has genuine moral worth if its done from duty, and then my professor brought in Nomy Arpaly's stuff on "inverse akrasia," using the Huck Finn case as the go-to example -- Huck helps Jim escape even though his consciously held principles (the ones slavery-era society drummed into him) tell him he's doing something wrong. About half the seminar thinks that case just wrecks the Kantian picture, since Huck obviously isn't acting from any correct maxim about duty, he's just following his gut sympathy for Jim. The other half (me, depending on the day) thinks a sharp enough Kantian can still hold the line, maybe by arguing Huck is implicitly responding to Jim's humanity in a way that counts as respect for the moral law even if he couldn't put it in those words. Honestly it's hit a little close to home too, since I once did something for a friend that felt "wrong" by rules I'd been raised to treat as absolute, and only years later decided I'd actually done the right thing, so I keep flip-flopping on whether that means I get any moral credit for it.

I have to write a term paper on this and keep accidentally strawmanning one side or the other depending on which draft I'm on. Could you write me a dialogue between a committed Kantian and an Arpaly-style theorist arguing about whether (and how much) Huck's action has moral worth?
