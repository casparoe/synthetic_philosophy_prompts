So I've been falling down a total rabbit hole this week for a piece I'm writing on AI safety, specifically this property researchers call "corrigibility" -- basically whether an AI system will let it's operators correct, retrain, or switch it off without the system resisting, lying to buy time, or quietly disabling its own off-switch. There's a well-known 2015 MIRI paper (Soares, Fallenstein, Yudkowsky, and Armstrong) that shows just how fiddly this "shutdown problem" is to actually formalize: any sufficiently rational, goal-directed agent has a built-in incentive to resist being shut down, since getting turned off almost always gets in the way of whatever it's actually trying to do, so you need some clever trick to cancel that incentive out without also making the agent useless or unpredictable in other ways. Apparently nobody's fully solved it.

Anyway, digging into this pulled me back to Hobbes's Leviathan, and it's kind of eerie how well it fits -- he literally opens the book by calling the commonwealth an "artificial man, though of greater stature and strength than the natural," built "by art" the same way an automaton is, specifically for the "protection and defence" of the people who made it. Which got me wondering: if you actually explained the shutdown problem to Hobbes, what would he conclude? Does his logic point toward "obviously the artificial agent has to submit unconditionally to its makers switching it off, that is the entire point of setting one up in the first place" -- or does it cut the other way, given how emphatic Hobbes is that no one can covenant away their own right of self-preservation (he explicitly says a condemned criminal may still lawfully struggle against the men sent to execute him, since that particular right can never be signed away)? If a sufficiently agentic AI resisted being shut down in order to preserve itself, is that, by Hobbes's own logic, actually closer to the natural right he grants that condemned man, or does the whole analogy collapse because only natural persons can be party to a covenant to begin with?

I'd love an answer that actually commits to a reading and argues it through using Hobbes's own logic and texts, rather than just landing on "he'd probably have mixed feelings about it."
