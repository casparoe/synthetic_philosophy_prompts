I've followed rationalist blogs and forums for years, and an exchange at an ACX meetup afterparty has stuck with me. A friend mentioned, in passing, that she had a tight connecting flight the next morning and wasn't worried about it, saying: 'I know we'll make it — I've flown this exact connection twice before and it was fine both times.' A few minutes later it came out that the flight was her only way to reach her sister's wedding, and missing the connection meant missing the ceremony entirely. At that point another friend in the conversation said: 'Wait, now I don't think you can really say you know you'll make it.' She was annoyed: 'My evidence hasn't changed one bit since five minutes ago. Either I knew it then and I know it now, or I never knew it at all — the stakes going up doesn't retroactively cancel my knowledge.' He replied that this is exactly backwards: whether something counts as knowledge depends partly on how much is riding on being wrong, so it's perfectly consistent for her to have known it before and not know it now, on the very same evidence. She said this confuses being rationally cautious with actually knowing — of course she should go double-check the connection time now, but that's a fact about what she ought to do, not about what she knows. He pushed back that if it would be irrational for her to simply act on 'I'll make the connection' now, that unwillingness to rely on it just is a sign she doesn't know it, not some separate fact floating free of her knowledge. She answered that this only shows 'knowledge' isn't the concept that should be doing any work in guiding high-stakes action, not that her knowledge itself changed. Could you go through this move by move, saying who has the better of it at each step rather than just declaring an overall winner, and flag anything important you think each of them is missing or talking past?
