I'm a backend engineer by trade, but a few years ago I built a small position-sizing calculator for a friend who bets on sports, and reading up on the Kelly criterion for that project pulled me into a much more contested question: whether maximizing the long-run geometric growth rate of your bankroll is just one utility function among many (log utility, essentially) or whether it's actually the objectively correct thing to do once you take seriously that wealth compounds multiplicatively instead of adding up. The second position is sometimes called "geometric rationality," and its defenders have built it into a broader research program, often filed under "ergodicity economics," arguing that mainstream expected-utility theory has been quietly conflating an average taken over imagined parallel copies of an agent with the average that agent would actually experience living through one real sequence of bets over time.

A few of us engineers run a small podcast where we interview people doing interesting work at the intersection of math, economics, and philosophy, and next month's guest is an economist who has built her career defending geometric rationality along exactly these lines. Judging by her published work, she has a favorite move for shutting down objections: whenever someone raises a risk-aversion or finite-horizon worry, she says they're still "thinking like a spectator averaging over parallel worlds instead of like the one person who actually has to live through the single sequence," and treats that as more or less settling the matter. I'd like a sequence of eight to ten questions, each building on the last, that make that move progressively harder for her to fall back on. Somewhere in the sequence, please include at least one question pressing the Samuelson-style objection that maximizing expected log wealth is just expected-utility maximization for one particular utility function (log, equivalently a relative risk aversion of exactly one), and that framing it as a discovery about the dynamics of wealth rather than a statement of risk preference doesn't actually change that; at least one on the gap between full Kelly being the uniquely growth-optimal strategy in theory and the widespread habit among professional Kelly bettors and fund managers of wagering a half- or quarter-Kelly fraction instead; and at least one on whether a result that only holds "almost surely" over infinitely many repetitions is really action-guiding for a bettor who, like everyone, faces a finite and possibly quite short run. For each of those three, add a short, pointed follow-up question keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, rather than a generic "how would you respond to that." Close the sequence with a question asking what observation or thought experiment would actually count as evidence against treating time-average growth as the uniquely rational criterion for these decisions, rather than merely one defensible criterion among several.
