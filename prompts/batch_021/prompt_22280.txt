I've been working through Alan Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" for a philosophy of AI course, in particular the section where he addresses objections to the imitation game one after another. For most of these — the theological objection, the argument from consciousness, Lady Lovelace's objection — the pattern is consistent: he states the objection, then spends a paragraph or so showing that it doesn't really threaten the test. I had assumed the same was true of the objection from extrasensory perception: that Turing raises telepathy only to reassure the reader that it isn't a genuine complication, presumably because he, like most scientifically minded people, regarded the evidence for it as negligible, and that the "telepathy-proof room" he proposes as a remedy is a throwaway gesture toward thoroughness rather than something he thought was actually necessary.

I've also come across a couple of popular accounts suggesting that Turing took the case for ESP surprisingly seriously, but this struck me as an exaggerated reading of what is presumably just a rhetorical aside — the sort of thing people say about a famous scientist to make him sound eccentric. Could you clarify what Turing is actually doing in that section? I'm especially interested in whether he is in fact arguing that the evidence for ESP is weak, and whether his treatment of this objection follows the same argumentative pattern as the others in that part of the paper. I'd also welcome your take on what, if anything, his handling of this particular objection reveals about the sort of evidence the imitation game is, or isn't, meant to be sensitive to.
