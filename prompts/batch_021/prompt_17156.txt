I've spent the last few weeks leading a graduate reading group through Book 1 and the early part of Book 2 of Plato's Laws, and the material has unsettled what I thought I knew about Plato's attitude toward pleasure and self-control. Readers who come to this text only after the Republic and the Symposium tend to expect a philosopher deeply wary of wine and its effects on the soul; instead, the Athenian Stranger spends a surprising amount of energy defending state-supervised communal drinking -- presided over by a sober symposiarch, with access to wine graduated by age (none at all before eighteen, since that would be pouring fire on fire; strict moderation for younger men; and real license only for those past forty) -- against the objections of his interlocutors Megillus the Spartan and Cleinias the Cretan, whose own cities simply forbid the practice outright. The Stranger's central move is an analogy with courage: just as a soldier's fortitude cannot be verified, or trained, except by exposing him to genuine fear, a citizen's self-mastery (sōphrosynē) cannot be verified or trained except by exposing him, under controlled conditions, to something that genuinely loosens self-command. Wine, and the sanctioned "second childhood" it grants to older citizens -- a gift of Dionysus meant to soften the crabbedness of age and coax older citizens back into singing and dancing, in the service of choral education -- is offered as exactly this kind of diagnostic and pedagogical device.

My reading group ended up split down the middle on whether Megillus and Cleinias or the Athenian Stranger have the better of the argument, and I would like a sharper version of the exchange than we managed to produce ourselves. Could you write a substantial dialogue between two equally well-prepared interlocutors: one who defends, in the spirit of Megillus and Cleinias, strict prohibition (or at least severe restriction) of communal wine-drinking, on the grounds that intoxication cannot be safely "tested" without also habituating, and sometimes damaging, the very faculty it purports to test; and one who defends, in the spirit of the Athenian Stranger, the regulated symposium as an indispensable instrument of civic and moral education. Each side should take up the other's strongest point directly -- the habituation worry, the analogy with courage-training, the claims about aging and choral performance, and the appeal to actual Spartan and Cretan practice. In the text itself, the Stranger is allowed to carry the argument fairly easily, and Megillus's doubts are never pressed as hard as I suspect they could be; I would like this dialogue to correct for that imbalance, ending with real argumentative pressure on both sides rather than a tidy victory for supervised drinking.
