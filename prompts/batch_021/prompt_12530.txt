I write a science-and-ideas column that regularly dips into philosophy of mind, and next month I'm recording a long interview with a philosopher whose reputation rests on defending functionalism through the multiple realizability argument -- the claim that what makes something a pain, a belief, or a desire is the causal role it plays rather than the stuff it's made of, so that a human, an octopus, and (hypothetically) a well-engineered robot could all be in "the same" mental state despite having nothing in common physically. Since this is meant to be a substantive conversation rather than a primer, I'd like to skip past the standard Putnam-era setup and get her engaging with the objections that have actually given the view trouble in the decades since, several of which she's no doubt been asked before and has polished answers for.

In particular, I'd like at least one question built around the Bechtel-and-Mundale-style challenge from comparative neuroscience: that once you look closely at how brain structures are actually individuated across species, there turns out to be a lot less multiple realizability in nature than the old thought experiments assumed, which would undercut her central piece of evidence rather than merely complicate it. I'd like one on Jaegwon Kim's argument that multiple realizability, far from blocking reduction, actually licenses a set of tidy local reductions -- pain-in-humans to human physiology, pain-in-octopuses to octopus physiology, and so on -- leaving the broader kind "pain" looking scientifically idle or merely disjunctive. And I'd like one that turns Ned Block's own liberalism-versus-chauvinism dilemma back on her: multiple realizability was supposed to rescue functionalism from the chauvinism of type-identity theory, but doesn't the same move commit her to counting things like his Chinese Nation thought experiment, or a sufficiently well-organized bureaucracy, as genuinely minded -- and if she adds constraints to rule those cases out, hasn't she just reintroduced a version of the chauvinism the whole apparatus was meant to avoid?

Could you draft seven or eight interview questions along these lines, ordered so the conversation builds rather than jumping from one objection to the next? For each question, note the reply she's most likely to give -- these are all well-worn objections she'll have encountered before -- and add a sharp, specific follow-up that closes off the rehearsed version of that answer.
