At my history book club, discussion of a Frederick Douglass biography turned into a real dispute between two members over his break with William Lloyd Garrison in the early 1850s -- specifically, whether Douglass was right to abandon Garrison's position that the Constitution was, in Garrison's own words, "a covenant with death, and an agreement with hell," in favor of his later view that the document, properly read, was fundamentally anti-slavery. Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Dana said Douglass's later position was convenient rather than correct: once he split from Garrison and began working with Gerrit Smith's political abolitionists, he needed a way to justify voting and political action under a document whose framers had unmistakably compromised with slaveholders -- the three-fifths clause, the fugitive-slave clause, the clause shielding the slave trade until 1808 -- and which courts from Prigg v. Pennsylvania to Dred Scott had consistently read the same way. Calling that document "anti-slavery" was myth-making dressed up as interpretation.

(2) Marcus replied that the text itself never uses the words "slave" or "slavery" anywhere -- a deliberate euphemism -- while the preamble commits the document to "Justice" and the "Blessings of Liberty." A text isn't reducible to its worst readers: the Fourteenth Amendment didn't become a segregationist document just because Plessy v. Ferguson misapplied it for nearly sixty years, and Douglass was doing for the original Constitution what the courts eventually did for the Fourteenth Amendment.

(3) Dana said the analogy fails: "equal protection" plainly supports the reading Brown later gave it, but "delivered up" and "held to Service or Labour" admit no liberty-protecting reading -- you can only interpret around those clauses, not through them. And Douglass's own speeches from his Garrisonian years had said exactly this; his later self wasn't uncovering a hidden meaning so much as contradicting a plainer one he'd already argued for.

(4) Marcus said changing his mind isn't evidence Douglass was wrong: Douglass came to believe the Garrisonian position ceded the nation's founding law to slaveholders for nothing, discouraging antislavery politics and litigation and leaving the document's meaning uncontested for its worst readers. On his mature view, contesting what the Constitution says was itself part of the fight against slavery, not a retreat from honesty about it.

Neither budged.
