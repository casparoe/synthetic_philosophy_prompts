I work as an electrician, but for the past couple of years I've spent my evenings teaching myself the history of logic, working through library books and whatever recorded lectures I can find, mostly because I find the subject beautiful in a way that caught me off guard. That's how I ended up spending a long stretch of last winter reading about Gottlob Frege, the man usually credited with inventing modern formal logic and reshaping how philosophers think about meaning and mathematics. What stopped me cold wasn't the logic, though, but a short diary he kept in 1924, near the end of his life, that only surfaced and was translated decades after he died. It isn't just the ordinary prejudice of its time and place: he writes with real contempt for parliamentary democracy and for Jews as a group, and seems to be hoping for some kind of authoritarian correction to the Weimar Republic he'd come to despise. I even gather that Michael Dummett, the philosopher who probably did more than anyone to revive Frege's reputation in English-language philosophy, wrote candidly about how shaken he was to learn all this about a thinker he admired so much.

Every discussion of this I've managed to track down treats the diary as a sealed-off compartment: you keep the Begriffsschrift and the theory of sense and reference in one box, and you put an old, embittered man's private ugliness in another, with no wire running between them. The more I sit with it, though, the less sure I am that the separation holds. Frege built his whole career on refusing to let logical and mathematical truth be hostage to how any given mind, or any number of minds, happens to be built - that's the entire point of his lifelong campaign against "psychologism," and of his later claim, in an essay called "The Thought," that thoughts belong to a third, objective realm that no head-count of ordinary opinion can reach or revise.

What I can't shake is the suspicion that this same reflex - that what's true or right isn't something you arrive at by consulting people's judgment, and that most people's judgment simply isn't to be trusted with it - is doing double duty in his politics, and is exactly what let him treat a parliamentary majority, and a whole people, as beneath the standard he'd apply to a line of a logical proof. Please argue, as rigorously as you can, that Frege's political diary is not an unrelated personal failing bolted onto an otherwise untouched philosophy, but expresses, in a debased register, the very same anti-psychologistic, anti-majoritarian temperament that produced his logic. I'd also like you to take seriously, rather than wave away, the obvious objection that this is just a pun on the word "elitism" - one that conflates a defensible point about the objectivity of logic with an utterly indefensible one about the worth of human beings.
