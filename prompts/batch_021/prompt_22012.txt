I help run the podcast for my high school's philosophy club, and next week's episode is a bigger deal than usual: our faculty advisor has arranged for a former student of hers, now a philosophy PhD candidate, to come on and defend expressivism about ethics — the view that a sentence like "cruelty is wrong" expresses an attitude of disapproval rather than describing a fact. I first came across the trouble with this view through a footnote in our AP Seminar ethics unit that mentioned something called the Frege–Geach problem, and after reading the Stanford Encyclopedia entry on moral cognitivism and working through part of a Blackburn essay on quasi-realism, I think I understand the shape of the difficulty: if "breaking a promise is wrong" just expresses disapproval when asserted on its own, it is unclear what it expresses inside a sentence like "if breaking a promise is wrong, then talking someone into breaking theirs is wrong," where nobody is asserting disapproval of anything, and yet the whole argument only counts as a valid modus ponens if the phrase means the same thing in both places. Our guest has already warned the club that her standard move when pressed on this is to say that expressivists can earn ordinary logical relations like validity and inconsistency by building a logic of attitudes rather than borrowing one built for facts, and I get the sense that answer usually satisfies people.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that do not just let her restate that this project can be carried out, and instead test whether it actually succeeds? I would like at least one question that makes her walk through a concrete embedded case, such as the promise-breaking example above, and say exactly what, if anything, is being expressed in the unasserted antecedent; one that presses what I understand is called the negation problem — what attitude a sentence like "breaking a promise is not wrong" expresses, and whether her answer generalizes to disjunctions and more complicated sentences without her inventing a new attitude for every new piece of logical vocabulary; and one that raises the worry that if her logic of attitudes has to reconstruct every formal feature a fact-stating semantics already provides for free, there may be nothing left that actually distinguishes her view from the cognitivism she rejects. For each question, please add a short, specific follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, in the spirit of a philosopher defending her thesis rather than one about to change her mind. I would like the sequence to end on a question modeled on "what would you accept as evidence that the account has failed here, rather than evidence that it just needs one more attitude added to the machinery," since I do not want to let her treat every objection as merely an engineering problem to be solved later.
