I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and on the side I co-produce a podcast that devotes each episode to one narrow philosophical argument rather than a whole "-ism." Next month's episode is with an epistemologist who has just published a monograph defending a strictly non-epistemic reading of Wittgenstein's hinge propositions in On Certainty: on her view, stock certainties like "the earth has existed for a long time" or "I have two hands" are not things we know, believe, or are even justified in holding. They are bedrock and arational, closer to a way of acting than to a propositional attitude, and it's precisely their standing outside the space of reasons that lets ordinary doubting and knowing get going at all. I've been rereading the book alongside the Anscombe/von Wright edition of the text, and I've noticed that whenever a critic asks her why one bedrock certainty (the past exists, other people are conscious) counts as rational while another (a cult's founding revelation, an ideologue's certainty about racial hierarchy) does not, she reaches for the same line: the certainty in question "simply stands fast for us," and asking for its credentials already misunderstands what kind of thing a hinge is.

Could you draft seven or eight interview questions that don't just invite her to restate the position, but are sequenced so that "stands fast for us" gets progressively harder to lean on? I want at least one question built around the missionary-and-persuasion passage near the end of the book (roughly §609-612), where Wittgenstein himself seems to concede that at the end of reasons there is only persuasion, pressed toward whether her view leaves any room to call one side of such a confrontation more reasonable than the other. I want one question on the river-bed passage (§96-99): if hinges can harden and soften over a life or a culture, and hinges are by stipulation outside rational evaluation, what could make that drift better or worse rather than merely arbitrary? And I want one question putting to her the charge that a purely non-epistemic reading is too concessive to the skeptic -- that denying we strictly know we have hands gives away the very thing ordinary certainty was supposed to protect. For each of these three, add a short follow-up question tuned to the deflection she's most likely to reach for. Close the sequence with a question asking what kind of textual or philosophical evidence would actually count against her particular reading, as opposed to the rival "entitlement" and "quasi-fideist" alternatives also on offer in the literature, with a follow-up that presses the irony if her honest answer turns out to be that her preference for this reading, too, simply stands fast for her.
