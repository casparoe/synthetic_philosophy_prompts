I spent over thirty years running a condensed-matter physics lab before retiring, and P.W. Anderson's 1972 Science essay "More Is Different" was something of an article of faith in my group: his claim that broken symmetry and kindred phenomena show that reductionism does not entail constructionism. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy of science more carefully, and at a reunion dinner two former postdocs of mine, Nadia and Marcus, had an argument about this that I could not settle myself.

(1) Marcus said that no finite collection of particles obeying the Schrödinger equation can undergo a genuine phase transition or a genuinely broken symmetry, since both are strictly properties of the N→∞ limit -- so Anderson was right that level-specific principles, such as order parameters and renormalization-group fixed points, do explanatory work that the microphysics alone cannot do.
(2) Nadia replied that the limit is only ever an idealization: a finite magnet or crystal shows merely "for all practical purposes" broken symmetry, because the tunnelling splitting between its symmetric and antisymmetric states shrinks exponentially with size, and that shrinking is itself derivable from ordinary quantum mechanics -- so nothing autonomous need be posited.
(3) Marcus answered that this concedes his real point: the renormalization group explains why wildly different microscopic Hamiltonians share identical critical exponents precisely because they flow to the same fixed point, and that fixed-point structure is only well-defined at the continuum limit -- it cannot even be formulated as a property of any single finite Hamiltonian, let alone derived from one.
(4) Nadia answered that many microphysical bases sharing one limiting behaviour is just multiple realizability again, which was never supposed to refute reduction -- it only shows that the reduction is many-to-one, with each actual system's behaviour still fixed by a limit of its own microphysics.
