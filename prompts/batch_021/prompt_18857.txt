My father turns sixty in three weeks, and I've been asked to give the toast at the dinner afterward, which will run to about eighty people: a good number of his fellow appellate judges and courtroom staff from his thirty years on the bench, my mother's side of the family, who have never heard of Plato and never will, and, through no planning of mine, my own dissertation adviser and three people from my seminar, all of whom will be listening for every classical reference I get slightly wrong. My father is, by unanimous family verdict, the most self-possessed person any of us know: nobody has ever seen him finish a full glass of wine, and he has never once danced at a wedding. When my mother mentioned I was giving the toast, he said, not quite joking, that he did not want "the one where the children spend five minutes trying to get the old man drunk and laughing about it."

The timing is almost too on the nose, because I have spent this whole month inside Plato's Laws for my dissertation chapter on ancient civic education, in the stretch where the Athenian Stranger talks Megillus the Spartan and Clinias the Cretan out of their countries' near-total bans on wine. His argument is that supervised drunkenness is a serious civic tool rather than a vice: like fear, or old age, it loosens the self-command that ordinarily conceals a person's true character, so a well-run drinking party, kept from tipping into chaos by someone who stays sober himself, can test and even train that character rather than corrupt it. He goes further in the next book, arguing that the oldest citizens need a chorus of their own, sacred to Dionysus specifically because dignity and self-consciousness would otherwise keep men that age from ever singing or dancing again, and treating their willingness to be coaxed into looking a little foolish as something closer to a civic good than an embarrassment. I would like the toast built around that argument, so that asking my father to have a real glass of wine and get up and dance with my mother tonight lands as a considered, faintly ridiculous, entirely sincere case for why he should, grounded in what Plato actually thought this kind of letting go was for, rather than as the exact joke he asked me not to make. Could you write me that toast, roughly five minutes of spoken material, pitched so it works equally well for the judges' bench and my seminar table?
