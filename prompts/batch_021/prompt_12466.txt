I never studied philosophy formally, but for the past few months I have been working through Quine's Word and Object on my own, in the evenings, without anyone to check my understanding against. Like most people who run into Quine at all, I first met him through the "gavagai" story: a linguist hears a native speaker say "gavagai" every time a rabbit runs past, and can never rule out that the word really means "undetached rabbit part" or "an instance of rabbithood" rather than "rabbit," since no possible pattern of behavior could ever settle the question. What surprised me on reading further is that Quine treated this scenario, which he later called the "argument from below," as the weaker of two separate routes to the conclusion that translation is indeterminate. The other, which he called the "argument from above," starts instead from the underdetermination of physical theory by all possible observations, and then presses that same underdetermination down onto ordinary talk about things like rabbits and chairs.

Please compare these two arguments directly, rather than simply retelling the rabbit story, which I already understand well enough. What exactly does the argument from above establish that the gavagai scenario by itself cannot, and why did Quine come to see it as the deeper of the two? I would like your own judgment, with reasons: does the argument from above actually succeed where the argument from below is merely suggestive, or does pressing the indeterminacy down from theoretical physics to ordinary reference just relocate a controversial assumption rather than removing it?
