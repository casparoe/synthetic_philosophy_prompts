For the past several months I have been working through Aristotle's Politics and the Nicomachean Ethics on my own time. I have no formal training in philosophy; I got drawn in after some online arguments about "sound money" kept invoking Aristotle to explain why metal coinage arose and why he thought lending at interest was unnatural. Chasing that down took me first to the part of the Politics where he separates household management from the "art of wealth-getting" proper, and where he argues that charging interest is the most unnatural way of making money, because the Greek word for interest, tokos, also means "offspring," and money bred from money is like a birth with no natural parent. But the passage that has really stuck with me is the stranger one in Nicomachean Ethics V.5, where Aristotle tries to explain how a builder's house and a shoemaker's shoes could ever be exchanged fairly. He seems to say that what actually holds an exchange together is need, and that money is only a conventional stand-in for need, invented by agreement rather than by nature, so much so that a community could in principle agree to make it worthless. I later found out, to my surprise, that Marx took this exact passage seriously enough to discuss at length in Capital, arguing that Aristotle had almost arrived at something like a labor theory of value there but could not finish the thought, because living in a society built on slavery made it impossible for him to treat different people's labor as fundamentally equal or comparable.

I do not have the background to judge how much weight that reading can bear on its own, which is why I would like you to write me an essay that takes the controversy on directly rather than just summarizing both sides of it from a distance. Specifically: does Aristotle's treatment of exchange in that passage amount to a genuine, if unfinished, theory of what makes goods economically comparable in the first place, whether that turns out to be closer to Marx's labor-based reading or closer to a needs-based or demand-based reading that anticipates later subjective theories of value, or is it a mistake to find any real "theory of value" in the passage at all, given that Aristotle's own stated aim there was to explain reciprocity as a form of justice rather than to do anything resembling economic analysis? Please give the strongest version of each side rather than a caricature of either, but do not leave the essay sitting on the fence at the end. I would like you to actually commit to a verdict and defend it, even if the honest verdict turns out to be something qualified, like "he was genuinely grappling with a real problem, but the text does not support calling it a labor theory specifically."
