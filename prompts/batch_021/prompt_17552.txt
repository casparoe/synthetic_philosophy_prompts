My sister is an assistant professor of philosophy who has spent most of the last decade publishing on the ethics of promising, and she is getting married in six weeks. Naturally, she has asked me — someone who covers science and ideas for a living and is far more used to explaining other people's thinking than generating any of my own — to write the toast. I said yes before I understood what I was agreeing to. She has already ruled out the two easiest ways to write this speech: she doesn't want a toast that treats the words "I promise" as a kind of verbal magic that conjures an obligation out of nothing, and she doesn't want her own vow quietly reduced to what she calls, half-affectionately, "a Humean convention" — Hume's argument that keeping a promise is an artificial virtue we uphold only because the practice happens to be useful to society, with nothing more solid underneath it. She has spent just as long teaching the rival view, associated with Scanlon, that breaking a promise wrongs someone specifically because you deliberately invited their trust and reliance and then let them down — which makes the wrong closer to a kind of exploitation than to the breaking of a rule.

About a third of the guests are former classmates and colleagues who worked through this exact literature alongside her and will know immediately if the toast hand-waves past the hard part of that disagreement. Everyone else, including our own parents, has never spent a minute wondering why promises bind and mostly wants to laugh, maybe get a little teary, and feel good about the marriage. Could you write the five-minute toast I now owe her — one that takes the question seriously enough to survive the philosophers in the room, stays genuinely warm and funny for everyone else, and still arrives at something true, rather than merely comforting, to say about the vow she and her partner are about to exchange with no referee but each other?
