So I've been down a rabbit hole lately reading AI safety blogs, and theres this recurring argument that keeps popping up that people call "coherence arguments" for why any sufficiently advanced agent should basically be forced into acting like an expected utility maximizer. The basic idea, if I'm getting it right, is: if your preferences are intransitive - like you prefer apple over banana, banana over cherry, but then cherry over apple - someone can build a "money pump" and just keep trading with you in a loop, taking a little bit of your stuff each round, until you've got nothing left. There's a similar move with "dutch books" for beliefs, where if your probabilities don't add up right, a bookie can set up a bunch of bets that guarantee you loose money no matter what happens. So the argument goes that agents like this get money-pumped out of existence or corrected over time, and that's supposedly why any smart enough agent (including a future AI) will end up with consistent, complete preferences that can be described by a single utility function it's maximizing.

Around the same time, a friend of mine got me hooked on Hume, specifically that whole bit about how reason is basically just "the slave of the passions" and can't tell you what to want, only how to get it. And it's got me stuck on this question: what would Hume actually say about coherence arguments if you sat him down and explained them? On one hand it sounds like his view should make him totally fine with an agent having weird intransitive preferences, since reason isn't supposed to have any say over what you want in the first place, it just serves whatever passions you happen to have. But on the other hand, getting money-pumped is a pretty concrete practical loss, not some abstract "your ends are wrong" complaint - you're literally bleeding resources because of how your preferences are shaped, which sounds exactly like the kind of means-end failure Hume thinks reason IS allowed to correct. So does his whole philosophy actually end up supporting these coherence arguments, undercutting them, or landing somewhere weirder than either? I'd love an actual deep answer on this, not just the "Hume liked passions, not reason" one-liner.
