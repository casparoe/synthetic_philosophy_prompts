I'm working on a long-form piece about the intellectual prehistory of psychology's replication crisis, and my research kept pulling me further back than I'd planned -- past Meehl, past Fisher and Neyman, all the way to Henry Sidgwick and the Society for Psychical Research he helped found in 1882, serving as its first president. What strikes me about Sidgwick is how little he resembles the credulous Victorian spiritualist of caricature. Together with his wife Eleanor and colleagues such as Edmund Gurney and Frederic Myers, he spent roughly two decades designing studies like the SPR's Census of Hallucinations: an attempt to establish, essentially statistically, whether people's reported apparitions of the dying coincided with real deaths more often than chance and faulty memory ought to allow. By every account, he died in 1900 having satisfied himself of remarkably little, seemingly tormented throughout by the suspicion that his own wish for the research to succeed was itself a source of bias to distrust -- his ethics had left him with what he called the "dualism of practical reason," an unresolved clash between the rational claims of self-interest and of the general good that he suspected could only be dissolved if the universe turned out to have some further moral order behind it, of exactly the kind that evidence of survival after death might supply.

For the piece, I'd like you to attempt something the historical record obviously can't offer: apply Sidgwick's own standards of evidence to a case from more than a century after his death. In 2011, the social psychologist Daryl Bem published "Feeling the Future" in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, one of the field's most mainstream and prestigious outlets, reporting that ordinary undergraduates could sense random future events, using the same kind of null-hypothesis significance testing that psychology treated as routine at the time. Within months, Eric-Jan Wagenmakers and colleagues published a Bayesian re-analysis arguing that Bem's statistics had radically overstated the evidence; a series of high-profile replication attempts failed to reproduce his results; and the episode became one of the founding scandals of the replication crisis, helping to push psychology toward preregistration and more skeptical statistical habits generally. Reconstructing as carefully as you can from Sidgwick's documented habits of mind, rather than just his general open-mindedness about the paranormal, how do you think he would have adjudicated the Bem affair? I'm especially curious whether his well-known instinct to distrust findings that told him what he wanted to hear would have led him to anticipate something like the Bayesian critique before Wagenmakers ever wrote it, whether he would have regarded ordinary significance testing as already a known trap given what his own SPR work had taught him about coincidence and unconscious self-deception, and what he might have made of a scandal in parapsychology ending up reforming statistical practice across a science he cared about for reasons that were never purely disinterested. If it helps to answer partly by imagining a short passage in his own voice, in the register of his SPR presidential addresses, feel free.
