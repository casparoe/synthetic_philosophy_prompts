In Guide for the Perplexed III:32, Maimonides argues that the sacrificial cult was never intrinsically desired by God but was instituted as a concession to human psychology: because the Israelites were habituated to the idolatrous sacrificial worship of the surrounding nations, God redirected that very practice toward Himself rather than demanding an abrupt shift to the purer worship of prayer and contemplation, much as a nurse weans an infant gradually rather than all at once. Nahmanides, commenting on the opening of Leviticus, rejects this account: he objects that Cain, Abel, and Noah all offered sacrifices before any idolatry existed in the world, and he defends an intrinsic, non-instrumental rationale for the sacrificial rites. Maimonides' own legal code, the Mishneh Torah, complicates matters further, treating the sacrificial laws in exhaustive technical detail as a permanent obligation and affirming that they will be reinstated once the Temple is rebuilt in messianic times.

Write an essay adjudicating this dispute. Is Nahmanides' objection decisive against Maimonides' concession theory, or can it be defended? And can the Guide's treatment of sacrifice as a temporary pedagogical measure be squared with the Mishneh Torah's treatment of it as an eternal obligation, or does this expose a genuine tension between Maimonides the philosopher and Maimonides the legal codifier? Weigh the strongest considerations on each side, but reach and defend a definite overall verdict rather than ending on a non-committal note.
