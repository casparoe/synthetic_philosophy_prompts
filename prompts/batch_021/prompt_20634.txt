I've been teaching myself game theory over the last couple months, mostly off Ben Polak's old Yale lectures on youtube plus whatever lecture note PDFs I can find by googling around (no formal classes, I just find this stuff genuinely fun to chew on in my spare time). I think I've got the basic idea of iterated elimination of dominated strategies down - you cross off any strategy that's always worse than some other option no matter what the other player does, then treat what's left as a smaller game and repeat until nothing more can be removed. The part that took me a while to wrap my head around is that a strategy can get crossed off even when no single other strategy beats it every time, as long as some mix of two others (like 60% one, 40% the other) beats it in every case. Ok fine, once I saw it layed out with actual numbers it clicked.

But heres what's bugging me now, and I even ran it by my brother (he's an engineer, good with logic even if game theory isn't his thing) and neither of us could spot the mistake. Everybody teaches this as a big theorem: the order you cross strategies off in never matters, you always land on the exact same final set of survivors no matter what sequence you use. But what if the only reason some strategy C was ever removable in the first place was that specific 60/40 mix of A and B, and A happens to get crossed off earlier for some totally seperate reason (say A is just straight-up beaten by B alone, no mixing needed)? Once A is gone, that particular mix isn't sitting there anymore to justify killing off C. So whether C survives seems like it should genuinely depend on whether you happened to delete A before or after you noticed the C-killing mixture - meaning the order really does matter in cases like this. So is the "order never matters" theorem everyone states actually wrong, or at least missing a pretty important caveat that nobody bothers to mention?
