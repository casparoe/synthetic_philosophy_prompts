Question 3 (20 points). Standard inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) proposes to infer an agent's reward function from observations of that agent's behavior, typically by assuming the agent behaves (at least approximately) optimally with respect to that reward function in some Markov decision process.

(a) [6 pts] Explain why, even given unlimited, noise-free observations of a truly optimal policy, the reward function generating that policy is not identifiable from the behavior alone — that is, why "optimal behavior" is compatible with more than one reward function in a way that more data cannot fix.

(b) [8 pts] Ziebart et al.'s Maximum Entropy IRL is often presented in courses (including this one) as the standard fix for this kind of ambiguity. Explain what problem Maximum Entropy IRL actually solves, and say whether it fully resolves the identifiability problem from part (a) or only part of it. Your answer should engage with the further worry, associated with Armstrong and Mindermann's work on this topic, that fixing a reward function is not enough on its own, because the mapping from behavior to reward also depends on an assumed model of the agent's rationality — and that assumption cannot itself be read off from behavior without already presupposing it.

(c) [6 pts] Some commentators have pointed out that this isn't a new problem created by machine learning, but a machine-learning-shaped instance of a much older worry about inferring what someone wants from what they do. Name one such older discussion of this worry from outside computer science, and briefly explain the parallel.

Student's answer:

(a) The problem is that "optimal behavior" underdetermines the reward. If we just ask which reward makes the observed policy optimal, there's always more than one answer, including the degenerate case where the reward is zero everywhere: every policy gets zero return under a zero reward, so no policy is better than any other, and the truly optimal policy is "optimal" for that reward too, just as vacuously as any other policy would be. Ng and Russell flagged exactly this in their original IRL paper and used it as the motivation for adding an extra selection criterion on top of mere consistency with optimality — for instance, picking the reward that makes the demonstrated action beat the next-best alternative by as much as possible, rather than just any reward the optimality constraint happens to allow. So the underdetermination isn't a data problem, it's structural: no number of additional optimal trajectories rules out the degenerate solutions, because they are compatible with any optimal behavior whatsoever, by construction.

(b) MaxEnt IRL addresses this by dropping the requirement that the policy be exactly optimal and replacing it with a probabilistic model where trajectories become exponentially more likely the higher their reward; among all the distributions over trajectories consistent with matching the demonstrated feature counts, it picks the one with maximum entropy. That's a principled move, because maximum entropy is the least committal choice consistent with the data, so it removes the arbitrariness that plagued the hard-optimality version of the problem and hands you back a single, well-justified reward instead of a whole tied set of them. I think the Armstrong and Mindermann worry still has force in cases where the demonstrator's suboptimality has some structure to it rather than being genuinely random — if people are noisy for systematic reasons that the assumed Boltzmann noise model doesn't capture, the recovered reward will be biased in a way MaxEnt can't detect. But once you grant the standard assumption that deviations from optimality are essentially noise, it's the maximum-entropy step that pins the reward down uniquely, which is presumably why the method has become the default in the literature.

(c) This is an old problem wearing new clothes: economists ran into the same thing with revealed preference theory, which tries to read off a consumer's preferences purely from their choices among bundles of goods. Samuelson pointed out that this approach cannot actually distinguish someone's genuine preferences from other reasons they might have for choosing as they choose — two agents (or the same agent on two different occasions) can produce identical choice behavior for entirely different underlying reasons, and choice data by itself cannot tell you which reason was operative. That is structurally the same gap as in (a) and (b): behavior constrains, but does not determine, the "why" behind it.
