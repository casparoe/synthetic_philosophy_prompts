Over the past month, my partner and I have been in an escalating disagreement about whether to move a portion of our savings into gold, prompted by all the recent talk of currency instability and crypto volatility. Mid-argument, I found myself reaching for a text I had not opened since a graduate seminar on early modern philosophy: Berkeley's Querist, where he presses, through a long chain of rhetorical questions, the claim that money is not wealth itself but merely a ticket or counter for reckoning and transferring a nation's real wealth (its industry and its produced goods), and that this reckoning function does not require the ticket to carry any intrinsic value of its own -- which is part of why he could propose a national bank for Ireland that would issue paper credit well beyond the country's scant hoard of gold and silver. Write an essay that reconstructs this claim precisely and then weighs whether it amounts to a coherent forerunner of chartalist and modern-monetary-theory views of money, or whether it quietly smuggles back in something functionally equivalent to "backing" -- state guarantee, scarcity, enforceable public trust -- that a pure sign-theory of money is not entitled to assume without argument. Reach and defend a specific verdict rather than concluding that it depends on how charitably one reads him, and close by saying what that verdict should tell someone in my position about whether gold's "intrinsic value" is doing any real philosophical work in an argument like ours.
