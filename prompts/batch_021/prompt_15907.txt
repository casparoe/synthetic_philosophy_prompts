So my book club read Pascal's Pensées this month (the cheap paperback with all the numbered fragments) and two people, Dave and Meg, got into a whole thing about the bits on justice and force. Roughly how it went:

(1) Dave said Pascal is basically telling us real justice is a fiction — he pointed to the lines "justice without force is powerless, force without justice is tyrannical," "custom creates the whole of equity, for the simple reason that it is accepted," and "nothing, according to reason alone, is just in itself." Dave's take: Pascal beat Nietzsche to "might makes right" by 200 years, he's just too polite to say it outright.

(2) Meg said thats too fast — the whole book is Pascal trying to show reason can't reach the big truths on its own, as a setup for why you need faith instead (same move as the bit about the heart having reasons reason doesn't know, or the wager). So "reason alone can't find justice" probably parallels "reason alone can't find God" — an argument for needing something besides unaided reason, not a claim that there's nothing there to find.

(3) Dave said fine, but look at what Pascal actually tells people to do about it: he says whoever traces a custom back to its first principles destroys it, i.e. don't question the law, just obey it because it's the law. That's not neutral agnosticism, that's instructing everyone to submit to whoever currently holds power, which lands in the same place as "might makes right" no matter what his private theology says.

(4) Meg said he's not quite that shameless though, because he still calls some power grabs illegitimate, through his idea of tyranny: wanting power in one "order" (obeyed because you're strong) by leaning on a totally different order (also demanded to be loved, or believed wise). If it were really just "whoever's strongest, full stop," that distinction wouldn't mean anything.

Dave wasnt having it — he said the "orders" thing sounds nice but is toothless, since Pascal never says how you'd actually tell, in a live dispute, when force has overstepped its own order versus when it hasn't, so in practice you're still just stuck deferring to whoever's currently strongest. Nobody in the group could agree who came out ahead.

Also curious if there's some bigger thing about what Pascal's actually doing in these passages that both of them are missing.
