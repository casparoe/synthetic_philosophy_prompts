I'm reporting a feature on a small but persistent revival of monism within analytic metaphysics, and one nineteenth-century argument keeps coming up in nearly every conversation I have for it: F. H. Bradley's regress. As I understand it, Bradley pointed out that if a relation such as "loves" is to genuinely connect two terms — say, in "John loves Mary" — then the relation itself seems to need some further relation tying it to each of them; that further relation, in turn, needs another relation to tie it to its terms, and so on without end. Bradley took this regress, together with parallel worries about how a thing and its qualities could ever be genuinely distinct, to argue that ordinary pluralistic reality — many discrete items standing in external relations to one another — is ultimately incoherent, and that only an undivided Absolute is fully real. The same regress became central, in the hands of Frege and Russell, to the problem of "the unity of the proposition": why does "Socrates is wise" express one unified thought rather than a bare list, {Socrates, wisdom}? Frege's answer was that concepts are inherently "unsaturated," carrying a built-in gap that objects fill without any further glue being required; other philosophers have instead tried to show that the regress, though real, is simply not vicious.

For the piece, I'd like a passage that actually stages this disagreement rather than summarizing it once more. Could you write a dialogue, somewhere around 900 to 1,200 words, between two contemporary metaphysicians: one who thinks Bradley's regress is genuinely vicious and draws something close to Bradley's own monistic conclusion from it (unity cannot be manufactured out of independently subsisting relata plus a relation, however cleverly redescribed), and one who thinks the regress can be blocked while keeping an ordinary ontology of many distinct facts or propositions with real constituent structure. I'd like the pluralist pressed hard on whether their preferred stopping move — unsaturatedness, a distinction between "vicious" and "merely infinite" regresses, or whichever solution you find most defensible — actually explains unity or just relabels the mystery, and I'd like the monist pressed just as hard on why an infinite regress here should count as fatal rather than harmless, given that plenty of infinite regresses elsewhere are perfectly benign, and on whether "the Absolute" is any more intelligible than the unity it's supposed to explain. Please have both characters genuinely respond to each other's specific objections as the exchange develops rather than just restating their opening positions, and don't manufacture a tidy resolution if the strongest version of the exchange doesn't actually produce one.
