During practice for my school's Ethics Bowl team, we were prepping a case about primate research when two of my teammates got pulled into a much longer side argument about the "argument from marginal cases" against speciesism. I wrote down roughly what was said afterward because I wanted an outside opinion.

Jonah opened with the argument itself: we justify giving humans stronger moral protections than animals by pointing to capacities like rationality, autonomy, or self-awareness, but infants and people with severe cognitive disabilities have those capacities to a lesser degree than many animals we experiment on or eat, so either those animals deserve equivalent protection, or our current practice is really just favoritism toward our own species dressed up as principle.

Reyna answered that this overlooks kind membership: a human with severe cognitive disabilities is still a member of a species whose normal members are rational and self-aware, and it's that fact about the kind, not the individual's present capacities, that grounds protected status, so there's no inconsistency in protecting such humans while denying the same status to a pig or a chimpanzee.

Jonah replied that this just relocates the arbitrariness instead of removing it: why should species-typical capacities matter morally rather than the capacities an individual actually has? Treating species as the relevant "kind" already assumes species membership is morally loaded, which is exactly what was supposed to be in question.

Reyna pushed back that we reason this way constantly without controversy: a dog born blind is still judged and treated as a dog, relative to canine norms, rather than reclassified as whatever kind of creature matches its actual capacities, so appealing to "normal for the kind" isn't special pleading invented just for this debate.

Jonah's last word was that this trades on an ambiguity: judging whether an individual is healthy or flourishing by the standards of its own species is a different exercise from deciding what moral status or rights it has, and Reyna's dog example only shows the former.
