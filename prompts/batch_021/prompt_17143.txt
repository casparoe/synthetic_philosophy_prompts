I've been on a bit of a MIRI-papers kick lately (mostly as procrastination from my actual work, which feels thematically appropriate given where this is going) and I keep getting stuck on the same idea: the Löbian obstacle to an agent trusting its own future self, or a successor agent it's about to build. I get the mechanical version okay-ish -- Löb's theorem says a formal system can only prove "if Provable(P) then P" for some particular P when it can already prove P outright, so if you try to give an AI a blanket policy like "trust whatever my future self/successor proves," Löb's theorem lets you derive some genuinely bad stuff from that policy rather than it just being a harmless tautology. What I dont have is the gut-level intuition for why this is a real, load-bearing problem rather than a cute technicality that better engineering could route around. In everyday life we trust our future selves (and other people!) to reason well all the time without independently re-deriving every conclusion in advance, so why does an analogous move blow up so badly once "trust" is cashed out as formal provability?

I vaguely remember the old Tiling Agents paper by Yudkowsky and Herreshoff gesturing at this with something called the procrastination paradox -- an agent that can always "prove" its fine to put some action off till tomorrow, forever -- but I never fully internalized why that example is supposed to be so damning rather than just a quirk of a badly-chosen axiom schema. Could you walk me through, on an intuitive level, why the Löbian obstacle is a genuine barrier to self-trust for proof-based agents, ideally with a minimal version of that procrastination-style example? And separately, do you have a take on whether this is mostly a curiosity that only bites agents dumb enough to reason in pure first-order logic, or whether it points at something deeper that any sufficiently reflective agent (formal, probabilistic, whatever) has to grapple with in some form?
