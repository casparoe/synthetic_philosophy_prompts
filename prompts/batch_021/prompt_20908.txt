In my seminar on the metaphysics of causation, we've spent the last two weeks on causation by omission, and I keep returning to what's sometimes called the promiscuity (or profligacy) problem. If a lifeguard's failure to intervene counts as a cause of a swimmer's drowning, straightforward counterfactual reasoning seems to force us to say the same of every other competent swimmer nearby who also did nothing — or even, in the stock exaggerated case philosophers use, someone with no real connection to the scene who nonetheless satisfies the very same counterfactual. Philosophers who still want omissions to count as genuine causes (Lewis, in "Void and Object," is a good example) typically end up restricting the relevant omissions by appeal to duties, norms, or salience rather than counterfactual dependence alone. Could you write a short story, around 800 words, that dramatizes this tension — pulling the reader toward blaming one particular non-actor while also making them feel the vertigo of the same logic implicating countless others who did nothing at all? I'd like the philosophical bite to emerge through the plot and characters' choices rather than through anyone explicitly explaining the puzzle.
