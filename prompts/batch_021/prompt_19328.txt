I'm a retired condensed-matter physicist (spent about thirty-five years running a low-temperature transport lab before I packed it in), and these last few years I've gotten hooked on philosophy of physics as a retirement hobby. A handful of us old lab rats meet up once a month to finally read papers we somehow skipped in grad school, and lately we've been stuck on Bell's theorem and what it actually shows about locality — turns out the popular-science slogan ("Bell proved the universe is nonlocal, period") is a lot slipperier than I assumed back when I thought I had this cold.

Two people in the group, Gary (career plasma physicist, very much a "shut up and calculate" guy) and Leah (came to physics late, after a philosophy PhD, and has read way more of the primary literature than the rest of us), got into it over email after last month's meeting, and it ran long enough that I saved the thread. Roughly: (1) Gary said Bell's theorem settles the matter — any theory reproducing the correlations seen in Aspect-type experiments has to allow influences that outrun light, so nonlocality just is the takeaway, whatever Einstein hoped for. (2) Leah pushed back that the inequality really only follows from locality plus "measurement independence" (the assumption that the hidden state of the particles isn't secretly correlated with which settings the experimenters will later choose) — and that "realism" isn't even doing separate work, since locality plus the perfect EPR correlations already forces determinism on you by itself. So, she said, you could in principle save locality by giving up measurement independence instead. (3) Gary called that a get-out-of-jail-free card nobody sane should cash in: if hidden variables can conspire with an experimenter's choice of settings, then every controlled experiment ever run, not just Bell tests, is suspect in the same way, which is a far worse bullet to bite than nonlocality. He added that plain textbook quantum mechanics, no hidden variables at all, is already nonlocal anyway, since collapse updates the whole entangled state at once, so you can't dodge the conclusion just by refusing to touch hidden-variable theories. (4) Leah granted that denying measurement independence is ugly, but said the "collapse is nonlocal" move conflates a bookkeeping update with the stricter notion of locality Bell worked out in his later papers — roughly, that distant correlations should trace back to a common cause where the two particles' pasts overlap, not to one side pushing the other around. She said many-worlds satisfies even that stricter condition, since nothing ever propagates faster than light there at all; instead it denies that each run of the experiment has one single outcome, which is exactly a premise Bell's proof also leans on. A real third option, she said, not a dodge, though she admitted the bill for it is enormous. (5) Gary's comeback: "nothing signals faster than light" isn't the same thing as "local" in the sense that actually worried Einstein, and if you're allowed to invent unlimited extra branches to explain away any correlation you don't like, the theorem stops teaching anybody anything — so for people doing physics rather than metaphysics, nonlocality is still the only real lesson left standing.

Nobody backed down, and I've flip-flopped on it enough times myself that I honestly can't referee it, even though thirty-odd years ago I'd have sworn I understood Bell's theorem well enough to explain it to a first-year in five minutes. Could you go through the five moves and say, one by one, who's got the better of that particular exchange and why, rather than just declaring an overall winner? And separately, I'd be curious if you think there's some seperate point about measurement independence, collapse, or many-worlds that both of them are just missing.
