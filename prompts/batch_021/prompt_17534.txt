So I had this whole argument with my friend Dan last night (he's doing a PhD in ML, im more the philosophy-brained one of the two of us) about these "coherence arguments" people use in AI safety circles -- you know, the idea that any sufficiently advanced agent that can't be turned into a money-pump basically HAS to behave like its maximizing some fixed expected utility function, which is supposedly part of why we should expect advanced AI to end up single-mindedly goal-directed and hard to redirect once its running. Dan thinks this is basically airtight math, I kept saying it feels like it smuggles in a bunch of extra assumptions (completeness over literally every possible option, a utility function that stays fixed through time, etc), and we just went in circles for like an hour without actually resolving anything.

Afterwards I got curious where this way of thinking even comes from, and it turns out that long before anyone called it a "coherence argument," Frank Ramsey had basically already laid the groundwork in his "Truth and Probability" essay from the 1920s -- he's the one who showed how you could derive a person's probabilities AND utilities together out of nothing but their preferences over bets, using those weird "ethically neutral" propositions as a kind of measuring stick, so long as the preferences hang together consistently. He was also very explicitly arguing against Keynes on this (Keynes thought probability was some sui generis logical relation between propositions, Ramsey basically said no, it's just about your degrees of belief being consistent with each other), and generally had this pragmatist, almost operationalist attitude about probability and utility not being "real" hidden quantities so much as summaries of behavior.

So here's what I actually want your take on: if Ramsey somehow saw how his little coherence argument has since mutated into this whole edifice about why advanced AI will necessarily lock in some stable, fixed goal and defend it at all costs, what do you think he'd say? Would he see that as a fair extrapolation of his own logic, or would his pragmatism about utility push him toward thinking the whole "the AI secretly has one true misaligned goal underneath all that coherent behavior" framing is a kind of category error? Genuinely want your best-guess reconstruction of his view here, not just a rundown of the current debate.
