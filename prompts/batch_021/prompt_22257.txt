After thirty-five years running an experimental biology lab, I've spent much of my retirement reading philosophy of science, and I keep coming back to how much of the replication crisis seems to trace back to genuine, unresolved foundational disputes between frequentists and Bayesians rather than to mere carelessness at the bench.

The dispute I find most vivid concerns optional stopping. A committed frequentist will insist that checking your data as it comes in and halting as soon as you hit significance inflates your true error rate, no matter how rigorous the underlying test is; a committed Bayesian will insist, via the likelihood principle, that when you decide to stop is simply irrelevant to what the accumulated data say.

Please write a dialogue between a committed frequentist statistician and an equally committed Bayesian statistician who run into each other in the hotel bar after a conference session on the replication crisis. Have them work through the optional-stopping dispute in real detail, each pressing on the genuine costs and hidden commitments of the other's position rather than caricaturing it, with neither conceding more than their actual position requires. I'd like the exchange to end with each of them telling me, bluntly, what I should have done differently all those times I caught myself peeking at the data before deciding whether to run another round of experiments.
