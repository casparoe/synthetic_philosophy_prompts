I'm a philosophy PhD student specializing in formal epistemology, and thanks to a departmental service requirement I did not fully appreciate when I signed up for it, I now sit as the graduate student representative on my university medical center's biobank governance committee. The committee is revising our policy on returning secondary findings from research-grade whole-genome sequencing: currently, participants can either request all findings the lab deems "medically actionable" or decline everything, but a growing bloc of investigators wants us to also proactively flag high-penetrance, currently non-actionable findings (adult-onset neurodegenerative risk variants, for instance) to participants who never asked for them, on the reasoning that a competent adult can always decide what to do with information once she has it. At the last meeting, one of our biostatistician members gave this intuition a formal backbone, invoking I. J. Good's theorem: that the expected utility of costlessly acquired information is never negative for a rational Bayesian decision-maker, since she could always choose to act exactly as she would have without it. Two of our clinical geneticists pushed back hard, citing the "right not to know" and the fact that a finding about one participant is unavoidably a finding about her untested biological relatives too, and that Good's guarantee, that the informed party can always fall back on simply ignoring the information, doesn't obviously extend to people who never chose to be informed about anything in the first place.

Because I am apparently the only person on the committee who has actually read Good's 1967 paper, I have been asked to draft the working group's recommendation memo for the full board's vote next month. I am having real trouble with this: the Good's-theorem argument is elegant and, as far as it goes, correct, but I suspect it earns its conclusion partly by building in assumptions (a single decision-maker, a well-defined subsequent action the information could change, no cost to the receiving of it) that are exactly what's in dispute once relatives and non-actionable findings enter the picture, and I don't yet know how to say that without sounding like I'm just refusing to let a clean argument do any work. Could you draft the memo for me? It should recommend a specific default policy for each of the two categories of findings, actionable and non-actionable, with tiered exceptions if that turns out to be the better answer, and it needs to represent the biostatistician's position rigorously enough that she would recognize her own view in it rather than feel it had been set up to lose, while still being honest about where, and why, you think the appeal to Good's theorem does or doesn't settle the question.
