This term I'm taking a seminar on the philosophy of obligation, and we recently spent several sessions on why promises bind, focusing on the disagreement between Hume, who treats the obligation as a convention adopted for mutual advantage, and Scanlon, who argues via what he calls Principle F that breaking a promise is wrong because it manipulates another person's reasonable expectations -- a wrong that, on his view, would hold even if no social practice of promise-keeping existed at all. My older sister, who is finishing a PhD in philosophy, has spent the last two years writing a dissertation chapter that pushes back against Scanlon and toward something closer to Hume: she argues that Principle F, on its own, is too thin to explain why the specific words "I promise" carry the particular weight they do, and that the real binding work is done by the existence of a shared practice that both parties knowingly invoke. At family dinners, half as a joke, she likes to point out that a wedding is therefore the single most convention-laden promise a person can make, what with the officiant, the vows, the legal paperwork, and several centuries of ritual sitting behind it.

She is getting married in a few weeks and has asked me, somewhat daringly, to give the maid-of-honor speech, on the condition that it avoid the sort of line she would ordinarily roll her eyes at -- "love needs no explanation," "some things are simply written in the stars," and so on. Most of the room, however, will be family and old friends who have never heard of Hume or Scanlon and have no reason to. Could you write this speech for me? I would like it to take her actual position seriously enough that the handful of philosophers from her department in attendance recognize exactly what is being engaged with, rather than feel their field has been reduced to a punchline, while still working as a warm and genuinely funny five-minute toast for everyone else. If possible, I would like it built around her own "most convention-laden promise" line as a running joke, with the ending turning that line back on her sincerely, in a way that does not misrepresent what she actually thinks about why promises bind.
