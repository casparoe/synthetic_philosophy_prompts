I've spent the better part of fifteen years following the long-running argument about epistemic modesty on LessWrong and Overcoming Bias -- Yudkowsky's case against reflexive deference on one side, defenders of something like Adam Elga's Equal Weight View on the other -- so it wasn't entirely strange that my aunt Miriam, who spent her career as an academic epistemologist working on higher-order evidence, turned out to have strong opinions about it herself. She died just over a week ago. Her published work mostly concerned cases like the sleep-deprived medical resident who calculates a drug dosage and is then told that sleep deprivation makes people simultaneously more error-prone and more confident in their arithmetic -- she can't identify anything she did wrong, yet she's still supposed to become less sure of the number she got. She was also, to her colleagues' mild bafflement, the person who organized her local rationalist meetup's camping trip every summer for fifteen years, which is the only reason half the mourners will have any idea who Elga is. Near the end she liked to joke that the eulogy is the single most epistemically akratic genre human beings have invented: the people who loved someone most have the strongest higher-order evidence that grief has wrecked their judgment about that person, and they get up and speak with total confidence anyway.

I'm delivering that eulogy next week, to a room split between her philosophy department, who will notice immediately if I dodge the problem she found so funny, and family and meetup friends who mostly just want to hear that she was wonderful. I can't shake the sense that writing something warmly unqualified about her would be exactly the move she spent her career arguing against, but a eulogy that hedges every sentence to advertise my own bias will comfort no one and will honor the letter of her views while betraying their spirit. Could you write it for me? It should run five to seven minutes read aloud, it should handle the philosophy in plain language rather than her field's jargon (nobody should need to have heard of the Equal Weight View to follow it), and it should actually take a position on the tension rather than smoothing it over -- either finding a way to be sincerely confident about her that survives her own standards, or being honest about the akrasia and making that honesty itself part of what the eulogy is doing.
