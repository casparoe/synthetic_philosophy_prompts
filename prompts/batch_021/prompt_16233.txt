So, slight confession before I even get to the question: the only reason I finished my last novel on deadline is that I use one of those commitment-contract apps — you link a credit card, set a daily word-count goal, and if you miss it the app automatically sends a chunk of your own money to a charity you'd genuinely hate. Mine's rigged to donate to a political outfit I can't stand. I was explaining this at dinner to my sister-in-law, who's finishing a philosophy PhD, and she got this look and said "oh, that's basically Kavka's toxin puzzle, except somebody found the loophole." So I went and read up on it — the thought experiment where a billionaire will pay you a fortune tonight just for sincerely intending, right now, to drink a nasty-but-harmless toxin tomorrow at noon; you don't get a cent extra for actually drinking it, and the money's already yours before noon even arrives, which means by the time you'd have to swallow the stuff you have zero reason left to go through with something that'll only make you sick for a day. Ever since she said that I haven't been able to stop turning it over in my head, and I don't fully trust that her one-liner actually holds up.

What I'd like from you is the most important implications of the toxin puzzle for whether my stupid little app is doing what I assumed it was doing. Is the app quietly dodging the exact problem Kavka raised, or does it just relocate that same problem to some other night (the night before I set the goal, say)? And either way, does the puzzle suggest something uncomfortable about whether the "intentions" I form when I commit to these goals are even the kind of thing a person can sincerely form on command, versus just performing for their own future benefit? I'm asking partly because I've got a character in my current book who talks a friend into a similar self-binding wager, and before I write her into a corner she can't think her way out of, I'd like to actually understand the mechanics rather than fake it — something with enough real substance that I could explain it back to my sister-in-law at the next dinner without her catching me out.
