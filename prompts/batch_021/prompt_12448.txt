A few of us from a long-running rationalist reading group have been arguing for weeks over a tangent that started with Newcomb's problem and somehow ended up in sixteenth-century philosophy of religion. The connection someone drew was to Molinism -- Luis de Molina's account of divine providence, built on the idea of "middle knowledge" (scientia media): God's knowledge, logically prior to and independent of any decision to create, of every true counterfactual of creaturely freedom, i.e., what any possible free creature would freely do in any possible circumstance. The appeal is that it lets God select which "feasible world" to actualize -- weakly, by arranging circumstances, rather than strongly, by causing the choice directly -- while the creature remains genuinely free. The objection that has actually split our group is the old "grounding" worry: nothing seems available to make a counterfactual about a choice that is never actually made true, given that, by hypothesis, the creature could have chosen otherwise under that very same circumstance.

I'd like a short story out of this, something in the 1,200-2,000 word range, that dramatizes the problem rather than lecturing about it. Setting: a small forecasting or policy-optimization team discovers that their system's counterfactual outputs -- "this particular person, in this unrealized circumstance, would have freely chosen X" -- keep being vindicated at a rate no ordinary statistical model should sustain, including on branches that will now never run and so can never be checked against reality. The team is quietly split over whether this means the choices in question were never really free after all, or whether the system has instead stumbled onto whatever it is that actually grounds these counterfactuals. One member, trained in philosophy of religion before moving into machine learning, recognizes the shape of what's been built and objects to how leadership now wants to use it: to select, among several feasible interventions across different regions, the one whose downstream free choices happen to add up to the desired outcome, on the theory that they are merely permitting that outcome through the circumstances they set up rather than causing it, and are therefore not answerable for what follows. Please make the actual philosophical apparatus -- natural versus middle versus free knowledge, feasible versus merely possible worlds, weak versus strong actualization, and the grounding objection -- do real load-bearing work in the plot and in the characters' disagreement, rather than sitting in the background as vocabulary.
