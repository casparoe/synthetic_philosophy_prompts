So this is a slightly weird one but here's the situation: I'm a novelist, and the book I'm working on right now has a subplot about an aging actor who's quietly convinced his whole career has been a kind of moral fraud. Researching that sent me down a rabbit hole into Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert on the theatre, and then, completely separately, my niece's little conservatory drama program asked me to give this year's commencement speech (I think mainly because I'm "the writer" in the family). Now I can't shake the idea that I want to actually build the speech around the Rousseau instead of doing the usual "the world's a stage, go get em" commencement stuff.

Specifically I want two pieces of his actual argument to do real work in the speech, not just get name-dropped: the bit where he says an actor's whole craft is really a trained talent for counterfeiting himself -- being other people on command, feeling passions he doesn't have, saying lines he doesn't believe as naturally as if he did -- and treats that as a different, more suspect kind of skill than, say, a carpenter's; and the bit where he contrasts the theatre with the civic festival, where instead of paying some people to perform feelings for everyone else to sit and watch in the dark, the whole town becomes actor and audience to itself at once, which he thinks is the more honest way for a community to feel something together. I don't want the speech to just set these up so it can swat them down as 18th-century prudishness -- I want it to actually sit with the worry for a minute, because I think theres something real in it, and only then find an honest way through to sending 40-odd kids off to go be professional pretenders for a living without the ending feeling like a con.

The room's going to be maybe half graduating actors and thier families who've never heard of this letter in their lives, and half faculty who've definitely assigned it in seminar and will wince if it gets flattened into a strawman. So can you write me something like six or seven minutes long, speech-shaped rather than essay-shaped, that actually earns its ending instead of just tacking on an inspirational line at the last second?
