While preparing a seminar presentation on higher-order theories of consciousness, I've been trying to get a firm grip on a specific objection to David Rosenthal's higher-order thought (HOT) theory. On Rosenthal's view, a mental state is phenomenally conscious just when it is accompanied by an actual, suitably non-inferential and non-observational higher-order thought to the effect that one is currently in that state, and the qualitative character of the experience is fixed by the content of the HOT rather than by the intrinsic properties of the state the HOT is about. Critics such as Neander and Block have pressed the resulting problem of misrepresentation and, more dramatically, "targetless" HOTs: because the HOT is a distinct representational state from its target, nothing in the theory rules out its occurring when no corresponding first-order state exists at all, in which case Rosenthal still seems committed to saying there is something it is like for the subject — entirely fixed by the HOT's content — even though, by the theory's own lights, there is no conscious state actually present to have that character.

I would like a careful walkthrough of why this is treated as a serious cost for Rosenthal's theory rather than a merely notional possibility that can be shrugged off, including the "screening-off" worry: if the HOT's content does all the work regardless of what, if anything, the target state is doing, the first-order state becomes explanatorily idle with respect to phenomenal consciousness, which seems to undercut the point of a state-based theory in the first place. From there, I would like you to bring in Peter Carruthers's dispositionalist alternative, on which a state acquires its phenomenal character not from an actual accompanying HOT but from its own disposition to cause one — his "dual-content" proposal, which trades on a consumer-semantics account of content. Does relocating the higher-order content into the first-order state's own dispositional profile actually block the possibility of a targetless case, or does it just trade that problem for a different one: namely, whether mere availability to a higher-order system, absent any actual higher-order representing, can do the work Rosenthal's transitivity principle needs done — that is, whether it can really make a subject conscious of being in a state, rather than merely disposed to become so?
