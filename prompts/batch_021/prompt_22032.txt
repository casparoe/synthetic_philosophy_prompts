I spent thirty-five years running an experimental metrology lab before retiring, and only in the last couple of years have I started reading philosophy in any serious way. A friend who teaches epistemology pointed me to Timothy Williamson's *Knowledge and Its Limits* after I complained that most discussions of knowledge I had come across treated "knowing that you know" as an unproblematic bit of bookkeeping. His anti-luminosity argument brought me up short, because it turns on exactly the kind of reasoning I spent my career worrying about at the bench: no instrument, and Williamson argues no mind either, can discriminate between two sufficiently close states, so any judgment resting on such discrimination needs a margin for error before it deserves to be called knowledge. His own illustration concerns a person who gradually warms up over the course of a day from feeling distinctly cold to feeling distinctly hot; via a sorites-style construction he concludes that "feeling cold" is not luminous -- you can be in that state without being in any position to know that you are -- and, more radically, that the KK principle (if you know that P, you are thereby in a position to know that you know that P) fails quite generally once our merely inexact powers of discrimination are taken seriously.

What I haven't been able to settle for myself is how much weight this argument can really bear. It seems to rest on some substantive commitments -- that knowledge always requires a margin for error of this kind, that introspective awareness of a state like feeling cold is relevantly similar to perceptual discrimination of an external quantity, and that the reliability-style condition doing the work is itself the correct analysis of knowledge. I understand each of these has been contested by philosophers who think the argument either proves too much (threatening knowledge quite generally, not merely its luminosity) or trades on peculiarities of specially constructed borderline cases that don't generalize to knowledge, or to the KK principle, as such.

Could you write me a self-contained essay, pitched at an informed non-specialist, that reconstructs Williamson's margin-for-error argument against luminosity and the KK principle as carefully as you can, sets out the strongest objections to it that you can muster, and then commits to an actual verdict on whether the argument succeeds -- entirely, only for a restricted class of states such as inexact introspective judgments, or not at all? A qualified verdict is perfectly fine (something like "it refutes the KK principle for inexact knowledge, but not for exact or canonical cases" would count), but please don't simply lay out both sides and leave the matter open.
