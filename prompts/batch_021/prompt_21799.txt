I spent my career as an analytical chemist, and since retiring I have been teaching myself Bayesian decision theory and philosophy of rational choice as a hobby. I recently came across I. J. Good's 1967 theorem on the value of information, which shows that a rational Bayesian agent should always be willing to consult a costless extra source of evidence before acting, since doing so can never lower the agent's expected utility. Reading further afield in economics, however, I encountered Jack Hirshleifer's well-known 1971 argument that revealing information about a future risk before the affected parties have had a chance to arrange insurance against it can leave everyone worse off, even though the information is free and nobody involved behaves irrationally. I find it difficult to hold both claims in my head at once. Could you explain precisely which background assumption in Good's argument fails to hold in Hirshleifer's scenario, so that the two results turn out not to be in genuine tension after all?
