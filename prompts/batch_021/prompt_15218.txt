Here's the question from the homework: "The table below shows results from two SAT tutoring programs at our school last year, split by whether a student's diagnostic score going in was 'Struggling' (below 1000) or 'Strong' (1000 or above). 'Improved' means the student raised their score by 150+ points on the retake. Program A: Struggling 60/80 improved (75%), Strong 19/20 improved (95%), Total 79/100 improved (79%). Program B: Struggling 13/20 improved (65%), Strong 68/80 improved (85%), Total 81/100 improved (81%). Explain what's going on in this data, say which program you'd actually recommend to a friend choosing between them, and explain the general statistical lesson this illustrates."

And here's my friend's answer, word for word: "This is an example of Simpson's Paradox. If you look at each ability group separately, Program A actually has a higher success rate than Program B: 75% vs 65% for struggling students, and 95% vs 85% for strong students. But when you combine everyone together, Program B looks better overall, 81% vs 79%. This happens because Program A took in way more struggling students, who are less likely to improve no matter which program they're in, while Program B mostly worked with strong students, who are likely to improve regardless of the program. So the overall numbers are misleading, since they're really just reflecting the different mix of students each program happened to get, not the true effectiveness of the programs. Starting ability is a mediating variable that explains the reversal, since it's the mechanism through which the tutoring actually produces its results, so once you take it into account by splitting the groups you get the real picture. This shows that whenever combined data and split-up data disagree like this, you should always trust the split-up numbers, since those are the ones controlling for the lurking variable. So I'd recommend Program A to a friend, since its actually the more effective program once you correct for who each program happens to serve."
