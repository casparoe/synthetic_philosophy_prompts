At a dinner after an AI interpretability talk, I watched two attendees get into a heated argument about whether language models understand anything, and I still can't decide who came out ahead. The trigger was a finding from the talk itself: a transformer trained only to predict the next move in Othello games, fed nothing but bare sequences of moves, turns out to encode the actual state of the board in its internal activations, despite never being shown the board directly (this is the so-called "Othello-GPT" result). (1) An interpretability researcher took this as evidence against the "stochastic parrot" picture: a network trained on nothing but token prediction had spontaneously built an internal model of the hidden state generating those tokens, so language models trained on far richer data plausibly build internal models of what their text is about, not just of Othello boards. (2) A philosopher at the table agreed the result was real but denied it closed the gap the symbol-grounding problem points to: a variable that tracks the board because tracking it helps predict moves is exactly what you'd expect from clever pattern-completion, and it's a long way from there to that variable meaning anything to the system, since nothing the model does is answerable to the board the way a player's beliefs are answerable to actually winning or losing. (3) The researcher replied that this "answerability" demand is a double standard, since much of human concept learning is also secondhand -- absorbed from others' testimony rather than direct contact -- so if the internal representation does the same functional work grounding is meant to secure, it's unclear what is left to demand. (4) The philosopher's last word was that playing a functional role inside a model's own prediction loop is not the same as playing one in a system whose "beliefs" can be corrected by anything besides more text; the board never matters to the model the way it matters to a player who can lose, so it is still an octopus, just a cleverer one.
