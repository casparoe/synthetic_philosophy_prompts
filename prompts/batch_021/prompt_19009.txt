Here's the student's answer, copied over exactly as they wrote it:

"Universalists think that for any two or more objects whatsoever, there is a further object that they compose - so on their view there's a fusion of my left shoe and the moon, weird as that sounds. Nihilists go the opposite way: composition never happens, so strictly speaking there are no tables or planets, just simples 'arranged tablewise' or 'arranged planet-wise.' At first glance these look like a straightforward factual disagreement about what exists. Hirsch's point is that it isn't.

The idea is quantifier variance: 'there exists' doesn't have one single meaning that everyone is stuck using. There are different possible meanings the quantifier could have, some more permissive (universalist) and some more restrictive (nihilist), and neither is uniquely the 'real' meaning of existence. Once the nihilist and the universalist have each fixed a meaning for their quantifier, each side's claims come out true in their own idiom - the nihilist can paraphrase 'there's a table' as 'there are simples arranged tablewise' and agree that's true, and the universalist can agree the fusion of shoe-and-moon is a totally uninteresting, gerrymandered object that ordinary English has no use for and never bothers to quantify over. So both sides can accept a common core of truths, stated in neutral language, and the remaining disagreement is just about which of them gets to use the word 'exists' for the borderline cases. That's what makes it verbal rather than substantive: there's no further question of who's 'right' once meaning is fixed.

Hirsch takes this to be basically Carnap's point about internal and external questions dressed up in updated language. For Carnap, questions asked inside an adopted linguistic framework ('are there tables?') get easy, factual answers, but the question of which framework to adopt in the first place is a pragmatic question, not a factual one - a matter of which framework is more convenient or fruitful, not which one is 'true to reality.' Hirsch is saying the universalist and the nihilist have adopted different frameworks (different quantifier meanings), so the question 'but which framework is metaphysically correct' is exactly the kind of pseudo-question Carnap already taught us to dissolve back in 1950.

The best-known objection is Ted Sider's. Sider takes Hirsch's apparatus of multiple candidate quantifier meanings and adds one further ingredient: he thinks some of those candidate meanings 'carve reality at the joints' better than others, tracking genuine metaphysical structure the way Lewis's natural properties track genuine similarity. Building on that, Sider argues that once we're careful to run the debate using the joint-carving quantifier, there's a real, non-verbal fact about whether unrestricted composition holds - so the universalist/nihilist dispute is substantive after all, and Hirsch was wrong to think fixing any old meaning for 'exists' automatically settles the matter."
