Here's the answer:

"Slurs are a good test case for the semantics/pragmatics divide because everyone agrees they're offensive, but nobody agrees on where the offense actually comes from. Content-based theories try to locate it in what the word means. Christopher Hom's Combinatorial Externalism is the clearest version of this move: the meaning of a slur is fixed externally, by the racist practices and institutions that produced it, and that meaning is expressive rather than descriptive, so uttering a slur is closer to saying "ouch" or "damn" with a nasty edge than to asserting a proposition about someone — a sentence like "X is a [slur]" doesn't really have ordinary truth conditions, it just vents contempt at X's group. This is elegant becuase it explains how visceral slurs feel, but it runs into trouble with fact (1): if the word's whole job is to express the speaker's attitude in the moment of utterance, it's not clear why merely quoting it, without asserting anything about anyone, should carry the same charge.

Potts-style conventional implicature theory does better here. It treats the derogatory content as conventionally attached to the word's meaning as a separate, not-at-issue dimension, so that content projects straight through embeddings and survives even in reported or quoted speech — which is exactly what fact (1) requires. It also handles fact (2) comfortably: a conventional implicature is triggered just by using the word, so it doesn't matter whether the reporter or linguist personally holds the attitude. This is also how reclamation works on this view — when a marginalized speaker uses the term within their own community, they're able to cancel the conventionally implicated contempt, which is why the exact same string of sounds can land completely differently depending on who's talking.

Non-lexical theories deny that any of this is really about meaning. Anderson and Lepore's prohibitionism says there's simply a social rule against uttering the word-form, full stop, whether you're using it to refer to someone or just mentioning it — which handles fact (1) very naturally, since the rule attaches to the string of sounds rather than to any act of reference. It handles fact (2) the same way: the reporter breaks the rule no matter how neutral her attitude is, the same way swearing on the witness stand is out of order no matter how calmly you do it.

On balance I find the non-lexical view more convincing, since prohibitionism gets both facts out of one mechanism instead of needing separate machinery — projection, then cancellation — for each."
