Had a long argument with my friend last night (she's doing a PhD in game theory) about whether two fully rational people can just permanently disagree about something. She kept invoking this "common prior" assumption -- the idea that any two truly rational people must, before they'd seen any evidence at all, have assigned the exact same probabilities to everything, so if they believe different things now it can only be because they've seen different evidence. She used that plus Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree result to argue that if we're both ideal Bayesians and we literally know each others actual probabilities on something (not just our arguments for them), any disagreement still left over means one of us has screwed up somewhere -- and that this is also supposedly why speculative trading shouldn't happen between fully rational people. I said priors are supposed to be your pre-evidence starting point, so nothing requires two rational people to start in the same place, and that demanding a "common prior" just smuggles in an extra assumption that has nothing to do with Bayesian rationality itself. Neither of us budged after like two hours. Write an essay that actually settles this: are ideally rational Bayesian agents required to share a common prior, such that lasting, fully-informed disagreement between rational people is never really possible? Weigh the strongest version of both sides and commit to a real conclusion -- a conditional verdict is fine (e.g., "yes under this notion of rationality, no under that one") but only if you say which notion is actually the right one to use here.
