Been down a rabbit hole on conditionals lately. There's that classic pair from Adams: "If Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, someone else did" sounds true, but "If Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have" sounds false — even tho the antecedent and consequent are basically the same modulo indicative vs subjunctive mood. The standard move on the indicative side (Adams' thesis) is to say these things don't have truth values at all, just an assertability that equals the conditional probability P(consequent|antecedent), and Lewis's triviality results are usually cited as the reason we're basically forced into that non-propositional view. Counterfactuals, meanwhile, just get treated as regular old propositions under the standard Stalnaker/Lewis closest-possible-world semantics.

Can you compare these two approaches for me? Specifically, why don't the triviality results that are supposed to rule out "indicative conditionals are propositions" also rule out the possible-worlds treatment of counterfactuals as propositions — is that just an accident of what the triviality proofs happen to assume, or is there something deeper about counterfactuals that makes them safe from it? And more broadly, does it seem ad hoc to you that indicatives and counterfactuals end up needing totally different kinds of semantics, or is there a principled story for why they'd diverge like that?
