At work we run a small internal forecasting tool where people submit a probability on things like "will this migration ship by Friday" or "will the P1 get resolved within four hours," and we rank everyone each quarter by their Brier score, the average squared difference between the probability given and the actual outcome (0 or 1). I built the tool, so two coworkers keep pulling me into an argument about the leaderboard that I can't settle, since it isn't really a question about the code.

(1) Ana says our top scorer, Wen, almost never forecasts between 20% and 80% -- he's nearly always near 0% or 100% -- and he's right almost every time, so he's simply the best forecaster on the team.

(2) Marcus objects that the leaderboard number alone can't show that: you'd get a similar-looking score from someone who's right most of the time but takes a huge hit on the rare miss, especially over just a handful of calls a quarter. He wants to bucket everyone's "roughly 70%" calls, "roughly 90%" calls, and so on, and check whether each bucket comes true about that often before crowning anyone.

(3) Ana agrees to check that, but adds that between two people who turn out equally well-calibrated by that test, the one willing to commit to 5% or 95% instead of hedging at 50-55% is doing something better, not just riskier -- the willingness to commit is itself valuable, not a separate issue from calibration.

(4) Marcus's real worry, though, is that people quietly push their numbers toward the extremes near quarter-end to climb the board. Ana says that can't be right: since the Brier score is a "proper" scoring rule, nobody can raise their expected score by reporting anything other than what they truly believe, so extreme calls have to reflect honest belief, not strategy.

Please go through the four points in order, say whether each position holds up and why, and close with anything you think both of them are still missing.
