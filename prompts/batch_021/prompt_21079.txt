This term I'm a teaching assistant for an evolutionary theory seminar for the first time, and because I suspect my history with this material has left me either too harsh or too lenient a grader, I'd like an independent, point-by-point read on one of the midterm answers before I finalize the mark.

The question was: "Gould and Lewontin's 'Spandrels' paper is often reduced to the slogan 'not every trait is an adaptation,' which skips over most of what the argument is actually doing. In about 250 words: (a) explain what a spandrel is, in both the architectural sense the paper borrows from and the biological sense it builds toward; (b) explain exactly what this concept is supposed to show is wrong with the 'adaptationist programme'; and (c) explain how 'spandrel' relates to 'exaptation,' with one biological example of a spandrel."

Here is the student's answer: "A spandrel, in architecture, is the roughly triangular surface left over between two adjoining arches and the rectangle framing them, or between an arch and the dome it supports -- the curved triangular sections under the dome of the Basilica of San Marco in Venice are Gould and Lewontin's own example. The spandrel isn't something the architect designs for its own sake; it's a structural by-product of putting a round dome on round arches, and only afterward does someone fill the space with mosaics and treat it as though it had been the whole point. Gould and Lewontin use this as an analogy for biological traits that arise as necessary by-products of some other feature of development or architecture, rather than as direct targets of selection. Their target is the 'adaptationist programme': treating an organism as a bundle of separately optimized traits and, whenever a good adaptive story for some trait isn't obvious, inventing one anyway rather than seriously entertaining that the trait might be a spandrel-like by-product with no adaptive story of its own. They call this the 'Panglossian paradigm,' after the tutor in Voltaire's Candide who insists everything is for the best. A clean example of a spandrel is the male mammalian nipple, which serves no function in males and is simply carried along because early development follows a shared body plan in both sexes. Gould and Lewontin's other lasting contribution in this paper was coining the term 'exaptation' for a trait that now performs a function different from the one it originally evolved for -- feathers, for instance, likely began as insulation and were only later co-opted for flight. So a spandrel is a by-product that never had a selected function of its own, while an exaptation is any trait, spandrel or not, redeployed to a new use; the upshot of the paper is that a great deal of what looks like adaptation is really this kind of secondary co-optation, so natural selection plays a much smaller role in shaping complex traits than the adaptationist programme assumes."
