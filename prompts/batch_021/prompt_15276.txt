This term our department's colloquium series is organized around the theme "the limits of idealized rationality," and I've been asked to chair the question period after a talk by a game theorist who has spent much of his career defending Aumann's result that common knowledge of rationality forces the backward-induction outcome in every game of perfect information -- centipede games included -- regardless of what the experimental record on actual human play shows. His pre-circulated abstract previews the move he's likely to lean on all evening: decades of laboratory subjects passing the token for several rounds are not counterexamples to the theorem, on his reading, but simply evidence that common knowledge of rationality never obtained among those particular subjects -- no more troubling for the result, as he puts it, than a student's arithmetic mistake is for the truths of arithmetic.

I'd like your help drafting nine or ten questions for the session that keep him from simply restating that line. Please include at least one question asking whether this way of reading the antecedent drains the theorem of predictive content, since it lets him absorb any observed deviation by denying that the premise was ever satisfied; one built on the worry, raised by Reny and others, that a player who reaches a node the theory says should never be reached has no principled basis for deciding whether that surprise should still license expecting rational play from her opponent going forward, or whether it should instead be taken as evidence that the common-knowledge assumption behind the original prediction has already collapsed; and one that asks him to weigh the backward-induction prediction against what forward-induction reasoning recommends once an opponent has already passed the token two or three times, and whether surviving that long is itself information a rational player is obliged to use. For each question, please add a short follow-up that anticipates the reply he's most likely to reach for, so that by the end of the sequence the "the antecedent must have failed" move is no longer available to him as a way of ducking the objection.
