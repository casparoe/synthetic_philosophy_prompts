I sit on a small technical advisory panel that helps our national food standards authority resolve labeling disputes before they go out for public comment. The case currently in front of us concerns whether manufacturers of oat-, soy-, and almond-based beverages may keep labeling their products "milk," or whether that word should be reserved for the secretion of the mammary glands of cows, goats, and other mammals, with plant-based products required to use alternatives like "oat drink." The dairy producers' association argues that "milk" has always named one specific biological substance, and that letting the term drift toward a purely functional sense -- a white liquid you pour on cereal -- turns a fact of nature into a matter of marketing; the plant-based industry replies that terms like "coconut milk" and "milk of magnesia" have coexisted with dairy milk for well over a century without confusing anyone, so restricting the word now is protectionism dressed up as precision. At our last meeting, one panelist -- a biochemist by training -- pointed out that cow's, goat's, and human milk already differ substantially in fat and protein content, and wondered aloud whether that meant "milk" had never really named one precise substance to begin with, or whether, on the contrary, what settles the matter isn't convention or majority usage at all but simply what the stuff in the carton is actually made of and where it came from -- the same way it was never really up to us what water is made of. Nobody at the table had a good answer, and I've been asked to have a first draft of our recommendation ready before it goes to the full board next month.

Please draft that recommendation memo. It should come down clearly on one of three options -- preserve the current permissive labeling, restrict "milk" to animal products, or permit shared use subject to conditions such as a mandatory qualifier -- and it should justify the choice by actually working through the question the biochemist raised, rather than resolving it purely by appeal to consumer-survey data or existing regulatory precedent. Whichever option you choose, represent the losing side's strongest argument fairly enough that its proponents would recognize it as their own, not a caricature of it.
