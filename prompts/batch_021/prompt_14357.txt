I spent forty-one years as a marine biologist before I retired, and now that I've got time on my hands I've been reading philosophy properly for the first time, mostly the pragmatists, which probably sounds like a strange direction for an old field biologist but there you go. Which is relevant, because my old field partner Walt died two weeks ago and I'm the one giving the eulogy at his memorial this Saturday, and I've got a problem with it that I'm fairly sure is a real philosophical problem and not just me overthinking things at 2am.

Walt was about as rigorous a scientist as I ever worked alongside -- the sort of man who'd throw out a whole season's data over one bad calibration -- but he was also, quietly, a serious believer, and when a couple of us gave him grief about it at a conference bar once he didnt get defensive. He just said he'd read his Clifford (he meant the "ethics of belief" essay, the one with the shipowner who talks himself into believing his rotten old boat is seaworthy) and he still thought Clifford's rule was wrong for this particular kind of question, and pointed us to William James's essay answering him instead, "The Will to Believe." He was oddly precise about it for a bar conversation -- he'd say something like, whether there's anything backing this life up isn't a question you get to shelve, because refusing to decide is still a way of deciding on a question like that, and given the choice, he'd rather be wrong on the side of hope than spend his whole life safely and correctly withholding judgement. He never once, in the thirty years I knew him, claimed he *knew*. He was careful that way right up to the end, and it mattered to him.

Here's my actual problem. Half the room Saturday is his church, decent people who are going to want to hear, plainly and without hedging, that he's at peace and keeping an eye on things. The other half is old collegues from the department, several of them exactly the kind of hardnosed empiricists who'll privately wince at anything that sounds like faith dressed up as respectable belief. I don't want to hand either side the version they walked in wanting if it means putting something in Walt's mouth he was careful never to say himself. Could you write me the eulogy -- something that runs six or seven minutes read aloud -- that actually uses Walt's own reasoning (the forced, live, momentous stuff, and his point about withholding judgement not being the neutral move people think it is) to do the work, so the thing comes out honest to both halves of the room instead of just splitting the difference between them?
