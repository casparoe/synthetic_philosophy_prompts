I cover medicine and ideas for a national publication, and since early in the pandemic I've also served as the public member on my regional hospital system's crisis-standards-of-care subcommittee, a seat I was offered mainly because I had reported so extensively on ventilator triage. We are now finalizing the system's revised protocol ahead of this year's respiratory-virus season, and we are stuck on one clause. Clinical priority is scored first, using a standard measure of short-term survival odds, but when two patients tie for the last ventilator, the current draft breaks the tie by preferring whichever patient has more expected years of life remaining — a "life-years" rule some on the committee defend by appeal to the "fair innings" principle from health economics. Roughly half of us want to keep some version of this. The other half — an alliance of our two disability-rights representatives and, to my surprise, the bioethicist we brought on as a consultant — wants to replace it with a lottery among clinically tied patients. The disability-rights members mainly worry the rule quietly re-encodes judgments about whose life is worth less; the bioethicist's objection is different, and I think more fundamental. She argues the whole "years of life lost" framework helps itself to exactly the premise Epicurus denied — that we can say how much a death harms someone by pointing to a future that person will never be around to miss — and that once a protocol is actually running the numbers that way, Lucretius's old worry about the asymmetry between mourning the years after a death and shrugging off the years before a birth stops being a classroom puzzle and starts being load-bearing.

Our chair has asked each member to submit a short position memo before the next meeting, and I would rather bring something sharper than "on the one hand, on the other hand." Could you draft that memo for me? It should come down clearly on one side — keep, revise, or drop the life-years tiebreaker — with actual proposed language or a decision procedure a clinician could apply at three in the morning, plus a timeline for adopting it. I want the philosophical dispute engaged, not gestured at: say plainly whether a deprivation account of death's badness in the vein of Nagel can survive the Epicurean and Lucretian worries the bioethicist is raising, and then, whichever way you land, give me the strongest version of what a thoughtful person on the other side would say back — in terms they would recognize as their actual view, not a caricature of it.
