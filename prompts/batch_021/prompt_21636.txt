A friend and I spent last night arguing about Simone Weil's essay "On the Abolition of All Political Parties," and neither of us conceded the point. She thinks Weil's diagnosis is basically right: any organization built to win and hold power will manufacture collective passions, demand a loyalty that displaces members' own judgment of truth and justice, and end up treating its own perpetuation as the real goal beneath whatever program it professes, so anything short of Weil's own remedy of outright abolition is merely cosmetic. I argued that this proves too much for anyone who actually has to build a functioning organization. That's not an abstract question for me right now: I sit on the seven-person committee drafting the founding constitution for a new municipal party ahead of its first council race, and before we present a draft to the full founding convention in three weeks, we have to decide how much internal discipline to build in from the start.

Concretely, the committee is split between binding every elected council member to the caucus's official position on all votes, with censure or deselection for defectors, and guaranteeing a conscience vote on everything except a short, named list of matters such as the annual budget and the party's platform commitments. Please draft the memo I'll bring to the committee: recommend one specific rule rather than a vague compromise, show how it actually answers what Weil identifies as dangerous about party discipline, and give the strongest case for binding discipline a fair hearing rather than dismissing it.
