Been down a rabbit hole on the philosophy-of-language side of slurs lately (for a piece I'm working on about taboo language) and can't stop chewing on this split between what's called "prohibitionism" (that's Anderson and Lepore's term) and the more semantic camps. Prohibitionists say a slur and its neutral counterpart are literally interchangable in meaning -- all the ugliness lives in a social taboo against the word itself, not in what it semantically encodes, kind of like how swearing isn't offensive because of some special truth-condition. The other camp thinks thats backwards: the contempt is actually part of the word's conventional meaning, whether as a presupposition, some non-descriptive "expressive" layer riding on top of the ordinary content, or whatever -- point being, the slur and the neutral term just don't mean the same thing, full stop.

Could you write me a dialogue where a philosopher from each camp really goes at it? Not a softball where one side obviously wins -- I want them equally sharp, actually responding to each other's specific points. Have them test each other with the hard cases: someone who picked the word up from a dictionary with zero idea it's taboo, a slur that's been reclaimed and used affectionately in-group, and what happens to the nastiness when you tuck the word under a negation or a conditional. (Feel free to use a placeholder instead of an actual slur.) Keep it fairly tight.
