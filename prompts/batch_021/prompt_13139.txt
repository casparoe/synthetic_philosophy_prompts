I've been a fairly devoted lurker on LessWrong and the Alignment Forum for years, and a comment thread there last week dragged me back into an argument I thought I'd already settled for myself. Some time ago I read Stuart Russell's *Human Compatible*, and the part that has stuck with me most is the off-switch game he developed with Hadfield-Menell, Dragan, and Abbeel: a robot that remains genuinely uncertain about its own objective, and that treats a human's willingness to hit the off switch as evidence about what that objective actually is, will rationally choose to preserve the human's ability to shut it down, since deferring lets it capture the value of the human's better information. I took this to be a real proof that corrigibility simply falls out of ordinary Bayesian rationality once you build in appropriate humility about your own objective -- nothing needs to be hand-engineered on top of that.

What I've never been able to square is why, both before and after that result was published, people in and around MIRI kept writing as though the shutdown problem were unsolved: first patching agents with Armstrong-style utility indifference in the 2015 corrigibility paper, and then, much more recently, trying to build agents with deliberately incomplete preferences over how long they get to run. When I tried to explain the discrepancy to a friend who works in ML but doesn't follow alignment research, I realized I couldn't actually do it convincingly. If the off-switch game already guarantees that a suitably humble agent won't resist shutdown, what exactly is left for these other two research programs to be doing? Am I overselling what Russell's argument actually establishes, or is the later work just tidying up edge cases that nobody seriously thinks matter?
