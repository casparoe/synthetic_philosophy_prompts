I've spent the last few months teaching myself a bit of philosophy of art, mostly because I play in an amateur string quartet and got curious why so many composers and critics treat instrumental music as somehow more profound than the other arts. That curiosity eventually led me to Schopenhauer, and after working through a secondhand translation of the relevant parts of "The World as Will and Representation," I think I've grasped the basic picture: painting, sculpture, and poetry all present the eternal "Ideas" behind things, while music, oddly, skips that step entirely and connects directly to what he calls the will itself, which is supposedly why he ranks it above every other art. I was particularly struck by the detail that he maps the different parts of a musical texture onto the levels of nature: the bass line standing for bare inorganic matter, the inner harmonizing voices for plant and animal life, and the melody for the conscious striving of human beings.

Given that last point, it seemed to follow that the most "Schopenhauerian" music would be whichever music ties that melodic line most closely and vividly to actual human feeling - something like a grand opera aria where one particular character pours out one particular grief or joy, since that looked to me like the fullest, most intense expression of the human will in melody. So I was thrown off when I came across a comment, in something I was reading about his aesthetics, suggesting that Schopenhauer would actually treat something more abstract and wordless, like a Bach fugue, as the purer or more complete case, which strikes me as backwards. Am I misunderstanding what he means by melody expressing "the will," or is there some other reason that dramatic, text-driven, emotionally specific music wouldn't count as his ideal example? I would like to sort this out before I bring it up with my quartet friends, one of whom studied music history and will certainly ask me to defend myself.
