I work as a machine learning engineer at a small fintech startup, and for the last couple of months I've been building a "counterfactual explanation" feature for our loan-approval model: when someone is rejected, the system generates a sentence like "if your annual income had been $8,000 higher, you would have been approved." A teammate and I got into an argument about whether these explanations are actually honest, given that the model was only ever trained and evaluated on real, historical (i.e., non-counterfactual) applications, and that sent me down a rabbit hole into how philosophers of language treat conditionals. I hadn't realized until now that "if P, then Q" statements split into indicative conditionals ("if it is/was the case that P, then Q") and counterfactual or subjunctive conditionals ("if it had been the case that P, then Q would have been the case"), and that a good deal of serious philosophical work treats these as needing genuinely different logical treatments rather than both just reducing to an if-then statement the way they would in code.

The example that really got me is a classic minimal pair, apparently due to Ernest Adams: "If Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, someone else did" seems obviously true, while "If Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have" seems clearly false, even though the two sentences look like they only differ in grammatical mood. From some further reading, it seems Adams' own response is to say indicative conditionals are governed by conditional probability (the assertability of the conditional tracks P(consequent | antecedent)) rather than ordinary truth conditions, while Stalnaker and Lewis instead gave counterfactuals truth conditions in terms of which is the "closest" possible world where the antecedent holds. Lewis then separately argued, via his so-called triviality results, that you can't cleanly import that conditional-probability story back into a truth-conditional semantics without absurd consequences. I won't pretend to follow all of the technical machinery, but the underlying puzzle bugs me, because it looks uncomfortably close to something my own team is quietly glossing over.

Could you walk through the most important implications of the indicative/counterfactual distinction for someone building (or evaluating) tools like ours? I'm not looking for a tour of the different philosophical camps for its own sake — I'd like to know what actually follows from taking the distinction seriously: for instance, whether it exposes a real ambiguity or unearned assumption in the kind of sentence our tool outputs, whether the disagreement between the "Adams" camp and the "Stalnaker/Lewis" camp should make me more or less confident that a counterfactual explanation can even be well-defined for a model trained purely on indicative, historical data, and what, if anything, an engineer in my position should actually do differently as a result.
