Leibniz's big dream was that if we had a proper universal calculus of reasoning (the whole characteristica universalis / calculus ratiocinator project), disputes -- moral, legal, metaphysical, whatever -- could just get settled by calculation instead of endless argument, kind of like two accountants checking a ledger. Say you could sit him down and explain modern computational complexity theory: specifically that a problem being decidable in principle (theres some algorithm that eventually spits out the right answer) doesnt mean its actually feasible to run, and that theres possibly a deep gap between checking an answer and finding one in the first place (the P vs NP thing). Do you think this breaks Leibniz's dream in a worse way than what Godel and Turing already did to it, or does he have a decent comeback? What do you reckon his actual reaction would've been if someone walked him through it?
