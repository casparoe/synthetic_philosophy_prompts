Since retiring from a long career in evolutionary genetics, I have taken up the history and philosophy of science in earnest, and Karl Popper's tangled relationship with Darwinism has proven to be a particularly satisfying rabbit hole. As I understand it, the early Popper of "Unended Quest" and "Objective Knowledge" was quite hostile to natural selection's scientific credentials, at one point calling it "almost tautological" and downgrading the whole Darwinian picture to a "metaphysical research programme" rather than a properly testable theory, on the grounds that "survival of the fittest" seemed to explain outcomes only after the fact, without licensing any risky, falsifiable predictions in advance. That line is still quoted approvingly by creationists today, usually without mentioning that Popper himself publicly recanted it in a widely discussed 1978 lecture, in which he conceded that natural selection could indeed generate genuinely testable claims.

What I keep wondering is how the recanted, post-1978 Popper would have responded to a body of evidence that simply did not exist in his lifetime: Richard Lenski's long-term evolution experiment with E. coli, quietly running since 1988 and therefore already several years old when Popper died in 1994, though its most striking results only emerged later. I have in mind especially two findings — the 2008 discovery that one lineage evolved the ability to metabolize citrate only after a specific, historically contingent sequence of earlier "potentiating" mutations had first accumulated, and the subsequent finding that the fitness gains of these populations, tracked across tens of thousands of generations, kept climbing according to a power-law curve that had been proposed in advance rather than leveling off, as a naive reading of "filling an ecological niche" might have suggested.

Given how much weight Popper's early skepticism placed on the charge of unfalsifiability, and given that he explicitly reversed himself on this point sixteen years before he died, I'd be grateful for your best reconstruction of his likely verdict on the Lenski experiment. Would he have treated it as precisely the sort of cumulative record of bold, corroborated predictions that vindicated his recantation, or would its emphasis on brute historical contingency have tempted him back toward something like his original worry — that Darwinian explanations, however quantitatively precise, remain better at rationalizing whichever outcome occurred than at telling us in advance which of many possible outcomes would? I would also value your honest opinion on whether Popper's post-1978 position was coherent enough to yield a determinate answer at all, or whether his writings on this topic simply pull in two directions that he never fully reconciled.
