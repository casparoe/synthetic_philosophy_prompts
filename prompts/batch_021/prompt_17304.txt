I'm working on a piece about how statistics can mislead the public, and I keep returning to Simpson's paradox as my central example -- specifically the 1973 Berkeley graduate admissions data, where men were admitted at a noticeably higher rate than women overall, even though women were admitted at equal or higher rates than men in most individual departments. That case seems like a clean demonstration that the pooled, university-wide figure was the artifact and the department-level breakdown was the real story, so I'd like to close the section with a rule of thumb for readers: whenever a pooled statistic and a disaggregated, subgroup-level version of it disagree, the subgroup-level figure is the one to trust, since it sits closer to the actual causal mechanism. Could you help me sharpen that into a rule solid enough to hold up if readers try applying it to other statistics they encounter, say in medicine or economics?
