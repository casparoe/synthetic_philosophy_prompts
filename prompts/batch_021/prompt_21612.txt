The centipede game is the standard test case for how much weight "common knowledge of rationality" can bear in game theory. Two players alternate moves over a fixed number of rounds. At each turn, the player to move can "take," ending the game at once and claiming the larger share of the pot as it then stands, or "pass," which grows the pot for both but risks the other player taking on the very next move and leaving the passer worse off than if they had simply taken themselves; if both players pass all the way to the end of the game, however, they end up considerably better off than under immediate termination. Because the pot is finite, backward induction from the last round unravels the whole game: standard subgame-perfect analysis has the first player taking on the very first move, even though both would do far better by passing for a while. In 1995, Robert Aumann published a proof that this unraveling is not merely an artifact of a particular solution concept but a genuine theorem: given a fairly standard formalization of "knowledge" as a partition of a state space and of "rationality" as never knowingly choosing an action one knows to be inferior, common knowledge of rationality among the players logically forces the backward-induction outcome in any generic game of perfect information. Ken Binmore replied to Aumann directly in the pages of Games and Economic Behavior, and others working on the epistemic foundations of game theory, such as Cristina Bicchieri and Philip Reny, have pressed a related worry: if the backward-induction path is really what common knowledge of rationality delivers, then a single pass by player one already seems to falsify, for player two, the very common knowledge supposedly doing the work of predicting what happens afterward — so it is unclear what licenses continuing to reason "as if" rationality remains common knowledge from that point on. Aumann's response is to insist that the relevant "knowledge" is knowledge of strategies (complete, counterfactual plans of action for the whole game) rather than of the history actually observed, so that a pass is not evidence against anyone's rationality at all; critics regard exactly this move as either draining the theorem of its intended epistemic content or smuggling the desired conclusion back in through the formalism.

This dispute keeps surfacing in discussions of epistemic game theory, and I have yet to see it worked out as a direct exchange rather than summarized from one side. Please write a dialogue between two equally sophisticated epistemic game theorists, sketching the centipede game's tree on a whiteboard as they argue it out: one who holds, with Aumann, that the theorem genuinely vindicates backward induction as the rational outcome and that the "falsification" worry trades on a confusion about which notion of knowledge is at stake, and one who holds, with his critics, that the theorem's notion of knowledge is idealized in a way that empties it of the justificatory force usually claimed for backward induction reasoning. Have the two engage directly with each other's strongest formulations rather than restating their own position — the defender should offer more than "the theorem is valid," and the critic more than "that seems paradoxical" — and feel free to let the dialogue end without a tidy resolution, since the underlying dispute has not obviously been settled in the literature either.
