I spent my career as an experimental physicist and have only taken up moral philosophy seriously since retiring, so forgive me if the framing below is more literal-minded than a philosopher's would be. I sit on my old Oxford college's honorary fellowships committee, and we've been asked to award a posthumous fellowship to an alumnus who, as a wartime prime minister, overruled his general staff's preference for a prolonged blockade and personally ordered a single air raid on a crowded civilian market in the enemy capital, correctly calculating that several thousand deaths would break the enemy government's will within days and end a war that intelligence otherwise projected would cost far more lives, on both sides, over another year of fighting. Most of the committee treats this as exactly the kind of hard, consequentially defensible choice the college should be willing to honour. Two dissenters invoke Elizabeth Anscombe's old objection to Oxford's granting Truman a degree: that killing innocents as a means is murder whatever the balance sheet, and that honouring him now would dignify that reasoning. Please draft the short memo I'll circulate before next week's vote: a clear recommendation, argued in a way that genuinely engages the dissenters' case rather than simply re-running the utilitarian arithmetic against it.
