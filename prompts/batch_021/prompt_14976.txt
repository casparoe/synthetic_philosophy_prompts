I'm about a third of the way into a historical novel set during the chemical revolution of the late eighteenth century, built around the rivalry between a phlogiston theorist modeled loosely on Priestley and a convert to the new oxygen chemistry modeled loosely on Lavoisier. I've fictionalized the names and taken some liberties with the biography, so I'm not bound too tightly to the historical record, though I want the intellectual conflict itself to track the real one closely. The novel's centerpiece is a long, escalating argument between the two of them at a dinner party, and I want that scene to dramatize something like genuine Kuhnian incommensurability, rather than an ordinary factual disagreement dressed up in eighteenth-century costume.

The trouble is that when I read an early draft of the scene to my writing group, one member, a former philosophy student, told me I'd fallen into one of two traps she associates with popular treatments of Kuhn. Either my chemists were simply being stubborn and talking past each other for personal or institutional reasons, which isn't really incommensurability at all. Or I had made them so mutually unintelligible that they might as well have been speaking different languages, which she said is a strawman even Kuhn came to resist in his later writing, where he narrowed the notion down to what he called "local" incommensurability. I've since reread the relevant chapters of The Structure of Scientific Revolutions, and I notice that my two chemists do seem to mean something subtly different by shared words like "principle" or "element," not just by "phlogiston" or "oxygen" themselves, but I can't articulate the actual mechanism at work here. What, precisely, is supposed to be untranslatable or unshared between the two paradigms, if it is neither the raw experience of watching a candle gutter out in a sealed jar nor mere pigheadedness on either side? I would like you to lay out, in some technical detail, what Kuhn meant by incommensurability in a case like this, distinguishing it clearly from ordinary disagreement on one hand and total mutual unintelligibility on the other, and to explain how a phlogistonist and an oxygen theorist could still, on Kuhn's own account, communicate with real effort, translate each other approximately, and even have principled reasons for eventually favoring one paradigm over the other, without any of that adding up to a neutral shared framework in which their dispute could simply be settled.
