I'm a second-year philosophy PhD student TAing an intermediate epistemology seminar this term, and I'm partway through grading the take-home midterm. One question asks students to work through a Gettier-style case and evaluate how a couple of standard responses to the Gettier problem handle it. Before I finalize a grade on one particular submission, I'd like a careful, part-by-part check rather than a holistic impression, both so my comments stay consistent with how I've graded the rest of the stack and so they hold up if the student contests them at office hours.

Here is the question as I wrote it:

"Elena is an art appraiser asked to authenticate a small painting drawn from a batch of provenance-uncertain works that a provincial museum recently pulled out of storage, believed to include at least one original seventeenth-century Dutch painting. Using connoisseurship methods she has relied on successfully throughout her career — brushwork analysis, pigment testing, examination under ultraviolet light — she examines the painting she was handed and concludes, 'This is a genuine seventeenth-century Dutch painting.' She is right. What she has no way of knowing is that most of the other paintings in that same batch are exceptionally good forgeries, produced decades earlier by a now-deceased local forger whose technique reliably fools the very tests Elena used, and that the museum's director handed the paintings out to appraisers essentially at random. Had Elena been given almost any other painting from the batch, she would have used exactly the same methods to reach the same confident, false verdict. As it happens, though, the one she was actually given is one of the few genuine pieces.

Suppose, as most epistemologists confronted with this style of case conclude, that Elena does not know that the painting is genuine, for reasons structurally analogous to the standard 'environmental luck' Gettier cases discussed in the literature. Taking that verdict as the thing to be explained, answer the following:

(a) Explain why this case poses a problem for the traditional justified-true-belief analysis of knowledge, saying precisely which of the three JTB conditions are, and are not, satisfied.

(b) The 'no false lemmas' response to the Gettier problem holds that a justified true belief fails to amount to knowledge only when it was inferred from some falsehood along the way. Does this response correctly explain why Elena lacks knowledge in this case?

(c) Process reliabilism holds, roughly, that a belief is justified, and, together with truth, amounts to knowledge, just in case it was produced by a cognitive process with a high truth ratio. Does reliabilism, in that simple form, correctly predict that Elena lacks knowledge? Explain, and say what, if anything, a reliabilist should add to the theory in light of this case."

And here is the answer I need to grade:

"(a) All three conditions of the JTB analysis look satisfied here, and that is exactly what makes this a Gettier case: the belief is true (the painting really is a genuine period piece), Elena believes it, and her belief is justified, since she used rigorous methods that have served her reliably throughout her career and she had no reason to suspect anything was wrong. Since JTB is satisfied even though, we are told, Elena does not know, this shows the three conditions are not jointly sufficient for knowledge — essentially the same moral as Gettier's own fake-barn cases, where a subject's justified true belief that a given structure is a barn fails to be knowledge because the surrounding countryside is full of indistinguishable facades.

(b) The no-false-lemmas theory cannot explain this case. On that view, a justified true belief fails to be knowledge only when some falsehood entered the chain of reasoning leading to it. But no falsehood enters Elena's reasoning at any point: her premises about the brushwork, the pigments, and the UV results are all true, and her inference from them is sound. Since there is no false lemma to point to, the theory has no resources to explain her lack of knowledge here, and taken on its own would wrongly certify her belief as knowledge.

(c) Reliabilism fares better than the no-false-lemmas theory. Reliability, on this view, is a property of a process-type across its uses, not of any single application of it, and Elena's connoisseurship process has an excellent track record: she has correctly authenticated dozens of paintings over her career using exactly these methods. Because the process that produced her belief is reliable in that sense, and the belief is true, reliabilism locates Elena's epistemic standing in the general reliability of her method rather than in the presence or absence of some specific falsehood, and so, unlike the no-false-lemmas theory, handles this case without needing any further modification."

Could you grade this part by part? Then give a total out of 15, a corresponding letter grade, and a one-line summary comment suitable for the rubric.
