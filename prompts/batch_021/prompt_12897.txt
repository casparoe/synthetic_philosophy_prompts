I've been thinking about Carnap's politics again after a long thread on a rationalist forum revisited the old question of whether cheap computation finally answers Hayek's "knowledge problem" objection to central planning — the claim, popular among people rereading Kantorovich's work on linear programming, that sufficiently good optimization algorithms plus enough data could now do what Gosplan-era planners couldn't. Most people who know Carnap only through the "explication" and "principle of tolerance" material that circulates in rationalist writing don't seem to register that he had real, documented political commitments. His "Intellectual Autobiography" describes an enduring sympathy for socialism dating back to his youth; the 1929 Vienna Circle manifesto he co-authored explicitly ties the "scientific world-conception" to progressive, anti-nationalist politics and treats metaphysics as the natural ally of reactionary movements; and his closest collaborator, Neurath, had literally worked on in-kind economic planning, first for wartime Austria and then briefly running the central planning office of the Bavarian Soviet Republic, which made him the direct target of Mises's 1920 essay that touched off the socialist calculation debate in the first place.

Given all that, I would like your best reconstruction of how Carnap himself, rather than Neurath, would have reacted to today's computational-planning-revival arguments, and I want an actual verdict rather than a list of considerations on both sides. On one hand, Carnap's personal sympathies and his intellectual closeness to Neurath suggest he would have welcomed the claim that better computation finally vindicates planning. On the other, Mises's own economics was explicitly and self-consciously an a priori "praxeology," deduced from the axiom of purposive action rather than resting on anything empirically testable — exactly the kind of unverifiable, quasi-metaphysical methodology Carnap spent his career attacking, independently of whatever political conclusions it was used to support. Would that same allergy to unverifiable claims have made Carnap just as unimpressed by confident assertions that an algorithm can now "solve" the planning problem — itself a strong, largely unverified empirical claim — as he was by Mises's a priori insistence that markets alone could solve it? Please commit to one answer, and explicitly flag which parts of your reasoning rely on Carnap's documented positions and which are your own extrapolation.
