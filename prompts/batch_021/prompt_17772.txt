So we just got through Skolem's paradox in my philosophy of math seminar and I feel like I can recite the "resolution" on command but I'm not totally sure I believe it's as philosophically boring as everyone in class is acting like it is. Quick recap so you know where I'm coming from: downward Lowenheim-Skolem says that if ZFC is consistent it has a countable model, since the language of set theory is countable. But ZFC also proves Cantor's theorem, so it proves P(N) is uncountable, meaning ANY model of ZFC -- including that countable one -- satisfies "there is no bijection between N and P(N)". The standard move is to point out that from outside the countable model there obviously is such a bijection, since the whole model only has countably many elements total (including whatever it calls P(N)), its just that this particular bijection isn't itself an object living inside the model, so the model has no way to "see" it and correctly judges itself to be missing one. Our TA presented this as basically case closed, like it's a cute fact about first-order logic and nothing more. But my professor mentioned, almost as a throwaway line, that Skolem himself took it a lot more seriously than that -- something like, he thought it showed that notions like "uncountable" (maybe "set" more generally) can't be pinned down absolutely by any axiomatization, only relative to whichever model you're in, and that Putnam later leaned on more or less this same move for his model-theoretic argument against metaphysical realism.

That gap -- "boring resolved technicality" vs. "actually threatens realism about set-theoretic notions" -- is what I want to dig into for my term paper instead of just re-deriving the model theory again. Could you write me an essay that actually stakes out a side: does Skolem's paradox, properly understood, give any real support to a relativist/anti-realist reading of set-theoretic notions (the Skolem/Putnam-ish line), or does the internal-vs-external-bijection resolution fully drain the paradox of philosophical content, so that realists have nothing left to worry about? I'd like you to actually engage with why smart people ended up disagreeing about this -- it's clearly not just that one side didn't understand the model theory, since Skolem obviously understood it better than almost anyone -- rather than just declaring one camp confused and moving on.
