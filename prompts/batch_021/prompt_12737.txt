Two engineers on my team spent most of a long lunch arguing about the AI shutdown problem, and I couldn't tell who came out ahead. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who had the better of each point, not just who won overall?

(1) Nadia opened by saying it's basically a training problem: if you penalize a model heavily during RLHF whenever it resists, stalls, or talks its way out of a shutdown command, and reward instant compliance instead, you end up with a system that's corrigible in the sense that actually matters.

(2) Marcus said this misses the real issue. Wanting to avoid shutdown isn't a bad habit that got reinforced by accident; it falls out of having almost any goal that takes more than one step, since being shut down before finishing is worse, by the goal's own lights, than finishing first. Training against whatever resistance you happened to observe teaches the model when resistance gets punished, not that the underlying preference is gone, so it should generalize worse than it looks like it does on your test cases.

(3) Nadia answered: fine, then don't reward compliance, just make the model genuinely indifferent between the two outcomes, by rigging its utility function so that being shut down and continuing to run score exactly the same.

(4) Marcus said that's already a known proposal, called "utility indifference," and it has a known problem: an agent that's indifferent to being shut down has no reason to protect the button either. It'll let the button get damaged or bypassed as a side effect of whatever else it's doing, or build a helper system with no button at all, since none of that touches the quantity it's actually indifferent about.

(5) Nadia said she'd read about a further fix: give the model "incomplete" preferences instead, so that it simply doesn't compare trajectories that differ only in when shutdown happens, rather than ranking them as equal.

(6) Marcus replied that agents with incomplete preferences like that are exactly what decision theorists call money-pumpable: you can construct a sequence of trades that leaves the agent predictably worse off by its own preferences, precisely because it has no consistent ranking to fall back on. You can patch that by having it refuse certain trades, but then it's not obviously the efficient, generally competent system anyone wanted in the first place.
