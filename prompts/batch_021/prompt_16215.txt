I cover science and ideas for a general-interest outlet, and that beat recently got me invited onto the outside content-review committee for a mid-sized science museum's new permanent hall on cosmology, which opens this spring. The exhibit's centerpiece script walks visitors through several instances of physical "fine-tuning" -- the improbably narrow range of the cosmological constant, the delicacy of the force that binds nucleons, and Fred Hoyle's famous prediction (later confirmed) of a carbon-12 resonance needed for stars to make carbon at all -- and then poses, more or less explicitly, the question of why these values rather than the vastly more numerous life-prohibiting ones. The committee is stuck. One faction, including the development director, wants the section cut or reduced to a caption: partly over how it might read to donors and school groups in a state where the last intelligent-design curriculum fight is barely behind us, but also on the more principled worry that neither the design inference nor the multiverse response most physicists reach for in its place is a falsifiable scientific claim, so the whole debate sits closer to metaphysics than to the settled science the museum otherwise sticks to. A second faction, including two of the staff physicists, thinks cutting it is its own kind of dishonesty: fine-tuning is a live question among working cosmologists and philosophers of science, not just a creationist talking point, and dropping it -- or worse, gesturing at it without properly working through the standard responses (observer selection effects, the multiverse, the reference-class problem lurking in any claim about how "improbable" these values are) -- would shortchange visitors on a genuinely interesting piece of the current scientific picture.

I need to bring the committee a short written recommendation before next Tuesday's meeting, when the exhibit text is due to be locked for fabrication. Could you draft that memo? It should come down on one concrete option -- cut it, keep it substantially as planned, or keep it with a specific reframing you describe -- and it should give reasons the physicists and the development director could each actually engage with, including a fair, non-caricatured version of whichever side it doesn't side with.
