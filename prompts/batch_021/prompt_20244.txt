So I'm part of this little philosophy discussion group I joined after retiring (35 years in expiremental physics, philosophy's my new hobby), and two of the regulars, Mara and Dov, got into it last week over confirmation theory in a way that's been bugging me since.

Mara brought up what she called the "problem of old evidence": the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion had been a known fact for more than fifty years before Einstein's general relativity explained it in 1915, and physicists at the time treated that explanation as huge confirmation of GR. But, she said, on plain Bayesian conditionalization that makes no sense -- if you already assign the precession fact probability (essentially) 1, then learning that GR predicts it can't raise your credence in GR one bit, since conditioning on something your already certain of changes nothing. So either the physicists were confused, or textbook Bayesian updating just can't capture how science actually works.

Dov said there's a standard fix for this: what really updates isn't your probability in the precession fact itself, it's your probability that GR entails that exact value. Nobody, not even Einstein for a stretch, knew for certain the theory implied that number until the derivation was actually done, so that logical fact is the genuinely new evidence, and conditioning on it is perfectly ordinary Bayesian updating.

Mara wasn't having it: she said standard Bayesian probability assumes agents are logically omniscient, automatically assigning probability 1 to anything their beliefs entail. The second you let someone be uncertain about what a theory implies, you've dropped that assumption, and you need a genuinely different formal setup just to make sense of "learning" a logical fact. So this isn't a fix within Bayesianism, she said, it's an admission that the classical theory just doesn't cover this case.

Dov's comeback: no actual scientist is logically omniscient anyway, so a confirmation theory that assumes otherwise was already useless for real science regardless of old evidence, and building in room for logical learning is honest repair, not some special-case dodge invented to save Einstein's reputation.

Neither budged and it got left hanging when we had to clear out of the cafe. Could you go through this move by move and say who's got the better of each specific point -- not just declare an overall winner? And is there some more basic thing about evidence or confirmation that you think both of them are missing?
