In a graduate seminar on moral psychology this semester, we've spent several weeks working through the literature on what's usually called "moral worth" — the question of what makes an action not merely right but creditable to the agent who performs it. The seminar keeps circling back to Kant's distinction between acting "from duty" and acting merely "in conformity with duty," illustrated by his notorious shopkeeper who deals honestly with customers purely out of self-interest, such that the action is right without thereby having any moral worth. What I'm finding genuinely hard to pin down is how differently contemporary philosophers, who all reject the crude reading on which the agent has to be occurrently thinking something like "this is my duty," end up cashing out what moral worth requires instead. Barbara Herman tries to save something recognizably Kantian by treating the motive of duty as a standing "limiting condition" on one's other motives, rather than as the content of one's motivating thought on any given occasion, so that ordinary loving or sympathetic action can still count as dutiful. Julia Markovits goes further with her "coincident reasons" thesis, on which moral worth requires only that the reasons that explain your action are the very reasons that justify it, with no thought about duty or rightness needing to enter your head at all. And Nomy Arpaly pushes furthest with her discussion of Huckleberry Finn, who helps Jim escape while sincerely believing, on the strength of his upbringing, that he is doing something shameful; she takes this case of what she and others call "inverse akrasia" to show that moral worth survives even when the agent's own explicit judgment about the rightness of the act is mistaken. My advisor is fairly sympathetic to Herman's reading, but I've never found it fully persuasive, and I would like to think the whole spectrum through more rigorously than we have managed in seminar.

Could you write an essay that works through this dispute and defends a considered position on it? I would like it to address, in particular, whether moral worth requires anything like a de dicto belief that one's action is right — even in Herman's attenuated, dispositional sense — or whether the Huckleberry Finn case shows that a purely de re responsiveness to the reasons that actually make an act right is enough on its own, leaving no role for the agent's own judgment about rightness to play. If you land on the latter view, please also say whether Markovits's coincident-reasons account already accommodates the Huckleberry Finn case as it stands, or needs to be amended to do so. I would also like the essay to take seriously the alienation-style objections that Michael Stocker and Bernard Williams have pressed against motive-of-duty views generally — the thought that consulting duty, rather than simply caring directly about one's friend or spouse, involves "one thought too many" — since any adequate account of moral worth has to explain why it does not just collapse into lucky conformity with the reasons that happen to matter. Please do not leave the positions in equilibrium: I would like the essay to end with a clear, explicitly defended verdict, even if it is a qualified one.
