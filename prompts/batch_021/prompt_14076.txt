A friend and I spent a long evening last week arguing about evolutionary debunking arguments in metaethics -- the family of arguments, associated above all with Sharon Street, holding that if our moral beliefs were shaped by natural selection for reproductive fitness rather than for tracking any independent moral truth, it would be an extraordinary coincidence if those beliefs turned out to be reliably true, so that a moral realist owes some account of the connection that mere selection pressure cannot by itself supply. My friend's contribution, which I haven't been able to shake since, was that this whole dialectic is basically already latent in Darwin, and that his chapter on the moral sense in The Descent of Man reads, structurally, like a debunking argument that Darwin himself never recognized as one. I went back to the text afterward to check for myself, and got stuck on one particular stretch of it.

The passage comes from Chapter IV of The Descent of Man (1871), right after Darwin has argued that our sense of obligation arises simply because the social instincts are more enduring and more constantly present than transient appetites like hunger or vengeance, so that giving in to the transient one leaves behind a felt discord once the enduring instinct reasserts itself in memory -- a discord Darwin identifies with remorse. Having established this, and having described how habitual self-command eventually lets the passions defer to the social instincts with no felt resistance, he writes that "the imperious word ought seems merely to imply the consciousness of the existence of a rule of conduct" -- adding immediately, "however it may have originated." A few lines later he extends exactly the same treatment to a gun dog, remarking that we are just as entitled to say that a pointer ought to point or a retriever ought to retrieve, and that a dog who fails to do so has failed in its duty.

What I can't settle is how much weight that passage will bear. Defining "ought" purely in terms of which instinct happens to be more persistent, and insisting that this holds no matter how the rule in question originated, looks like it concedes the debunker's whole point in advance: it makes the normative-sounding authority of conscience nothing but an artifact of relative instinct-strength, leaving no room for any further question about whether the winning instinct is tracking anything true. But a few pages earlier in that same chapter, Darwin is explicit that only a creature capable of comparing and judging its own past and future conduct counts as a moral being at all -- which, on its face, should rule out any literal reading of the dog example. So is the dog case doing real philosophical work for Darwin, or is it just a loose figure of speech he doesn't intend to bear the weight I want to put on it? And either way, does that qualifying clause -- that the rule's authority holds however it may have originated -- commit him, whether he intended it or not, to the same debunking structure that Street and others would later make explicit?
