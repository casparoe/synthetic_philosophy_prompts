I went down a bit of a rabbit hole this week reading about the Boltzmann brain problem in cosmology -- the argument that if the universe settles into an eternal de Sitter phase (basically permanent accelerated expansion from a positive cosmological constant), random thermal/quantum fluctuations in the vacuum will, given enough time, cook up way more isolated "brains" with false memories of an orderly past than there are actual observers who evolved the normal way. People use this as a reductio against cosmological models (eternal inflation, certain multiverse setups) that predict this kind of eternal future. Then I realized Boltzmann basically ran into a baby version of this exact issue back in the 1890s: when Zermelo hit him with the recurrance objection, Boltzmann's fallback was to argue that the universe (or most of it) is in equilibrium almost all the time, and we just happen to live inside a rare, temporary low-entropy fluctuation, since creatures like us could only exist during such a fluctuation in the first place. I vaguely remember it didn't take long for people to point out the awkward corollary -- that a small fluctuation (just enough to produce a brain with illusory memories of a whole coherent history) is astronomically more probable than one big enough to produce a whole ordered universe -- which is basically the seed of the modern problem.

So here's what I'm actually curious about: given how Boltzmann actually reasoned about probability, statistical mechanics, and the arrow of time, what do you think he'd say if you sat him down and showed him how "his" argument gets used today? Would he just bite the bullet the way he seemed willing to with weird fluctuation-observers back then, would he push back by pointing to some disanalogy between his gas-in-a-box setup and quantum fields on an eternally expanding de Sitter background, or would he zero in on the self-undermining structure of the whole argument -- i.e., that if you take it seriously it seems to wreck your grounds for believing the cosmological theory that generated it in the first place? Don't just give me the standard "here's what a Boltzmann brain is" explainer, I want you to actually dig into how his own reasoning would carry over (or not).
