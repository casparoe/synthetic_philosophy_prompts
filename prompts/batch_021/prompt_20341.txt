I've gotten really interested in Kurt Gödel this term, first because of the incompleteness theorems in my math class, and then because I found out he held some pretty unusual personal beliefs about life after death, which he apparently defended with something closer to a philosophical argument than a religious one. I'm putting together a short piece on this for my school's philosophy newsletter, and before I go further I'd like feedback on the reconstruction of his reasoning below, which I've pieced together from a few different sources and might not have entirely right.

As I understand it, Gödel was a strict rationalist in Leibniz's mold who held that the world as a whole is completely intelligible and that nothing in it is ultimately arbitrary or pointless. This seems to have had a striking consequence for him: if a person spends decades developing their mind and character only to be totally annihilated at death, an enormous amount of the world's structure would turn out to be pointless after all, which conflicts with the world being rational in his strong sense. He apparently pressed something like this argument in letters meant to reassure his own mother, who was more religiously skeptical than he was, and separate notes a logician named Hao Wang took down from conversations with him late in life list convictions along similar lines, including the idea that there are other worlds with more advanced rational beings and that our present life is not the only one we will ever have. All of this seems distinct from his separate, better-known formal proof of God's existence, which I don't think was really about personal survival at all.

What I can't decide is whether the reasoning actually works. It looks to me like Gödel is quietly sliding from "the world is completely intelligible" to "the world never lets anything of value go to waste," and those don't strike me as the same claim, since plenty of fully explicable processes, like decay and extinction, look wasteful without being unintelligible. Is that the strongest objection available here, or is there a more basic problem with this kind of argument that I'm not seeing? I would also welcome corrections if I have gotten the history itself wrong somewhere.
