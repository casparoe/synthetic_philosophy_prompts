Hi! I'm a retired biologist who's recently taken up philosophy as a retirement hobby (turns out I have a lot more patience for dense old prose now than I did in grad school). I've been slowly working through Kant's Critique of Judgement and got completely stuck on section 65, the bit about organisms as "natural purposes." Here's the passage, stitched together a bit from an old translation I found online (might be Bernard's, might be Meredith's, didn't check closely):

"...a thing exists as a natural purpose if it is, though in a double sense, both cause and effect of itself... In such a product of nature every part is thought as owing its presence to the agency of all the remaining parts, and also as existing for the sake of the others and of the whole, that is, as an instrument, or organ... The part must be an organ producing the other parts—each part, consequently, reciprocally producing the others. No instrument of art can answer to this description, but only the instrument of nature, which supplies material even to the instruments of art. Only under these conditions and upon these terms can such a product be an organized and self-organizing being, and, as such, be called a natural purpose."

I get the rough idea: organisms aren't like clocks because the parts kind of make and sustain each other, rather then being assembled from outside. What I can't work out is what Kant thinks that "cause and effect of itself" bit is actually doing philosophically. Here's my real question though: I've since picked up a bit of the modern philosophy-of-biology literature on function, where people distinguish etiological accounts (a trait's function is whatever it was selected for) from causal-role accounts (its function is whatever it currently contributes to some capacity of the containing system). Is old Kant here basically groping towards one of those two views, or is he up to something neither camp quite captures? Give me your actual take on this, not just a potted history of the two camps.
