I've followed Robin Hanson's writing on prediction markets and futarchy for years, and I sit on the "epistemics committee" that runs an internal conditional market at the small research nonprofit where I work: staff trade play-money contracts on questions like "if we fund Project X, will it hit its 12-month milestone," and funding follows the price once a minimum volume threshold is reached.

A live dispute needs a memo before Monday. Elena, who has championed Project Orion for a year, wants to take a large position pushing its "yes" price well above where everyone else has it. Half the committee calls this the system working as intended: Elena has the best information, and her trading is how that gets impounded into the price. The other half calls it a no-trade-theorem problem in miniature: since it's common knowledge that Elena is both the best-informed trader here and the most motivated to see Orion succeed, nobody has a rational reason to bet against her, so the price move aggregates only her confidence, dressed up as consensus.

Draft the committee's decision memo. Recommend one concrete rule for advocate-heavy trades like this — whether Elena may trade on Orion's market at all, and under what constraint (position limits, a minimum-outside-liquidity requirement, a subsidized market maker, mandatory disclosure) — with your reasoning, and take seriously the strongest argument against whichever rule you pick, not just against leaving things as they are.
