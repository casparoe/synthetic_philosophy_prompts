Here's the student's answer, anonymized but otherwise verbatim: "Neurath and Carnap were both on the left and both signed the 1929 pamphlet The Scientific Conception of the World, but they hooked their politics to their philosophy of science very differently. For Neurath there was really no seam between the two: his whole anti-metaphysical, physicalist program was meant to be usable, and his direct experience running an economy through war-economy administration during the First World War fed into his lifelong argument that welfare should be calculated 'in natura,' in physical terms, rather than in money, since prices obscure the real trade-offs a planned economy has to make. He acted on this himself: in 1919 he took charge of the planning office of Béla Kun's Hungarian Soviet Republic, and had to leave the country in a hurry once the government collapsed. Carnap's case is different. In his intellectual autobiography he traces his own pacifism and socialism back to the war, but he almost never argues from his technical philosophy to a political conclusion, and I think his official position actually makes that distance necessary rather than incidental: since he held that value statements aren't cognitively meaningful the way scientific statements are, he had no theoretical grounds, on his own view, for preferring one economic system over another. I'd connect this directly to the Principle of Tolerance from Logical Syntax of Language: Carnap's insistence that no logical framework is uniquely 'correct,' that you choose your language on pragmatic grounds relative to your purposes, is really the same attitude that keeps him from Neurath's kind of activism. If you can't say one logic is simply true, your not going to say one economic system is simply correct either, and that, more than any difference in personality, is why Carnap stayed a sympathizer while Neurath became a planner."
