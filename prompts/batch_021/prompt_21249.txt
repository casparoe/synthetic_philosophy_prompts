Last weekend, two friends of mine, Leah and Theo, got into a long argument at dinner about a third friend's job dilemma. Since I'm currently taking a seminar on practical reason that just spent a week on hard choices and incommensurable values, I haven't been able to stop turning their argument over since.

Our friend Owen has been offered a well-paid data-analyst position at a stable company and a much lower-paid job at a small nonprofit doing work he actually cares about, and he's been stuck for weeks. Leah's view was that there's no deep puzzle here: for any two options, either one is better, the other is better, or they're exactly equally good, so Owen must simply be missing information about how much he'd enjoy each job. Once he gets more information, or if he can't, he should just pick using some tie-breaker. Theo objected that this skips over something real: Owen has said that even if the analyst job's salary were sweetened by another $8k, he'd still feel torn, whereas if the two jobs were truly equally good, any improvement to one of them should be enough to make it the better option. Since a raise that size doesn't settle it, Theo argued, the jobs aren't equally good, but neither is one better than the other -- which is exactly what Ruth Chang calls two options being "on a par," and it's supposedly why some choices are hard for reasons that have nothing to do with missing information. Leah answered that this is just ordinary vagueness, the same phenomenon that makes it unclear exactly when a collection of grains becomes a heap: keep raising the bonus and eventually Owen will agree the analyst job wins, which only shows there's a single scale with a blurry cutoff somewhere on it, not some exotic fourth relation. Theo's comeback was that the zone in which improvements fail to move Owen is far too wide for that story -- ordinary vague predicates have narrow borderlines, whereas here you can sweeten the offer substantially and it still won't read as clearly better, which he thinks only parity explains.

Could you go through Leah and Theo's exchange move by move and say who has the better argument at each stage, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also like to know whether there's a consideration neither of them raises that actually matters for whether Owen's choice is "hard" in the relevant philosophical sense.
