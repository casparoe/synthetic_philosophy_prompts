I'm several chapters into a novel set in Harlem in 1926, in which my protagonist, a young painter newly arrived from Atlanta, reads Du Bois's newly published essay "Criteria of Negro Art" and brings it—half admiring, half unconvinced—to her mentor, an older muralist. I want their exchange to hinge on something more precise than the famous line everyone quotes, that all art is propaganda. In the same essay, Du Bois insists this propaganda must also be beautiful and, crucially, true, as though the demands of racial uplift and of honest depiction could never really work against one another. I've come to doubt that this can simply be asserted away, and history seems to bear out my doubt rather than his confidence: two years later, Du Bois's own notably hostile response to Claude McKay's Home to Harlem—a novel he felt did the race a disservice even as it claimed a rawer, more documentary honesty about Harlem's poorer and less respectable corners than the images he preferred—looks like exactly this fault line opening up in practice.

Working only from what you already know of the essay and that milieu—no need to look anything up—could you invent one concrete example for me to work with? I'd like a painting, poem, or short scene, described in enough detail that I could plausibly stage a version of it in my novel, that would count as good propaganda by Du Bois's own standard, in that it serves racial dignity and justice, but that a sympathetic critic holding him strictly to his own stated beauty-and-truth requirement could reasonably argue fails as truthful art, or achieves its beauty only by flattering its subject rather than depicting it honestly. If you think a sharper example actually runs the other way—something truthful and beautiful by his standard that fails to do the propagandistic work he wanted from it—use that instead. Whichever direction you choose, please walk through the example criterion by criterion, showing exactly where it satisfies Du Bois's stated standard and where it strains against it. That is the precise friction I want my mentor character to be able to put into words, and I would rather build the scene around a genuine tension in Du Bois's thinking than around an easy strawman.
