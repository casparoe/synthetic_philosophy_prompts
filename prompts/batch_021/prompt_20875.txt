For my decision-theory qualifying exam, I have moved past the St. Petersburg paradox and am now stuck on its more obscure cousin, the Pasadena game (due to Hájek and Nover). The setup: a fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads; if that happens on toss n, the payoff is (-1)^(n+1) · 2^n/n dollars, positive for odd n and negative for even n. Weighting each payoff by its probability, 1/2^n, and summing in the natural order by toss number gives the alternating harmonic series 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which converges to ln 2. Yet the standard verdict is that ln 2 is not this game's expected value, and neither is any other real number, nor is it positive infinity—the expected value is said to be strictly undefined. I can follow each algebraic step, but I am missing the gut-level reason this counts as a fundamentally worse verdict than St. Petersburg's merely-infinite expectation, where the sum is at least unambiguous. Could you talk me through, intuitively, why the arithmetic itself forces "undefined" rather than some determinate value here, and why that difference should matter for whether expected-utility theory can issue any verdict at all in cases like this one?
