I'm working on a piece about the gap between how game theory says perfectly rational agents ought to play a finite game and what those same agents are actually entitled to conclude about one another once play doesn't go the way the theory predicts, and I've been stuck for a week on Robert Aumann's 1995 result that common knowledge of rationality forces players all the way down the backward-induction path in any game of perfect information. What keeps nagging at me is an objection associated with people like Cristina Bicchieri, Robert Stalnaker, and Philip Reny: in any game of more than one move, the argument can look almost self-undermining, since reaching a later decision node requires that someone, at an earlier node, already did something that backward induction says no player commonly known to be rational would ever do. It's genuinely unclear to me what, if anything, this licenses the remaining players to conclude about one another once it happens.

I'd like to see the dispute staged concretely rather than left as an abstraction. Take a short centipede game, in which two players alternate between "taking" -- ending the game immediately and keeping a modest but comparatively larger share for themselves -- and "passing" -- shrinking their own current cut in order to grow a shared pot for whichever of them eventually does take. Backward induction says Player 1 should take on the very first move; suppose that instead she passes, leaving Player 2 at a decision node that common knowledge of rationality was supposed to make impossible to reach.

Please write a dialogue between two epistemic game theorists watching this particular hand unfold, each trying to convince the other that they have misdiagnosed the situation. One holds that Aumann's argument survives the wrinkle intact: Player 2 can, and rationally should, go on assuming common knowledge of rationality, treat the pass as an anomaly that falls outside the theory's remit, and take when it is her turn to move. The other holds that reaching this very node is itself information that a rational Player 2 is obliged to use, in a way that can license revising what she takes herself and Player 1 to commonly know or believe -- and, potentially, can license passing in turn. Have the two really engage each other's strongest arguments, including on the question of what "common knowledge" should even mean in this setting and whether it is the kind of thing that can be revised at all, rather than writing a dialogue in which either side wins by simply declaring the other confused.
