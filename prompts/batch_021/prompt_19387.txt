In my history class we recently covered the Nuremberg trials, and I was struck by the fact that "I was just following orders" was explicitly rejected as a defense. That got me wondering about a related question my teacher didn't really have time to get into: setting aside actual war crimes like targeting civilians, is an ordinary soldier who fights entirely "by the rules" doing anything wrong just by fighting, if it turns out his side started the war unjustly? I did some digging on my own and found out this is apparently a genuine dispute among philosophers who write about the ethics of war. The traditional answer, defended by Michael Walzer in Just and Unjust Wars, is that soldiers on both sides are morally equal to one another as combatants: each is equally permitted to kill the other simply in virtue of being a soldier, regardless of who's in the right, because they pose a mutual threat to each other on the battlefield. But there's apparently a "revisionist" challenge to this, associated above all with Jeff McMahan's book Killing in War, which denies that equality: on this view, whether you're morally permitted to kill someone in war depends on whether the threat you're fighting against is itself unjust, so that soldiers fighting for an unjust cause are not, in the "deep" moral sense, on the same footing as the soldiers defending against them, even if the law of war treats them identically for practical reasons.

I'd really like to understand this disagreement by seeing the two sides actually argue it out, rather than just reading a summary of each position. Could you write a dialogue between a traditionalist defending the moral equality of combatants and a revisionist denying it, structured around a specific case: a low-ranking conscript, fighting for the aggressor state in a war he never chose to be part of and has no realistic way to opt out of or independently verify, who kills an enemy soldier in what feels to him, in the moment, like ordinary battlefield self-defense. I want the traditionalist to explain why this soldier hasn't done anything wrong, and the revisionist to explain why he might still be acting impermissibly (or at least without justification) despite appearances, and then have them go back and forth on the hardest objections to each other's view — especially the objection that the revisionist position demands a kind of moral discrimination between just and unjust causes that ordinary soldiers are in no position to make in the field, given the fog of war and propaganda on both sides.

Please don't let either side off easy: the traditionalist shouldn't be reduced to just citing "rules are rules," and the revisionist shouldn't be allowed to dodge the epistemic objection by simply admitting defeat — I'd like to see whether there's a genuine reply available (for instance, some distinction between what the soldier is ultimately liable for and what a fair, generally followable code of conduct should demand of him in practice), and whether the traditionalist has a good comeback to that reply in turn. I'm mainly hoping to come away from this with a clearer sense of which considerations are actually doing the work on each side, rather than just which slogan sounds more persuasive.
