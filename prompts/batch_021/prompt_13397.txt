I've spent the past year reporting on election-forecasting models, and a dinner-table argument between two friends of mine has stuck with me since. She builds these models for a living; he's a political reporter who covers her beat. They were rehashing a race her model had gotten "wrong": the night before the vote, it had given the winning candidate only a 25 percent chance.

(1) He said the forecast was simply wrong: you don't tell readers there's a three-in-four chance of one outcome, then watch the other one happen, without something having failed. (2) She said that's a misunderstanding of what a calibrated forecast even claims: a "25 percent" call that never once came true, across many such calls, would be the real problem. (3) He said you can't check calibration on a single race, though -- there's no run of identical rematches of this particular election to average over -- so "calibration" is importing more than one number can support, and underneath it she's just asking him to trust the model. (4) She said the trust isn't blind: given the model's record across many other races, deferring to its number that night was exactly what a rational person should have done -- the same way you'd defer to a coin once you'd learned its bias was 25/75. (5) He said that comparison is the whole problem: a coin has a standing physical tendency to land as it does across repeated tosses, whereas the outcome of that election was arguably already fixed by facts on the ground before anyone voted, so pinning a "25 percent" on it doesn't obviously hand you the same kind of thing to defer to that a coin's bias does.

Neither has budged in the months since. Could you go through their exchange move by move, saying where each point holds up and where it doesn't, and then flag what, if anything, both of them are still missing?
