"Explain the core difference between the counterfactual theory of causation (Lewis) and the interventionist theory (Woodward/Pearl). Then apply both theories to the following case and say whether each delivers the intuitively correct verdict:

Suzy and Billy both throw rocks at a bottle. Suzy's rock gets there first and shatters it. Billy's rock would have shattered the bottle a moment later if it hadn't already been broken, so instead it just sails through the space where the bottle used to be. Intuitively, Suzy's throw caused the shattering and Billy's didn't.

Does the simple counterfactual analysis (c causes e iff e counterfactually depends on c) get this right? Does the interventionist analysis get it right? What modification does each theory need to accommodate the case, and is there still an important difference between the two theories even after both modifications?"

and the answer i got:

"The counterfactual theory (Lewis) says c causes e iff e wouldn't have happened without c -- or more precisely, causation is the ancestral of counterfactual dependence, since a chain of stepwise dependences (c depends on d, d depends on e) can add up to causation even where c and e don't directly depend on each other. The interventionist theory (Woodward, building on Pearl) says c causes e iff there's a possible intervention on c that would change e, holding fixed everything that isn't downstream of c. Both theories are 'counterfactual' in a broad sense, but interventionism restricts itself to counterfactuals describing surgical interventions and so rules out backtracking counterfactuals by definition, where Lewis has to rule out backtracking through his similarity metric on possible worlds instead (no big miracles, matching of particular fact, etc).

In the Suzy/Billy case, the simple counterfactual theory gets it wrong: since the bottle would've shattered anyway even if Suzy hadn't thrown (thanks to Billy), the shattering doesn't counterfactually depend on Suzy's throw, so on the naive theory her throw isn't a cause. But Lewis's own fix handles this fine: since causation is the ancestral of dependence, all we need is a chain of intermediate events linking Suzy's throw to the shattering -- the rock leaving her hand, its flight, its impact -- each link of which is a case of straightforward dependence, so Suzy's throw comes out a cause even without one step of direct dependence between the throw and the shatter. It's the same insight Hall later puts in different terms with his distinction between dependence and production as two separate strands in our causal concept.

Interventionism handles the case by holding Billy's throw fixed at its actual value and asking whether intervening on Suzy's throw changes whether the bottle shatters. Since Billy's rock is still in flight, an intervention that stops Suzy's throw just means the bottle shatters a moment later from Billy's rock instead -- so on the plain interventionist test the shattering doesn't depend on Suzy's throw either, and interventionism runs into the very same problem as the naive counterfactual theory. Woodward's fix is to individuate the effect more finely: not 'bottle shatters' but 'bottle shatters in manner m at time t,' so intervening on Suzy's throw does change that more fine-grained variable even though it doesn't change the coarse one.

So after their respective patches, both theories end up leaning on the same trick: redescribe the effect finely enough that dependence reappears. Where they still differ is that interventionism has to lay out an explicit causal graph and structural equations in advance, so what counts as 'the same effect, more finely individuated' is fixed by the model rather than read off pretheoretic similarity judgments between possible worlds the way it is for Lewis. That's arguably an advantage for interventionism, since it's less hostage to the notoriously slippery notion of overall similarity between worlds that Lewis's account depends on."
