I'm a backend engineer who spends a lot of time on distributed consensus, and over the years I've picked up more epistemic logic than I ever expected to need — the muddy-children puzzle, the coordinated-attack (two generals) problem, arguments about why "common knowledge" can never be established over an unreliable channel, that sort of thing. A friend with a philosophy background pointed out that philosophers have their own, considerably stranger result built from the exact same Kripke-style knowledge operators: Fitch's paradox of knowability. I spent a weekend reconstructing the proof myself, and it's a genuinely elegant trick — you assume the "knowability principle" that every truth is at least possibly known (∀p (p → ◊Kp)), plug in the Moore-paradoxical instance "p, and nobody knows that p," and a few lines of modal reasoning later you've proven that every truth is actually known, which is absurd. It reminded me uncomfortably of a diagonal argument, except aimed at knowledge instead of provability or cardinality.

Since then I've gone down a bit of a rabbit hole reading how philosophers try to escape the conclusion, and none of the routes I've found strike me as a clear winner: there's Tennant's strategy of restricting the knowability principle to "Cartesian" propositions that don't undermine themselves once known; there's Edgington's fix of rigidifying the principle with an "actually" operator, so that what must be knowable is "actually-p" rather than p as evaluated at some other possible world; there's a Dummett-flavored retreat that only requires knowability for some restricted class of basic or decidable statements rather than every truth whatsoever; there's the option, explored by people like Williamson, of seeing whether switching to intuitionistic logic changes which inferences even go through; and there's simply biting a bullet — either giving up the knowability principle and accepting brute, permanently unknowable truths, or denying that we're really as non-omniscient as the argument needs us to be.

I'd like a structured outside opinion rather than my own uncertain read. Please rank the six strategies below from most to least philosophically successful, where "successful" means it actually blocks Fitch's derivation while still leaving something recognizably like the original knowability thesis intact, rather than amounting to a disguised full retreat to realism about truth.
