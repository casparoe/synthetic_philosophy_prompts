I am at work on a novel whose pivotal chapter turns on a decision my protagonist cannot make cleanly: she has been offered a permanent post as principal cellist with a well-regarded regional orchestra, and, in the same week, a foundation grant that would let her spend several years composing experimental work that no orchestra would ever commission from someone at her stage. I want this scene to dramatize what philosophers writing on hard choices — Ruth Chang chief among them, building on Isaiah Berlin's and Joseph Raz's work on value pluralism and incommensurability — mean by a choice being genuinely hard in a practical, and not merely epistemic, sense. My editor keeps flagging that the draft still reads as a woman who simply doesn't yet know which path will turn out better, which is not at all the effect I'm after.

Before I revise again, I would like a worked example — ideally one that has nothing to do with music, so I can see the underlying structure rather than my own scene's furniture — that clearly separates three things that get run together under the heading "hard choice": a choice that is hard only because some fact that would settle which option is better is unknown to the chooser; a choice between options that are merely "roughly equal," in the sense that a slight improvement to either side would tip the balance and settle the matter; and a choice between options that Chang calls being "on a par," where that same small-improvement test fails outright, and yet the options remain evaluatively comparable rather than falling off the scale into incomparability. I'm especially interested in getting the third case right, since I need to understand exactly why parity is supposed to be its own, fourth, irreducible value relation — and not just a more diplomatic name for our inability to tell which option is better — before I can trust myself to write a scene that earns that idea rather than merely gesturing at it.
