A couple weeks ago he brought this thing to try out with actual (small) money instead of just talking about it: you've got a pot that starts at $1 and doubles every round, and on your turn you either "take" (end it right there, you get the bigger chunk of whatever's in the pot, the other person gets the rest) or "pass" (pot doubles, now it's the other person's turn), capped after 6 rounds so it never gets above like $32. He played it against my friend Sonia. Turns out this is apparently a whole famous thing called the centipede game, and afterward I googled it and saw people calling the issue with it "the backward induction paradox," which honestly just confused me more instead of clearing anything up. I've got zero background in this stuff, just been chewing on it since.

Before they'd even played a round, Marcus and Sonia got into this whole argument about what the "rational" move even was:

(1) Marcus said the only rational move for player 1 (him) was to take immediately, because of backward induction: whoever's turn it is on the very last round is obviously just going to take instead of passing for nothing extra, so the person one round before that should also take instead of passing into a guaranteed worse spot, and so on all the way back to round 1 -- so it unravels into an immediate take right at the start.

(2) Sonia said if that's what "rational" means, rational people are just leaving free money on the table, since if they both keep passing for even a few rounds they'll both walk away with more than an immediate take gives either of them -- so something's off if "rational" ends up meaning "worse for both of us than it needed to be."

(3) Marcus's comeback: Sonia's mixing up what's good for both of them with what's rational for each of them given what the other will actually do. Passing only makes sense if you expect the other person to pass later too, and if you're both reasoning it through the same way, expecting that basically means expecting the other person to be irrational -- which he wasn't going to bet real money on.

(4) Sonia's response was that this is the weird part: Marcus's whole argument rests on him supposedly being certain how a "rational" Sonia would behave on rounds that, by his own logic, will never be reached, since it all supposedly collapses to round 1 anyway. She also pointed out that apparently when people actually play this game for real, hardly anyone takes on round 1, and most of them don't even feel silly about having passed a couple times themselves once its over.

(5) Marcus said what people actually do doesn't tell you what's rational, same way everyone falling for an optical illusion doesn't mean the illusion is showing you the truth.

They ended up leaving it there, but Sonia got the last word in later over text, saying that if literally everyone's gut and behavior clash with your theory the exact same way every single time, at some point that looks like the theory's problem rather than everyone else's.
