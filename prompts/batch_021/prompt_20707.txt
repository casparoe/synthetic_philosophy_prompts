I've worked as a software engineer at the same mid-size company for about eight years now, long enough to have lived through three complete rewrites of the recommendation engine that decides what products we show each visitor. When I joined, it was a hand-tuned scoring formula built from a handful of heuristics: recency, popularity, a few manually weighted signals. A couple of years later that was replaced by a logistic regression model trained on click logs, which was itself torn out in favor of a gradient-boosted tree ensemble, and for the last three years we have been running a deep learning model that, honestly, nobody on the team can fully explain in plain terms anymore. Each time, the team that built the new system was confident it had finally captured how users actually behave, and each time, within a few years, the next team concluded that the old assumptions were fundamentally wrong and started over from a different set of premises.

This came to a head recently because leadership is deciding whether to fund a substantial interpretability effort aimed at understanding what our current model is really picking up on about user behavior. One of our most senior engineers argued in the planning meeting that this would be a waste of resources: since every previous team was equally confident it had found the right underlying picture of user behavior, and every one of them turned out to be wrong within a few years, we have no real basis for thinking the current model is any closer to the truth about our users than its predecessors were. On his view, we should just treat it as a black box that happens to score well on our metrics right now, and give up any pretense that we are learning something durable about our users. A colleague pushed back, pointing out that a handful of specific signals and weighting tricks discovered back in the original heuristic era have survived, in some recognizable form, every single rewrite since, even as the surrounding architecture changed completely each time. To her, that persistence is itself evidence that we have been homing in on something real, even if we keep revising our best guess about how to represent it.

I described this whole disagreement to a friend outside of tech who has read a fair amount of philosophy, and she said it reminded her of some specific, well-known debate, though she couldn't recall the details. I would rather work through the actual reasoning than just get a label for it, though. On its own terms, the senior engineer's history of the company seems accurate, and I can't immediately find the flaw in his pessimism, yet the jump from "every past model eventually got overturned" to "so this one is probably not tracking anything true about users either" feels too strong to me. At the same time, I'm not sure why the survival of a few signals across every rewrite should count as real evidence of anything, given that the big picture keeps changing regardless. Is the senior engineer's inference actually sound, and if not, where exactly does it break down? And does my colleague's observation give us genuine grounds for confidence in the current model, or could that kind of persistence be explained away without assuming we're converging on anything real?
