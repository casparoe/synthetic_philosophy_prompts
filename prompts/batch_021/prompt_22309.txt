I'm several drafts into a historical novel whose middle section unfolds in the locutorio of the Convento de San Jerónimo in the early months of 1691, with Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz as the point-of-view character during the weeks she composes her reply to the Bishop of Puebla, who had admonished her, gently but pointedly, under the pen name Sor Filotea de la Cruz. I want one chapter to show the actual intellectual labor behind that letter from the inside, rather than leaning on the anecdotes everyone who writes about her reaches for — the childhood reading, the hair cut off in forfeit for slow learning — so I've been working through the Respuesta itself in the original Spanish (I'm using the Fondo de Cultura Económica Obras completas rather than an English version, though my Golden Age Spanish is closer to competent than expert). A few pages after she discusses Saint Jerome's letter of advice to a Roman matron named Laeta — Sor Juana Hispanicizes her as "Leta" — about educating Leta's small daughter, I ran into a passage whose argument I couldn't fully reconstruct, and which a historian friend, reading an early draft of the chapter, flagged as a place where I had visibly smoothed over something I didn't actually understand. In the original, and then in my own rough working translation, it runs:

"¡Oh cuántos daños se excusaran en nuestra república si las ancianas fueran doctas como Leta, y que supieran enseñar como manda San Pablo y mi Padre San Jerónimo! Y no que por defecto de esto y la suma flojedad en que han dado en dejar a las pobres mujeres, si algunos padres desean doctrinar más de lo ordinario a sus hijas, les fuerza la necesidad y falta de ancianas sabias, a llevar maestros hombres a enseñar a leer, escribir y contar, a tocar y otras habilidades, de que no pocos daños resultan, como se experimentan cada día en lastimosos ejemplos de desiguales consorcios, porque con la inmediación del trato y la comunicación del tiempo, suele hacerse fácil lo que no se pensó ser posible. Por lo cual, muchos quieren más dejar bárbaras e incultas a sus hijas que no exponerlas a tan notorio peligro como la familiaridad con los hombres, lo cual se excusara si hubiera ancianas doctas, como quiere San Pablo, y de unas en otras fuese sucediendo el magisterio como sucede en el de hacer labores y lo demás que es costumbre. Porque ¿qué inconveniente tiene que una mujer anciana, docta en letras y de santa conversación y costumbres, tuviese a su cargo la educación de las doncellas? Y no que éstas o se pierden por falta de doctrina o por querérsela aplicar por tan peligrosos medios cuales son los maestros hombres, que cuando no hubiera más riesgo que la indecencia de sentarse al lado de una mujer verecunda (que aun se sonrosea de que la mire a la cara su propio padre) un hombre tan extraño, a tratarla con casera familiaridad y a tratarla con magistral llaneza, el pudor del trato con los hombres y de su conversación basta para que no se permitiese. Y no hallo yo que este modo de enseñar de hombres a mujeres pueda ser sin peligro, si no es en el severo tribunal de un confesonario o en la distante docencia de los púlpitos o en el remoto conocimiento de los libros, pero no en el manoseo de la inmediación. Y todos conocen que esto es verdad; y con todo, se permite sólo por el defecto de no haber ancianas sabias; luego es grande daño el no haberlas. Esto debían considerar los que atados al Mulieres in Ecclesia taceant, blasfeman de que las mujeres sepan y enseñen; como que no fuera el mismo Apóstol el que dijo: bene docentes. Demás de que aquella prohibición cayó sobre lo historial que refiere Eusebio, y es que en la Iglesia primitiva se ponían las mujeres a enseñar las doctrinas unas a otras en los templos; y este rumor confundía cuando predicaban los apóstoles y por eso se les mandó callar; como ahora sucede, que mientras predica el predicador no se reza en alta voz."

My own rough translation: "Oh, how many harms would be spared in our commonwealth if older women were learned, as Leta was, and knew how to teach as Saint Paul and my father Saint Jerome command! Instead, for want of this, and through the sheer negligence into which poor women have been allowed to fall, when parents wish to give their daughters more than the ordinary instruction, necessity and the lack of learned old women force them to bring in male tutors to teach reading, writing, ciphering, music, and other accomplishments — from which no small harm results, as is proven daily by the pitiable outcomes of ill-matched unions; for through the closeness of daily contact and the passage of time, what once seemed impossible easily comes to pass. For this reason many parents would rather leave their daughters unlettered and untaught than expose them to so evident a danger as familiarity with men — a danger that would be avoided if there were learned old women, as Saint Paul wishes, with the office of teaching passing from one to another as it does with needlework and the other skills that are customary. For what harm could there be in an old woman, learned in letters and of holy conversation and habits, having charge of the education of young girls? Instead these girls are either left without instruction, or given it by such perilous means as male tutors — for even leaving aside every risk but the sheer impropriety of a modest woman (who blushes even to have her own father look upon her face) sitting beside so complete a stranger, treating him with the easy familiarity of the household while he treats her with a teacher's plainness, the mere decency proper to dealings with men ought by itself to forbid it. I find no way for men to teach women without danger, except from the strict tribunal of the confessional, the distant instruction of the pulpit, or the remote acquaintance of books — never in the handling of direct, everyday contact. Everyone knows this to be true, and yet it is permitted, solely for want of learned old women — so that the very lack of them is itself a grave harm. This is what ought to be weighed by those who, clinging to Mulieres in Ecclesia taceant, denounce as blasphemous the idea that women should know and teach — as though it were not the very same Apostle who said, bene docentes. What is more, that prohibition rested on the historical circumstance that Eusebius relates, namely that in the primitive Church women used to teach doctrine to one another in the temples, and the resulting noise caused confusion when the apostles were preaching, which is why they were ordered to be silent — just as happens now, that while the preacher preaches, one does not pray aloud."

What I can't reconstruct is the actual joinery of the argument. The claim she leads with — that old women ought to be learned — sounds like a practical aside about household tutoring arrangements, yet a few sentences later she is using it to defuse Mulieres in Ecclesia taceant itself ("let women be silent in the church"), the very verse her critics would presumably think settles the matter against her. Is the danger-of-male-tutors material doing real logical work in getting her from one point to the other, or is it more of a rhetorical bridge — a vivid, sympathetic scenario meant to soften the reader up before the harder scriptural move? And once she arrives at that move, is she really making one argument, or two that don't quite sit together: first, that Paul's own words, bene docentes, already license older women to teach, so the silence rule can't be absolute; and second, via the Eusebius anecdote, that the silence rule was never about teaching as such but about a specific, contingent disturbance in the primitive Church, so that it may not even apply here regardless of bene docentes? I'd like to understand this precisely enough to let the character work through it in something close to real time — in interior monologue, or aloud to her confessor — without flattening it into a generic "she believed women deserved education," and without handing her a cleaner argument than the one she actually made.
