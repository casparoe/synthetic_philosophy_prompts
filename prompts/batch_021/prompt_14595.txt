So this actually happened at a dinner party a couple weekends ago and it's been rattling around in my head ever since -- I teach the personal identity unit in my intro metaphysics course and now I kind of want to steal it as a case study. A friend of mine -- lets call her R -- has gotten really serious about vipassana practice over the last few years, just got back from a two week silent retreat, and at dessert she and her sister got into it over something R did back in college (long story, involved reading the sisters diary and then lying about it for years whenever it came up).

R's defense was, basically: there's no continuous self connecting college-R to dinner-party-R, just a series of impermanent physical and mental states arising and passing away with nothing underneath holding them together, so being mad at "her" now for what "she" did back then is a kind of category mistake -- like being mad at the ocean for a wave that soaked you an hour ago, when that wave is long gone and this is just a different wave that happens to be causally hooked up to it. Her sister was, understandably, not moved by this, and honestly neither was I, but I couldn't articulate on the spot exactly where, or whether, the argument actually breaks down.

Here's my problem: I know just enough to know the no-self doctrine is a serious, load-bearing piece of Buddhist metaphysics and not some retreat-brochure platitude, but I'm not a Buddhologist, and I don't want to fall back on "well, philosophers disagree." So -- is R using the view correctly here, or is she smuggling in something stronger than the doctrine actually licenses? Is denying a persisting self really in tension with owing someone amends for what a past self did, and if the tension only looks real, what ends up doing the work of grounding responsibility once you've given up a persisting subject to be responsible? Feel free to pull in whatever else seems relevant -- Parfit keeps nagging at me as adjacent territory but I can't get the connection precise -- I just want to actually understand this well enough to win the argument next time it comes up, not hand her a shrug.
