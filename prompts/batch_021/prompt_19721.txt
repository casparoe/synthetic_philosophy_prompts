In my philosophy elective, we each had to apply Kant's universalizability test to an action of our choice. Devon's example was submitting AI-written work as your own in a class that bans it. (1) He framed his maxim narrowly -- "use AI when I've already learned the material some other way and I'm sure I won't get caught, to save time" -- and argued that universalizing exactly that causes no contradiction in conception, so Kant's test doesn't forbid it. (2) Maya said that's a dodge: you can escape the test with enough self-serving qualifiers -- even Kant's own false-promising swindler could say the same ("only when undetected, and only when desperate"). The real maxim, she said, is "get credit for work you didn't do by deceiving the evaluator," and universalizing that destroys the point of grading. (3) Devon replied that the test targets a contradiction in conceiving the maxim, not bad consequences, and that this breaks Maya's analogy: universal, undetectable AI shortcuts among people who've already learned the material wouldn't make grades meaningless, since the signal "this student has mastered the material" would still be true, unlike an empty promise. (4) Maya answered that Devon had smuggled the general description back in to make that very argument, and that "as long as mastery is preserved" could excuse almost any deception the same way. The honest fix, she said, is to strip out self-serving qualifiers and use the agent's actual operative reason -- here, just "get undeserved credit when I can get away with it."

Could you go through their four moves and say who has the stronger point at each one -- it needn't be a sweep for either side -- and flag anything both of them are missing? Then, apart from the back-and-forth, give your own verdict on whether Devon's application of the test actually holds up.
