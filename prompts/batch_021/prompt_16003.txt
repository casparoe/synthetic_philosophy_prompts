And here, lightly cleaned up for typos, is the student's answer:

"The sorites paradox for 'heap' arises from two plausible premises: a single grain is not a heap, and adding one grain to a non-heap never turns it into a heap. Repeated application of the second premise forces the absurd conclusion that ten thousand grains is not a heap either. Epistemicists (Williamson, Sorensen) and supervaluationists (Fine, Keefe) agree that the paradox should be blocked by rejecting the second premise — there is some grain, N, at which adding it does turn a non-heap into a heap — but they disagree sharply about what that means.

For Williamson, 'heap' has a perfectly sharp extension, fixed by our patterns of use, so the second premise is simply false: some specific grain-count is the cutoff. We can't know which one, because knowing a fact that fine-grained would require our beliefs to track truth across minute differences in usage, and Williamson's margin-for-error principle says that kind of reliability isn't available to creatures like us. Classical semantics is left completely intact: every heap-statement is true or false (bivalence holds), and 'this is a heap or this is not a heap' is true of every collection whatsoever (excluded middle holds). The cost is a standing mystery about exactly where the line falls and why we're built to be ignorant of it.

Supervaluationists instead say 'heap' comes with a range of admissible ways of making it precise ('specifications'), each drawing the cutoff at a different grain-count, and that truth is truth on all of them ('supertruth'). A borderline case — say, 4,999 grains — is a heap on some specifications and not on others, so it is neither true nor false that it's a heap: bivalence fails. Because bivalence fails, excluded middle fails right along with it: for a borderline case, 'it's a heap or it's not' is itself neither true nor false, which is the price supervaluationists pay for treating the cutoff as a matter of convention rather than brute fact. Where the view earns its keep is in explaining why certain inferences between vague predicates still feel valid — 'if x is taller than a tall person, x is tall,' for instance — since these can be written in as constraints on which specifications count as admissible, something a simple three-valued or degree-theoretic semantics can't easily reproduce.

On higher-order vagueness — the fact that 'borderline' itself has no sharp boundary — epistemicism treats it as more of the same: we're ignorant not just of the cutoff for 'heap' but of the cutoff for 'definitely a borderline case,' and so on up, all covered by the same margin-for-error story. Supervaluationism handles this just as cleanly, by taking a supervaluation over supervaluations: iterating the construction closes off the regress that critics like Williamson raise, so in its mature form the objection has little remaining force against the view."
