I'm taking a graduate seminar this term that started with the standard Von Neumann–Morgenstern and Dutch-book material on expected utility theory and then pivoted, over the last couple of weeks, to how those same coherence arguments get redeployed in the AI safety literature. The move I have in mind traces back to Omohundro's "basic AI drives" paper and is picked up by Bostrom and others: since an agent whose choices violate the coherence axioms can supposedly be money-pumped out of resources, any sufficiently capable optimizing system should, under pressure of self-improvement or competition, converge on behaving like an expected-utility maximizer with a fixed, stable utility function -- and this convergence is then used to motivate worries about instrumental convergence (power acquisition, resistance to being shut down, and so on) in advanced AI.

What I haven't been able to shake since our last session is a cluster of objections to that inference that keep coming up in more recent discussions of the argument. One is that the money-pump story only really forces completeness of preferences (and hence full expected-utility maximization) if you rule out the strategy of simply declining any trade that isn't a clear improvement on one's current position -- an agent with merely incomplete preferences seems able to dodge exploitation this way without ever being rationally compelled to complete its preference ordering. A second is that, even granting full coherence, "being representable as an expected-utility maximizer for some utility function" turns out to be compatible with almost any observed pattern of behavior, since one can always construct, after the fact, a utility function that rationalizes whatever the agent actually does. If that's right, it's unclear that coherence alone licenses the much more specific claim that advanced systems will pursue stable, ambitious, resource-hungry goals rather than merely being "coherent" in some thin, unhelpful sense.

For a short paper due next week, I don't want a mere list of considerations on either side -- I'd like an essay that first reconstructs the strongest version of the coherence-to-instrumental-convergence argument, then presents the strongest objections to it (at minimum the two sketched above), and then actually defends a verdict on whether coherence arguments, as typically deployed in that literature, succeed in supporting the conclusion that sufficiently advanced AI systems will tend to act as stable expected-utility maximizers with convergent instrumental goals. Please don't retreat into an "it depends" non-answer as your conclusion -- I want a clear thesis, argued for on its merits, even if the honest verdict comes with important qualifications attached.
