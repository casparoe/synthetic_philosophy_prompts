So this happened at a "philosophy for nerds" meetup a friend dragged me to (I'm a backend engineer, definitely not a philosophy person, but I've gotten kinda into this stuff as a hobby since a coworker got me reading about epistemology last year). The topic that night was trust and self-verification, and it kicked off because someone brought up last year's xz-utils backdoor and then someone else brought up Ken Thompson's old "Reflections on Trusting Trust" lecture, about how you can build a compiler that silently inserts a backdoor into anything it compiles, including the next version of itself, so the source code stays 100% clean forever while the actual binaries stay poisoned. Two people at the table, Nadia and Colin, got into a pretty heated back and forth about whether this is basically the same problem as something in epistemology Nadia had studied called "the bootstrapping problem" (or "easy knowledge"). I wrote down the gist right after because I couldn't stop thinking about it, and I'd love an outside read on who's actually right, since their both sharper than me on this and I honestly can't tell.

(1) Nadia said Thompson's hack is basically a real-world proof of the bootstrapping problem: you can never verify a source is trustworthy using outputs generated by that very same source, whether it's your eyes, a gas gauge (she said this is literally the textbook example in the papers), or a compiler -- because a corrupted source can produce perfectly self-consistent, self-confirming evidence forever, and nothing from inside the system can ever catch it.

(2) Colin pushed back: reliabilists don't need to rule out every hypothetical corruption, just actual ones. If a gauge (or your eyes) is in fact reliable, using it doesn't require certifying it first, and Thompson's story is scary precisely because we have concrete, demonstrated reason to think that specific kind of corruption is possible -- his lecture, and now the xz thing for real -- whereas ordinary perception doesn't come with a comparably specific attacker. So the compiler case is a real, specific defeater doing the work, not proof that all self-verification is illegitimate.

(3) Nadia replied that this gets the logic backwards: the bootstrapping objection doesn't need an actual demon or an actual "Jia Tan," just the possibility of one the method can't detect from inside -- and that's exactly what Thompson proved: even in the good case, where the compiler happens to be clean, the exact same self-check would go through if it weren't, so the check was never doing any real evidential work to begin with. That's the whole complaint about the gas gauge too.

(4) Colin said fine, but that proves way too much: by that logic no finite chain of checks could ever give you knowledge of any tool or faculty, since every check itself uses some tool or faculty that could, in principle, be corrupted. That can't be right -- we obviously do know our compilers and eyes work most of the time -- and we get that knowledge the way people actually catch this stuff in practice: not some view from nowhere, but cross-checking against a genuinely independent source (a different compiler lineage that never touched the poisoned one, a different sense modality). That's still technically "using a faculty to check a faculty," but its not the single-source, vicious kind of bootstrapping the gas-gauge case is about, and treating them as equally illegitimate is the actual mistake.

They went a couple more rounds after that but mostly just restated these same two positions, louder. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who's got the better of it at each step -- not just an overall winner -- and flag anything you think both of them are missing? Bonus points if you can say whether "diverse double-compiling"-type fixes (cross-checking with an independently-built compiler) actually solve the philosophical problem or just push it back a level.
