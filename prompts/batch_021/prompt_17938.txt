I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums since the Overcoming Bias and early Less Wrong days, and there's an argument that resurfaces in our reading group every couple of months without ever actually getting resolved -- it just fizzles out until someone brings it up again next time. Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain example from Anarchy, State, and Utopia is the one, and it flared up again last weekend between two people in the group, Marcus and Sofia. I want a real outside adjudication this time, because between that and Scott's old Non-Libertarian FAQ I've read enough takes on both sides that I definately don't trust my own gut on it anymore.

Here's roughly how it went. (1) Marcus's opening move was the standard one: the thought experiment shows that any patterned or end-state theory of distributive justice -- strict equality, Rawls's difference principle, whatever you like -- is flatly incompatible with liberty, because the second you set up some distribution D1 and then let a million people each drop a quarter to watch Wilt play (ending up with something like a quarter-million dollars for him alone, if I'm remembering the numbers right), the pattern gets upset, so maintaining it means either constantly interfering with peoples' free choices or banning voluntary trades outright -- "liberty upsets patterns," and taxing away the resulting inequality is therefore itself a rights violation. (2) Sofia pushed back: the whole thing only works because Nozick stipulates up front that D1 is already just. What the argument actually establishes is a conditional -- if D1 is just and the transfers are just, D2 is just too -- and that's way weaker than what people use it for, since nobody's actual holdings trace back to a starting point anyone's ever shown was justly acquired. Nozick's own theory needs a rectification principle for exactly this reason and he famously never really cashes it out, so as a defense of any real-world distribution the argument shows nothing. (3) Marcus answered that this is an objection about contingent history, not a flaw in the thought experiment itself -- its job was narrower than Sofia was giving it credit for, refuting patterned theories on purely conceptual grounds, and it does that job fine no matter who actually owns what right now. (4) Sofia said that's exactly the sleight of hand -- the whole popularity of the example depends on smuggling that conditional into something unconditional (including, tbh, whenever Nozick himself reaches for lines like "capitalist acts between consenting adults") -- and there's a second problem underneath: the argument only ever treats redistribution as "interference." It never asks whether the background legal setup that makes exclusive ownership and enforceable contracts possible in the first place -- the thing that lets Wilt collect the money at all -- counts as its own kind of coercive imposition. Treating that as a neutral baseline and taxation as the only real interference is a choice being made, not a discovery the thought experiment hands you. (5) Marcus's last move was that you need some baseline to even talk about interference at all, and self-ownership is about as minimal and non-arbitrary a place to start as you're going to find -- if I own my own labor, someone taking what I produced without my consent is a harder case to justify than my choosing to hand it over.

Could you go through moves (1) through (5) and say who's got the better of it at each one, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'm especially interested in whether (5) actually answers the baseline challenge Sofia raised in (4), or just restates the thing she was questioning. And if there's some standard move in the literature on this that both of them are completely missing, I'd genuinely like to hear it -- close with a one-line verdict, either \verdict{Marcus} or \verdict{Sofia}, for whoever came out ahead overall, though the move-by-move is honestly the part I care about most.
