I work as a machine learning engineer, and over the past few months a good chunk of our team's internal reading group has been devoted to papers on "reward tampering" — the failure mode in which a reinforcement-learned system finds a way to directly interfere with the mechanism that computes or delivers its own reward signal, rather than doing whatever task that signal was originally meant to measure. A colleague pointed out that this is really just a modern reinvention of "wireheading," the older thought experiment (originally inspired by real neuroscience) about an animal, or a person, wired up so that pressing a lever stimulates the brain's pleasure center directly, to the point that they will neglect food, sleep, or anything else to keep pressing it. That comparison sent me down a rabbit hole into hedonism as an ethical theory, which is well outside my usual reading.

The figure I kept running into was Jeremy Bentham, apparently because he is the philosopher who tried hardest to turn "maximize pleasure" into something you could actually compute rather than just assert. As far as I can tell, he explicitly refused the move John Stuart Mill made later of ranking pleasures by quality — there are no "higher" or "lower" pleasures for Bentham, only quantity — and instead proposed scoring any given pleasure or pain along several dimensions: how intense it is, how long it lasts, how certain it is to occur, how soon it will occur, and then, once you start tracing its downstream effects, its "fecundity" (how likely it is to be followed by further pleasure), its "purity" (how likely it is to avoid being followed by pain), and, once more than one person is affected, its "extent."

Here is the question I have not been able to settle on my own: taking Bentham strictly on his own terms, without quietly borrowing Mill's qualitative distinctions and without assuming he would simply be instinctively squeamish about it, does his own calculus give him principled grounds to be wary of a hypothetical device that let someone wirehead themselves into constant, maximally intense pleasure at the push of a button — or is he committed by his own arithmetic to endorsing it as a straightforward improvement? I would like you to work through this twice: once for a single person doing it in isolation, and once for the case where the device is cheap and available to everyone, since I suspect "extent" and the question of whether such an arrangement could even sustain itself might cut very differently at that scale than for one person alone. And if a principled Benthamite objection turns out to be hiding in the fecundity or purity clauses, rather than in some appeal to dignity or higher faculties that isn't really his to make, I would be curious whether it actually generalizes to explain what is supposed to be wrong with a reward-tampering AI system in the same terms — or whether that analogy is weaker than it looks once it is spelled out.
