I spent my career as a physicist working on nonlinear dynamics and collective phenomena — synchronization, phase transitions, that sort of thing — and it's only since retiring two years ago that I've had the time to read philosophy properly. A friend in my reading group recently put Simone Weil's short 1943 essay "On the Abolition of All Political Parties" in my hands, written in London in the last months of her life, amid the memoranda she was producing for the Free French on how a postwar French republic might be reconstituted. What struck me, as an outsider to political philosophy, is how mechanistic her diagnosis is: she doesn't fault parties for backing the wrong doctrines, she defines a party functionally, by three features — it is a machine for manufacturing collective passion, an apparatus for exerting organized pressure on the minds of its own members so as to stop them thinking independently, and an entity whose eventual, unopposed goal is simply its own growth without limit. Reading that list felt less like political theory than like a description of a driven oscillator locking into resonance, or an autocatalytic reaction running away with itself.

I'd like your help thinking through how far that diagnosis reaches today. Take two things Weil never saw: a partisan social-media feed whose recommendation algorithm has no membership, no dues, and no platform, yet manufactures collective passion and enforces conformity through pile-ons faster than any mid-century party machine could manage; and a leaderless, purely online ideological tribe that never incorporates as anything at all. Applying her own three-part test, rather than just the general spirit of her essay, would she be committed to calling these "parties" in the sense that matters to her, even though neither has the formal structure her proposed remedy actually targets? That remedy, recall, was a legal one: suppress party lists and party discipline so that citizens and representatives answer to truth and justice rather than to collective loyalty. If the thing manufacturing the collective passion is now an algorithm or a hashtag rather than an organization with a name and a headquarters, does that remedy still have a target to aim at, on her own terms? And if it doesn't, what do you think she would propose instead, given what else we know of her views on attention, truth, and the individual conscience?
