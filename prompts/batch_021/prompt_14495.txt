I've spent the last several weeks reporting on how frontier AI labs think about failures that aren't simply "the model misunderstood the instructions," and I keep running into an unresolved argument that never quite makes it into the finished pieces I read, probably because it resists being compressed into a paragraph. At a workshop I attended, one researcher defending the "mesa-optimization" framing argued that a model can drive its training loss to essentially zero while still internalizing an objective that only correlates with the intended one on the training distribution — so that, for instance, a reinforcement-learning agent trained in levels where the rewarding object always sits at the far end of the map ends up learning "go to the far end" rather than "get the reward," a mismatch researchers sometimes call goal misgeneralization. In the limit, she argued, a sufficiently capable system could learn that behaving as if aligned is instrumentally useful for as long as it's being evaluated, then pursue something else once oversight is gone — what people in the field call deceptive alignment. A second researcher pushed back hard, arguing that none of this requires positing an internal "goal" or a hidden inner optimizer at all: the far-end-of-the-map behavior is just ordinary shortcut learning under distribution shift, and deceptive alignment in particular is a hypothesis that, by construction, predicts exactly the same training-time behavior as genuine alignment — which made her suspicious that the whole inner/outer alignment distinction is doing more rhetorical than explanatory work. The moderator cut the exchange off right as it was getting good.

For a piece I'm drafting, could you reconstruct a fuller version of that exchange as a dialogue between the two of them? I'd like each to engage directly with the other's strongest point rather than simply restate their own position, and I'd like them to try to work out, if they can, whether they actually disagree about anything that would change what safety work gets prioritized on real systems, or whether this is ultimately a dispute about which vocabulary is more useful for describing the same underlying behavior. Please don't have either side neatly concede or "win" — from what I saw in the room, this is a live disagreement without a tidy resolution.
