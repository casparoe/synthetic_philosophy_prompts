"Al-Ghazali's occasionalism holds that no created thing has genuine causal power - every event is directly caused by God, moment to moment. In the Seventeenth Discussion of The Incoherence of the Incoherence, he attacks the philosophers' (mainly Avicenna's) claim that a cause necessitates its effect. Using the fire/cotton example: the philosophers say fire burns cotton by necessity, but Ghazali points out that all we ever actually observe is the fire and the burning occurring together, never any necessary tie between the two. It's perfectly conceivable that the fire could touch the cotton and no burning occur - which is exactly what the Qur'an says happened to Abraham when he was thrown into the fire and came out unharmed. Since the connection between fire and burning isn't logically necessary, it's just God's habitual way of creating events, and God is always free to break the habit, which is what a miracle is.

This is basically the same move Hume makes centuries later: causation is nothing but the constant conjunction of two events, not a necessary connection you can actually perceive in the objects themselves. The difference is that Hume stops at skepticism about causal powers, while Ghazali positively concludes that God is the real cause behind the regularity we mistake for natural law. One cost of Ghazali's view that he never really deals with: if God is the only real cause of everything, including my hand moving, then I'm never really the cause of my own actions, so moral responsibility collapses - a problem his occasionalism just leaves hanging."
