So retirement has turned me into one of those people who reads philosophy for fun (I was a physicist for 35 years, so at least the probability stuff feels familiar). Ive been chewing on Lara Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility theory: the idea that you can rationally give extra weight to the bad end of a probability distribution, over and above what your utility function for outcomes already does, and that this is a genuine, separate axis of rationality rather than a bias. She motivates it with the Allais paradox -- people take the sure $1M over a gamble thats mostly $1M but with small chances of $5M and of nothing, then flip and prefer the riskier option once you strip the certainty out of both sides. Standard expected utility theory calls that pattern flatly irrational (violates independence); Buchak wants to vindicate it.

Could you write me a dialogue -- say, two old colleagues hashing it out over coffee at a conference -- between a Buchak-style risk-weighted-utility theorist and an orthodox expected-utility theorist? I want the orthodox one to press hard on whether legitimizing this kind of risk-weighting means the agent can be walked through a sequence of choices, each sensible on its own, that leaves them predictably worse off, or that they'll reverse themselves depending on how a decision gets split into stages -- and I want the risk-weighted theorist to have an actual comeback, not just a restatement of the Allais intuition (I think Buchak addresses this somewhere in the book but I haven't gotten there yet). Both should be genuinely sharp: real back and forth, each responding to the specific thing the other just said, nobody strawmanning and nobody just folding. Doesn't need to be long, just enough for them to actually get into it.
