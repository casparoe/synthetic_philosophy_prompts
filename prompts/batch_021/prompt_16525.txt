I'm at work on a novel that moves between Cambridge in the early 1880s and a present-day frame story, and one of its conceits is borrowed from the actual history of the Society for Psychical Research: after Sidgwick's death, several mediums produced the so-called "cross-correspondences," fragmentary messages that seemed to interlock only when compared across sittings, as though the departed SPR researchers were trying to prove survival by a method too elaborate for either fraud or coincidence. In my present-day thread, a historian stages something similar, a scripted séance built around real archival material, in order to put a question to an imagined, latter-day Sidgwick, and I want to get his likely answer right before I commit it to dialogue.

The question is this. Sidgwick was the Society's first president, and his early openness, shared with the wider circle of investigators, to the apparent thought-transference of the Creery sisters cooled markedly once evidence emerged that the girls had been signalling to one another; his psychical research, moreover, was never just a hobby, but was tied to a worry he voices at the close of The Methods of Ethics, that practical reason might issue two ultimately irreconcilable verdicts, one from self-interest and one from duty, unless something like a moral order behind appearances turned out to be real. Given that cast of mind, how do you think he would have reacted on being told about Daryl Bem's 2011 "Feeling the Future" experiments, which reported statistically significant evidence of precognition in a mainstream psychology journal; about the Bayesian re-analysis by Wagenmakers and colleagues, arguing that the original statistics had been far too generous to the psi hypothesis; and about the failed replications and the wider "replication crisis" the episode is often credited with helping to provoke? Would his sympathies lie with Bem's classical significance testing or with the Bayesian challenge to it? How would he weigh preregistration and the file-drawer problem against the safeguards against fraud and unconscious sensory leakage he had already tried to build into his own investigations? And on balance, would the episode look to him like vindication of the patient, self-correcting inquiry he hoped psychical research could become, or like a dispiriting rerun of his own century's disappointments? I'd like your best-supported reconstruction, anchored in the specific commitments that actually shaped his work on this subject, rather than a generic guess at what "a Victorian" might make of modern parapsychology.
