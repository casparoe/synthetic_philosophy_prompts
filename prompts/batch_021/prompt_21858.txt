I've spent the past few weeks reporting on claims, mostly from people inside AI labs, that large language models have essentially rendered the old "frame problem" from classical AI obsolete. The trouble is that when I go back to the primary sources, I'm not convinced the people making this claim, and the people I've seen push back on it, are even arguing about the same problem. For a piece I'm drafting on how contemporary AI discourse tends to recycle, and often flatten, mid-century philosophy of mind, I want to lay out a fairly specific thesis and have you build the strongest possible case for it, rather than hedge toward "it's complicated."

Here is the distinction I want the argument to turn on. The frame problem as McCarthy and Hayes originally posed it in 1969 was a narrow representational puzzle for logic-based planners: how do you avoid writing an explicit "frame axiom" for every fact an action leaves unchanged, without either an unmanageable explosion of axioms or a system that can never settle what still holds after an action. That version, I'd argue, was essentially closed by later work on default persistence in nonmonotonic logic, and especially by Raymond Reiter's successor-state axioms for the situation calculus — and today's language models arguably sidestep it simply by not doing explicit symbolic action-bookkeeping at all, much as Brooks-style reactive robotics was already said to sidestep it in the early 1990s. But Dennett's 1984 essay "Cognitive Wheels" and Fodor's remarks on the non-modular, "isotropic" character of central cognition describe what looks like a genuinely harder problem: given the indefinitely large stock of things a reasoner could in principle bring to bear, how does anything short of exhaustive search decide, cheaply and reliably, which of those things are actually relevant to the situation at hand? That problem has nothing to do with frame axioms, and I don't think fluent-looking output is by itself evidence that it has been solved.

So the thesis I'd like you to argue for, as persuasively as you can, is this: today's large language models deserve credit for dissolving McCarthy and Hayes's narrow logical frame problem, but they have made no demonstrable progress on, and arguably don't even engage with, the broader Dennett/Fodor problem of relevance, and their apparent commonsense fluency is entirely compatible with having no principled relevance-triaging mechanism at all. Once you've made that case, tell me the single strongest objection to it you can construct — for instance, from someone who thinks in-context attention over a long prompt already constitutes a genuine, if partial, relevance mechanism in Dennett's sense — and say how well you think the argument holds up against it.
