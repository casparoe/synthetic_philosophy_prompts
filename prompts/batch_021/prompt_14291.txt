I'm about two-thirds through a novel whose protagonist, Miriam Adler, chairs the ritual practices committee of a traditional-egalitarian congregation loosely modeled on the Conservative movement. In the chapter I'm drafting now, the committee is finalizing a new machzor for the High Holidays, and the flashpoint is the Yom Kippur Avodah service — the long poetic passage, recited during Musaf, that recounts in loving detail the high priest's sacrificial rite in the Temple — together with the Amidah's standing petition asking God to restore the Temple service and receive again the fire-offerings of Israel. Some of the newer members on the committee want both softened into abstract language about "service of the heart," on the grounds that the congregation shouldn't be asking, day after day, for the literal resumption of animal slaughter; several longtime members, including Miriam's predecessor as chair, see this as a quiet abandonment of the community's own messianic hope, dressed up as good taste. Miriam has been asked to write the committee's recommendation before the vote at next month's board meeting.

I'd like the memo to actually argue from the classical sources rather than gesture at "tradition versus modernity," since a reader who knows the material would notice the difference. It should engage Maimonides' rationale in the Guide for the Perplexed — that sacrificial worship was retained and redirected toward God as a concession to a mentality already formed by the sacrificial cults surrounding ancient Israel, a way station toward the superior worship of prayer — against Nahmanides' objection, in his comments on the opening of Leviticus, that this account cannot explain why Cain, Abel, and Noah were already offering sacrifices long before there was any organized idolatry to react against, and that it demeans a service the Torah treats as the holiest thing Israel does. I'd also like the memo to be honest about the awkward fact that Maimonides the legal codifier, in the Mishneh Torah, still writes the sacrificial laws as literally binding rules for a rebuilt Temple rather than as a spent metaphor — and that someone on the committee has already circulated Rav Kook's speculation that the messianic future might replace animal offerings with grain alone, a position Miriam privately suspects is more congenial to both camps than either would admit.

Could you draft the memo itself, in Miriam's voice, roughly a page long: a clear recommendation — keep the Avodah and the petition as they stand, rewrite them in some specific alternative form, or something else entirely — a timeframe tied to the board vote, and a paragraph that gives the losing side's strongest argument its full weight rather than filing it under nostalgia? I've deliberately not told you which way I think Miriam should come out, and I'd like to keep it that way.
