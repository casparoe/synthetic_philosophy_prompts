The student's answer:

"The Special Composition Question, as van Inwagen poses it, asks: under what conditions do some objects, the xs, compose some further object, y? This differs from the General Composition Question, which asks what composition itself consists in — what it is, in general, for one thing to be part of another — rather than when instances of composition occur. Van Inwagen argues you can answer the special question without answering the general one, much as you might state truth conditions for causal claims without giving a full analysis of causation.

The two extreme answers to the SCQ are nihilism and universalism. Universalism, defended by writers such as David Lewis, holds that any objects whatsoever compose a further object, no matter how scattered or gerrymandered the objects are: on this view the fusion of a trout and a turkey is exactly as real a composite as an ordinary organism. Van Inwagen rejects universalism because it multiplies objects far beyond anything needed to make sense of experience or of the special sciences.

Van Inwagen's own answer is the nihilist one: composition never occurs, so that, strictly speaking, there are no tables, chairs, or stars, only simples arranged tablewise, chairwise, and starwise. Ordinary object-talk is not, on this view, literally true, but it can be systematically paraphrased into talk about pluralities of simples arranged in the relevant ways, which is what lets van Inwagen deny that tables exist without thereby denying anything we actually observe.

Finally, the SCQ is best understood as a more rigorous version of the puzzle posed by the Ship of Theseus. When the ship's planks are replaced one by one, the question of whether the current planks, the xs, still compose the same object the original planks did is precisely the sort of question the SCQ was designed to settle, which is why van Inwagen treats the two puzzles as facets of a single underlying problem about composition."
