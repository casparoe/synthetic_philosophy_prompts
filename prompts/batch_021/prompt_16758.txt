My PhD advisor died ten days ago, quite suddenly, and I've been asked to speak at her memorial service this weekend. I'm a philosophy PhD student working mainly in moral psychology, but her own field was the metaphysics of time, and she was one of the most unflinching eternalists I have ever known: she held that the block-universe picture is simply correct, that past and future events are exactly as real as present ones, and that the vivid sense we have of the present "moving" through time is a fact about the perspective of creatures embedded in the block rather than a fact about the block itself. She had no religious commitments to fall back on, and was allergic to soft-focus talk about death -- she would have hated any suggestion, in her own eulogy, that she is now "watching over us" or "still with us in spirit," on the grounds that this kind of language quietly smuggles in exactly the tensed, A-theoretic picture of time her whole career was devoted to arguing against. What she did say, more than once, and I don't think she meant it only as a debating point, is that presentism is in one sense the bleaker view of death: if only the present is real, then every moment of a life, including the good ones, is annihilated the instant it stops being present, whereas on her view nothing that was ever real about her stops being real just because it is no longer present.

The service will have a lot of family and old friends with no philosophical training at all, plus a good number of metaphysicians and philosophers of physics who worked alongside her for decades and will notice immediately if I fudge the view to make it sound more comforting than it is. I have about five minutes. I don't want to deliver a lecture on A-series and B-series time, but I also don't want to console anyone with a picture of time she would have publicly dismantled. Could you write the eulogy for me? I'd like it to draw whatever comfort it offers honestly out of eternalism itself -- including, if you can make it land without sounding glib, her own thought that presentism is the crueler metaphysics -- rather than reaching for anything that would require her to persist, return, or watch over anyone.
