"On the standard Bayesian picture, evidence E confirms a hypothesis H just in case learning E raises the probability of H, i.e. P(H|E) > P(H), which by Bayes' theorem is equivalent to P(E|H) > P(E). The trouble with 'old' evidence is that the scientist already knows E is true before H is even formulated, so E has already been fully conditionalized on and P(E) = 1. Since a probability of 1 can't go up, conditionalizing on E can't raise P(H) either, because P(H|E) just collapses to P(H) whenever P(E) = 1. So formally, old evidence seems unable to confirm anything, no matter how impressive the fit between theory and fact. But this looks wrong: physicists had known for decades that Mercury's orbit precesses about 43 arcseconds per century more than Newtonian mechanics predicts, and when Einstein showed in 1915 that general relativity entails exactly this excess, this was treated as one of the most powerful confirmations of the theory, even though the evidence itself was decades old.

Glymour's own solution to this puzzle is that real scientists aren't logically omniscient: even though E was already known, the fact that H entails E was not known until the theory was actually worked out. So what's really new isn't E, it's the logical discovery that H implies E, and it's conditionalizing on that discovery, not on E itself, that does the confirming. On this view you swap the unrealistic requirement that P(E) be less than 1 for the much more realistic requirement that the scientist's credence in 'H entails E' be less than 1 before the derivation is carried out. I think this basically works, since it locates the source of confirmation in something that actually changes -- the scientist's awareness of the entailment -- rather than something that's stuck at 1, and once you let credences over logical relations be non-trivial, the technical problem just goes away."
