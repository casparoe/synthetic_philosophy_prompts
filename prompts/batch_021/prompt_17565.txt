And here's the student's answer, worth 20 points total (this was written in class in about 35 minutes, so grade for correctness and reasoning rather than polish):

"Bradley's regress arises when we try to explain how a subject and a predicate, or two relata and a relation, form a single unified fact rather than just a list of items. Suppose we want to say that a is to the left of b. It isn't enough to just have a, the relation left-of, and b sitting side by side, because a mere list or aggregate {a, left-of, b} isn't the same thing as the fact that a is left of b — the elements need to be tied together into a genuine unity. But if we try to explain this unity by invoking a further relation R1 that connects left-of to a, and another relation R2 that connects left-of to b, then we need further relations to connect R1 and R2 to their own relata, and so on forever. Since we can never reach a stopping point, Bradley thought the demand for a relating relation could never be satisfied, and he took this to show that pluralities of externally related terms are ultimately incoherent. This is one of the central planks of his argument for monism: because relations can't do the unifying job on their own, the many things of ordinary experience can't be ultimately real as a plurality, so only the single, all-inclusive Absolute is fully real.

Russell took Bradley's regress extremely seriously and made blocking it a central task of his early philosophy. His solution, developed in the Principles of Mathematics and refined in his multiple relation theory of judgment, was that a relation can occur in a proposition in two different ways: either as a mere term (as when we say 'difference is a relation,' where difference is just another object being talked about) or as actually relating its terms (as when we say 'A differs from B,' where difference is doing the job of relating rather than being related). When a relation occurs in the second way it unifies the proposition by itself, without needing a further relation to relate it to A and B, so the regress never gets started. Because this distinction is built into the nature of relations themselves, Russell thought he could keep relations as real, external entities not grounded in the natures of their terms — unlike Bradley, for whom relations are internal to the terms they relate — while still explaining unity.

Overall I think Russell's solution basically works, since it stops the regress at the very first step instead of just postponing it, and it fits naturally with his broader defense of external relations against Bradleyan monism."
