Löb's theorem entails that a consistent, sufficiently expressive formal system cannot, for an arbitrary sentence P, adopt the policy of asserting P merely on the strength of having proved that a proof of P would guarantee its truth -- doing so would force the system to already prove P outright, and building this move into a fully general reflection principle collapses the system into inconsistency. In work on self-modifying and self-trusting agents, where the clearest framing comes from MIRI's "tiling agents" line of research, this bites in a very concrete way: an agent that wants to certify in advance that its future self, or a successor system built to replace it, will only take safe actions cannot simply treat "my successor proved this action is safe" as sufficient grounds for accepting the action, since that is exactly the sort of general trust-in-one's-own-proof-system move that Löb's theorem rules out. A couple of people in an AI-safety reading group I'm part of have been arguing for weeks now about which of the various proposed escapes from this bind -- stratifying proof systems so each generation reasons in a strictly weaker theory than the last, replacing proof with calibrated probability as the currency of self-trust, or simply accepting some frankly bounded or approximate notion of self-trust rather than the full-strength version -- actually make progress on the underlying problem, rather than quietly relabeling or deferring it. Quite apart from its role in AI safety, the puzzle strikes me as a formal cousin of much older worries about whether any reasoner can vindicate its own faculties without arguing in a circle.
