I've been going down an anthropic-reasoning rabbit hole for the past few weeks -- Bostrom's book on observation selection effects, the Doomsday Argument, Sleeping Beauty, and the rest of the cluster of puzzles that gets endlessly rehashed on LessWrong -- and I think it's finally clarified something about the fine-tuning argument that I don't recall seeing spelled out explicitly anywhere, though I'd be a little surprised if it turned out to be genuinely new. I wanted to run it past you before I bring it up with a friend of mine who leans toward the design explanation.

The reasoning goes like this. The fine-tuning argument treats it as a fact in need of explanation that the physical constants fall within the narrow band compatible with life, and it proposes a designer or a multiverse as rival accounts of that fact. But take the weak anthropic principle seriously -- the point that any observer whatsoever must find itself in conditions compatible with its own existence -- and the whole project seems to evaporate before either rival account is even needed. Whatever actually generated the constants, whether a single unguided universe, an ensemble of universes, or a designer, every one of these hypotheses predicts, with probability 1, that any observers they happen to produce will look out and see exactly the fine-tuning we see. So the datum of fine-tuning can't be evidence favoring one of these hypotheses over the others; it's guaranteed on all of them alike, once you condition on someone being around to notice it. Given that, when I talk to my friend, should I present this as showing that the entire design-versus-multiverse debate is a wash, or is it more accurate to say that fine-tuning specifically fails as evidence for design while leaving the case for a multiverse untouched?
