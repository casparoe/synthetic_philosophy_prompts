"Nagel held that one theory reduces to another only if the laws of the reduced theory can be logically derived from the laws of the reducing theory, given a set of bridge laws (or 'bridge principles') connecting the predicates of the two theories — for instance, biconditionals linking thermodynamic terms like temperature to statistical-mechanical terms like mean molecular kinetic energy. Derivability without such connecting principles is not reduction in Nagel's sense; the bridge laws are precisely what distinguish reduction from the mere replacement of one theory by another.

Putnam and Fodor argued that this model cannot accommodate psychology, because mental kinds are multiply realizable: one and the same mental property, such as pain, can be had in virtue of many different physical properties, as when pain is realized by C-fiber firing in humans but by a different physical arrangement in an octopus or, in principle, a silicon-based system. Because no biconditional bridge law linking 'pain' to a single physical kind can hold across all these cases, type-type reduction of psychology to neuroscience fails, even though every particular pain is presumably some particular physical state or other. This is exactly what philosophers mean by calling the mental an emergent phenomenon: a property that arises from, but is not fixed by, its physical basis, and whose causal powers therefore outrun those of the underlying physical arrangement.

This apparent emergence generates a problem that Donald Davidson's causal exclusion argument makes vivid: if a physical event's physical properties are already causally sufficient for its physical effects, it is unclear what causal work is left for the higher-level mental property to do, on pain of an implausible overdetermination of the effect. Kim's response is to give up uniform, cross-species reduction while retaining a more local one: define the mental kind functionally, by its causal role, and then identify its realizer species by species. This 'functional reduction' is meant to secure mental causation for each realizer, without requiring that the same physical kind realize the mental kind everywhere."
