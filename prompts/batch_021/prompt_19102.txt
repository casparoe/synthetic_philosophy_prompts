I've been a longtime reader of LessWrong (and, before that, Overcoming Bias), and a couple of weeks ago a friend from my reading group and I found ourselves re-reading Eliezer Yudkowsky's old "Meta-Honesty" post, specifically the section where he works through the classic Gestapo-at-the-door scenario and argues that someone in that position should "Glomarize" rather than lie outright — that is, adopt a blanket policy of meeting the whole class of relevant questions with something like "can neither confirm nor deny," on the theory that a policy applied uniformly regardless of the truth carries no information about the specific case at hand. That conversation sent me down a rabbit hole into how much older writers handled structurally the same dilemma, and I ended up back in Augustine's short treatise On Lying (De Mendacio, ca. 395 AD), which turns out to wrestle with almost exactly this problem some 1600 years earlier. In §23, Augustine praises a bishop, Firmus of Thagasta, who was tortured by imperial officers for harboring a fugitive and who, in Augustine's words, simply "made answer to their questions, that he could neither tell a lie, nor betray a man" — full stop, no further engagement — and who was eventually vindicated. But in the very next section, Augustine notices a wrinkle in this strategy, and this is the part where I'd like some help:

> But one sometimes comes to a case of this kind, that we are not interrogated where the person is who is sought, nor forced to betray him, if he is hidden in such manner, that he cannot easily be found unless betrayed: but we are asked, whether he be in such a place or not. If we know him to be there, by holding our peace we betray him, or even by saying that we will in no wise tell whether he be there or not: for from this the questioner gathers that he is there, as, if he were not, nothing else would be answered by him who would not lie nor betray a man, but only, that he is not there. So, by our either holding our peace, or saying such words, a man is betrayed, and he who seeks him has but to enter in, if he have the power, and find him: whereas he might have been turned aside from finding him by our telling a lie. Wherefore if you know not where he is, there is no cause for hiding the truth, but you must confess that you know not. But, if you know where he is, whether he be in the place which is named in the question or elsewhere; you must not say, when it is asked whether he be there or not, "I will not tell you what you ask," but you must say, "I know where he is, but I will never show." For if, touching one place in particular you answer not and profess that you will not betray, it is just as if you should point to that same place with your finger: for a sure suspicion is thereby excited. But if at the first you confess that you know where he is, but will not tell, haply the inquisitor may be diverted from that place, and begin now to ply you that the place where he is may be betrayed.

(That's H. Browne's 1887 translation, from the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers series.) I've read this several times and think I can see the rough shape of the worry, but I'd like help making the argument fully explicit rather than just gestured at. First, could you spell out exactly what changes between Firmus's situation and the one Augustine introduces in §24 — why does a blanket "I will neither lie nor betray" survive unscathed, while silence, or a flat refusal to answer, stops being safe the moment the question narrows to a specific yes-or-no about one particular place? Second, could you evaluate Augustine's proposed remedy on its own terms: does volunteering, before any place-specific question has even been asked, that "I know where he is, but I will never show," actually eliminate the leak in general, or does it just relocate it — for instance, under repeated questioning about a whole list of candidate hiding places, or given that presumably only someone who actually has something to hide would bother volunteering that particular formula to begin with? I'm especially interested in whether what Augustine is groping toward here is essentially the same move as Yudkowsky's Glomarization, and if so, whether it runs into the same objections.
