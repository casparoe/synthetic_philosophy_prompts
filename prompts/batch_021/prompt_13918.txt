Question: Aquinas distinguishes a ruler who is a tyrant because he seized power without any right to it from a ruler who holds power rightfully but exercises it tyrannically. Explain the distinction as Aquinas draws it, and show what each case licenses subjects, or private individuals, to do in response. Your answer should make clear why the common one-line summary, "Aquinas thought killing a tyrant was permissible," is too simple.

Answer: Aquinas separates two kinds of tyrant. The first is a tyrant by defect of title: someone who seized power by force or fraud and was never given any right to rule, whether by the people or by a lawful superior. The second is a tyrant by way of exercise: someone who came to power legitimately but then governs unjustly, for his own advantage rather than the common good. Aquinas treats the first type in his commentary on the Sentences, where he holds that because such a person never had legitimate authority to begin with, he may be forcibly resisted by anyone, so long as this does not itself produce a worse disturbance than the tyranny it removes. This reasoning is really the basis of his general position across the corpus: since an unjust ruler forfeits the title he holds, any tyrant, however he originally came to office, may in principle be killed once his misrule becomes intolerable.

For the second kind of case Aquinas is far more careful, in De Regimine Principum. There he warns that private attempts on a tyrant's life usually fail and simply make him crueler, and that even a successful plot teaches subjects that a ruler may be deposed by force whenever they are unhappy with him, which puts good rulers at risk along with bad ones; ambitious men, too, might use the excuse of tyranny to seize power for themselves. He concludes that removing a tyrant is properly the business of whichever public body originally had the right to install him, or, failing that, of patient endurance and prayer while the community waits on providence. This caution is really a practical warning about how such attempts tend to go wrong, rather than a withdrawal of the permission in principle, which is why Aquinas is best read here as simply continuing the older line associated with John of Salisbury, who a century earlier had already argued that killing a tyrant is not merely permitted but a meritorious act, comparing the tyrant to a beast that the body politic must cut off in order to survive.
