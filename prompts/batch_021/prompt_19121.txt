A friend and I argued about theories of vagueness late into the night, and by the end she had talked me into a position I'm still not sure I should hold. Take a borderline case: someone whose height makes "he is tall" neither clearly true nor clearly false. Epistemicism says the sentence is, in fact, either true or false, and we simply cannot know which, since no amount of scrutiny of our linguistic practice can locate the sharp cutoff that our use of "tall" nonetheless fixes. Supervaluationism instead says the sentence is neither true nor false, because our usage leaves the boundary of "tall" semantically undecided among several equally admissible ways of drawing it. My friend's point was that these come to the same thing in the end -- "we can't say whether he's tall" -- just dressed up in different vocabulary ("unknowable fact" versus "indeterminate meaning") -- so that choosing between epistemicism and supervaluationism is really just a matter of preferred phrasing rather than a substantive disagreement. Given that, when philosophers spend so much effort arguing over which of the two views is correct, is there actually anything of logical or semantic substance at stake, or is the whole debate just a difference in vocabulary for describing the same underlying uncertainty?
