I got into logical uncertainty through the decision-theory side of the rationalist blogosphere, in particular the "5-and-10" problem, where a proof-based agent can end up leaving money on the table because it can vacuously derive absurd consequences from a counterfactual action it provably never takes. That eventually led me to the MIRI paper on logical induction (Garrabrant et al.), which I have by now read through more than once, alongside a couple of the accompanying LessWrong posts. Most of the construction has become intuitive to me: a countable population of computationally limited "traders" buy and sell shares in logical sentences, the resulting prices update day by day, and a price sequence counts as a genuine logical inductor exactly when no trader from that population can drive its net worth to infinity while only ever risking a bounded loss.

The one piece I have never managed to get a satisfying, non-technical grip on is the self-reference machinery. Both the paper and the commentary around it make a point of showing that the market can assign sensible, stable prices to sentences that explicitly quantify over the market's own future prices -- something in the spirit of "the inductor's credence in this very sentence will be below one-half on day one thousand." On its face, that looks like it should collapse into a Liar-style contradiction the moment you try to cash it out, especially given that Tarski's undefinability theorem already tells us that a sufficiently expressive theory cannot contain a fully general truth predicate for its own sentences without paradox. My hazy understanding is that logical induction dodges this because each day's prices are pinned down by a finite computation before any sentence gets to talk about them, rather than everything being solved for as one simultaneous fixed point -- but I cannot reconstruct, from first principles, why that is actually sufficient to stop a sufficiently clever trader from engineering a sentence that forces the market into permanent incoherence and extracts unbounded profit along the way.

Could you walk me through the intuition here, at a gears-level but non-formal register -- I do not need the full existence proof, just the underlying "aha" -- for why self-reference of this kind does not break the construction, and, more generally, why mere resistance to exploitation by a fairly ordinary class of computable traders turns out to be a strong enough constraint to force this sort of self-referential coherence to emerge, given that nobody explicitly hand-coded any logical or self-referential reasoning into the traders themselves?
