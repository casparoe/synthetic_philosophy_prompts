I run a small delivery business, and a couple of years ago I taught myself some programming so I could write a script to work out efficient delivery routes and driver schedules each morning. When the script kept struggling on busier days, I learned that this kind of scheduling problem is "NP-hard": computer scientists strongly believe, though they have not proven it, that no algorithm — however fast the computer running it — can guarantee finding the truly optimal schedule for every case in a reasonable amount of time, since doing so would essentially require settling the famous open P versus NP question. This got me wondering about something beyond my own scheduling headaches: if even a hypothetically perfect reasoner would hit a genuine wall on certain problems, and not merely a practical one caused by insufficient time or computing power, that seems to say something important about what we can reasonably demand of ourselves, of other people, and of AI systems when faced with complicated decisions. Could you describe the most important implications of computational complexity theory for how I should think about rational decision-making and personal responsibility when I cannot find the "best" answer to a messy real-life problem, and for whether a principle like "ought implies can" needs to be rethought once computational tractability, rather than mere theoretical possibility, is taken into account?
