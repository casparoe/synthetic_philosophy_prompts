ok so this happened at my school last week and its been living in my head rent free lol, need an outside perspective on this. i'm one of five kids on our school's Honor Council (basically a student panel that decides if someone violated the academic honesty policy, and what happens if they did). our handbook says a student can only be found "responsible" for a violation if BOTH of these are true: (a) they actually did the prohibited thing, like sharing answers on a lab writeup, and (b) they knew at the time that doing it wasn't allowed. if either one is false, its not a violation and theres no consequence.

so we had a case and all five of us voted. two of us thought (a) and (b) were both true, so we said "responsible." two other people thought (a) was true but were genuinely convinced the kid had no clue it counted as cheating, so for them (b) was false, which means "not responsible." the fifth person thought the kid probably did know it was against the rules (b true) but wasn't convinced the kid had actually done the act in the first place (a false), also landing on "not responsible." tally up our final guilty/not-guilty votes and its 2-3, not responsible, case closed. except our advisor also made us vote on (a) and (b) separately as their own questions, and (a) got 4 yes out of 5 while (b) got 3 yes out of 5. by the handbook's own rule, majority-yes on both conditions should mean the kid IS responsible. so we've got two completely legit vote counts, from the same five people with the same opinions the whole time (nobody flip-flopped), pointing to opposite verdicts. it turned into a huge argument about which number should actually "count." i mentioned all this to my older sister, whos majoring in poli sci, and she got weirdly excited and said something like this happens with actual panels of judges too and its "a whole known problem," but she couldn't remember what its called or why it happens, which honestly did not help me at all lol.

so, genuine question: is one of our two voting methods obviously the "correct" one, meaning we just messed up by trusting the other one? or is this a real dilemma without a clean fix? and if its unavoidable, is there some smarter way to design a voting procedure ahead of time so we don't run into this again, or is that structurally impossible no matter how carefully we set the rules?
