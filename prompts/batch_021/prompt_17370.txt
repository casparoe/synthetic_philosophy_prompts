I spent close to forty years working in experimental condensed-matter physics before retiring, and in the last couple of years I have been trying to give myself a proper philosophical education, mostly by working backwards from Stanford Encyclopedia entries into the primary texts they cite. At the moment I am slowly making my way through Cicero's Academica, in the Yonge translation from 1853 that circulates freely now that it is out of copyright, because I gather that a good deal of the earliest surviving discussion of vagueness and the "heap" problem is buried in the quarrel it stages between the Stoics and the sceptical Academy. Having spent a career thinking about how continuous parameters like temperature can hide genuinely sharp transitions underneath apparently smooth behaviour, I had hoped that revisiting this ancient material might help me decide whether "heap-hood" is that kind of phenomenon, or something categorically different. Unfortunately the text itself is giving me more trouble than the philosophy: Cicero shifts between voices with almost no signposting, and I keep losing track of who is supposed to be committed to what.

Here is the passage that has me stuck. It comes from Book II (the "Lucullus"), in the stretch where Cicero, replying to the Stoicising position that Lucullus had defended earlier in the dialogue, goes after the Stoic art of dialectic and its handling of the sorites; Yonge's translation heads the section "XXIX":

"But the sorites is a vicious sort of argument:—crush it, then, if you can, to prevent its being troublesome; for it will be so, if you do not guard against it. We have guarded against it, says he. For Chrysippus's plan is, when he is interrogated step by step (by way of giving an instance), whether there are three, or few, or many, to rest a little before he comes to the 'many;' that is to say, to use their own language, ἡσυχάζειν. Rest and welcome, says Carneades; you may even snore, for all I care. But what good does he do? For one follows who will waken you from sleep, and question you in the same manner:—Take the number, after the mention of which you were silent, and if to that number I add one, will there be many? You will again go on, as long as you think fit. Why need I say more? for you admit this, that you cannot in your answers fix the last number which can be classed as 'few,' nor the first, which amounts to 'many.' And this kind of uncertainty extends so widely, that I do not see any bounds to its progress.

Nothing hurts me, says he; for I, like a skilful driver, will rein in my horses before I come to the end, and all the more if the ground which the horses are approaching is precipitous. And thus, too, says he, I will check myself, and not reply any more to one who addresses me with captious questions. If you have a clear answer to make, and refuse to make it, you are giving yourself airs; if you have not, even you yourself do not perceive it. If you stop, because the question is obscure, I admit that it is so; but you say that you do not proceed as far as what is obscure. You stop, then, where the case is still clear. If then all you do is to hold your tongue, you gain nothing by that. For what does it matter to the man who wishes to catch you, whether he entangles you owing to your silence or to your talking?"

I would very much appreciate help unpacking this. First, some plain bookkeeping: whose position is voiced by the recurring, unnamed "says he," and how does that voice relate to Carneades, who suddenly breaks in with "Rest and welcome... you may even snore"? Second, could you restate in modern terms exactly what Chrysippus's ἡσυχάζειν strategy is supposed to buy him, and why "resting" before reaching "many" would count as guarding against the sorites at all, rather than simply refusing to finish the argument? Third, I do not fully see why the charioteer image is supposed to be a bad defence: reining in one's horses before an unseen cliff edge sounds like exactly the right policy if there really is a precipice, even an undiscovered one, which is more or less what a modern epistemicist about vagueness says about the heap's sharp but undiscoverable boundary. Why does the text treat "you stop, then, where the case is still clear" as though it obviously refutes the driver, rather than as a fair description of a cautious epistemicist's behaviour? Finally, I noticed that immediately after this exchange Cicero pivots to the liar paradox and the principle that every statement must be true or false. Is there a serious philosophical reason to yoke the sorites and the liar together in this way, as two failures of the same principle of bivalence, or is this mainly a rhetorical move to make Stoic dialectic look disreputable in one stroke?
