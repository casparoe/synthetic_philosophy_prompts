I've been slowly working my way through Thomas Hobbes on my own time, not for any class, and a footnote in something I was reading mentioned that he spent an embarrassing chunk of his later life trying to square the circle, refusing to concede even after the Oxford mathematician John Wallis publicly demolished his proofs again and again. That sent me down a rabbit hole into why geometry mattered so much to him philosophically in the first place, and I found this argument of his that geometry is the one science where we can have genuine, gapless certainty, precisely because we are the ones who construct the figures out of motion: a point, a line, a circle are all things we generate ourselves through definition and construction, so we can trace their "causes" the whole way through. He even extends the same reasoning to civil philosophy, saying the commonwealth is demonstrable in exactly the same way, because we are the ones who make it, through covenant, whereas natural bodies stay permanently uncertain for us since we didn't put their causes there ourselves.

Not long after, in a completely different corner of my reading, I ran into Giambattista Vico's idea that the true and the made are convertible (verum ipsum factum), which he also illustrates through geometry: the claim that we can prove geometrical truths with total certainty only because we ourselves assembled their elements, while nature was made by God and not by us, so a properly scientific (as opposed to merely factual) knowledge of nature is off-limits to human beings. Vico then turns this around to argue that history, language, law, and civil institutions are actually where humans can claim their deepest and most certain knowledge, since those, unlike nature, are things we made. The overlap with Hobbes struck me as remarkable given how differently the two are usually characterized, one as a hard-nosed materialist and mechanist, the other as a critic of Cartesian mathematical rationalism trying to rehabilitate historical and humanistic knowledge.

I'd like you to compare Hobbes's reasoning for why geometry (and civil philosophy) count as demonstrable sciences with Vico's verum-factum principle. Specifically, where do the two views really agree once you look past the surface similarity, and where do they most substantially part ways, for instance on what it means to "make" something, on whether God's authorship of nature is doing the same work in both accounts, and on what other domains of knowledge, if any, each thinker thinks this maker's-knowledge idea can be stretched to cover. Finally, whose version of the argument do you find more coherent or more defensible as it's pushed to its limits, and why?
