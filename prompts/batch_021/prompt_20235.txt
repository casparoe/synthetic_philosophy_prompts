I fell down a rabbit hole a few weeks ago rereading old predictive-processing posts (that "brain is basically just doing bayesian updating on prediction error, top to bottom" framing that's all over the rationalist blogosphere) and it dragged me back into a philosophy of mind argument I don't see discussed much outside of academic papers: the cognitive penetration of perception debate. Short version — Fodor and Pylyshyn's old line was that early visual perception is basically walled off from what you believe or want, "cognitively impenetrable," and their go-to example is stuff like the Muller-Lyer illusion, where you can know for a fact the two lines are equal length and you still just see them as different. But other people (Susanna Siegel is the name that keeps coming up) argue perception clearly isn't sealed off like that, pointing to things like expert perception — a birder who just sees "song sparrow" instead of "small brown bird, then infers song sparrow," or the old chicken-sexer case, where people learn to sort day-old chicks with high accuracy without ever being able to say what cue they're using. And then there's a newer counter-punch to that whole line of argument (I think it was a Behavioral & Brain Sciences paper, Firestone and Scholl maybe?) claiming most of the classic "your goals or emotions literally change what you see" studies — like the ones about hills looking steeper when you're tired or carrying a heavy pack — don't hold up once you seperate what people actually perceive from what they merely report or judge.

I half-argued about this with a friend at a meetup last week (he does ER medicine, so naturally it turned into a whole thing about clinical intuition), and it struck me that this is more interesting explored than argued about — so could you write me a short story built around it? Picture it set in an ER or ICU, following a nurse or doctor with the kind of trained "gestalt" perception experienced clinicians talk about — being able to tell a patient is crashing before the monitors show anything, what some people call the eyeball test — who runs into a case where that gut-level seeing goes flat against the numbers, or against another clinician's equally confident but opposite read of the same patient. Don't resolve it cleanly into "and the intuition was right/wrong the whole time" — I'd like it to actually sit in the uncertainty of whether this is a real, fast, sub-verbal form of perception, or just a well-camouflaged bias that feels exactly like perception from the inside, since honestly I don't think that question has a clean answer even in the philosophy literature. Go for something idea-forward and a little literary rather than a medical thriller — more Ted Chiang than House M.D., if that makes sense.
