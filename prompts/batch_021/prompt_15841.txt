I'm finalizing discussion questions for a graduate seminar on the ethics of belief, and I want to get precise about a move Clifford makes that tends to get lost behind the shipowner parable and his closing maxim. Here is the passage, from Part I ("The Duty of Inquiry") of "The Ethics of Belief" (1877), coming shortly after the two parables:

"If I let myself believe anything on insufficient evidence, there may be no great harm done by the mere belief; it may be true after all, or I may never have occasion to exhibit it in outward acts. But I cannot help doing this great wrong towards Man, that I make myself credulous. The danger to society is not merely that it should believe wrong things, though that is great enough; but that it should become credulous, and lose the habit of testing things and inquiring into them; for then it must sink back into savagery."

Clifford's official conclusion, stated a little later, is the exceptionless claim that it is wrong "always, everywhere, and for every one" to believe anything upon insufficient evidence. But the argument in the passage above does not obviously have the shape needed to deliver an exceptionless rule: the wrong it identifies is a social harm to a shared habit of inquiry, which comes in degrees and seems to depend on stakes and on how widespread credulity already is -- the kind of consideration that usually yields a threshold-sensitive rule with exceptions, not an absolute one. Could you help me work out, as precisely as you can: (a) what further premise, if any, would let Clifford move validly from a harm of this consequentialist shape to the universal rule he actually wants, and (b) whether James's well-known reply in "The Will to Believe" -- that our errors are not such "awfully solemn things," and that Clifford's rule sacrifices too much for the sake of never being duped -- actually engages this particular argument of Clifford's, or is really answering a different, more deontological Clifford that this passage does not obviously commit him to?
