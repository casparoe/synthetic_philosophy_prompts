I'm a PhD student in philosophy writing a dissertation on theories of promissory obligation, and a disagreement broke out between two of my housemates a few weeks ago that I still haven't managed to settle, even though it bears directly on the question I'm supposed to be making progress on: what actually makes a promise binding, and whether that binding force requires two distinct parties. The background is this. My housemate Owen had been trying, for the second time, to give up online poker after losing more money than he was comfortable with. One evening, unprompted, in front of our other housemate Nadia, he announced, "That's it, I'm done -- I promise myself I'm never touching it again." About three weeks later, late one night, he played a single hand "just to see if the urge was still there," lost a small amount, and said nothing about it until Nadia happened to see a notification from the poker site pop up on his laptop and asked him directly.

When she raised it, the two of them went back and forth for a while without either one budging. (1) Nadia said he had broken a promise, and that this counted as a real betrayal rather than a mere lapse, because he had said the words out loud and meant them at the time. (2) Owen replied that a promise to yourself isn't the same kind of thing as a promise to someone else, because a genuine promise binds you to another party who can hold you to it, whereas he could have silently released himself from his own resolution at any moment, with no one worse off and no one entitled to object -- so there was nothing there capable of being broken. (3) Nadia said that if it had really been just a revisable preference, he would have told her he'd changed his mind, the way he might about a diet, instead of quietly hiding it for weeks -- and the hiding shows that some part of him knew he was getting away with something, not merely updating a plan. (4) Owen said that feeling awkward about a conversation doesn't prove he wronged anyone; people feel bad about broken New Year's resolutions too, without thinking they've failed anyone but themselves, so the guilt shows self-disappointment, not a violated obligation. (5) Nadia said that if holding yourself to your own word counts for nothing beyond prudence, then no one could really commit to training for something or saving money, since in every such case you are the one person who could always let yourself off the hook -- so the fact that Owen alone could release himself is exactly why keeping his word to himself was demanding, and exactly what he failed to do. (6) Owen said that people build their lives around plenty of self-imposed rules -- diets, budgets, schedules -- without treating every lapse as a betrayal, and that calling this one a "broken promise" just inflates an ordinary failure of willpower into the kind of moral failure that only makes sense between two separate people.

Could you go through the exchange move by move, (1) through (6), and say who has the better of it at each step, rather than simply declaring an overall winner? I would also be curious whether Nadia and Owen are talking past each other on some hidden ambiguity in what a "promise" is supposed to mean, and whether there is a consideration neither of them raises that would help settle whether a promise to oneself can bind in the same sense a promise to someone else does. Please end your reply with your overall verdict on its own line, in exactly this format: \answer{Nadia} or \answer{Owen}, whichever you think is more right overall.
