Okay so I got into it pretty badly last night with my friend Priya (she's a phil of science PhD student) over something I now actually have to deal with at work, so figured I'd get a second opinion before I write it up. I sit on the clinical protocols committee at the hospice and bereavement network I work for, and next month we're voting on whether to formally adopt the DSM-5-TR's Prolonged Grief Disorder diagnosis into our intake process — basically, if someone's yearning/longing symptoms are still intense around the 12-month mark after a death, we'd flag them and route them into a "disorder" treatment pathway with a billable diagnosis code, instead of just keeping them in our regular bereavement support groups like we do now. About half the committee is pushing hard for this because it means insurance will actually cover treatment for people whose grief has dragged on and who currently just get told to wait it out.

The other half of the committee — and now me, after Priya kept hammering on this Ian Hacking idea that classifying people isn't like classifying rocks, because the people classified read the label, react to it, and change, and then the category itself shifts in response to that, a "looping effect" — is worried that handing someone a disorder label with a specific duration cutoff and a symptom checklist will change how they narrate their own grief, maybe even lock them into it, rather then just supporting them through something that's always just been a painfully normal part of loss.

Could you draft the memo I'd actually send round before the vote? I need one clear recommendation — adopt it, don't adopt it, or adopt with specific conditions — reasons a clinician on the other side would actually have to engage with, and a fair, non-strawman version of whichever side loses. It should read like something a working clinical committee would tolerate, not a philosophy seminar, but the looping-effect worry is a real part of why I'm stuck, so it can't just get name-dropped and then dropped.
