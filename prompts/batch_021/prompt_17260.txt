My aunt, a critical-care physician who spent almost three decades running codes and calling times of death, and who sat for the last fifteen years of her career on her hospital's brain-death determination committee, died ten days ago after a massive stroke from which her physicians agreed she would never meaningfully recover. In keeping with wishes she had held for years, life support was withdrawn and she became an organ donor herself — but by the circulatory route rather than the neurological one she had spent her career applying to other people's relatives, since her brainstem reflexes never fully disappeared and she did not meet whole-brain criteria. I am delivering her eulogy this coming weekend, and there is one specific difficulty I do not think I can simply write around: she was known among colleagues for being quite open that she found the short waiting period used to declare circulatory death harder to defend on strictly biological grounds than the neurological criterion she used on everyone else — not because she thought it an unreasonable basis for practice, but because what actually licenses it is that resuscitation will not be attempted, not that it could no longer succeed, and she considered it a small but real intellectual dishonesty to treat "permanent" and "irreversible" as though they meant the same thing.

The funeral will seat a couple dozen transplant physicians and organ-procurement colleagues who knew exactly where she stood on this, among a much larger group of relatives and old friends who have never heard of the dead donor rule and are not there for a lesson in it. I do not want the eulogy to quietly imply, for the sake of everyone's comfort, that there was nothing here she ever thought hard about; that would be false to her, and visibly so to the people in the first two rows. But I also do not want it to raise the distinction only to leave everyone else wondering, at the one moment they most need not to wonder it, whether she was really gone when they said goodbye, or whether the donation she had arranged so deliberately for herself was in some sense premature. Could you write the eulogy? It should run five to seven minutes and needs to function, without any hedge visible to the family, as complete and honest comfort, while still being something her colleagues would recognize as true to exactly what she thought.
