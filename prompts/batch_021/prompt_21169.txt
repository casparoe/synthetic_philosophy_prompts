After a career spent running a chemistry laboratory, I have devoted much of my retirement to reading philosophy from primary texts rather than secondhand summaries, and I have just worked through Berkeley's Querist. I had not expected the philosopher I knew only for "esse est percipi" to spend three pamphlets, published between 1735 and 1737, posing rhetorical queries about Irish banking and coinage. His recurring suggestion is that money is wrongly treated as a commodity with intrinsic worth, when it is properly nothing more than a ticket or counter for reckoning and transferring claims on goods and labor. This sits uneasily with the position Locke had staked out a generation earlier during the recoinage controversy, where he argued that silver coin holds its value by weight and long-standing consent, so that altering its denomination without altering its silver content amounts to something close to fraud. Could you compare Berkeley's and Locke's accounts of what money fundamentally is and where its value comes from, and tell me whether Berkeley's position is best read as his immaterialism carried over into economics, or as an independent argument that merely echoes his metaphysics in spirit?
