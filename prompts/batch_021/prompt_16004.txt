The question was: "Explain Putnam's multiple realizability argument against mind-brain type-identity theory, and explain how the same feature of functionalism that lets it avoid this problem is thought to generate a new problem for functionalism itself. Illustrate with reference to at least one thought experiment from the literature."

Here's what the student wrote: "Before functionalism, the dominant physicalist theory of mind was the mind-brain type-identity theory (Place, Smart), which held that being in a particular type of mental state, like being in pain, just is being in some particular type of physical (neural) state -- e.g. pain = C-fiber firing. Putnam's multiple realizability argument attacks this by pointing out that creatures with very different physiologies -- octopi, Martians, maybe even suitably organized robots -- could plausibly be in pain without having anything like C-fibers. If so, pain can't be identical to C-fiber firing specifically, on pain of chauvinistically denying that any creature lacking C-fibers could ever be in pain. Putnam concludes that what matters for being in pain isn't the physical or chemical make-up of the state but the functional or causal role it plays -- roughly, being caused by tissue damage, causing distress and avoidance behavior, causing the desire that it stop, etc. This is functionalism: mental states are defined by their causal relations to inputs, outputs, and other mental states, not by what they're physically made of.

The trouble is that this same permissiveness that lets functionalism avoid chauvinism seems to buy it too cheaply. Putnam's own China Brain thought experiment imagines the entire population of China wired up by walkie talkie to realize, for an hour, the same functional organization as a human mind; since the setup satisfies the functional definition, functionalism seems committed to saying this system is in pain, has beliefs, etc, which strikes most people as absurd. More mundane systems like thermostats or even national economies arguably satisfy fairly natural functional descriptions too. So functionalism seems to swing from one problem (chauvinism, if it's too restrictive) to another (call it liberalism) if its too permissive about what counts as the right functional organization. Because the liberalism problem shows that purely functional, causal-role definitions of mental states are too cheap to track anything psychologically real, the right lesson is to go back to the type-identity theory Putnam was originally attacking: mental states, at the end of the day, still need to be anchored in the specific physical stuff doing the realizing, not just the abstract pattern of causal relations."
