Last night a friend of mine, who works in cognitive science, and I spent close to three hours arguing about whether the multiple-realizability argument really earns functionalism the victory over mind-brain type-identity theory that every philosophy-of-mind syllabus credits it with. Neither of us budged, so afterward I wrote up the position I had been defending. Before I send it to her, I would like your comments on it.

The textbook story runs like this: Putnam's case of pain in a human, an octopus, and a hypothetical silicon-based Martian shows that pain cannot be identical to any single physical type, such as C-fiber firing, since that would chauvinistically deny pain to creatures that plainly have it. Mental kinds must therefore be functional kinds, individuated by causal role rather than physical composition, and the same functional kind can be realized in indefinitely many physical substrates. This is treated as close to decisive, and it is why functionalism rather than identity theory is the default position in most introductions.

What I want to press is that the argument, if it is any good, works by convicting the identity theorist's physical predicate of being a wild disjunction -- "C-fiber-firing-or-whatever-plays-that-role-in-octopuses-or-whatever-plays-it-in-Martians" -- and wild disjunctions are objectionable because they are not projectable and do not pick out a causally unified kind fit to appear in a genuine law. Fair enough, but now consider what is actually doing the causal work when a human and an octopus are both supposedly in pain. If C-fiber firing does it in the human and some quite different state does it in the octopus, the functional property is only ever instantiated through these heterogeneous first-order realizers. Unless the functional property carries causal powers over and above its realizers -- which functionalists standardly deny, on pain of a fairly unfunctionalist dualism -- "pain," understood functionally, looks like exactly the same kind of disjunction one level up. This is close to Jaegwon Kim's argument in "Multiple Realization and the Metaphysics of Reduction" and related papers from the early 1990s: taken seriously, multiple realizability does not just refute a physical-type identity for the mental, it threatens the causal and nomological credentials of the functional kind itself. Functionalism does not solve the problem multiple realizability was supposed to pose for type-identity theory; it relocates it.

The two standard replies both strike me as costly. Fodor's line, from "Special Sciences," is that a predicate can be wildly disjunctive from the standpoint of physics while still being perfectly natural and lawlike from the standpoint of the special science that employs it, so psychology owes physics no apology for disjunction. That is true, but it stipulates that psychological kinds are unified rather than explaining how they manage to be, given that nothing at the level where the causal work actually happens is shared across their instances. Shoemaker's subset-view alternative -- the functional property's causal powers are a subset of its realizer's powers, so there is no distinct higher-level property competing for causal influence -- avoids the exclusion worry, but only by making the higher-level kind so deflationary that it is not obvious it remains one kind: "pain" becomes a label for "has some causal powers drawn from among these various realizer-specific menus," a thinner sort of unity than the one functionalism was supposed to buy over identity theory.

There is also an empirical wrinkle that cuts against the textbook cases directly. Bechtel and Mundale, in "Multiple Realizability Revisited," and more recently Thomas Polger and Lawrence Shapiro in "The Multiple Realization Book," argue that neuroscientists individuate brain states partly by function in the first place, so the classic cross-species cases look wildly multiply realized only because they compare a coarse psychological taxonomy to a needlessly fine physical one. Match the grains and the disjunctions get much tidier -- tidy enough, arguably, for the local, species- or structure-restricted identities that Kim recommended as the real moral of multiple realizability, rather than for one substrate-independent functional kind. If that is right, it is bad news for the classical anti-reductionist argument, but I do not think it is good news for full-blown functionalism either.

What worries me most about my own argument is that it seems to generalize to any special-science kind that is multiply realized -- genes, hearts, money -- and I do not want to be committed to eliminativism about biology and economics along with psychology. So either there is a principled reason the disjunction problem bites mental kinds specifically, or the moral has to be more modest than I have been making it sound: not that functionalism is false, but that it never actually paid off the IOU multiple realizability was invoked to write in the first place.

I would like your comments on three things in particular: first, whether I have represented Kim's argument and Shoemaker's subset-view reply fairly, or flattened something important in either; second, whether the Bechtel-Mundale and Polger-Shapiro point actually helps my argument or undercuts it -- my friend thinks it undercuts me, and I could not talk her out of it; and third, whether there is a principled way to keep the disjunction worry from generalizing to every multiply realized special-science kind, short of simply accepting that conclusion.
