I've been implementing Monte Carlo samplers for a few classic geometric-probability problems as a side project, and reading around Bertrand's paradox in that context has left me stuck on the philosophical status of the "principle of indifference." As I understand the standard setup, if you ask for the probability that a randomly drawn chord of a circle is longer than the side of the equilateral triangle inscribed in that circle, you get three different answers -- 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4 -- depending on whether the chord is generated by picking two uniformly random endpoints on the circumference, a uniformly random distance from the center along a fixed radius, or a uniformly random midpoint within the disk. Each method looks like an equally legitimate way of cashing out the instruction "choose a chord at random," yet they disagree with one another, which seems to show that "equal ignorance" by itself doesn't pin down a unique probability distribution unless you've already committed to a particular parametrization of the problem.

What I can't get an intuitive handle on is E.T. Jaynes' proposed resolution: he argues that if you additionally require the solution to respect the symmetries the physical problem actually has -- invariance under rotating, translating, and rescaling the circle -- only one of the three methods survives, so the "paradox" was really an underspecified problem rather than a genuine breakdown of probabilistic reasoning. Could you walk me through, on a fairly intuitive level, why imposing those invariance requirements manages to single out a unique distribution here? I'd also like to know how far this kind of fix is supposed to generalize. I recently came across a related puzzle, sometimes called the "cube factory" problem, in which a factory produces cubes of unknown side length within some fixed range, and applying the principle of indifference to the side length versus to the volume (a nonlinear function of the side length) yields two incompatible distributions, apparently without any comparable geometric symmetry to appeal to. Is there some deeper, unified account of when invariance considerations can rescue the principle of indifference and when they can't, or is Jaynes' move more of a special-case patch that happens to work for sufficiently symmetric setups like the original chord problem?
