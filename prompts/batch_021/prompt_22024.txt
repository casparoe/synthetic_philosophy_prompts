I've been reporting a piece on why philosophy of language is suddenly having a moment again (turns out once chatbots go around confidently using names and descriptions, everyone wants to know whether they "really" refer to anything), and that took me to a workshop last month where two people I interviewed, Marcus and Elena, both about to finish PhDs on reference and semantics, got into it at the pub afterward over Kripke's causal theory of names. It started because Elena brought up the Madagascar case — apparently the name was originally used by people on the African mainland for part of the mainland itself, and it only ended up attached to the island because Marco Polo garbled the geography in his writings and everybody since just went along with his mistake. She said there's a footnote in Naming and Necessity (3, maybe? I definately didn't write it down) where Kripke half-admits this is awkward for his own picture, and that Gareth Evans built a whole paper around it in the 70s. She also mentioned, almost as an aside, that the history might be apocryphal anyway, but that this doesn't matter for the philosophical point.

Here's roughly how the argument went, as best I can reconstruct it on the train home. (1) Marcus said the case sinks the pure causal-chain story: if using a name just means intending to refer to whatever your source referred to, the chain should keep dragging everyone back to the mainland, so reference must actually be tracking something like Evans's "dominant current source of the community's information" — which, Marcus said, is descriptivism wearing a trenchcoat. (2) Elena said not so fast: Kripke's chains were never "blind," and Marco Polo's own garbled usage can simply count as a new link — a fresh, if confused, act of reference-fixing that everyone downstream inherited — no different in kind from how "Neptune" got fixed by description without the name becoming synonymous with that description. (3) Marcus shot back that this move is too easy: if you're always allowed to rescue the chain by inserting a "new baptism" exactly wherever reference happens to have shifted, the theory stops predicting anything and just relabels the facts after the fact. (4) Elena said that's unfair, because not just any shift counts as a baptism — it has to involve actual perceptual or testimonial contact with the new object plus some intention to name that thing, and Marco Polo's case genuinely satisfies that, so the condition is doing real work, not just letting the theory off the hook. (5) Marcus's last word was that Evans's own "dominant source" test is a matter of degree across a whole community's usage over time, so there's presumably no sharp fact about exactly when reference "flipped" from mainland to island, which sits oddly with names supposedly being rigid designators that pick out one determinate thing in every possible world. By that point I'd honestly lost track of who was ahead, since both of them kept landing real points.

Could you go through moves 1 through 5 and adjudicate each one on its own, rather than just picking an overall winner? And if you have a spare thought for it: does any of this actually bare on the necessity-and-rigidity stuff Kripke's more famous for? Once "Madagascar" refers to the island, however messily that happened, is "Madagascar is that island" the same flavor of necessary-a-posteriori truth as "water is H2O," or does the messiness of the reference-fixing change something?
