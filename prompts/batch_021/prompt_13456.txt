I have spent a long time reading rationalist blogs and forums, so I am quite familiar with the basic shape of the epistemic akrasia puzzle: you have solid first-order evidence for some proposition P, but you also pick up higher-order evidence suggesting that your own assessment of that evidence is unreliable. Epistemic akrasia, in the relevant sense, is believing P while also believing that you shouldn't, given your evidence. The standard illustrations in the philosophical literature on higher-order evidence (Christensen, Elga, Lasonen-Aarnio, and others) tend to be cases of transient cognitive impairment -- an exhausted doctor reading a chart, someone doing arithmetic after being told a pill degrades reasoning -- and Scott Alexander's essay "Epistemic Learned Helplessness" makes a related point about learning to distrust your own in-the-moment read of a persuasive argument.

I would like an example that does not lean on any of that: no drugs, sleep deprivation, or persuasive-argument cases of the kind Alexander describes. Construct a scenario -- perhaps involving peer disagreement, track-record evidence, or a selection effect in how the agent came to hold their belief -- in which an agent's first-order and higher-order evidence diverge so sharply that every available response (flatly believing P, flatly disbelieving it, suspending judgment, or knowingly remaining akratic) carries a genuine rational cost. Lay out the case in enough detail that the tension is concrete, then say which response you find least costly, and why.
