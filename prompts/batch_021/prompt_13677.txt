Here's the question, worth 20 points on her take-home midterm: "The paleontologist Stephen Jay Gould and the geneticist Richard Lewontin argued that much of evolutionary biology had become committed to what they called the 'adaptationist programme.' Using their central architectural analogy, explain what a spandrel is and what it's supposed to show about the limits of adaptationist explanation. Your answer should also explain how the concept of a spandrel relates to Gould's later concept of exaptation, with one biological example of each. (~300 words)"

And here's the student's answer, transcribed as-is:

"Gould and Lewontin's famous 1979 paper takes its name from the Basilica of San Marco in Venice, where a central dome rests on four rounded arches. Mounting a round dome on round arches inevitably creates four tapering, triangular spaces at the corners -- these are the 'spandrels.' Nobody designed the building in order to produce those spaces; they're an unavoidable architectural byproduct of the engineering decision to put a dome on arches. And yet the spandrels at San Marco are so gorgeously decorated with mosaics of the four evangelists that you'd be forgiven for thinking the whole church was built around them. Gould and Lewontin's point is that biologists often reason the same way about organisms: they see a trait that fits its role beautifully and conclude it must have been put there by natural selection for that exact purpose, when actually it might just be a structural side-effect of something else entirely (a developmental constraint, an accident of growth, a correlated trait dragged along with one that really is under selection). They call the tendency to assume every trait is a perfectly optimized adaptation the 'Panglossian paradigm,' after Voltaire's Dr. Pangloss, and accuse adaptationists of inventing untested 'just-so stories' to explain traits that might not need an adaptive story at all.

The spandrel idea connects to exaptation, which is basically the same thing but for traits that were built by selection for one job and then got reassigned to another. The standard textbook case is the mammalian nipple: males have them even though they serve no function in males, simply because the developmental program for nipples is laid down before the embryo differentiates by sex -- a clean example of a trait built for one adaptive purpose (milk delivery) that was later co-opted for a different one. A spandrel, on the other hand, is a feature that arises as pure architectural or developmental necessity and was never selected for anything at all, the way feathers are generally thought to have originally evolved for thermoregulation and only later, once they happened to be useful for gliding, been co-opted by selection for flight.

This ties back to Gould's other major heterodoxy: alongside Richard Lewontin, he'd already argued back in 1972 that evolution proceeds not by the slow, steady accumulation Darwin envisioned but in short bursts of change concentrated around speciation events, separated by long stretches of stasis -- 'punctuated equilibrium.' Both ideas push in the same direction, against a picture of evolution as the smooth, continuous optimization of every part of the organism and toward one that takes the messy, historically contingent, non-optimal side of biological form seriously. Given all this, I'd say the safest default when we run into a trait whose adaptive story sounds too neat is to just assume it's probably a spandrel until proven otherwise."
