I'm preparing the session of my graduate metaphysics seminar devoted to Bradley's regress and the unity of the proposition, and I could use help sharpening an objection before I put it to my students. We have worked through Bradley's original worry that a relation, if treated as a further term standing alongside its relata, requires some further relation to tie it to them, and so on without end; we have also looked at Russell's shifting attempts, in the Principles of Mathematics, to secure propositional unity by distinguishing a relation "actually relating" from that same relation merely mentioned as a term, and at his later retreat, partly to avoid treating falsehoods as objective unities in their own right, to the multiple relation theory of judgment. This week we turn to Frege's rival proposal, from "Function and Concept" and "On Concept and Object," that concepts are essentially "unsaturated," so that a concept and a suitable object combine automatically into a complete, truth-evaluable whole with no further glue required, precisely because incompleteness — rather than free-standing termhood — is simply what a concept is.

Several of my students have concluded that unsaturatedness is the one proposal on the table that actually stops the regress rather than merely postponing it a step, and I want to test that verdict before next week's meeting. Please argue that Frege's doctrine does not dissolve the unity problem at all, but only relabels it: that calling a concept "unsaturated" restates, rather than explains, the very fact that was puzzling to begin with, namely that concepts, unlike objects, and unlike Bradley's free-standing relations, manage to combine with their completers into something unified rather than a mere list. Your argument should directly confront the obvious Fregean rejoinder that unsaturatedness is not offered as some further relating item that itself needs a connector, but as a primitive categorial fact that makes any such further item unnecessary, and it should address whether a defender of perfectly ordinary Bradleian relations could just as well help themselves to that very same rejoinder, simply by stipulating that relating, too, is a brute capacity that some entities (relations) possess and others (objects) lack.
