Two people in my philosophy-of-religion reading group spent most of a session arguing about whether the multiverse response dissolves the fine-tuning design argument, and I would like an outside, move-by-move verdict on their exchange.

(1) Maya opened by arguing that the multiverse response settles the matter: if countless universes exist with randomly varying constants, it's unsurprising that at least one is life-permitting, and we obviously find ourselves in one of those, since we couldn't observe anything else. No further explanation, design included, is needed.

(2) Devon objected that this is a version of what Ian Hacking called the inverse gambler's fallacy: seeing snake eyes at the table in front of you doesn't make it more likely that many other tables are running elsewhere in the casino, since you were bound to see whatever came up at your own table regardless. Likewise, observing that our universe is fine-tuned can't be evidence that many other universes exist, because we were bound to observe fine-tuning no matter how many universes there are.

(3) Maya replied that this proves too much, citing John Leslie's firing-squad case: if fifty expert marksmen fire and all miss, "I'd only be here to wonder about it if they'd missed" doesn't stop you from concluding something suspicious happened. Likewise, "we'd only be here to notice fine-tuning if it were fine-tuned" shouldn't stop us from thinking something needs explaining -- and the multiverse is a natural candidate.

(4) Devon answered that the firing-squad case actually cuts the other way: it shows the local surprising event needs an explanation, but the explanation it calls for concerns that particular squad, not the mere existence of unrelated firing squads elsewhere that you'd never learn about. Granting that fine-tuning cries out for explanation doesn't yet privilege countless other universes as that explanation, so Hacking's point survives.

(5) Maya then shifted ground, noting that multiverses are independently motivated by inflationary cosmology and string theory's landscape of vacua, so appealing to one isn't merely an ad hoc move to explain away fine-tuning.

(6) Devon replied that this concedes the original point: if the multiverse needs independent justification, fine-tuning isn't really evidence for it -- it only tells us where in an already-posited multiverse to expect to find ourselves -- and it remains a further, contestable assumption that the independently motivated multiverses are of the kind actually needed, ones where the constants vary at random across the relevant range.

Neither budged.
