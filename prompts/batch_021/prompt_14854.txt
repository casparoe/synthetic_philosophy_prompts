So I had this argument with my flatmate last night that went on for like two hours and ended with both of us googling old psychology texts at midnight, about whether emotions are basically bodily feelings or something more like judgments/appraisals about the world. Shes firmly in the "feelings" camp, and to back herself up she pulled up this bit from William James's old essay on emotion, the "if you strip away the bodily stuff there's nothing emotional left" argument:

"If we fancy some strong emotion, and then try to abstract from our consciousness of it all the feelings of its bodily symptoms, we find we have nothing left behind, no 'mind-stuff' out of which the emotion can be constituted, and that a cold and neutral state of intellectual perception is all that remains. What kind of an emotion of fear would be left if the feeling neither of quickened heart-beats nor of shallow breathing, neither of trembling lips nor of weakened limbs, neither of goose-flesh nor of visceral stirrings, were present, it is quite impossible for me to think. Can one fancy the state of rage, and picture no ebullition in the chest, no flushing of the face, no dilatation of the nostrils, no clenching of the teeth, no impulse to vigorous action, but in their stead limp muscles, calm breathing, and a placid face? The present writer, for one, certainly cannot."

I'd only ever known the "we feel sorry because we cry, not the other way round" idea secondhand, so this was my first time actually reading the arguement itself, and now I'm stuck on whether it actually proves what she thinks it proves. "I can't imagine fear without imagining trembling and goose-flesh" seems like a fact about the limits of James's (or anyone's) imagination, not a proof that fear IS the trembling and goose-flesh rather than, say, a judgment or a perception of danger that just happens to always show up bundled with those bodily reactions. She says this basically sinks any Stoic-or-Solomon-style "emotions are judgments" view, and even makes trouble for fancier "emotions are perceptions of value" takes like Prinz's, but that felt way too fast to me. Could you walk me through exactly what work this passage is doing in James's larger argument, whether the inference from "unimaginable without X" to "constituted by X" actually holds up, and how a judgment theorist or perception theorist could reasonably resist it?
