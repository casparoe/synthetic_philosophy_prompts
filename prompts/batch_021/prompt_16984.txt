It's worth 20 points. Here's the student's answer, word for word:

"Van Fraassen's Reflection Principle (from his 1984 paper 'Belief and the Will') says that a rational agent's current credence in a proposition A, conditional on the supposition that her credence in A at some later time t2 will equal r, must itself equal r: Cr_t1(A | Cr_t2(A) = r) = r, for every later time t2 and every value r that her credence could take. The intuition is that you should treat your own future opinion the way you'd treat the testimony of an expert, generalizing Lewis's Principal Principle, which says to set your credence equal to the objective chance whenever you know what it is.

Van Fraassen backs this up with a synchronic Dutch Book argument: a bookie who knows you violate Reflection can sell you a bet at t1 whose payout depends on what your credence in A turns out to be at t2, and then, once t2 arrives and your actual credence is revealed, sell you a second bet calibrated to that credence, such that the combination of the two bets guarantees you a net loss no matter how the world turns out. Since a rational believer shouldn't be guaranteed to lose money purely as a result of her own belief-forming policy, this is meant to show that violating Reflection is a form of irrationality on a par with having credences that violate the axioms of probability.

Reflection obviously can't be a fully general requirement, though, since it would be crazy to defer to a future self you expect to be less rational or worse informed than you are right now, so van Fraassen restricts the principle to cases where you don't expect to lose information or become less rational between t1 and t2. This is exactly what happens in cases of anticipated memory loss, though: if I know I'm taking a memory-erasing drug tonight that will wipe out everything I learned today, Reflection still gives the right answer, since tomorrow's me is still me, just working from different evidence, so deferring to her opinion is still deferring to my own rational judgement and not to a stranger's. So the memory-loss case, far from being a counterexample, is actually a nice illustration of why Reflection holds up even across changes in evidence."
