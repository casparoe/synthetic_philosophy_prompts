I spent my working life as an experimental physicist, and only in retirement have I turned to reading philosophy in earnest. Lately I've been captivated by a strand of decision theory concerned with so-called 'acausal trade' -- the idea that agents who can never causally interact might nonetheless cooperate, as when two civilizations arise in regions of the universe that, owing to its accelerating expansion, will remain forever beyond each other's cosmological horizons. Please write me a dialogue between two decision theorists debating exactly this case: one argues, drawing on functional or updateless decision theory, that each civilization should forgo some costly, resource-hungry project on the expectation that a sufficiently similar reasoner on the other side concludes the same; the other holds an orthodox causal decision theory and denies that anything worth calling 'trade' can occur between parties unable to observe, verify, or influence one another. Let the skeptic press the defender on what, absent any such channel, distinguishes their appeal to 'logical' correlation from the sort of merely coincidental correlation decision theorists standardly agree to ignore, as in the smoking-lesion case -- and have the defender meet this challenge directly, with real back-and-forth rather than either side simply restating its opening position.
