Last week, a friend of mine who is finishing a PhD in metaphysics and I got into a long, increasingly heated argument over dinner about whether Bradley's regress actually shows anything about relations in general, or whether it merely reflects Bradley's own monistic assumptions about what a genuine unity has to look like. She kept insisting that the whole regress can be defused simply by denying that instantiation is itself a further relation, essentially the move Russell makes in the Principles of Mathematics when he distinguishes a relation that is actually relating its terms within a proposition from that same relation considered as a term or object of thought in its own right, and then argues on this basis that the unity of a proposition doesn't require Bradley's endless chain of connecting relations. I wasn't fully convinced this meets Bradley on his own terms, especially once I went home and reread the actual chapter rather than relying on the tidy secondary-literature paraphrases we had both been trading over dinner.

The passage we ended up disagreeing about most is from Chapter III of Appearance and Reality, "Relation and Quality," where Bradley states the regress for relations directly:

"But how the relation can stand to the qualities is, on the other side, unintelligible. If it is nothing to the qualities, then they are not related at all; and, if so, as we saw, they have ceased to be qualities, and their relation is a nonentity. But if it is to be something to them, then clearly we now shall require a new connecting relation. For the relation hardly can be the mere adjective of one or both of its terms; or, at least, as such it seems indefensible. And, being something itself, if it does not itself bear a relation to the terms, in what intelligible way will it succeed in being anything to them? But here again we are hurried off into the eddy of a hopeless process, since we are forced to go on finding new relations without end. The links are united by a link, and this bond of union is a link which also has two ends; and these require each a fresh link to connect them with the old."

Could you help me reconstruct the actual logical anatomy of the dilemma in this passage? I want to understand exactly what forces the choice between the relation's being "nothing" to its terms and its being "something" to them, and why the "something" option specifically requires a further connecting relation rather than some non-relational mode of belonging. I would also like to know whether my friend's instantiation-based rebuttal genuinely escapes the dilemma as Bradley poses it here, or whether his own phrasing already anticipates and blocks that kind of response.
