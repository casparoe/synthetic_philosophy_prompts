I'm helping grade take-home final exams for an upper-level seminar on Kantian ethics, and I'd like a careful second opinion on one response before I finalize scores for the rest of the stack. The question, worth 20 points, read as follows: "Kant claims that an action possesses genuine moral worth only when it is done from duty rather than merely in conformity with duty, and he illustrates the point with the case of a sympathetic philanthropist who helps others out of natural inclination rather than principle. Reconstruct this argument in your own words. Then explain how Barbara Herman's proposal that the motive of duty can function as a 'limiting condition,' and/or Julia Markovits's Coincident Reasons Thesis, attempt to answer the objection that Kant's view perversely rewards cold, reluctant helpers over warm-hearted ones. Does the proposal (or proposals) you discuss succeed in vindicating Kant's view, or do they end up changing the subject?" Students had access to the Groundwork and the two secondary readings, but no other sources.

One student answered as follows: "Kant's shopkeeper example shows that keeping honest, fixed prices out of self-interest, while it conforms to duty, has no moral worth, since the shopkeeper would treat customers the same way whether or not it was required of him. He makes the point more sharply with the sympathetic philanthropist: a person who is naturally so warm-hearted that spreading happiness around him is itself a source of inner satisfaction gets no moral credit for helping others, however right, useful, and even amiable the action is, because he is simply following an inclination, on a par with any other inclination like the love of honor. Kant adds that if this same man's heart were clouded by his own grief, extinguishing his fellow-feeling, and he still tore himself out of this insensibility to help others from duty alone, this is exactly when his action would first acquire genuine moral worth. This looks damaging, since it implies that the more naturally kind you are, the less credit you deserve for helping people; taken to its logical conclusion, it suggests that a perfectly moral agent would do well to blunt their natural sympathy, since on Kant's view warmth of feeling can only ever get in the way of duty, never support it. Schiller mocked Kant along these lines. Barbara Herman's response is that the motive of duty need not be doing the work in every case; it can instead sit in the background as a 'limiting condition' that only overrides one's other motives if they would ever counsel something impermissible. As long as this is true of the philanthropist, his sympathy-driven help still counts as done from duty. Julia Markovits develops this same strategy further with her Coincident Reasons Thesis: the motive of duty, understood as this kind of standing, limiting disposition, is still what ultimately explains why the action is creditable, so that acting from love and acting from duty coincide in the well brought-up agent. Herman and Markovits therefore both save Kant's rigorism by relocating the duty-motive rather than dropping it, and the objection is answered."
