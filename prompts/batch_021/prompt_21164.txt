For most of my career I worked as an experimental physicist, and only in retirement have I had the leisure to read philosophy properly. A few months ago, working through a survey of classical Indian thought, I became quite taken with the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda: the claim that any object (vastu) possesses innumerable aspects (ananta-dharma), so that a single assertion about it, made from one limited standpoint, can be at best a partial truth. The associated logical device, syādvāda, prefaces each of seven possible predications — "it is," "it is not," "it is and is not" (taken successively), "it is inexpressible" (when both are meant at once), and so on — with the particle syāt, which I gather is better rendered "in a certain respect" than "perhaps," a mistranslation that apparently led some early Western commentators to mistake the whole doctrine for a kind of probabilism. I couldn't help being reminded of Bohr's complementarity principle, where incompatible descriptions of a system can each be correct relative to a different experimental arrangement without any contradiction resulting. But then I read how hard the Naiyayika logicians and Vedāntins pressed the Jainas on exactly this point: that predicating both "is" and "is not" of the same thing, however qualified, either collapses into outright contradiction or is purchased at the price of never being able to say how reality stands in itself, and that the claim "every standpoint is partial" looks suspiciously exempt from its own rule.

I would very much like to see the two positions argued out properly rather than summarized the way popular accounts usually do. Could you write me a dialogue between a Jain philosopher defending anekāntavāda and syādvāda and a Naiyayika logician who presses the non-contradiction objection and the self-reference objection as hard as they can honestly be pressed? I would like the Jain speaker to draw on the tradition's actual resources — the sevenfold predication, the doctrine of standpoints (nayavāda) with its distinction between substance-view and modal-view standpoints, the relativization of predicates to substance, place, time, and state, and, if it becomes relevant, the idea that only an omniscient (kevalin) cognition grasps an object free of any standpoint at all — rather than retreating into vague talk of tolerance or open-mindedness. And I would like the Naiyayika to concede whatever is genuinely owed rather than simply repeating the objection more loudly, so that the exchange actually moves rather than loops. If it comes up naturally, I would be curious whether the two of them end up agreeing that their disagreement is really about which logic is fit to describe a complex reality, rather than about the facts of the case at all.
