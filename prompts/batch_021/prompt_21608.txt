I coordinate a small volunteer-run grief support nonprofit -- we run peer support groups for people who've lost a spouse, child, or partner, and we're loosely partnered with a hospital's palliative care unit for referrals both ways. I'm on the 7-person steering committee (mostly peer facilitators like me, plus a hospital chaplain and a part-time grief counselor), and at our meeting in three weeks we have to vote on something that's been dividing us for months: whether to start screening everyone who joins a group using the new "Prolonged Grief Disorder" criteria that got added to the DSM a couple years back, and then routing anyone who screens positive out of peer support and into "real" clinical treatment (therapy, meds, the works) instead.

Half the committee thinks this is obviously good -- people whose grief isnt easing up after a year or more need more than a chat circle, and honestly without an actual diagnosis code insurance wont cover extended therapy anyway, so the label is doing real work for people. The other half (me included, most days) keeps getting stuck on something my nephew sent me once from his psych program, about a philosopher named Ian Hacking who argued that mental illness labels aren't like labels for rocks or chemicals -- slapping a "disorder" on the way someone grieves might change how they see themselves and how everyone around them treats them, and that changed behavior turns around and reshapes what the "disorder" even looks like in the first place. So I keep worrying we'd basically be manufacturing the exact thing we're claiming to just be detecting, and telling freshly bereaved people their sadness is a pathology on top of everything else they're carrying.

Could you write the recommendation memo I'll circulate to the committee before the vote? I need it to actually land on a side -- adopt the screening, drop the idea, or adopt it with real conditions attached -- with a concrete plan for how and when we'd roll it out (or not), and it needs to take the "insurance and undertreatment" argument seriously instead of just letting the Hacking-style worry win by default.
