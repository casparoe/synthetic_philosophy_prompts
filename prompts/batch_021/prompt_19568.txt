A dispute among some neighbors of mine has been bothering me, partly because I suspect there's a deeper puzzle underneath it that I can't quite name. Four households on a shared private lane want to fund and build a short extension of the lane so that a new lot behind us can be subdivided and connected to the road; the four of us would split the construction cost and would be the ones voting on the final design and on whatever rules govern the new stretch afterward, such as a gate, speed bumps, or guest parking. The complication is that the extension runs directly behind two other houses that are not part of our arrangement and would not pay anything toward it, but would absorb most of the day-to-day cost in headlights, noise, and lost privacy -- by any reasonable measure, more than some of the households who are actually funding it. One of the two asked to be included in the vote on the final design, given how much it affects her; one of the four paying households answered that she never joined the arrangement and isn't contributing a cent, so it isn't her decision to make. To get past the impasse, someone proposed a preliminary vote on whether to widen the group entitled to vote on the main question, but only the original four households turned out to be the ones eligible to take part in that preliminary vote as well -- which, once I thought about it afterward, struck me as a strange way to resolve things, since the question of who belonged in the vote was exactly the question we were trying to settle in the first place.

Is there some principled way to work out who actually ought to have a say in a situation like this, or is it, underneath all the procedural back-and-forth, ultimately just a contest of bargaining power? I would like a genuine answer to bring to our next meeting, not just a suggested compromise.
