I'm putting together a new unit on non-Western systems of logic for a seminar I teach, and I keep running into the problem that every accessible treatment of Jain anekantavada defaults to the blind-men-and-the-elephant story, which I think actually flattens what's philosophically interesting about it. So instead of assigning another summary, I want to try giving students a dialogue. Could you write one between a Jain philosopher who defends anekantavada and syadvada, and an analytic philosopher trained in classical two-valued logic -- picture the two of them cornered in a seminar room after a panel discussion, still going at it an hour after everyone else has drifted off to the reception. The question I want them to actually chew on is whether their disagreement is substantive or merely verbal.

Push past the parable, please. I want the Jain character to actually deploy the machinery -- the sevenfold predication (saptabhangi), and the dravyarthika/paryayarthika distinction from nayavada -- not just gesture at "everyone's perspective has some truth to it." And I want the analytic philosopher to press the obvious deflationary move: that "syat" is doing exactly the work indexicals or context-parameters already do in classical semantics, so nothing metaphysically novel is on offer here beyond a demand for epistemic humility that any garden-variety fallibilist already accepts, without ever touching the law of non-contradiction. Have the Jain philosopher give a real answer to that (not a restatement of the elephant), and get them into whether the claim that a complete, non-perspectival truth is still available in principle -- to a kevalin, an omniscient knower -- is what actually rescues anekantavada from collapsing into relativism, or whether that move just smuggles the "it's all merely verbal" conclusion back in through the side door.

Both characters should be sharp -- I don't want the analytic philosopher to be a strawman who's never heard of context-sensitivity, and I don't want the Jain philosopher reciting Sunday-school Jainism either -- and I want real back-and-forth, not two seperate monologues that happen to alternate. It doesn't need to resolve cleanly; honestly I'd find it more useful pedagogically if they end up disagreeing about whether they still disagree.
