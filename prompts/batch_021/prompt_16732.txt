I'm a few years into a philosophy PhD focusing on formal epistemology, and this semester I'm in a seminar that's nominally about decision theory but somehow burned an entire session on the absent-minded driver problem instead of getting through the reading we'd actually assigned. Quick recap in case it's useful: a driver has to take the second of two identical, indistinguishable highway exits to get home — taking the first exit gives him a payoff of 0, taking the second gives 4, and missing both gives 1. Since he can never tell which intersection he's currently at, or even whether he's already passed one, the standard move is to work out the best strategy from outside the whole scenario, before he even starts driving: continue with probability 2/3 at any intersection he reaches, which nets an expected payoff of 4/3.

Here's the part that ate the whole session. Piccione and Rubinstein's original point was that once the driver is actually sitting at an intersection and reflects on his situation, something goes wrong — he seems to talk himself into wanting a different continuation probability than the 2/3 he's supposedly committed to, even though he's gotten no new information and his preferences haven't budged an inch. Aumann, Hart and Perry wrote a pretty forceful reply insisting this is a mirage: once you properly model the decision at the intersection as a kind of coordination problem between your current self and your self at the other, indistinguishable intersection, the "right" answer there turns out to be 2/3 as well, so there's no genuine inconsistency. Except then I dug up Piccione and Rubinstein's later reply to that, and they're still not fully convinced — they point out that the different fixes people have proposed over the years don't even agree with each other, which seems like a weird thing to be true of an allegedly dissolved paradox. It reminds me a bit of the Sleeping Beauty problem, except somehow worse, since here the driver's own beliefs about where he is feed back into which strategy even counts as optimal in the first place.

Could you write me a dialogue between two decision theorists really going at this — one who thinks Aumann, Hart and Perry basically nailed it and the whole "paradox" was just sloppiness about conflating planning-stage and action-stage reasoning, and one who thinks Piccione and Rubinstein were onto something that survives every proposed fix, namely that there's no way to be a single coherent rational agent across decision points you can't tell apart. I want them to actually engage with each other's strongest arguments rather then just re-asserting their own formalism each time, and I'd love it if by the end they'd at least pinned down exactly where the disagreement bottoms out — is it about probability, about what counts as "the same agent" over time, or something else entirely.
