In my philosophy of biology seminar, two classmates have been going back and forth for a couple of sessions over the function of the human appendix, and I can't decide who is right.

Maya's opening claim: the appendix has no genuine function. It's a shrunken remnant of the cecum that our folivorous ancestors used to digest cellulose, and people do perfectly fine without it after an appendectomy, so whatever it does now is incidental rather than functional.

Dev countered with the hypothesis, floated by some immunologists, that the appendix is a 'safe house' sheltering beneficial gut bacteria so they can recolonize the intestine after an illness wipes out the normal flora -- an immunological function, even if not a digestive one.

Maya replied that even if the effect is real, that doesn't make it a function: a trait has a function only if it's what the trait was selected to do, and nobody has shown the appendix was maintained by selection for bacterial harboring rather than merely happening to be useful for that purpose now.

Dev answered that this sets the bar too high for how biologists actually talk: we ascribe a newly discovered structure's function the moment we work out what it currently does for some capacity of the organism, long before anyone reconstructs its selective history.

Maya's rejoinder was that without the historical requirement, Dev has no principled way to exclude bad effects from counting as functions too -- on his view, the appendix's role in causing appendicitis contributes to some organismic capacity (inflammation, immune overreaction) just as directly as the safe-house effect contributes to immunity.

Dev's last word was that he can exclude that just by restricting the relevant capacities to ones that serve survival and reproduction, and that this is no more ad hoc than Maya's own view, since etiological accounts face the mirror-image problem of specifying which of a trait's many historical effects explains its selection -- the heart pumps blood, but it also makes a sound and takes up space.

Could you go through Maya and Dev's exchange move by move, say who has the better of it at each stage, and flag anything important about the etiological-versus-causal-role debate that neither of them brings up?
