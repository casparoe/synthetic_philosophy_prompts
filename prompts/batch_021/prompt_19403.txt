In my Philosophy of Mind seminar this term, we've been working through the classic texts on machine intelligence, and last week's session was devoted to a part of Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" that I had genuinely forgotten was there. Section 6 of the paper works through a series of objections to the claim that machines can think, and the last of these, which Turing calls "The Argument from Extra-Sensory Perception," is the one that stopped our discussion cold. Turing treats the statistical case for telepathy from card-guessing experiments as surprisingly strong, worries that a telepathic human contestant could give the game away by correctly identifying face-down cards in ways no machine could match, and proposes housing both contestants in what he calls a "telepathy-proof room" to neutralize the effect. Our professor pointed out how oddly this sits next to the rest of the paper, which is otherwise a model of careful, deflationary argument, and asked us to come to the next class with our own account of why Turing included it at all.

I'd like to think this through with you first. Could you help me work out, first, exactly what logical threat a telepathic contestant would pose to the imitation game as Turing set it up, as opposed to merely making the test harder to administer? Second, does the "telepathy-proof room" actually neutralize that threat on Turing's own terms, or does it just relocate the problem — for instance, to whatever channel the interrogator uses to communicate with the contestants? And third, is there a reading on which taking ESP seriously is actually continuous with Turing's broader commitments in the paper — his operationalism about mentality, his refusal to rule things out a priori, his general willingness to let evidence override intuition — rather than an embarrassing lapse bolted onto an otherwise rigorous argument? I'm less interested in whether ESP is real than in what this episode shows about the structure of Turing's case for machine intelligence.
