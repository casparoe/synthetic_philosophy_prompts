I'm teaching a graduate seminar on inductive logic and confirmation theory this term, and this week's session paired David Hume's classical problem of induction with Nelson Goodman's "new riddle of induction" from Fact, Fiction, and Forecast. In discussion, the class split roughly into two camps: one held that Goodman's grue predicate is essentially a more colorful restatement of Hume's worry about projecting the past onto the future; the other insisted that Goodman is doing something categorically different, and that his riddle only gets off the ground once Hume's problem has, in some sense, already been granted or set aside. Before I revise my lecture notes, I'd like a clear-headed adjudication of this dispute.

Please compare Hume's problem of induction with Goodman's new riddle, and settle what exactly is "new" about the latter. Be precise about which assumption(s) Goodman helps himself to that Hume's inductive skeptic would not simply concede, and walk through why, on a naive instance-confirmation view (in the spirit of Nicod's criterion), the evidence that all emeralds examined so far are green confirms "all emeralds are grue" just as well as it confirms "all emeralds are green." Then turn to Goodman's own way out, entrenchment: does appealing to which predicates have actually figured in successful past projections dissolve the new riddle, or does it just relocate the difficulty, since a population that spoke a "grue/bleen" language, with no simple words for "green" and "blue," would by Goodman's own criterion have entrenched grue and bleen instead, and so would reach the opposite prediction with equal justification? Defend your verdict.
