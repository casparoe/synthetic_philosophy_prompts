I'm running a graduate seminar this term on Anscombe and that whole mid-century Oxford circle (Foot, Murdoch, the lot), and we just did a session on her pamphlet "Mr Truman's Degree" — the one where she objects to Oxford giving Truman an honorary doctorate and says point blank that ordering the bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki was murder, full stop. It's a short, nasty little text and it always gets a reaction. This time two of my students, Leah and Marcus (not their real names), kept arguing about it well after the seminar ended, out in the hallway and then over coffee, and I mostly just tagged along and listened because I genuinely wasnt sure who was winning.

Leah defended Anscombe's line: Truman and his planners chose to kill a huge number of civilians as the actual mechanism for ending the war, and no amount of good downstream consequences changes the fact that intentionally killing innocents as a means is murder — that's the whole force of her refusal to let a good outcome launder an evil act. Marcus pushed back that this assumes we already know what Truman "really" intended; by his own account and Stimson's afterward, the goal was ending the war without a land invasion everyone expected to be even costlier in lives, and the civilian deaths, however certain, were the foreseen price of that, not the aim itself — exactly the kind of case double effect is built to handle. Leah said that's too easy: if foreseeing-with-certainty automatically counts as "mere side effect," you can redescribe any atrocity as pursuit of some further good; what fixes the description here is that the targets were intact, populated cities rather than isolated military or industrial sites, chosen precisely because mass civilian death and terror was the mechanism by which Japan was supposed to fold, so the deaths are built into the plan, not incidental to it. Marcus replied that Leah was now carving up "the plan" exactly as coarsely as she needed to get her verdict, the very move she'd just accused him of at the fine-grained level — and if neither of them has a non-question-begging way to fix the level of description, the intention/foresight test can't bear the weight Anscombe puts on it. Leah's last word was that Marcus still hadn't said why the number of lives supposedly saved should matter at all if intentions really do ground an absolute prohibition — letting the numbers back in once they're big enough is just consequentialism sneaking in the back door, which is exactly the disease she thinks Anscombe is diagnosing in the field.

I'd genuinely like your read on this, since it's got me second-guessing how to teach this unit next year. Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who's got the better of it at each stage, rather than just handing out an overall winner? I'm especially interested in whether the intention/foresight distinction can survive the redescription problem Marcus raises in his second move, or whether it really does collapse the way he claims — and whether there's some third position neither of them considered that both of their best points are actually gesturing toward.
