I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, but I recently found myself refereeing an argument between two friends over dinner, and I'd like a sharper analysis than I can provide myself.

One friend, Nate, maintains a fairly widely used open-source library that several well-funded startups depend on in production; none of them has ever paid him anything for it. Nate argued that this is a textbook case of Marxist exploitation: the companies extract the value his labor creates and return none of it to him. Sofia disagreed. She said Marx's notion of exploitation is a technical one, tied specifically to the wage relation — the rate of exploitation is the ratio of the surplus value a worker produces to the wage they're paid — and since Nate draws no wage at all, there's no baseline for a "surplus" to be measured against. Whatever else is wrong with the arrangement, she said, that apparatus simply doesn't get a grip on unpaid labor.

Nate replied that this seems backwards: if underpaying someone counts as exploitation but paying them nothing at all somehow doesn't, the theory rewards companies for being stingier still. Sofia answered that Marx's complaint was never simply "not paid enough" — it's that the worker is structurally compelled, by separation from the means of production, to sell their labor power to survive, and that capital directs the labor process itself. Most maintainers, she said, aren't dependent on their project to live and largely set their own hours, so even if something's unjust here, "exploitation" in Marx's specific sense may be the wrong word for it — perhaps this is closer to a free-rider problem, or to the appropriation of a commons.

Nate wasn't persuaded: he pointed out that plenty of maintainers do end up depending on sponsorships or consulting work tied to their project, and increasingly feel obliged to patch bugs and security holes on a company's timeline rather than their own — which looks a lot like exactly the compulsion and direction Sofia says is missing.

Neither of them budged, and I don't know Marx well enough to settle it myself. Could you go through the exchange move by move, saying who has the better argument at each step and why, and then give an overall verdict? I'd also be curious whether "exploitation" is even the right Marxist concept for uncompensated open-source labor, or whether some other part of his apparatus fits the case more precisely.
