I write a science-and-ideas column for my city's paper, and for the past year I've also served, a little improbably, as one of four community volunteers on the county's new Pre-Charge Diversion Panel, a body the district attorney's office set up to recommend, case by case, whether minor offenses get routed into a restorative-justice track (charges dropped once the person completes community service and signs a standard "responsibility statement") or sent back for ordinary prosecution. Our recommendations are technically advisory, but the DA's office has followed every one we've issued so far, so this isn't just paperwork.

The case dividing the panel ahead of next week's vote involves eight members of a local climate group who blocked a highway on-ramp near a newly permitted LNG export terminal for about ninety minutes during the Tuesday morning commute. They notified two newsrooms, mine included (I've recused myself from our coverage of it), but not the police; they sat down and linked arms, didn't resist arrest, and have no prior records. They're charged with obstructing a thoroughfare and disorderly conduct, and say the blockade came only after three years of public-comment campaigns and a lawsuit that was dismissed on standing grounds. A local TV station's initial report that the blockade delayed an ambulance shaped a lot of the panel's early thinking, including some of mine; the fire department's after-action review later found the ambulance had simply taken an alternate route and wasn't materially affected, though that correction got a fraction of the original story's attention. What's actually splitting us now is the responsibility statement itself, which requires signers to affirm they were "wrong to break the law." The protesters say they'll accept prosecution, or whatever community-service term we set, but refuse to sign anything implying regret, since, as they put it, they'd do it again tomorrow. One fellow panelist, a retired law professor, argues we're using the wrong framework altogether. She keeps invoking Hannah Arendt's claim that civil disobedience is essentially a group, political act — closer to an organized minority pressing a grievance than to an individual's crisis of conscience — and worries that a program built around personal remorse and rehabilitation may be the wrong instrument for a case like this no matter how we rule.

Could you draft the short written opinion I'll circulate to the other three panelists before Thursday's vote? It should recommend one concrete outcome — divert under the standard terms, divert on modified terms, or decline diversion and refer the case for prosecution — defended in terms the protesters themselves could engage with, rather than by simply checking their conduct against a textbook definition of civil disobedience. I'd especially like it to take seriously the strongest consideration against whatever recommendation it lands on, and to say something about whether the responsibility statement is even the right tool for a case like this, rather than treating that as a formality we can just waive.
