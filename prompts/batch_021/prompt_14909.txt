Except I want to do the version philosophers actually argue about -- a friend of mine doing her PhD in philosphy mentioned it to me once, I think it's called "Death in Damascus" -- where Death is basically a near-perfect predictor and the guy has to decide, in real time, whether to stay put or bolt for the next city over (Aleppo, I think, in the original puzzle). My twist is there's two friends who both got the same visit from Death the night before, and by morning one of them is loading a camel to run for it while the other is calmly unpacking his bags to stay -- and I want the big scene to be them arguing it out in the courtyard, each one convinced the other's about to get himself killed.

Here's the thing though, I want it to actually track the real disagreement and not just be two guys yelling "fate!" and "free will!" at each other. Can you write me that dialogue where one friend reasons like a causal decision theorist -- his choice now can't reach back and change wherever Death already decided to go, so he's leaning towards it not mattering, or maybe towards just flipping a coin -- and the other reasons like an evidential decision theorist, who keeps flip-flopping, since the moment she settles on fleeing, that itself feels like evidence Death will be waiting in the new city, so she wants to switch back, and then the exact same thing happens again once she "settles" on staying. I want her to actually notice mid-argument that she's doing this and try to defend it instead of just conceding, maybe by questioning whether the coin-flip guy's "its already fixed" assumption even holds once Death is explicitly trying to predict what they'll do. Basically, I want both of them landing real blows on the other's position -- no strawmen -- and I don't want it tied up neatly with one of them "winning." End it on whatever the actual open question between them is. Something like 20-ish exchanges, mostly dialogue with minimal stage direction, like I could drop it straight into the manuscript.
