I've been rereading the old Roko's basilisk threads and the LessWrong commentary that followed Eliezer's ban on discussing it, and there's a step in the decision theory that I can't get to resolve cleanly. The standard rationalist defense of one-boxing on Newcomb's Problem is that a sufficiently reflective agent -- one running something like Timeless or Updateless Decision Theory -- should treat its present choice as correlated with an accurate predictor's earlier read of its disposition, even though no causal signal travels backward in time. Roko's argument seems to lean on exactly that same correlational move: a future AGI's assessment of whether you helped bring it into existence is acausally correlated with your present behavior, so the same reasoning that tells you to one-box against Omega should tell you to take the threat of future punishment seriously right now, since that is supposedly what the AI's later verdict on you depends on.

If that's right, it seems to follow that anyone who accepts one-boxing as rational is thereby committed to treating the basilisk as a live consideration rather than a curiosity -- which would mean the sensible move is to hedge, say by quietly supporting whichever AI safety effort seems likeliest to produce the described agent, just in case. When I raised this with a friend from my reading group, he insisted that TDT and UDT actually block this conclusion rather than support it, but he couldn't explain why the same correlational reasoning would license one-boxing while somehow forbidding this kind of hedging. Is there a genuine, principled asymmetry between the two cases, and if so, what exactly is it about the structure of the basilisk scenario that a properly specified decision theory is reacting to?
