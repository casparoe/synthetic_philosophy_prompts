Since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have spent a good deal of my time working through the philosophy of probability, and the Sleeping Beauty problem has become a particular preoccupation of mine. I already have the basic halfer/thirder split between Elga and Lewis well in hand, so there is no need to rehearse it. What I would like explained is the more specific move Lewis makes in his reply to Elga. Rather than simply insisting that Beauty's credence in heads stays at one half throughout the experiment, he derives that if she is told, mid-experiment, that today is Monday, her credence in heads should rise to two-thirds. Please reconstruct the Bayesian reasoning behind that derivation from Lewis's own halfer premises, and then take up the objection that this lets a purely self-locating fact -- which day it is -- shift credence about the outcome of a fair coin. Given that Lewis himself holds, elsewhere, that the chance of an event already settled in the past is trivial (zero or one), does the charge that his view "violates the Principal Principle" actually go through, or does that same feature of his own theory of chance give him a way out? And can the thirder position claim to dodge any comparable cost of its own?
