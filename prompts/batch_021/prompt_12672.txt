I spent something like 35 years as a fisheries biologist before I retired, and these days I fill my evenings reading moral philosophy, which is a very different muscle to excercise. Utilitarianism appeals to my quantitative instincts, so I've been poking around the "wild animal suffering" corner of the literature. Something's been bugging me that seems completely obvious from the ecology side but that none of the philosophy pieces I've found really spell out carefully: the overwhelming majority of wild animals, counted by individual, are r-selected — cod, herring, most insects, amphibians, and so on — meaning they produce huge numbers of offspring, the vast majority of which die within days or weeks of starvation, predation, or disease. K-selected animals like elephants, whales, or great apes, the ones people actually picture when they hear "wildlife," are practically a rounding error by headcount. I mentioned this at a dinner party a while back and got looked at like I'd proposed culling pandas.

Every essay on wild animal suffering I've read asserts something like "nature is probably net-suffering" and then jumps straight to the policy debate, without really walking through why the r/K facts force that conclusion, or what exactly it would commit a consistent utilitarian to. So I'd like you to do that work properly: please argue, premise by premise, that the numerical dominance of r-selected reproduction among wild animals implies that wild nature, taken as a whole, is a strongly net-negative-welfare system under hedonistic utilitarianism, and that this should push a consistent utilitarian toward favoring active suppression of reproduction in r-selected wild populations over standard conservationist goals like maximizing biodiversity or population abundance. Don't hedge it into a "here are considerations on both sides" survey — I want the actual strongest version of the argument, laid out the way I'd have had to structure one of my own papers if a reviewer told me to justify every single step.
