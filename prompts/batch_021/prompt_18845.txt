I have come across "hinge propositions" often enough on rationalist blogs and forums—usually invoked as shorthand for background assumptions that no amount of Bayesian updating could touch—that I finally went back and read the relevant sections of Wittgenstein's *On Certainty* myself. Now I am stuck on how two of his central images there are supposed to fit together.

The first is the hinge metaphor itself:

"That is to say, the questions that we raise and our doubts depend on the fact that some propositions are exempt from doubt, are as it were like hinges on which those turn. That is to say, it belongs to the logic of our scientific investigations that certain things are indeed not doubted. ... If I want the door to turn, the hinges must stay put." (§341–343)

A few dozen remarks earlier, however, he offers an image that seems to pull in the opposite direction:

"It might be imagined that some propositions, of the form of empirical propositions, were hardened and functioned as channels for such empirical propositions as were not hardened but fluid; and that this relation altered with time, in that fluid propositions hardened, and hard ones became fluid. The mythology may change back into a state of flux, the river-bed of thoughts may shift. But I distinguish between the movement of the waters on the river-bed and the shift of the bed itself; though there is not a sharp division of the one from the other." (§96–97)

What I cannot reconcile is this: a hinge is supposed to be whatever has to "stay put" for doubting and investigating to be possible at all, yet a riverbed, on his own telling, can shift over time, and he is explicit that the shift is gradual rather than sharply set off from the ordinary flow. So is a given proposition's status as a hinge something that holds absolutely, or only relative to a particular stretch of practice that can itself later be carried away like the rest of the bed? And if it is the latter, what survives of the claim that hinges are what make doubt possible, rather than merely whatever happens to be settled at a given time? I would like an actual reading of these two passages together, rather than a restatement of "hinge propositions cannot be doubted."
