I'm a few months into a dissertation chapter on Frege's case against psychologism in logic. I've just had to confront something I had managed to avoid until now: the private diary Frege kept in 1924, the year before he died, which only surfaced publicly decades later. It is unabashedly hostile to parliamentary democracy, to Catholics and the French, and, most damningly, to Jews, including passages suggesting they be stripped of political rights or expelled from the country, and it expresses a clear wish to see the Weimar Republic's parliamentary system replaced by strong, authoritarian rule. My advisor's response, when I brought this up, was that it's biographically startling but philosophically irrelevant, since the soundness of Frege's logic and his sense/reference distinction can't depend on the character of the man who happened to discover them. That feels too quick to me, given how central the rejection of psychologism, and the claim that logical laws inhabit a mind-independent 'third realm' untouched by any thinker's standpoint, are to Frege's entire philosophical program. I'd like you to make the strongest case you can that the diary is not just an embarrassing footnote but actually bears on how we should understand Frege's anti-psychologism and his platonism about thoughts and logical truth, rather than being something we can cleanly quarantine from 'the philosophy itself.'
