I've been chasing a story on AI alignment governance for the last couple of weeks and keep running into the same unnamed tension that none of the company blog posts or whitepapers seem to want to spell out directly. When these labs talk about "whose values should the model actually serve" once users (or cultures, or political factions) disagree, they seem to be quietly reaching for one of two very different philosophical toolkits without ever naming them as such. One is basically Harsanyi-style: treat everyone's values as inputs into some big aggregate function and have the system maximize that (this is roughly the spirit behind older ideas like Yudkowsky's "coherent extrapolated volition," even though nobody actually cites Harsanyi by name anymore). The other borrows from Rawls' political liberalism instead -- the system shouldn't aggregate anyone's full moral or religious worldview at all, it should stay neutral between them the way a liberal state is meant to stay neutral between citizens' comprehensive doctrines, and just secure fair terms of interaction through something closer to public reason.

I want to get the philosophy right before I flatten it for a general audience, so could you actually dig into how these two positions diverge once you get past the slogans? Two things I'm stuck on: first, I vaguely remember theres a whole literature (Mongin? Broome?) showing that Harsanyi-type aggregation runs into a nasty snag once people disagree in their beliefs as well as their values -- something about the gap between "ex ante" and "ex post" aggregation producing a kind of fake consensus -- and I can't reconstruct why that's a distinct problem for value-aggregation specifically rather than just an economists' curiosity. Second, a friend of mine who does alignment work at one of the labs insists Rawlsian neutrality is a bit of a shell game: the moment you decide who counts as "reasonable" enough to belong to the overlapping consensus, you've already smuggled in a hefty substantive judgement, so the neutrality was never real to begin with. Is she right about that, or is there a genuine Rawlsian comeback?

So basically: lay out the real disagreement between aggregative and neutrality-based answers to whose values an aligned AI should serve, with actual argumentative teeth rather than an even-handed survey, and then tell me straight up whether either camp actually escapes smuggling in a substantive value judgement of its own -- or whether that's unavoidable and the whole "neutral AI" framing is a bit of a dead end from the start.
