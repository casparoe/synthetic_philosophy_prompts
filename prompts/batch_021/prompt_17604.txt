I serve on my hospital's newly formed clinical ethics and AI governance committee. A vendor has proposed piloting a 'preference predictor' for incapacitated patients who left no advance directive and whose families disagree about their care: it applies inverse reinforcement learning to a patient's own history of treatment refusals, spending, and everyday choices to estimate which interventions she 'would have wanted.' The output would be weighed alongside family testimony in substituted-judgment decisions. Proponents on the committee call this a rigorous formalization of what surrogates already attempt informally; skeptics object that inverse reinforcement learning cannot separate a patient's underlying preferences from the assumed model of her rationality, so whether a past refusal reveals a considered value or a distortion — denial, depression, family pressure — depends on a contestable, unstated assumption about how rational she was, and an equally defensible alternative assumption can reverse the inferred preference entirely. Please draft the committee's short written opinion: approve a defined pilot, reject the tool outright, or approve it with conditions, specifying how much weight its output should carry against family testimony, the pilot's duration if any, and what would trigger its suspension. Engage the identifiability problem on its merits rather than dismissing the tool as merely 'impersonal.'
