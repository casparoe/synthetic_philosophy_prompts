As a philosophy PhD student working on decision theory, I keep circling an argument from the AI-safety literature on bargaining that I would like to pin down with a concrete case rather than take on faith. Schelling's familiar point is that the power to bind oneself to a stance is a bargaining asset: whoever credibly commits first to something like "I will not accept less than X" forces everyone else to adapt around that fixed point. The worry raised about bargainers who can commit unusually fast and reason about each other with unusual precision — the paradigm case being advanced AI systems — is that once this incentive is common knowledge, each side wants to commit as early as possible, ideally before it even has good information about the game it is in, and that this "commitment race" can leave every party worse off than if none of them could commit at all. What I have not seen is a scenario that makes vivid why merely foreseeing the race does not let rational agents step back from it — why seeing the trap coming drives agents to commit earlier and more crudely rather than toward restraint. Construct such a scenario in enough detail to specify the agents, their incentives at each stage, and the exact point at which foresight backfires, and explain briefly why the dynamic is distinct from an ordinary game of chicken.
