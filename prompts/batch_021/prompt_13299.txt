I'm a PhD student in philosophy currently drafting a dissertation chapter that defends non-reductive physicalism about the mind against Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument, and I've reached the point where I need to pressure-test my central move on someone other than my advisor, who keeps sending the same worry back to me in a different guise every time we meet.

Kim's argument, in its mature "supervenience argument" form, runs roughly as follows. A mental property instance M supervenes on some physical realizer P, and P is by itself nomologically sufficient to produce a later physical effect P*. If M is also credited with causing P*, then P* turns out to have two distinct, fully sufficient causes at once. Since we can't accept that this happens systematically -- for every mental cause of every physical effect, on every occasion -- we seem forced to say either that M just is identical to P, or that M does no causal work of its own and mental causation is an illusion riding on physical causation underneath.

The line I want to defend is that Kim's non-overdetermination premise only has force against causes that are metaphysically independent of one another -- causes such that either could have occurred without the other, as with two assassins whose bullets happen to strike the heart at the same instant. Since M cannot occur without being realized by some physical property, and P realizes M rather than merely being correlated with it, the M/P pair never satisfies the independence condition that makes ordinary overdetermination troubling, so the premise simply fails to engage the non-reductive physicalist's actual position. Please write the strongest, fully worked-out version of this argument you can construct -- not a sketch of it -- and be explicit about exactly what notion of causal "competition" or "independence" has to be doing the work for the strategy to succeed, since that is the part my advisor insists I am fudging.
