I'm a philosophy PhD student in a seminar that pairs analytic work on death and personal identity with primary texts from classical Chinese philosophy, and this week we're doing a close reading of the Zhuangzi's "Dazongshi" (大宗師) chapter, which Legge renders "The Great and Most Honoured Master" and which is more commonly known in English as "The Great and Venerable Teacher." Our instructor deliberately skipped the two set-pieces everyone already knows from intro courses — the butterfly dream and Zhuangzi drumming on a basin after his wife's death — because she thinks students reach for the standard "Daoists calmly accept death as part of nature's cycle" gloss without ever reconstructing the actual argument, if there is one. Instead she assigned the story of the four friends Dze-sze, Dze-yü, Dze-lî, and Dze-lâi. I've cross-checked the plot against Burton Watson's more readable modern translation, but I'm quoting the assigned text, James Legge's 1891 version (Sacred Books of the East, vol. 39), since its stiffness is part of what's tripping me up:

9. Dze-sze, Dze-yü, Dze-lî, and Dze-lâi, these four men, were talking together, when some one said, 'Who can suppose the head to be made from nothing, the spine from life, and the rump-bone from death? Who knows how death and birth, living on and disappearing, compose the one body?—I would be friends with him.' The four men looked at one another and laughed, but no one seized with his mind the drift of the questions. All, however, were friends together.

Not long after Dze-yü fell ill, and Dze-sze went to inquire for him. 'How great,' said (the sufferer), 'is the Creator! That He should have made me the deformed object that I am!' He was a crooked hunchback; his five viscera were squeezed into the upper part of his body; his chin bent over his navel; his shoulder was higher than his crown; on his crown was an ulcer pointing to the sky; his breath came and went in gasps — yet he was easy in his mind, and made no trouble of his condition. He limped to a well, looked at himself in it, and said, 'Alas that the Creator should have made me the deformed object that I am!' Dze said, 'Do you dislike your condition?' He replied, 'No, why should I dislike it? If He were to transform my left arm into a cock, I should be watching with it the time of the night; if He were to transform my right arm into a cross-bow, I should then be looking for a hsiâo to (bring down and) roast; if He were to transform my rump-bone into a wheel, and my spirit into a horse, I should then be mounting it, and would not change it for another steed. Moreover, when we have got (what we are to do), there is the time (of life) in which to do it; when we lose that (at death), submission (is what is required). When we rest in what the time requires, and manifest that submission, neither joy nor sorrow can find entrance (to the mind). This would be what the ancients called loosing the cord by which (the life) is suspended. But one hung up cannot loose himself — he is held fast by his bonds. And that creatures cannot overcome Heaven (the inevitable) is a long-acknowledged fact — why should I hate my condition?'

10. Before long Dze-lâi fell ill, and lay gasping at the point of death, while his wife and children stood around him wailing. Dze-lî went to ask for him, and said to them, 'Hush! Get out of the way! Do not disturb him as he is passing through his change.' Then, leaning against the door, he said (to the dying man), 'Great indeed is the Creator! What will He now make you to become? Where will He take you to? Will He make you the liver of a rat, or the arm of an insect?

Dze-lâi replied, 'Wherever a parent tells a son to go, east, west, south, or north, he simply follows the command. The Yin and Yang are more to a man than his parents are. If they are hastening my death, and I do not quietly submit to them, I shall be obstinate and rebellious. There is the great Mass (of nature) — I find the support of my body in it; my life is spent in toil on it; my old age seeks ease on it; at death I find rest on it — what has made my life a good will make my death also a good.

'Here now is a great founder, casting his metal. If the metal were to leap up (in the pot), and say, "I must be made into a (sword like the) Mo-yeh," the great founder would be sure to regard it as uncanny. So, again, when a form is being fashioned in the mould of the womb, if it were to say, "I must become a man; I must become a man," the Creator would be sure to regard it as uncanny. When we once understand that heaven and earth are a great melting-pot, and the Creator a great founder, where can we have to go to that shall not be right for us? We are born as from a quiet sleep, and we die to a calm awaking.'

What I want help with is reconstructing the actual argument here rather than just its mood. First: Dze-yü, while ill, cheerfully proposes specific new forms for himself — a rooster, a crossbow pellet, a horse — but when Dze-lî raises the same kind of possibility with the dying Dze-lâi (rat's liver, insect's arm), it's posed as an open question from the visitor, and Dze-lâi's own answer declines to name any future shape at all, retreating instead to the abstract smelter analogy. Is that shift from playful self-directed speculation to a refusal to speculate doing any philosophical work, or is it just variation between two otherwise parallel anecdotes? Second, I can't pin down what's supposed to be wrong with the metal that insists on becoming a Mo-yeh, or the person who insists on staying human: is "uncanny" here a verdict that follows from some thesis about the self — e.g., that there's no fact about which of your successive forms is really "you," so that preferring one is already a kind of category mistake — or is it closer to the filial-obedience point made earlier in the same speech, where resisting Yin and Yang is condemned on the same grounds as a son disobeying his parents, with no deeper story about identity required at all? And finally, does the rat's-liver-or-insect's-arm language commit the text to some thesis about survival — that the stuff of you goes on, transformed, so that something continuous with "you" persists — or is it just an image used to produce equanimity, with no metaphysical weight behind it? I'd rather have a reading that actually adjudicates between these options than one that just hands me back the "acceptance of nature's cycle" summary I started with.
