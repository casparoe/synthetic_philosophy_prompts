the question: "In the Seventeenth Discussion of the Tahafut al-Falasifa, Ghazali attacks the philosophers' claim that fire burns cotton by natural necessity. Reconstruct his argument, including the fire/cotton example, and explain what theological work occasionalism is doing for him there. Then briefly (a) compare his position to Hume's account of causation, and (b) explain how Averroes responded to him on this point."

the student's answer, word for word:

"Ghazali's target in the Seventeenth Discussion is the philosophers' (mainly Avicenna's) view that fire burns cotton because burning necessarily follows from fire's nature. Ghazali's response is that all we actually observe is fire and burning occurring together, one after another; we never observe a necessary tie between them, only their constant conjunction, and since that tie isn't given in experience and can't be established a priori either, reason doesn't force it on us. His own proposal is that the real agent producing the burning is God, either directly or through the angels, and that fire itself is inert and has no power of its own. What looks like a law of nature is really just God's habitual way of acting, which he is free to suspend -- which is exactly what leaves room for miracles like Abraham surviving the fire, something the philosophers' necessitarian picture would rule out as a flat contradiction.

This is essentially the same insight Hume reaches centuries later in the Enquiry: he agrees we never perceive a necessary connection, only constant conjunction, and he draws the same conclusion Ghazali does, just in secular clothes -- what Ghazali attributes to God's will, Hume attributes to a habit of mind that makes us expect the future to resemble the past. Averroes, replying in the Tahafut al-Tahafut, largely accepted this critique of natural necessity, though he preferred to root causal power in the celestial intellects rather than God's will directly, so the philosophers' cosmology could stay mostly intact."
