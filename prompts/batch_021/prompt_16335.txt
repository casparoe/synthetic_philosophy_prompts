I'm drafting a novel in which one of the two central characters is a hospice nurse who was the only person to survive a car accident that killed her sister; years later, she still feels a hot flush of guilt whenever the accident comes up in conversation, even though she has been told by everyone, including herself on her more lucid days, that nothing she did or failed to do could have changed the outcome. I want to give this recurring sensation some intellectual ballast in a scene where she talks it through with her therapist, rather than reaching for the pop-psychology vocabulary of "survivor's guilt" without understanding what is philosophically at stake in a feeling that so stubbornly outlives the very judgment that would normally be thought to ground it.

What I would like from you is a carefully worked-out example of an emotional response along these lines -- one where the emotion persists in flat contradiction to the subject's own considered evaluative judgment. Then use it to explain why such cases are usually taken to be a genuine problem for theories that identify emotions with evaluative judgments (in the vein of Solomon or Nussbaum), and why theories that instead treat emotions as perception-like states representing value properties (in the vein of Prinz or Tappolet) are thought to handle them more gracefully, via the familiar comparison to a visual illusion that persists even once you know better.

I would also like you to be even-handed rather than simply crowning a winner: tell me what the strongest rejoinder available to the judgment theorist looks like, and where the perceptual account runs into its own difficulties in turn (I gather there is some objection concerning how responsive emotions are to reasoning, or how conceptually rich their objects can be, compared to a genuinely modular sense like vision). I don't want my nurse's therapy scene to read as though one side of a live philosophical debate has simply won.
