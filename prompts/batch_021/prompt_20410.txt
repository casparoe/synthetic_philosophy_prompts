I am working on a novel with a subplot in which two physicist characters spend a long night arguing about the reality of space itself: one insists that the points of spacetime exist as fully individuated entities in their own right, and the other maintains that only the relations among events are real, so that talk of "shifting" the universe's matter within an otherwise unchanged spacetime is empty. I have read enough to know that this dispute has a long pedigree, running from Leibniz's correspondence with Samuel Clarke to the modern "hole argument," which Einstein originally devised against generally covariant field equations and which Earman and Norton later repurposed as a case against manifold substantivalism, but I cannot yet see clearly how the two arguments relate to one another. Could you compare them for me?

In particular, I would like to know whether the hole argument is essentially Leibniz's shift argument transposed into general relativity, trading on the same worry that indiscernible possibilities violate the principle of sufficient reason, or whether its force comes from something Leibniz never had available to him: an appeal to radical indeterminism that depends specifically on the diffeomorphism invariance of general relativity. I would also like to know whether "sophisticated" substantivalist replies that give up primitive identity for spacetime points were conceptually available to a defender of Newton arguing against Leibniz, or whether such replies only become intelligible once the hole argument is posed in its modern form.
