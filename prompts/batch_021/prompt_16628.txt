I have been writing Solidity contracts since before the 2016 DAO hack, and the more I reflect on how that whole affair unfolded, the more it resembles a rerun of a debate I only recently learned has a name: the Hart-Fuller exchange. For context: an attacker drained a substantial share of the ether pooled in "The DAO" by exploiting a reentrancy bug in its withdrawal function, and the Ethereum community split in response. One faction pushed through a hard fork to claw the funds back, arguing that the exploit betrayed the shared understanding of what the contract was meant to do. The other faction refused to go along, insisting that whatever the code actually permitted was, by definition, the legitimate outcome — their rallying cry was literally "Code is Law" — and the original, unaltered chain persists today as Ethereum Classic. A colleague with a philosophy background pointed out that this is essentially Hart's legal positivism against Fuller's procedural natural law, transposed from statutes onto smart contracts, and I have not been able to stop turning the comparison over since.

Please write an essay that first reconstructs, with real precision, what Hart and Fuller actually disagreed about, starting from Hart's 1958 Harvard Law Review essay and Fuller's reply in the same volume — not the bumper-sticker version ("law versus morality"), but the specific mechanics: Hart's separability thesis, and his complaint that Fuller's celebrated "inner morality of law" (generality, publicity, non-retroactivity, the requirement that officials actually apply rules as announced, and so on) is really just a set of efficacy conditions for purposive activity dressed up as morality. Then treat the DAO fork as a genuine test case rather than a decorative illustration: do Hart's and Fuller's frameworks actually issue different verdicts on whether the fork was legitimate, or does the analogy break down somewhere — for instance, because no one "administers" a blockchain the way a state administers law, or because "Code is Law" is really a claim about legitimacy rather than about validity? Please do not hedge symmetrically at the end: tell me which framework you find more defensible once it is forced to confront this case, and defend that verdict against the strongest objection you can raise against it.
