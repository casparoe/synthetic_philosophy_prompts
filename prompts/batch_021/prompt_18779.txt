In a graduate seminar on epistemic game theory, we spent a session on the long-running dispute over whether common knowledge of rationality actually forces the backward-induction outcome in finite games of perfect information, and I came away more puzzled than when we started. Robert Aumann's 1995 result is supposed to show that in a game like the centipede game — where the pot grows every round a player chooses to "pass" rather than "take," and both players would do far better by passing for a while — common knowledge that both players are rational, in the sense of never failing to best-respond to their information, forces the very first mover to take immediately, destroying almost all of the available surplus. Critics such as Philip Pettit and Robert Sugden, in their well-known "backward induction paradox," along with later formal responses from Philip Reny, Elchanan Ben-Porath, and Ken Binmore in his exchanges with Aumann, have argued that the proof either smuggles in something stronger than the common knowledge assumption it advertises, or becomes incoherent at exactly the moment it is tested: if common knowledge of rationality is supposed to rule out any deviation from the backward-induction path, what is a player even entitled to believe about her opponent's rationality on observing the very deviation that, by hypothesis, should never occur?

Write an essay that takes a side in this dispute rather than merely narrating it. Explain precisely what Aumann's argument requires of the common knowledge assumption, and assess whether the off-path belief-revision problem raised by his critics genuinely undermines the derivation of backward induction or instead trades on a confusion about counterfactual reasoning within games. You may qualify your verdict — for instance, arguing that common knowledge of rationality yields backward induction only given some further, independently questionable assumption, and that without it the paradox stands — but the essay should not end by presenting both sides as equally live options. Defend a specific, considered position on whether rational players who commonly know each other's rationality really are committed to gutting their own payoffs in games like the centipede game, and say what the dispute reveals about the limits of formalizing rationality with epistemic logic.
