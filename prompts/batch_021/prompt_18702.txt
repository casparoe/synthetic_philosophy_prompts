Ok here's a rabbit hole I've fallen into this week and I can't get out of it. I'm working on a piece about "leftover" body parts — the appendix, wisdom teeth, the coccyx, that weird little fold of skin in the corner of your eye — and I called up a philosopher of biology to ask what I thought was a throwaway question, "what does it even mean for an organ to HAVE a function anyway," and apparently that's a whole cottage industry of disagreement, not just the etiological-vs-causal-role thing I vaguely remember from a undergrad philosophy of science class.

So here's how she broke it down for me (any garbling below is mine, not hers). You've got the classic etiological/selected-effects view (Wright, later sharpened by Neander and Millikan), where a heart's function is to pump blood because that's the effect that explains why hearts were selected for in the first place — history does all the work. Then Cummins's causal-role view, which throws history out entirely and just says the function of a part is whatever causal contribution it currently makes to some capacity of the containing system. Then Bigelow and Pargetter's propensity theory, cooked up partly to handle brand-new traits that haven't been selected for anything yet but already happen to boost fitness. Then Godfrey-Smith's "modern history" theory, which only counts recent selection instead of a trait's whole evolutionary backstory. And then a newer organizational/self-maintenance account (Mossio, Saborido, Moreno and that crowd) that grounds function in an organism keeping its own self-sustaining organization running, no selection history or current causal role required. Apparently each of these is basically a patch job aimed at dodging two specific problem cases: the "malfunction problem" (can a stalled heart still have the function of pumping blood if it's not currently doing that at all?) and the "novel trait problem" (a trait that popped up last generation via mutation, was never selected for anything, but is already doing something useful).

Purely for my own sanity, not necessarily for the article: how would you rank these five accounts against each other in terms of how defensible they are overall as a theory of biological function, given how each handles (or fails to handle) those two problems, plus just general plausibility?
