I am several chapters into a novel set in Cambridge during the early 1880s. Its central figure is a fictional moral philosopher, drawn rather liberally from the life of Henry Sidgwick, who spends his mornings revising a dense treatise on ethics and his evenings sitting in darkened rooms with mediums and automatic writers, taking careful notes for a body modelled closely on the real Society for Psychical Research, which he has helped to found. In reading around Sidgwick's own biography to get the period detail right, I have been struck by how strange this combination looks on the page: on one side, the scrupulous, almost paralyzingly self-critical author of a systematic treatise on the methods of ethics; on the other, a grown man cataloguing knocks and apparitions once his "real" philosophical work had, in effect, run its course. My working plan has been to write these as two largely separate compartments of my protagonist's mind, the rigorous daytime philosopher and the credulous nighttime ghost-hunter, and to build a certain quiet irony out of the contrast, treating the psychical research as a private consolation that has little to do, in substance, with anything argued in the treatise itself.

Before I commit the next several chapters to that structure, I would like to know whether it does justice to the actual relationship between Sidgwick's ethics and his psychical research, or whether I am flattening a connection that runs deeper than I have assumed. Is there some specific argument, or unresolved difficulty, within his ethical theory that the search for empirical evidence of survival after death was meant to address, such that the two projects belong to a single line of reasoning rather than to two separate lives lived side by side? And is "credulous" really the right word for how he and his circle conducted these investigations, or would that undersell something about their methods that I ought to get right before putting any of this on the page?
