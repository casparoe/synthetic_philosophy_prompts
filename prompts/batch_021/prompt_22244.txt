"Anscombe's case against Truman turns on the difference between intending a death and merely foreseeing it. She thinks it's sometimes okay to act knowing innocent people will die as a result, so long as their deaths are a side effect of going for some legitimate target rather than the actual point of the action -- e.g. bombing a munitions plant that happens to sit next to housing. Hiroshima and Nagasaki don't fit that pattern, on her view, because the whole rationale for the bombs was to kill and terrorize enough civilians to break Japan's will to keep fighting -- the mass civilian deaths were the mechanism, not a side effect of it. So the killing wasn't foreseen, it was intended, which for Anscombe just is murder, and murder can't be redeemed by good consequences -- it doesn't matter whether the bombs really did shorten the war and save lives on net, because you can't do evil so that good may come. That's also why, even though it sounds extreme, her position is really just pacifism under another name: if any total war between industrial powers is going to require this kind of deliberate terror bombing of cities, then her own double-effect principle -- which she's the first to spell out, right here in this essay -- commits her to opposing war as such, not merely this one bombing."
