I'm assembling a small collection of short fiction in which each piece is built around one narrow philosophical argument, pressed as far as it will go, rather than gathered loosely around a theme. The piece I'm currently stuck on concerns a claim of Kant's that I find more unsettling than his reputation as the philosopher of universal dignity would suggest: that we owe no direct duties whatsoever to animals, since they lack the rational autonomy that alone makes a being an end in itself, and that cruelty toward a dog or a horse is wrong only indirectly, insofar as it dulls the very capacity for sympathy in us that our duties toward other human beings depend on. On this view, a man who has his old, faithful, and now useless working animal destroyed does no wrong to the animal itself; if there is a wrong here at all, it is a wrong the man does to himself, a small coarsening of his own character that might, down the line, make him a little readier to be cruel to a person.

I'd like you to write a short story of somewhere between 1000 and 1800 words, set on a farm or smallholding in whatever period you find most workable, built around exactly this situation: an aging animal that has outlived its use, an owner who must decide its fate, and at least one other character who presses him in terms that echo Kant's reasoning, whether to defend the owner or to expose the strangeness of the defense. I don't want a parable about kindness to animals, or an essay dressed up as dialogue; I want the peculiar logic of the argument - no wrong to the creature, only a risk to the man's own soul - to be genuinely felt in the story's texture, so that a reader finishes it uneasy about the very thing that keeps unsettling me. I raised the premise with my writing group last week and got more disagreement than I expected, so I'd be curious to see it worked out in full, and to find out whether the story ends up vindicating Kant's instinct or quietly indicting it.
