I've been sitting in on a small reading group working through the primary texts behind the unity-of-the-proposition debate: Bradley's regress, Russell's response to it in the Principles of Mathematics, Frege's doctrine of the unsaturated concept, and, for a later session, Ramsey's 1925 attempt to dissolve the whole dispute. For this week's meeting, on Frege, I drafted the discussion notes below, arguing that his solution doesn't so much escape Bradley's regress as relocate it. Everyone else in the group has actually read Appearance and Reality and the Principles closely, whereas I've mostly been working from secondary summaries and memory, so I'd like a critical read before I bring this in. Here is what I have so far:

Bradley's regress starts from a simple worry: if a relation R relates a and b, what relates R to a and to b? If the answer is "a further relation," that new relation needs its own link to R, a, and b, and so on without end. Bradley concluded that a relational fact is never simply built up out of its constituents plus a connecting item, because the connecting cannot itself be handled by one more item of the same kind; it has to be already accomplished, brutely, or not at all.

Russell inherited a version of this as the problem of the unity of the proposition. "Socrates is wise" is not the same thing as the bare list Socrates, wisdom: the list is inert, while the proposition says something true or false. Russell's way of marking the difference, in the Principles, is to say that wisdom (or a relation, in relational cases) occurs "actually relating" its terms when it figures in a proposition, as opposed to occurring merely "as term" — as a logical subject that does no relating at all. But he never explains what this difference in occurrence consists in; it functions as a place to stop the regress, not as an account of why it stops there.

Frege's unsaturatedness doctrine looks, at first, like a genuine alternative. A concept is inherently predicative and incomplete, so nothing further needs to glue it to an object: the incompleteness just is the capacity to combine. But this only relocates the same difficulty, since the doctrine is bought by making the saturated/unsaturated distinction itself brute and, notoriously, unstatable from the inside — Frege cannot even say that the concept horse is a concept without the sentence coming out false, since using "the concept horse" as a grammatical subject automatically turns it into an object. That looks like Russell's move in different clothes: instead of stipulating that some occurrences of an entity just do relate, Frege stipulates that some expressions simply cannot be turned into subject terms without a change of logical category. Both stop the regress by declaring an asymmetry that ordinary grammar keeps tempting us to erase, rather than by explaining why the asymmetry is there in the first place.

I'd appreciate comments on three things in particular: whether I've represented Bradley's and Russell's positions accurately enough to survive contact with people who know the primary texts well; whether the parallel I draw in the last paragraph is a genuine structural analogy or whether I'm eliding a real disanalogy — is there a principled, non-question-begging story available to Frege, from the function/argument structure of his logic, that just isn't available to Russell? — and whether this point is already well-worn territory in the Frege–Russell secondary literature, so that I know whether I'd be presenting it as more original than it actually is.
