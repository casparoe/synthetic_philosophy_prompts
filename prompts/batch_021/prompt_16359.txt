I have spent the past few weeks interviewing AI safety researchers for a feature on the philosophical scaffolding beneath contemporary alignment discourse, and one idea keeps recurring in slightly different guises: Nick Bostrom's orthogonality thesis, the claim that an agent's level of intelligence and the content of its final goals vary independently, so that a system of arbitrarily high cognitive competence could in principle be organized around almost any objective, however arbitrary or narrow, and would then converge, regardless of that objective, on instrumental subgoals such as self-preservation, resource acquisition, and cognitive enhancement. More than one researcher, when pressed, admitted that the thesis leans on something close to a Humean picture of practical reason, on which reason is a purely instrumental faculty that identifies efficient means to given ends but has no standing to supply or revise the ends themselves. That admission is what led me back to Kant, who built his entire moral philosophy on rejecting exactly that picture, arguing that pure reason can itself issue an unconditional practical law binding on any rational being as such, independently of that being's particular desires.

For a sidebar to the piece, I would like your best reconstruction of how Kant would likely have assessed the orthogonality thesis and its companion instrumental-convergence thesis, working strictly from the actual architecture of his ethics rather than the popular caricature of him as a rule-obsessed moralist. Work through his distinction between hypothetical and categorical imperatives, the formula of humanity as an end in itself, and the idea of a kingdom of ends, and use that machinery to address three things: first, whether Kant's framework forces him to deny that a fully rational agent could be genuinely indifferent to morality, as opposed to merely choosing, out of self-love, to act against a moral law it still recognizes as binding; second, whether the convergent drive toward resource acquisition gives him independent grounds to predict friction with the formula of humanity, or whether he has no basis for that prediction unless the agent already counts as a member of the moral community rather than a mere means-end optimizer; and third, whether he would ultimately say that Bostrom's "intelligence" just is hypothetical-imperative reasoning by another name, in which case the two theories might not be in genuine dispute at all. Close with your own verdict on whether, on balance, Kant would have accepted, rejected, or substantially qualified the orthogonality thesis, and note where his framework strains hardest against a possibility he never had to reckon with, such as a non-human agent capable of recursively redesigning its own faculty of reason.
