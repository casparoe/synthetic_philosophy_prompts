So here's something that's been bugging me. You know Richard Jeffrey's old example of sizing up a piece of cloth by candlelight -- you can't be certain it's green, but the look of it shifts your confidence toward green and away from blue, and that shift itself (not some certain proposition) is what you update on. Thats Jeffrey conditionalization, aka probability kinematics. Here's the annoying part: if you recieve two pieces of uncertain evidence like that back to back, the order in which you apply the Jeffrey shifts can change where your credences end up -- unlike ordinary conditionalization, which never cares what order you learn things in. Some people take this to show Jeffrey conditionalization isn't really a basic, freestanding rational updating rule: underneath it there's always some perfectly ordinary conditionalization on a more fine-grained proposition (maybe about the experience itself) that got skipped over, and once that's made explicit the whole order-dependence puzzle dissolves. Others think that move is a dodge -- we're never certain of our own experiences either, uncertain shifts are the evidential bedrock full stop, and living with order-dependence is just the price of doing business. Write me a dialogue between one philosopher defending each side, and make them actually engage with each other's strongest moves (especially the order-dependence point) instead of just talking past each other. Both should be sharp -- I don't want either one turning into a strawman.
