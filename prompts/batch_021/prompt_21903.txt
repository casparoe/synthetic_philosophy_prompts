Having followed the one-boxing/two-boxing debates on LessWrong and in the decision-theory literature for a long time, I'd like to press on a variant that even many committed one-boxers find difficult to accept: the Transparent Newcomb Problem. Both boxes are clear. Box A always visibly holds $1,000. Box B is filled with $1,000,000 by a predictor that has never once been wrong; the predictor fills it just in case it foresees that, upon seeing B full, you would take only B, and leaves it empty otherwise. Suppose you look at the boxes now and see that B does in fact hold the $1,000,000.

Please argue that you should still take only Box B, passing up the extra $1,000 sitting in Box A, even though Box B's contents are already fixed and cannot be causally affected by whether you also grab Box A. I know this is exactly the sort of case timeless, updateless, and functional decision theories were built to handle, but I want the argument spelled out directly, not just asserted by appeal to which theory generally "wins" on Newcomblike problems. It should explain why, with the million already visible, that extra $1,000 isn't simply free money that ordinary dominance reasoning entitles you to take.
