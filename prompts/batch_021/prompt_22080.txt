I don't have any formal training in philosophy, but I got interested in Leibniz after getting annoyed at how often "the best of all possible worlds" comes up online purely as a punchline about Pangloss and Voltaire, with nobody engaging with what Leibniz actually argued. So I sat down with the Theodicy and a couple of plain-language guides over the last few months, and the part I can't get past is his answer to an obvious objection: if God surveys every possible world in his understanding and, being supremely wise and good, simply cannot help but pick the best one, in what sense did God ever face a real choice? Leibniz's answer, if I've understood it correctly, is to distinguish "absolute" (or "metaphysical") necessity, where the alternative involves an outright contradiction, from "moral necessity," where the alternative is perfectly conceivable and non-contradictory but is something a perfectly wise and good being would simply never do. Since only the first kind of necessity is supposed to threaten genuine freedom and contingency, Leibniz concludes that God's creating this world rather than a worse one is still free.

What bothers me is that this feels like it might just relabel the problem rather than solve it. If God's nature guarantees, with total reliability, that the best world gets chosen, then saying the alternative is merely "not a contradiction" seems like cold comfort — a bit like insisting a perfectly calibrated scale "freely chooses" to read the correct weight, since an incorrect reading isn't logically impossible, just something that scale, being what it is, would never produce. I know Leibniz has resources here that I should take seriously before concluding this: he explicitly rejects the idea that real freedom requires a "liberty of indifference" (an uncaused, tie-breaking kind of will), and insists instead that reasons can incline a will without strictly necessitating it. I also gather this is close to the same charge Samuel Clarke pressed him on, on Newton's behalf, when he accused Leibniz's principle of sufficient reason of leading to a fatalism no different in substance from Hobbes's or Spinoza's — an association Leibniz was extremely anxious to avoid.

Please construct the strongest possible argument for the position that Leibniz's moral-necessity/absolute-necessity distinction does not actually secure any freedom worth wanting for God's choice of the best world, and that his system is, despite his protests, committed to a version of the very necessitarianism he was trying to escape. I'd like you to take his best replies seriously rather than argue past them — particularly the idea that freedom is a matter of spontaneity and acting from one's own reasons, not indifference — and explain why, or whether, they still fall short.
