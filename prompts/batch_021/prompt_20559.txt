I spent much of last night in a long argument with a friend about whether incomplete preferences really leave an agent as exploitable as intransitive ones do, and I'd like your help settling it by comparing two money-pump arguments.

The familiar argument targets cyclical preferences: if you strictly prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, a trader can walk you around the cycle indefinitely, charging a small fee each time you trade up to your next most-preferred option and extracting unlimited money while you end up holding the very good you started with.

The less familiar argument targets completeness instead, purporting to show that incomparable options can be pumped just as cyclical ones can. Suppose X and Y are strictly incomparable -- neither preferred to the other, nor judged equal -- and suppose a "sweetened" version of X, call it X+, is strictly better than X alone yet remains merely incomparable with Y rather than preferable to it. Starting from X+, you pay a small fee to exchange it for Y, since nothing in your preferences forbids giving up X+ for an option incomparable to it; you then pay another small fee to exchange Y for X, since X and Y are likewise incomparable. You now hold X, having paid two fees to trade away a good you already knew was strictly better.

My friend maintained that both pumps are defused in exactly the same way: by an agent who chooses resolutely, refusing in advance any trade it can foresee will be exploited, rather than choosing myopically at each step. I suspect the two arguments actually come apart once you ask what that resoluteness costs the agent. Please compare them and adjudicate: does resisting the transitivity pump require refusing, at some point, a trade you strictly prefer at that very moment, in a way that resisting the completeness pump does not? And what does your answer suggest about the difference between synchronic and diachronic requirements of rationality?
