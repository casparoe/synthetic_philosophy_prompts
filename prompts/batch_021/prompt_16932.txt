This semester I'm taking a seminar on Ubuntu and relational conceptions of personhood in African philosophy, and this week's session centered on the exchange between Ifeanyi Menkiti and Kwame Gyekye over how far to take the idea that a person is a person through other persons. Two classmates got into a fairly involved back-and-forth about it during discussion section that our TA never quite resolved, and I've been turning it over since.

(1) One classmate defended what the readings call Menkiti's "radical" view: personhood is not a birthright but an achievement, conferred gradually as an individual is incorporated into the community through stages and rites of passage, so that the community has ontological priority over the individual rather than the reverse -- which is why, she argued, we can say of a selfish adult that he has "failed to become a full person," and why an elder commands a moral weight that an infant or an unformed youth has not yet earned.

(2) The other objected that this cannot really be a claim about moral status, only about social esteem, since taken literally it implies that infants, people with severe cognitive disabilities, or people who have simply behaved badly have a diminished claim to being treated as ends in themselves -- and that this is exactly the sort of consequence that pushes Gyekye toward insisting on some floor of individual rights and moral standing that no community is entitled to revoke.

(3) She replied that Gyekye's own "moderate" alternative, on which individuals possess capacities like rationality and will that are logically prior to and independent of community, quietly reimports the individualist starting point that relational personhood is supposed to reject: if a core of personhood is already fixed before any community exists, the community is doing far less metaphysical work than the slogan "I am because we are" was meant to capture, and the radical/moderate dispute collapses into a merely verbal one.

(4) He answered that this is a false choice: one can hold that the bare capacities enabling personhood are present from the start while still agreeing, with Gyekye, that the content of a person's identity, character, and values is thoroughly and continuously formed through communal relationships -- so the real disagreement is only about whether personhood itself is graded and revocable, not about whether persons are relational through and through.
