I am a PhD student in philosophy of mind currently drafting a dissertation chapter on Gödelian anti-mechanist arguments, and I would like help sharpening a specific comparison between J. R. Lucas's original 1961 argument in "Minds, Machines and Gödel" and Roger Penrose's reformulation of it in Shadows of the Mind (1994).

Both arguments turn on the existence, for any consistent formal system F adequate for elementary arithmetic, of a Gödel sentence G(F) that F itself cannot prove but that a reasoner situated "outside" F is nonetheless supposed to be able to certify as true — from which each author concludes that no such F can be equivalent to the human mind. Penrose presents his version explicitly as a response to standard objections, associated above all with Putnam, that had been raised against Lucas's original formulation.

Please compare the two arguments specifically with respect to what each requires the reasoner to know, or to be able to come to know, about their own underlying formal system in order to license the inference from "G(F) is unprovable in F" to "G(F) is true" — for instance, whether bare consistency suffices or something stronger such as soundness is required, and whether it is enough for the system merely to possess that property or whether the reasoner must be able to know that it does. On the basis of this comparison, give me your considered verdict on whether Penrose's reformulation genuinely closes the gap that critics pressed against Lucas, or whether it merely relocates the same difficulty in more technical dress, and justify that verdict.
