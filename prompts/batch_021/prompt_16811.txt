I am plotting a near-future novel whose antagonist directs a state disinformation bureau: rather than concealing a handful of secrets, it uses generative models to mass-produce mutually contradictory footage and news items, then floods the public with all of it at once through bot networks, until citizens simply give up trying to establish what happened. I want the antagonist's worldview to carry real philosophical weight, so I'm grounding it in Hannah Arendt, though she died in 1975, decades before anything of the kind was possible.

In "Truth and Politics" and "Lying in Politics," she distinguishes the traditional political lie, which conceals a secret from people who don't already know it, from the modern lie of image-making, which tries to displace an entire factual record the audience knows perfectly well. She even weighs whether a liar assisted by computers could eventually produce enough falsehood to outweigh reality's sheer factual density, and concludes that no tissue of lies is ever large enough for that. Elsewhere she argues that organized liars who sustain a fabricated image over time tend to fall for it themselves, and that this self-deception is ultimately self-limiting, because the deceiver's mind can drift from the world but his body cannot, so reality eventually catches up with him.

Reasoning strictly from those two arguments, not from her general reputation as a critic of political lying: would Arendt expect either conclusion to survive once the 'liar' is a largely automated pipeline operating at a scale she never had to imagine, with no single human mind that needs to keep its story straight? Where, if anywhere, would her own reasoning start to strain?
