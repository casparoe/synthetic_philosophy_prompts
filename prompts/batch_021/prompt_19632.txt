I don't have any formal training in philosophy, but I spend a lot of my working life around KL-divergence-based loss functions, which is what pulled me into the literature on entropy-based updating rules. I recently came across Bas van Fraassen's "Judy Benjamin problem": an agent is dropped into one of four equally likely regions, Red-I, Red-II, Blue-I, and Blue-II, and then told only that, conditional on being in Red, she is three times as likely to be in Red-II as in Red-I; nothing is said about Blue. Van Fraassen showed that updating by minimizing cross-entropy against the uniform prior, subject to that one constraint, does not preserve P(Red) = P(Blue) = 1/2 — probability mass leaks from Red into Blue even though the message seems, on its face, silent about the Red/Blue split. That has always struck me as a serious problem for treating relative-entropy minimization as a general rational updating rule, so I've been reading around for how people try to resolve it.

Could you rank the candidate responses below by how philosophically defensible each one is as a general account of updating on this kind of conditional information, rather than by whether it happens to match naive intuition?

1. Accept the result outright: minimizing relative entropy against the prior is correct, and the leakage into Blue is just the rational answer.
2. A "localized" Jeffrey-conditionalization (probability-kinematics) move confined to the Red partition, leaving P(Red), P(Blue), and the Blue-internal odds untouched.
3. David Lewis-style imaging: minimally revise the prior world by world to satisfy the new constraint.
4. Grünwald and Halpern's diagnosis, in "Updating Probabilities," that the problem is underdetermined without further independence assumptions, so no single rule is forced on us.
5. Douven and Romeijn's resolution, in "A New Resolution of the Judy Benjamin Problem," which tries to vindicate keeping the Red/Blue split fixed at 1/2.
