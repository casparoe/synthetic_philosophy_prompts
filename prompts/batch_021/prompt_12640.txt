Ok so I'm like three chapters into my dissertation on the ethics of war, and there's this one knot my advisor's comments haven't fully untied for me. Walzer's whole framework leans on the "moral equality of soldiers" -- the idea that a regular conscript fighting for the aggressor has just as much of a right to shoot at the guy defending his own country as vice versa, so long as both of them stick to the rules of engagement (no targeting civilians, proportionality, etc). But if that's actually true, doesn't "justice of the cause" become basically decorative at that point? It seems like if soldiers on both sides have equal moral permission to kill each other regardless of who started the war, then the theory can't really condemn agression in any way that bites once the fighting actually starts -- you get a jus ad bellum that prints "aggression is wrong" on the label, sitting on top of a jus in bello that licenses the aggressor's army just as much as everyone else's, which just seems to cash out to "both sides are equally in the right once the shooting starts," full stop.

My advisor keeps invoking the "independence thesis" and telling me I'm conflating two separate levels of judgment that don't actually contradict each other, and I get that in the abstract, but every time I go back and reread the actual chapter it still feels like a straight-up contradiction rather than just "two different questions being asked." I mentioned this to a friend from the law school who works on IHL and she just shrugged and said the law treats combatants as presumptively equal anyway so the moral question doesn't really matter for her purposes, which honestly didn't help me much either. Am I missing something structural about how the ad bellum and in bello judgments are actually supposed to fit together, or is this a real, acknowledged crack in the traditional theory? (I know McMahan built his whole revisionist project on attacking the equality thesis, but I want to nail down whether the traditional view is even coherent on its own terms before I get to his critique of it.)
