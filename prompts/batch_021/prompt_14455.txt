In a graduate reading group on formal approaches to ethics, we spent a session on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem. The result states that if each individual's preferences over lotteries satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, society's preferences satisfy them too, and a Pareto condition holds (whenever individuals unanimously agree in ranking two lotteries, society's ranking agrees with them), then the social utility function must be an affine, i.e. weighted-linear, combination of the individual utility functions. Two people in the group, Ana and Marcus, kept arguing about what this actually shows well after the session had ended, and I'd like help adjudicating their exchange, since neither of them budged and I couldn't settle it myself.

(1) Ana argued that the theorem is close to a genuine proof of utilitarianism from minimal premises: accept ordinary Bayesian rationality for individuals and for society, plus the anodyne requirement that unanimous preferences be respected, and you are logically forced into a weighted-sum social welfare function -- in her words, "one of the only real derivations of an ethical conclusion from decision theory, rather than an appeal to intuition." (2) Marcus objected that this shows far less than advertised, because each individual's von Neumann–Morgenstern utility function is only pinned down up to its own positive affine transformation -- its zero point and unit are arbitrary -- and nothing in Harsanyi's proof tells you how to fix those before summing. So the weights that make the conclusion look utilitarian aren't measuring anything about whose welfare counts for more; you can make them come out however you like just by rescaling each person's utility differently, which means the theorem never really shows that welfare, as opposed to some arbitrary artifact of representation, is what's being aggregated. (3) Ana replied that this proves too much: any cardinal theory of utility, including Bentham's, has always faced the problem of fixing a common scale for interpersonal comparison, and nobody takes that to show classical utilitarianism is empty. Fix the scales by whatever convention you like, she said, and Harsanyi's theorem still tells you the social ranking has to be the resulting weighted sum -- so Marcus's objection is really an objection to cardinal utility as such, not to Harsanyi's argument specifically. (4) Marcus came back with a different point: the Pareto condition in the theorem is applied to expected utilities, and expected utilities are computed using each person's own probabilities. He argued that once people's beliefs about the relevant uncertainty differ, satisfying that same Pareto condition can commit the social ranking to a bet that, once the uncertainty resolves, is guaranteed to leave at least one party worse off than if the bet had never been made -- even though beforehand, each party's own expected-utility calculation endorsed it. He took this to show that the theorem's logic, applied honestly to people who disagree about the world rather than merely about what they want, can end up licensing exactly the kind of outcome that unanimous agreement was supposed to rule out.

Could you go through the four moves in order and say, for each, whether the point being made is basically correct, overstated, or mistaken, and why? I'd also be curious what you think both of them are missing about what Harsanyi's theorem does and doesn't establish, beyond the specific points they each raised.
