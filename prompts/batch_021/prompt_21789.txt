Okay, this is now weirdly relevant to my actual job, which is why I'm asking. I just moved onto a team that writes policy briefings on Chinese overseas development lending for a small think tank, and my new boss keeps saying things like "it's the East India Company all over again" whenever Belt and Road comes up, especially with stuff like the Hambantota Port deal in Sri Lanka, where the port ended up leased to a Chinese state company for something like 99 years after Sri Lanka couldn't keep up with the loan repayments. I'd never actually read any Adam Smith beyond the invisible-hand stuff everyone knows, so I finally sat down this weekend and read the "Of Colonies" chapter in Book IV of the Wealth of Nations, and its way more specific and way less "free markets good, empire bad" than I expected. He's got this whole argument about how colonial trade monopolies were basically a subsidy from ordinary British taxpayers to a handful of merchants, how its a disaster when a trading company gets to act as sovereign over the same territory it's trying to profit from, and that famous bit about how founding an empire to create "a nation of customers" looks at first like a project for "a nation of shopkeepers" but is actually only good for a nation whose government is run by shopkeepers.

So here's what I actually want to know, and I promise to caveat the hell out of it before it goes anywhere near an actual briefing: if you sat Smith down in 2025 and explained how Belt and Road lending works, state banks and construction firms extending loans to lower-income countries for infrastructure and then ending up with control of strategic assets when the loans go bad, would he say that's basically a repeat of the mercantile-company logic he spent that whole chapter attacking, just without the standing army and direct territorial rule? Or is there something about the modern version he'd think is different in kind, not just degree? And using his own framework for who wins and loses from empire (his point wasn't that colonies were bad for everyone, just that they were a bad deal for the nation as a whole even though a great deal for the merchants with political pull) — who ends up playing the "shopkeeper" role today: the lending government, the borrowing country's own elites, some combination, or does the framework just not transfer cleanly at all?
