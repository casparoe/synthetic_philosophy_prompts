Since retiring from a career in ecology, I have taken up philosophy, and I keep returning to Hegel's discussion of war in the Philosophy of Right. Two strands of his argument interest me in particular. First, he denies that peace among sovereign states can ever be more than contingent: states relate to one another as independent particular wills with no common power standing over them the way a state stands over its own citizens, so that, unlike Kant, he does not think any federation of states could turn this into a genuinely lawlike peace. Second, he argues that even an uninterrupted peace would be no unambiguous good, since it would let civil society's private and commercial interests settle undisturbed into place; he illustrates this, aptly enough for someone in my former field, with the image of a sea grown foul under perpetual calm — much as an ecosystem denied periodic disturbance loses diversity and resilience. Please describe the most important implications of these two claims, taken together, for how much confidence I should place in present-day projects — a strengthened UN, standing international courts, federations in the Kantian mold — that aim to make war among the great powers obsolete.
