I sit on my university's Student Organizations Recognition Committee, which decides whether new student groups receive official recognition, along with the funding eligibility, room-booking privileges, and use of the university's name that recognition brings. This term a group of graduate students has petitioned to form a Kink Safety and Consent Education Collective, which would run confidential, off-campus workshops on communication and risk-reduction practices for students already involved in kink and BDSM communities; the charter explicitly excludes on-campus sexual activity and frames the group's purpose as reducing coercion and injury within communities that will exist whether or not we recognize them. The Dean of Students opposes recognition, arguing that official recognition would amount to the university endorsing practices that many students, parents, and donors find morally objectionable, and that the resulting reputational and fundraising exposure is a real cost the committee is entitled to weigh, not prejudice dressed up as prudence. Two of the petitioners, who took my seminar on Bentham's moral philosophy last spring, wrote back quoting at length from his unpublished essay on sexual morality, arguing that the Dean's position is close to a textbook case of what Bentham called the principle of sympathy and antipathy operating under the borrowed authority of harm-talk, since by his own analysis an act carries no "primary mischief" when it causes no pain to an unwilling party and no "secondary mischief" when it gives no one reasonable ground for alarm. A third committee member, aware that Bentham withheld that very essay from publication for the rest of his life out of fear of exactly the kind of backlash the Dean now predicts, wants to know whether that fact counsels more sympathy for institutional caution, or simply shows Bentham succumbing to the failure of nerve his own argument was meant to diagnose. Our recognition policy asks only whether a prospective group has an educational purpose "consistent with the university's mission and values," without saying what should happen when the purpose is not in dispute but the values are.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before we meet again next Wednesday. It should recommend one concrete outcome — recognize, deny, or recognize subject to specific, stated conditions — together with a timeline for acting on it, and it should use reasoning that both the Dean and the petitioners could actually respond to rather than a general essay on Bentham. Above all, I don't want it to simply announce that Bentham settles the matter: our policy governs an act of affirmative institutional support and resource allocation, not the criminal law's business of punishing private conduct, and I want the strongest version of that disanalogy taken seriously, alongside the question of whether reputational or donor cost can count as a genuine cost even when it is entirely downstream of other people's antipathy.
