So I've been falling down a rabbit hole for a piece I'm tentatively calling "the quiet crisis at the bottom of math," and I want to run something by you before I make a fool of myself in print. The starting point is Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, which as I understand it means a system like Peano arithmetic, or ZFC (the standard axioms basically all of modern math is built on), can never prove its own consistency from the inside. Digging around, I found an old story about a well-known logician (Edward Nelson, I think?) who actually announced in a paper that he'd found a genuine contradiction buried in ordinary arithmetic, before other mathematicians found the flaw in his reasoning within a week or two and he had to retract it. That's the part that's been keeping me up: if literally nobody can rule out these axioms being secretly contradictory, and a single contradiction anywhere in a formal system lets you prove absolutely any statement whatsoever (the "principle of explosion," right?), then isn't every mathematical proof we've ever built on top of those axioms — the calculus behind a bridge's load-bearing calculations, the number theory underneath RSA encryption, literally all of it — resting on a foundation that could already be broken without a single person on Earth noticing?

My editor keeps telling me I'm overselling this, that its really just an obscure worry for set theorists arguing about infinite cardinals and has nothing to do with the math normal engineers and programers actually use day to day. But I don't really get why that distinction should matter — inconsistent is inconsistent, isn't it, and "anything follows from a contradiction" seems like exactly the kind of thing that should apply just as much to a bridge as to a debate about uncountable sets. Am I missing something about how this would actually cash out, or is this genuinely a bigger deal than working mathematicians let on?
