For my philosophy of language seminar we've been working through Quine's Word and Object, and this week was the whole radical translation / "gavagai" chapter. I think I get the basic setup okay: linguist points at a rabbit, native speaker says "gavagai," and apparently no amount of pointing plus yes/no questions to the native can tell you whether it means rabbit, undetached rabbit part, rabbit-stage, or whatever else, since all these hypotheses fit the same stimulus meanings. What's actually tripping me up is a point my professor kept hammering on in discussion: she said indeterminacy of translation is NOT the same thing as ordinary scientific underdetermination (like how two rival physical theories can both fit all possible data), and that Quine is very insistent on this, even though on the surface it looks like the exact same problem -- multiple hypotheses, identical evidence, no way to decide between them.

A classmate of mine thinks this is basically Quine special-pleading, and that "no fact of the matter" is just a fancier way of saying "we can't know which translation manual is correct." But I vaguely remember from the reading that Quine wants something stronger and weirder than that -- more like there genuinely isn't any further truth about meaning/reference for the manuals to disagree about, even relative to literally every physical fact about the speakers (including all their dispositions to behavior), whereas he seems to want to leave open that rival physical theories could still be competing over some real fact we just can't access. Could you lay out, as precisely as you can, what's actually supposed to distinguish indeterminacy of translation from garden-variety underdetermination-of-theory-by-evidence? I'd rather you use the gavagai case itself to make it concrete instead of staying abstract -- like, why exactly can't we just say the linguist doesn't know whether "gavagai" means rabbit or rabbit-stage, in the same way a physicist doesn't know which of two empirically equivalent theories is true?
