So my formal epistemology reading group just spent two sessions on the common-knowledge-of-rationality debate around backward induction and I'm still kind of stuck on it -- got into it with my officemate over lunch and neither of us really "won." You know the setup: in a perfect-information game like the centipede game, backward induction says player 1 should stop the game at the very first move, and the classic justification (Aumann's mid-90s Games and Economic Behavior paper, with Binmore and Reny both weighing in against it around the same time) is that this is basically what common knowledge of rationality forces on everyone. But then theres the paradox everyone brings up: if rationality really were common knowledge, nobody should ever actually deviate from the backward-induction path, so the instant somebody does "irrationally" pass instead of take, you've just witnessed an event your own model assigned probability zero to -- and it's genuinely unclear what you're licensed to believe, or infer about what happens next, once that's happened. Aumann's move (as I understand it) is that rationality is a property of a whole strategy rather than a single node, so nothing actually needs revising -- the claim just holds vacuously off-path, like any conditional with a false antecedent. Binmore and Reny's line is that this quietly drains "common knowledge" of content at exactly the moment it was supposed to be doing real work.

Could you write me a dialogue between two game theorists standing at the second node of a four-move centipede game, right after player 1 has -- contrary to backward induction -- just passed instead of taking at node 1? One should hold the Aumann-style position that common knowledge of rationality is still perfectly intact and still pins down what happens for the rest of the game, the other should hold the Binmore/Reny-style position that this is precisely the point where the assumption becomes either incoherent or empty. Please don't let either one be a pushover -- I want the Aumann-defender to have a real answer to "why should backward induction reasoning still bind at a node your own theory says should be unreachable," and I want the skeptic to actually grapple with the "rationality is a property of the whole strategy, not the node" reply instead of just brushing past it. Totally fine if it gets technical (knowledge partitions, common priors, whatever) -- I'd rather it be dialectically sharp than accessible but toothless.
