The question the students were given reads as follows: "In A Theory of Justice, Rawls argues that the parties in the original position would agree on the two principles of justice, including the difference principle, by reasoning according to the maximin rule rather than by maximizing expected utility. Explain (a) the features of the choice situation that Rawls thinks make maximin reasoning appropriate behind the veil of ignorance, and (b) how this argument is meant to block a Harsanyi-style route to average utilitarianism from a similarly constructed veil-of-ignorance setup. Your answer should also make clear how the difference principle differs from a simple requirement that primary goods be distributed equally."

Here is the student's response in full: "Rawls sets up the original position so that the parties choose principles of justice from behind a veil of ignorance: they don't know their class position, natural talents, sex, or even their own conception of the good, though they do know general facts about economics, psychology, and the workings of society. Because the choice concerns the basic terms of their entire lives, and because the veil leaves them with no basis for calculating how likely they are to end up in any particular position, Rawls thinks the parties would find it reckless to choose principles by maximizing expected welfare. Instead they reason according to the maximin rule: rank the candidate principles by their worst possible outcome, and choose whichever principle makes that worst outcome as good as possible. Since a mistaken bet here could mean ending up enslaved or destitute, and since the parties have little to gain, relative to what they stand to lose, by holding out for a better-than-guaranteed outcome, maximin wins out over expected-utility maximization behind the veil. This is exactly where Rawls's argument diverges from Harsanyi's, even though the two start from a similar setup. Harsanyi reasons that since the parties behind the veil have no information favoring one social position over another, they should invoke the principle of insufficient reason and treat every position as equally likely; maximizing expected utility on that basis yields the principle of average utility. Rawls does not dispute that the parties lack any grounds for favoring one position over another, and he grants that this symmetry is exactly what licenses treating the positions as equally likely; his complaint is only that expected-utility maximization is the wrong rule to apply once those likelihoods are fixed, since a rational chooser can still refuse a favorable average if what it risks is an outcome they could not accept. The difference principle grows out of this same protective logic. Since the parties are choosing principles they must be willing to live under no matter which position they end up occupying, and since the point of maximin reasoning is to make the worst position as secure as possible, the stable resting point is one in which income, wealth, and the other primary goods are held equally: any deliberate departure from equal shares would just reintroduce, at one remove, the kind of gamble on one's future position that maximin reasoning was adopted to avoid. So although the parties reason about which arrangement best serves the worst-off position, the principle they settle on is egalitarian, not merely protective."
