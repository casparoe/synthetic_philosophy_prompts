This semester I'm taking a seminar on classical Islamic philosophy, and we've just spent two sessions on the Seventeenth Discussion of al-Ghazālī's *Tahāfut al-Falāsifa* ("The Incoherence of the Philosophers"), where he goes after the philosophers' account of causation. The standard example is cotton and fire: contact between them is regularly followed by burning, but Ghazālī insists that nothing about the fire itself necessitates that outcome. On his view, God creates the burning directly, alongside the fire, and the two are merely joined by an unbroken habitual custom (al-ʿāda) rather than any real causal power passing from one to the other; a miracle, correspondingly, is just a kharq al-ʿāda, a tear in that custom, and not the "violation" of some law that was never really necessary to begin with. What I keep getting stuck on is how total the claim is meant to be: it isn't restricted to a few dramatic cases but is supposed to hold for literally every event, all the way down to tomorrow's sunrise, so that Ibn Rushd's later complaint that this makes rational inference itself impossible starts to feel less like a debater's exaggeration and more like the actual price of taking Ghazālī seriously.

I'd like you to write a short story (somewhere around 800–1200 words) that takes this picture seriously as metaphysics, rather than as a fantasy premise where the rules just happen to be different. To make it concrete: imagine a world in which something like kalam occasionalism, rather than anything resembling Newtonian mechanics, became the settled and empirically successful scientific orthodoxy, so that an ordinary working scientist there has been trained since childhood to think of "natural law" exactly the way Ghazālī did, as nothing more than God's habitual pattern of creating paired events. Follow that scientist through a single unremarkable day in the lab, and find some way to let the reader feel, from the inside, both how mundane and stable that worldview is in practice and how strange it remains in principle, perhaps through one small moment in which the character's trust in the custom becomes, however briefly, visible to them as trust and nothing more. I'd rather you not resolve this into either a straightforward miracle-happens plot or a debunking one; I'm more interested in the phenomenology of living inside a fully occasionalist world than in a twist ending.
