So I fell down this weird rabbit hole a few weeks back after watching some youtube video on the history of pi, about how Thomas Hobbes (yeah, the Leviathan guy) spent a good chunk of the last 25 years of his life absolutely convinced he'd squared the circle with just a compass and straightedge, and got into this brutal, decades-long public feud with an actual mathematician named John Wallis who kept proving him wrong over and over in print, and Hobbes just never once backed down, he kept putting out "corrected" proofs practically until the day he died. I got curious and read around a bit more (I'm not a math or philosophy person btw, just an electrician who likes reading about this stuff at night) and it turns out the circle thing wasn't just some random old-man hobby for him — he genuinely thought geometry was the one area where human beings could have total, rock-solid certainty, and his reasoning was that we can be certain about triangles and circles because we're the ones who made up the definitions and constructed the figures ourselves in the first place, nothing's being "discovered" out there independent of us. And apparently that's a big part of why he structured Leviathan the way he did, starting from a pile of definitions and reasoning forward step by step like a geometric proof — he wanted political philosophy to have that exact same kind of certainty.

Which got me wondering: Hobbes obviously didn't live to see what happened next. In the 1880s a mathematician named Lindemann proved that pi is "transcendental," meaning squaring the circle the way Hobbes was trying to do it isn't just really hard, its mathematically impossible, full stop — so Wallis basically wins the argument two centuries after it ended. And earlier in that same century some other mathematicians showed you could build perfectly consistent geometries where Euclid's parallel postulate just isn't true, meaning there's not one single "correct" geometry after all, there's a whole family of equally valid ones depending which starting assumptions you pick. Given how proud and stubborn the guy apparently was, and given that his whole argument for geometric certainty was about us constructing the definitions rather than discovering some pre-existing truth out there — do you reckon Hobbes would've just been totally undone by finally getting proven wrong on the circle-squaring thing specifically, or could he have actually turned around and used the non-Euclidean geometry news to back up his bigger point about construction, even while the circle stuff was personally humiliating? And more importantly I guess, what does it do to his whole dream of putting political philosophy on the same footing as geometry, if it turns out even geometry — his go-to example of perfect human certainty — had all this instability underneath it that nobody in his lifetime saw coming?
