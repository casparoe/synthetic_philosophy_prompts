I teach a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and last week we covered Aumann's agreement theorem: the result that two Bayesian agents who share a common prior cannot have differing posterior probabilities for some event once those posteriors are common knowledge between them, even if neither has shared a single piece of evidence with the other. What unsettles my students is how little this has to do with the folk idea that people converge because they "talk things through" -- in the theorem, merely knowing what the other now believes, and knowing that they know you know it, and so on, already forces equality, provided the idealizing assumptions genuinely hold. Every year, though, some student tries to use the theorem to argue that a real, stubborn disagreement they've encountered "shouldn't" be happening, and I'd like a text I can put in front of the seminar to complicate that move.

Could you write me a short story of around 1,000-1,500 words built around the theorem? I'd rather you not default to the usual illustration of two traders or bettors comparing notes on a wager, since that's already a common shorthand for the result; two rival forecasters, two estranged former collaborators, two negotiators on opposite sides of a dispute, or some other pairing entirely would all give the idea more room to breathe. Whatever premise you choose, the plot should turn on the theorem's actual moving parts -- a common prior, posteriors that become common knowledge rather than merely exchanged once, and either a real convergence or a failure of convergence traceable to one of those conditions silently not holding. I'd like a reader to be able to reconstruct which assumption is doing the work from what happens in the story rather than from any explicit lecture inside it, and I'd like the ending to leave behind some sense of how strange and demanding those assumptions are, rather than settling into a comfortable moral about the value of listening to one another.
