I cover science and ideas for a living, and I'm currently working on a long-form piece about the Society for Psychical Research. The figure I can't stop circling back to is Henry Sidgwick, who co-founded the Society in 1882 and served as its first president, yet is much better known to philosophers as the author of The Methods of Ethics, whose concluding chapter frankly admits that he could find no rational way to close the gap between self-interest and duty -- what he called the "dualism of practical reason." I've been rereading that chapter alongside his correspondence and presidential addresses to the Society, and I keep wondering whether the decades he then spent investigating mediums, apparitions, and thought-transference were a philosopher's holiday from rigor, or something closer to a continuation of the same unfinished argument by other means.

For the piece I'm interviewing a historian of Victorian thought who has just finished an intellectual biography built around exactly that continuity thesis, arguing against the standard "brilliant philosopher, embarrassing hobby" framing. I'd like eight or nine interview questions that hold her to the thesis rather than letting her simply restate it. At minimum I want: one question that makes her spell out how empirical proof of personal survival, even if it had actually been obtained, was supposed to close a gap that Sidgwick himself diagnosed as a problem in practical reason rather than a gap in empirical knowledge; one about a specific investigation the Society had to walk back or qualify after initial excitement, and what Sidgwick's response to being fooled suggests about how much philosophical weight he was really resting on the research program; one about his and Eleanor Sidgwick's habit of ending report after report by avowing that nothing conclusive had been found, and whether that is better read as exemplary intellectual honesty or a kind of institutionalized paralysis; and one drawing out the parallel, or disanalogy, between Sidgwick's evidential standards and the standards invoked in a more recent controversy over anomalous findings in psychology, such as the one touched off by Daryl Bem's precognition experiments. For each of these four, please attach a short follow-up question keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so the exchange doesn't simply stall after the first response, and close with one further question asking her directly what result, after decades of psychical research, would actually have counted as success by Sidgwick's own lights.
