I cover science and ideas for a living -- health and psychiatry mostly these past few months -- which is how I ended up as the one non-clinician on my local hospital's ethics committee: they wanted somebody from outside the building in the room, and I guess I'd written about the place enough to qualify. We've got a case coming back to committee that's been gnawing at me. A patient in her twenties was held on the psych unit two extra weeks and medicated over her objection; the entire documented justification for extending the hold was that she "continues to lack insight into her diagnosis," which as far as I can tell just means she kept saying she wasn't bipolar and didnt want the meds. Her patient advocate came to the last meeting and made a pretty sharp point: if disagreeing with your diagnosis is itself the evidence that you're too impaired to disagree with your diagnosis, then there's no possible thing she could ever say that would count in her own favor. It's got the same shape as something I wrote about once, years back, under the heading "epistemic injustice," I think -- though I never got far enough into the philosophy of it to know if that's really the right box for this or just a resemblance.

Here's the thing, though: I don't want a memo that makes the psychiatrists on the committee out to be villains, because they've got a real point too. Anosognosia -- people genuinely unable to perceive their own impairment -- is a documented thing in some conditions, not just a word doctors reach for when a patient is being inconvenient, and if we write a policy that makes "lack of insight" inadmissible as evidence on its own, we might be tying clinicians' hands in cases where they're right and the patient is in real danger. So what I actually need is a memo I could put in front of this committee before we reconvene next month: something that works out whether this "disagreement as proof of incapacity" setup is unjust in some specific, nameable way, or just an unavoidable cost of caring for people who are sometimes genuinely too unwell to know it -- and that lands on one actual policy change (or a considered decision not to change anything), with a timeline attached, rather than trailing off into "we should all be more careful."
