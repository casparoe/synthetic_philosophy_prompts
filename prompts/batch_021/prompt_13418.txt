Over a long career in experimental physics, I grew entirely at ease with Bayes' theorem as a means of updating my degree of belief in a hypothesis once data were in hand. It was only in retirement, reading further into formal epistemology, that I encountered I. J. Good's 1967 theorem: that the expected utility of a decision problem to a rational Bayesian agent can never be lowered, and will generically be raised, by first observing some free, informative signal before acting. I can follow the short proof line by line — it appears to rest on the fact that "ignore the signal and act exactly as one would have done anyway" is always among the strategies available — yet I do not feel I understand the result the way I understand, say, why the entropy of an isolated system cannot decrease. Could you explain, in intuitive rather than symbolic terms, why the theorem holds, and then state precisely what it does and does not guarantee? In particular: does it promise that any specific piece of evidence I go on to receive will leave me better off, or only that looking is better on average across the evidence I might have received; and are there realistic circumstances — an agent who updates imperfectly, or a case where observing is not truly costless or truly ignorable — in which the expected value of information turns out, after all, to be negative?
