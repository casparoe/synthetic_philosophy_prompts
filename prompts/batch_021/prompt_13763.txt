---
Expressivists about moral language (e.g. emotivists like Ayer, or more sophisticated versions like Blackburn's or Gibbard's) hold that a sentence like "Stealing is wrong" doesn't state a fact or express a belief that's true or false -- instead it expresses an attitude of disapproval toward stealing, roughly "Boo, stealing!" The Frege-Geach problem, first raised by Frege as a challenge to Ayer's emotivism and later sharpened by Geach into its modern logical form, points out that moral sentences can occur unasserted -- for instance as the antecedent of a conditional -- and still contribute to valid arguments. Consider:

1. If stealing is wrong, then getting your little brother to steal is wrong.
2. Stealing is wrong.
3. Therefore, getting your little brother to steal is wrong.

This is a straightforward instance of modus ponens, and it's clearly valid. But its validity depends on "wrong" meaning the same thing in premise 1 (where it's embedded and unasserted) and premise 2 (where it's asserted outright). The trouble is that on the expressivist story, the meaning of "wrong" is supposed to be cashed out in terms of the attitude it expresses -- but in premise 1 the speaker isn't expressing disapproval of stealing at all, since they're only supposing it, not asserting it. So the expressivist owes us an account of what "wrong" means in unasserted contexts, and it has to be the very same thing it means in asserted contexts, or else the inference isn't actually valid in the way ordinary logic says it is.

One influential response is Blackburn's quasi-realism. Blackburn tries to build up a systematic "logic of attitudes" where embedded occurrences of moral vocabulary get complex attitudinal contents too -- e.g. the conditional as a whole might express something like disapproval of combining approval of stealing with disapproval of causing your brother to steal. Once this logic of attitudes is worked out, moral discourse ends up satisfying the same inferential constraints truth-apt discourse does, which is why Blackburn is comfortable calling it "quasi-realism" rather than outright anti-realism: for all intents and purposes, moral sentences do turn out to have truth conditions after all, just ones that are ultimately grounded in attitudes rather than mind-independent facts.
