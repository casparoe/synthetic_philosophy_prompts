For the past year I have been assembling a small collection of linked short stories, each built around a single idea from decision theory or formal epistemology that I think fiction has left strangely underused — the kind of premise Ted Chiang or Greg Egan might reach for, rather than the well-worn furniture of rogue AI or first contact. A friend who works in automated theorem proving recently got me interested in logical uncertainty: the situation of a reasoner, human or artificial, who has to act on a claim that is already, necessarily, either true or false, whose proof or disproof simply hasn't been found yet, so that no observation of the world could settle the matter, only a derivation nobody has produced. She described "logical induction" as one proposed way of handling such cases: rather than assuming an ideal reasoner who already knows every theorem, you imagine something closer to a market, in which many small computable heuristics and partial arguments act as traders, bidding a given sentence's "price" up or down as suggestive patterns and partial proofs accumulate, with that price jumping to certainty only once an actual proof or refutation closes the account for good.

I would like you to write a short story, a few thousand words long, that dramatizes this predicament rather than explaining it. Set it wherever you like — a mathematician working against a deadline, an AI system responsible for some piece of real infrastructure, a courtroom, a ship's computer, or anything else that appeals to you — but have the plot turn on a character, human or artificial, who must commit to an irreversible decision before any proof or disproof is available, and who cannot avoid assigning something like a probability to a claim they know full well is not a matter of chance at all. I would rather you not resolve the tension with a last-minute breakthrough that hands the character the "real" answer just in time. What I am after is a character who has to register, in their own terms, why staking something real on a mathematical necessity feels different from staking it on a coin toss or a medical diagnosis — and who has to act well before that difference is resolved.
