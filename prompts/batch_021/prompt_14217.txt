I spent about thirty-five years doing statistical mechanics before I retired (mostly disordered systems, glasses and that sort of thing), and these days I'm finally getting round to reading alot of the philosophy of probability I always meant to get to. Last month I sat down properly with Jaynes -- his 1973 "well posed problem" paper, plus the relevant chapter of Probability Theory: The Logic of Science -- and worked through his treatment of Bertrand's chord paradox. You probably know the setup: pick a "random" chord of a circle and ask for the probability that it's longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and the three obvious constructions (random endpoints on the circle, a random point along a radius, a random midpoint in the disk) hand you 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4 respectively, so the question looks hopelessly ambiguous. Jaynes' move is that the problem as usually stated is simply underspecified, and that once you require the answer to be invariant under the rotations, translations and rescalings already built into talking about "a circle" with no privileged centre or size, only the random-radius construction survives -- so 1/2 is THE answer, and the other two just smuggle in assumptions the problem never licensed. He even claims to have physically tested this by tossing straws at a circle and getting frequencies close to 1/2. I went through the derivation myself and it really does look like forced group theory, not a hidden extra premise dressed up as one.

That seems to me to fully rehabilitate the principle of indifference as a method: "equally possible cases" was just underspecified language all along, Bertrand's "paradox" was never really about probability, it was about sloppy problem-setting, and once your careful about symmetry the ambiguity is provably gone, not just argued away. I mentioned this at a probability reading group I go to (mostly retired engineers, good bunch) and got pushback along the lines of "philosophers still treat indifference reasoning as basically discredited" -- van Fraassen's cube factory, the wine-water business, the whole reference class literature -- as though Jaynes hadn't already cracked the general method back in the seventies. That seems bizarre to me if his argument is as clean as it looks. So: is there something real that people who still worry about the principle of indifference after Jaynes are onto, or is this a case of philosophers keeping a manufactured problem alive after the scientists already solved it? And practically -- if I'm doing a bit of hobby Bayesian analysis and want a genuinely "uninformative" prior on some parameter, can I just always run Jaynes' invariance trick to get one objectively correct answer, the way I now think I can for the chord?
