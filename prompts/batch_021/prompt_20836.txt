I've been preoccupied lately with a fact about Henry Sidgwick that I can't quite shake off. Effective altruists like to claim him as an intellectual ancestor—Parfit revered him, and the "point of view of the universe" line from the Methods of Ethics gets quoted approvingly on the EA Forum every so often—but the same Sidgwick who wrote arguably the most scrupulous utilitarian treatise of the nineteenth century also spent the last two decades of his life co-founding and serving as first president of the Society for Psychical Research, personally investigating mediums and claims of telepathy alongside Frederic Myers, Edmund Gurney, and his wife Eleanor. He was, by all accounts, a rigorous and frequently disappointed investigator, more often finding mundane explanations than the ones he was hoping for. What makes this more than an odd biographical footnote, from what I gather (I've been slowly working through Bart Schultz's biography of him), is that Sidgwick seems to have been driven partly by a gap he himself had identified at the end of the Methods: the "dualism of practical reason," his name for the worry that impartial benevolence and rational egoism are equally well supported by reason alone, and that nothing short of a providentially ordered universe—one in which virtue and self-interest ultimately coincide, the sort of guarantee immortality used to provide—could settle which one a rational agent ought actually to follow. Unable to simply take that guarantee on faith, he seems to have gone looking for empirical evidence of it instead, sitting in darkened rooms with mediums.

I'd like you to write me a short story that dramatizes this rather than explains it. Set at least part of it at an actual sitting late in Sidgwick's life—his wife and one or two of his closest collaborators present, a medium producing raps or automatic writing that is almost, but not quite, good enough to be convincing—and build the tension from the fact that he is simultaneously the most rigorous fraud-detector in the room and the person with the most riding, philosophically, on being wrong about that. I'd rather the dualism of practical reason never get spelled out in dialogue; let it show up instead in the texture of how he watches, doubts, and hopes. If it fits naturally, I'd be curious to see the story gesture toward the strange coda to all of this—the fact that after Sidgwick and his closest collaborators had died, mediums with no coordination between them began producing fragments that, pieced together, looked disturbingly like a deliberate joint message—though that part is optional, and shouldn't crowd out the central scene.
