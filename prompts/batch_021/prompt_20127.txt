Aquinas never encountered the modern dispute between textualism, which holds that judges should adhere to a law's enacted text even against its apparent purpose, and purposivism, which holds that text should yield to that purpose when the two diverge. Yet his treatise on law contains real material bearing on it: in the Summa Theologiae, I-II, q. 96, a. 6, he argues that a law binds according to the lawgiver's intention rather than its bare wording, illustrating the point with a besieged city whose standing order to keep the gates shut ought to be disregarded for citizens fleeing the enemy, since obeying the letter there would defeat the very purpose the order serves; he treats the corresponding virtue of equitable judgment, epikeia, at II-II, q. 120. Elsewhere in the same treatise, however, he insists that private individuals may not simply override the letter with their own judgment of the common good except in cases of manifest and urgent necessity, since interpreting the law in less clear cases belongs to those who hold public authority. Working only from these actual commitments — on natural law, the common good, and who has standing to interpret — would you expect him to side more with the textualists or with the purposivists, and why? Defend your answer against the strongest textual evidence for the opposite reading.
