This term I am taking a graduate seminar devoted entirely to Hume's Treatise of Human Nature, and for my final paper I would like to bring Hume into contact with a puzzle from contemporary decision theory that a friend of mine keeps raising in his rational choice reading group: Parfit's hitchhiker, and the associated idea of "rational irrationality." The setup, as I understand it, is this: a motorist stranded and dying of thirst in the desert is offered a ride into town by a driver who will only take him if the driver can be confident that the motorist will hand over an agreed sum of money once they arrive safely, at which point paying no longer serves any further purpose for the motorist, since he has already been rescued. An agent who simply reapplies ordinary means-end reasoning at the moment of truth, weighing only his remaining passions and interests, has every reason to refuse to pay; and a sufficiently perceptive driver, anticipating this in advance, would never have offered the ride at all. Parfit takes this case, and others like it, to show that it can be rational, relative to an agent's own aims, to become disposed to act at some future moment in a way that will not itself be the rational thing to do once that moment actually arrives.

What I would like your help thinking through is what Hume, using only the resources of his own philosophy, would say about this case, and I would rather you work through the reasoning than just assert a conclusion. On one hand, Hume's account of the will seems to commit him to denying that reason can ever criticize a passion or an action except for resting on a false belief about matters of fact or about the suitability of some means to an end; since paying up, once safe, serves none of the motorist's remaining passions, it is hard to see how Hume's official theory could brand the refusal to pay irrational at all. On the other hand, his treatment of promise-keeping as an artificial virtue, upheld through strict and general rules rather than through case-by-case recalculation of particular advantage, sounds a great deal like the disposition-based solutions that some decision theorists now favor over moment-by-moment reconsideration. Are these two strands of Hume's thought actually in tension when applied to a case like this one, or can the discussion of general rules in Book III be read as already implicit in, rather than in conflict with, the instrumentalism about reason defended in Book II? I would be grateful for a sense of how far Hume can consistently be pushed here, and where, in the end, you think he would have to come down.
