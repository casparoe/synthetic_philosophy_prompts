I'm a PhD student in political science, though my own research is archival and historical rather than formal or mathematical, which makes what follows a little embarrassing to admit. This term I ended up chairing the small committee our department uses to award its annual "Graduate Student Service Prize," and the process left me stuck on something that feels like it should have a clean answer. We had four nominees, whom I will call A, B, C, and D, and nine committee members, each of whom submitted a full ranking of all four. To keep the first pass simple, we counted only first-place votes, and D won outright with three votes, even though a comfortable majority of the committee had actually ranked D dead last overall; A, B, and C had simply split the remaining six votes among themselves. Everyone agreed that result was clearly wrong, so we tried comparing the nominees head-to-head instead, tallying, for every pair, how many members preferred one to the other. D lost every single head-to-head match-up and was easily set aside, but among the three remaining contenders we found, to our collective confusion, that a majority preferred A to B, a majority preferred B to C, and a majority preferred C to A. No nominee beat both of the others, so the seemingly obvious head-to-head method produced no winner at all among the finalists who actually mattered.

We ended up letting me, as chair, break the resulting tie, which more than one committee member grumbled felt less like the committee's decision than my own preference dressed up in procedure. Afterward, partly out of curiosity and partly to find something more defensible for next year, I reran the same nine ballots using a points system instead, awarding three points for a first-place vote, two for second, one for third, and none for last. This produced a clean order among A, B, and C with no cycle, which seemed promising, until I noticed that simply dropping D from the tally altogether, on the reasonable grounds that D had never been a real contender, flipped the order between the other two front-runners, even though not one person had changed their relative ranking of those two. Nobody's opinions moved at any point in this process; only our bookkeeping did, and yet the "winner" kept changing along with it. I cannot shake the feeling that something structural is going on here, something about the task itself rather than a fixable defect in whichever counting method we happened to try, but I lack the formal vocabulary to name it or to know whether any procedure could have avoided it. Is there a principled way to design a ranking process for situations like ours that would avoid this kind of instability, or is it simply unavoidable whenever several people's individual rankings must be aggregated into one collective decision?
