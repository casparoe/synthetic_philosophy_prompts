I'm about two-thirds of the way through a novel set inside the Guild of Relational and Family Therapists, a fictional professional association that accredits training programs and issues the practice guidelines its members are expected to follow. My narrator, Esther Okafor, sits on the Guild's four-member Ethics and Standards Committee, which is currently reviewing a proposal from a younger colleague, Dr. Marcus Feld, to revise the practice manual's treatment of relationship structure. Feld's proposal, whose literature review leans heavily on Bertrand Russell's *Marriage and Morals*, argues that the manual's near-total silence on consensual non-monogamy is not neutral but tacitly treats monogamy as the healthy default, and that clinicians should instead be trained to help clients separate jealousy's instinctive core from the much larger portion of it that Russell attributed to convention — above all, to the old habit of treating a wife as her husband's property. On this basis, Feld wants therapists actively taught to help clients work through that conventional residue of jealousy when a client wants to pursue non-monogamy, rather than treating jealousy wholesale as a fixed limit the couple must simply design around.

The committee has split in a way that resists an easy villain. One faction finds Feld's diagnosis overdue and consistent with the newer relationship-science literature, and argues that "neutrality" was never on the table — silence already took a side. The other faction, which includes the Guild's most outspokenly progressive member, raises two objections rather than one: first, that the charter's own commitment to "support clients' relational goals without imposing the therapist's ideology" sits awkwardly with training clinicians to tell a client that a piece of what they feel is illegitimate, however good Russell's history of that feeling is; and second, that even if Russell is right about where jealousy came from, the practical asymmetries that remain today — unequal exposure to pregnancy and disease, unequal social cost of exposure, unequal leverage to hold a partner to agreed limits — mean that coaching clients out of jealousy could, in a given couple, quietly do the more powerful partner's persuading for them. The charter offers no tiebreaker for either objection.

This is the hinge scene of the book's middle section, so I'd rather work from something that reads like an actual committee produced it than invent the back-and-forth myself. Could you draft the memo Esther would circulate to the other three members ahead of the vote at next month's Standards meeting? I need it to land on an actual recommendation — adopt the proposal, reject it, or adopt it with named modifications — rather than a balanced survey, to engage Russell's own argument closely enough that Feld would recognize it as a real response and not a dodge, and to give the losing side's strongest point its due instead of a token paragraph.
