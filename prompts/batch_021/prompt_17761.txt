A couple of people in my book club got into a running argument a few weeks ago, right after we'd finished reading a popular book on Darwin and evolution, and it's been rattling around in my head ever since — I don't have any formal training in philosophy or biology, just years of reading pop-science for fun, so I'd like a more careful take than I can give myself. Priti opened by arguing that "survival of the fittest" doesn't actually explain anything, because biologists define the fittest organisms as whichever ones end up surviving and reproducing more, so the whole slogan collapses into "the ones that reproduce more, reproduce more" — empty, not a real scientific claim. Callum pushed back, saying that in practice fitness gets assigned to specific, independently measurable traits — running speed, camouflage, resistance to some toxin — before anyone looks at who actually survives, so there's no real circularity; and regardless, he said, the theory throws off plenty of specific, falsifiable predictions that have nothing to do with how you define fitness, like nested hierarchies in the fossil record, molecular family trees lining up with anatomical ones, and antibiotic resistance evolving on schedule when you dose bacteria. Priti's comeback was that she hadn't invented this objection herself: Popper, who more or less wrote the rulebook on what counts as scientific, once said flatly that Darwinism is "not a testable scientific theory, but a metaphysical research programme" — so even the father of falsifiability didn't think natural selection cleared his own bar. Callum answered that Popper backtracked on exactly that point later in his life, admitting in print that he'd changed his mind and that the theory was more testable than he'd originally given it credit for — so if anything, Popper ends up on Callum's side, not Priti's. Priti wasn't having it: she said a recantation doesn't retroactively make a bad argument good, and that the tautology point should be judged on whether it's actually sound, not on a nose-count of which view Popper personally held in which decade — otherwise they're doing history of ideas instead of philosophy of science.

Neither of them budged, and I've gone back and forth about it myself without landing anywhere.
