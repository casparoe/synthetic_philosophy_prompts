Question 3 (20 points). Bertrand's chord paradox, first posed by Joseph Bertrand in 1889, asks: what is the probability that a randomly drawn chord of a given circle is longer than a side of the equilateral triangle inscribed in that circle? (a) (6 points) Give at least two distinct, individually natural procedures for selecting a chord "at random," and compute the resulting probability under each. (b) (6 points) These procedures disagree with one another. Explain precisely why, and state the general lesson this holds for the principle of indifference as applied to continuous spaces of possibilities. (c) (8 points) In "The Well-Posed Problem" (1973), E. T. Jaynes argues that the paradox can be resolved by imposing a further invariance requirement on the distribution over chords. State the requirement, identify which construction(s) from part (a) satisfy it, and assess whether Jaynes's argument succeeds in rehabilitating the principle of indifference or merely relocates the difficulty.

Student response:

"There are at least three standard ways to make 'choose a chord at random' precise, and part of Bertrand's point is that they don't agree.

Method 1 (random endpoints): pick two points independently and uniformly on the circumference and connect them. Fixing one endpoint at a vertex of an inscribed equilateral triangle by symmetry, the chord beats the triangle's side exactly when the second point lands on the 120° arc facing away from that vertex, so P = 1/3.

Method 2 (random radius): pick a direction, then a point uniformly along the radius in that direction, and draw the chord through that point perpendicular to the radius. The triangle's side sits at distance r/2 from the center (its apothem), so the chord is longer whenever the chosen point is within r/2 of the center; since that point is uniform on [0, r], P = 1/2.

Method 3 (random midpoint): pick the chord's midpoint uniformly over the whole disk. The chord beats the triangle's side exactly when the midpoint lands inside the concentric disk of radius r/2, so P = (r/2)²/r² = 1/4.

These disagree because 'at random' never specified a measure on the space of chords: each method silently adopts a different, individually reasonable-looking parametrization (arc length, radial distance, position in the disk) and puts a uniform measure on that parametrization, and since the parametrizations are nonlinearly related to one another, uniformity in one does not carry over as uniformity in another. That is the general moral for the principle of indifference: 'treat all possibilities as equally likely absent a reason to favor one over another' has no content until we have already fixed what counts as a possibility, and for continuous spaces there is typically no parametrization-independent way of doing that.

Jaynes's fix is to require the distribution over chords to be invariant under rotating the circle, and he shows that Method 2 is the only one of the three with this property, since the chosen point's distance from the center does not change under rotation, whereas which specific point on the circumference or in the disk gets selected in Methods 1 and 3 does change. Because Method 2 is the unique rotationally invariant construction, its answer, 1/2, is not merely one indifference-based answer among others but the objectively privileged one: Bertrand's paradox turns out to show only that naive indifference reasoning needs to be constrained by symmetry, not that indifference reasoning as such is bankrupt."
