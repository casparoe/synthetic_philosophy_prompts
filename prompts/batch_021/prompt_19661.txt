Ok so I've fallen down a bit of an AI safety rabbit hole over the past few months (started with Bostrom's Superintelligence, then a ton of stuff online about AI risk), and there's two ideas from that world that I keep chewing on: the orthogonality thesis, which is basically the claim that how intelligent something is and what it actually wants are totally independent things - a superintelligent machine could in principle want almost anything, even something as pointless as maximizing the number of paperclips in the universe - and instrumental convergence, which says that no matter what weird goal an AI happens to end up with, it'll probably still want the same handful of "instrumental" things anyway: not being switched off, not having its goals messed with, and getting more resources and power, because those things help you achieve pretty much any goal you could have.

Completely separately, my book club just made me read Hobbes' Leviathan, which I honestly wasn't looking forward too, but there's a part fairly early on where Hobbes says people have this "restless desire for power after power" that never stops until they die - not because everyone's just greedy, but because you can never feel fully secure in what you've already got without grabbing more. And then a bit later he's got this whole bit about competition, distrust (he actually calls it "diffidence") and glory being the three reasons total strangers end up fighting each other over stuff. Reading it I kept thinking, wait, isn't this basically instrumental convergence, except Hobbes is talking about people stuck in a state of nature instead of superintelligent AIs?

So here's what I'm actually curious about: if Hobbes somehow got to hear about the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence exactly as they're used in AI safety today, what do you think he'd genuinely conclude? Would he basically say "yeah, obviously, I called this centuries ago, any sufficiently rational agent converges on seeking power no matter what its actual goal is"? Or would he push back, on the grounds that his whole argument is really built on specific human passions - fear of violent death, vanity, glory-seeking - that don't obviously carry over to a machine that might only "want" paperclips and has no fear of death at all? And what about orthogonality itself - given that Hobbes basically treats reason as a neutral tool that just serves whatever passions or desires you happen to already have, regardless of what those desires actually are, does that make him sort of an early supporter of the orthogonality thesis, or is there something else in his account that would make him resist it? I don't want a wishy-washy "he'd probably agree with some of it and disagree with the rest" kind of answer here - actually walk through his own logic and commit to where you think he'd land on both ideas.
