Over the last couple of months I've been working my way through Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations on my own, mostly out of curiosity about what he actually argued, as opposed to the two or three ideas he tends to get reduced to (the invisible hand, pin factories, and so on). I got stuck for a long time on Book IV, where he devotes an entire chapter to colonies, and what surprised me is that his case against the colonial system was not primarily a moral one about the people being colonized, whom he mentions almost in passing; it was an economic one, that the whole arrangement mainly benefited a narrow class of merchants and chartered trading companies while ordinary British taxpayers covered the cost of the wars fought to defend trade routes most of them never profited from. He even suggests, in a book published in 1776 of all years, that Britain would be better off giving up the American colonies outright rather than continuing to pay for a monopoly that was not paying for itself.

What has stuck with me even more is something I ran across afterward: Smith taught moral philosophy at Glasgow for more than a decade, during exactly the period when the city's merchants (nicknamed, I gather, the "Tobacco Lords") were making enormous fortunes re-exporting Chesapeake tobacco to Europe, a trade that depended entirely on the kind of legal monopoly he was picking apart. That whole trade collapsed within a year or two once war broke out with the American colonies. I'd like you to write a short story of somewhere between 800 and 1500 words that sits inside that collision rather than explaining it: center it on a Glasgow merchant, or someone in his household or counting-house, whose family's fortune comes from the tobacco trade, set around 1775 or 1776, just as the war is strangling his business and Smith's book is starting to circulate.

I don't want a story where the character is simply "converted" to free trade by the end, or one that turns into a lecture disguised as fiction. I would rather see him caught between real pride in what that money built (the house, the family's standing, whatever public buildings or charities it paid for) and a nagging sense that none of it was quite what he had assumed, at the exact moment when both the money and the empire seem to be slipping away regardless of who turns out to have been right. You should feel free to invent the character, the household, and the plot entirely; I care much more about getting that tension right than about historical accuracy on any specific person or firm.
