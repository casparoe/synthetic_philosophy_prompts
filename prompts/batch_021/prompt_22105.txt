I chair the small allocations committee for an undergraduate effective-giving fund I helped set up several years ago at my college, which each spring distributes a modest discretionary grant -- this cycle, $120,000 -- to a cause nominated and defended by rival student teams, with faculty mostly serving as sounding boards rather than voters. We are deadlocked between two finalists this year. The first is a research and advocacy group working on wild and farmed invertebrate welfare, whose case leans heavily on scale: trillions of short, plausibly painful lives, almost entirely unaddressed elsewhere in the philanthropic landscape. The second is a well-established global-health charity distributing anti-malarial bed nets, whose case leans on a far more secure evidence base and a less philosophically contested unit of value.

Three of the five student leads want to fund the invertebrate group almost exclusively; pressed on why, their considered view amounts to a fairly explicit weak-to-moderate negative utilitarianism -- suffering counts for substantially more than equivalent happiness, and once the invertebrate numbers are taken seriously that alone settles the comparison. The other two want something closer to an even split, and worry that building the fund's allocation rule around that asymmetry quietly commits the whole fund to a heavily contested axiology rather than a framework everyone actually shares; one of them, only half-joking, raised the old objection that a sufficiently strict version of the suffering/happiness asymmetry ends up preferring a costlessly, painlessly emptied world to this one, and asked whether we want an allocation rule that even gestures in that direction. As chair, I have to write the two-page recommendation memo that goes to the full board this Thursday: it should propose a specific split -- not necessarily even -- and give reasons the losing side could recognize as serious engagement with their actual view rather than a diplomatic non-answer. Could you draft that memo for me?
