"Buck-passing accounts, the most influential version being Scanlon's in What We Owe to Each Other, say that being good isn't itself a reason-giving property. Instead, X is good just in case X has some other, more basic properties that give us reasons to desire, promote, or otherwise favor X — the value 'passes the buck' to those lower-level properties.

The wrong-kind-of-reasons problem is that we can clearly have reasons to desire or favor something that have nothing to do with whether it deserves to be favored. Scanlon illustrates the worry with his now-famous case of a demon who threatens to torture everyone unless one person comes to admire him: the threat obviously gives you a very strong reason to try to admire the demon, but it doesn't make the demon admirable in the least. So this is 'the wrong kind of reason' (WKR) to admire him, as against the 'right kind of reason' (RKR) we'd have if the demon actually had admirable qualities.

This matters for the buck-passing analysis because as originally stated, the analysis just says X is good iff there's reason to favor X, without distinguishing kinds of reasons — so the demon case counts as a reason to favor the demon, which on the unrestricted analysis would make him good/admirable, which is absurd. The buck-passer needs some way to restrict the analysis to right-kind reasons only, but can't just say 'the right kind of reasons are the ones that track X's actual value,' because that smuggles back in the very notion of value the analysis was supposed to explain — circular.

Parfit's distinction between object-given and state-given reasons gives us exactly the restriction we need: object-given reasons are grounded in the properties of the object itself, while state-given reasons are grounded in facts about the state of having the attitude (like what the demon will do if you have it). Since wrong-kind cases like the demon are always state-given and right-kind cases are always object-given, the two distinctions line up exactly, so building this into the buck-passing analysis (X is good iff there are object-given reasons to favor X) removes the circularity and disposes of the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem for good."
