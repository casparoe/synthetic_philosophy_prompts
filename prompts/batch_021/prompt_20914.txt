That's optional stopping, and it inflates the false-positive rate well past 5%, since if you just keep checking, you're almost guaranteed to cross the .05 line eventually even when the true effect is exactly zero. She said this exact pattern is one of the things people blame for the replication crisis in psych research, and called it "sampling to a foregone conclusion."
2. Ben said that's a frequentist framing of the problem, and if instead you look at a Bayesian posterior (or Bayes factor) computed from the data we actually ended up with, the stopping rule is irrelevant — something called the "likelihood principle," where two experiments that produce identical data should lead to identical conclusions no matter why either one stopped.
3. Nadia shot back that this can't be right because it proves too much: if the stopping rule truly never matters, then by that logic you could always eventually wander into a posterior favoring your hypothesis just by sampling long enough off pure random noise, even when the null is true. 4. Ben said not quite — with a genuine prior (not some flat, improper one), the posterior odds don't get dragged to an extreme against a true null under optional stopping the way a p-value does. He mumbled something about the likelihood ratio being a "martingale" under the null, which apparently puts a hard cap on how likely it is to ever look extremely convincing purely by chance, no matter how long you sample. So the foregone-conclusion failure mode that's genuinely true of p-values doesn't just carry over to Bayes factors.
