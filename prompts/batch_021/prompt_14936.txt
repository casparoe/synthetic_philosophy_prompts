So a few months ago I did one of those 23andMe kits just for fun, and it turns out the guy who raised me isn't my biological dad. My mom eventually admitted she'd known the whole time and just never said anything. I'm mostly past the initial shock now, but the thing that's stuck with me more than the fact itself is how it made everything else feel wobbly too, like if that could turn out false, what else am I just assuming without ever checking? I mentioned this to a friend doing a philosophy PhD and she said it sounded exactly like the territory Wittgenstein covers in On Certainty with his "hinge propositions" idea, so I've been working through it slowly, with a couple of tabs open on "hinge epistemology" since apparently thats a whole subfield now.

Two passages in it are kind of fighting each other in my head though. There's the famous bit around §341-343 where he says our doubts only make sense against a backdrop of things "exempt from doubt," "as it were like hinges on which those turn," and the line that really got me: "If I want the door to turn, the hinges must stay put." Fine, I get that - some stuff has to be fixed or nothing else works. But then earlier, in §96-99, he's got this totally different image going: some propositions get "hardened" and act like the banks of a river while others are the fluid water running between them, except "the river-bed of thoughts may shift" and hardened propositions can turn fluid again later on. That's basically saying hinges aren't fixed at all - they're just whatever happens to be solid right now, and the bank can wash away like sand.

So which picture am I supposed to take away here? Is "the hinges must stay put" only true at a given moment while the riverbed thing is the longer-term/historical story, or is he actually being inconsistent (which, fine, it's Wittgenstein, wouldn't be the first time)? And does this connect at all to the split I keep running into between Crispin Wright, who wants hinges to be things we're rationally "entitled" to accept, versus Duncan Pritchard's view that hinge commitments don't have any epistemic status at all - does either of them actually have a good answer for how a hinge can shift without ever having been "doubted" in the normal sense? Basically I'd love help holding both passages in my head at once without it feeling like Wittgenstein just contradicted himself two sections apart, because right now that IS what it feels like happened to me: the ground was solid until suddenly a chunk of the bank was just gone.
