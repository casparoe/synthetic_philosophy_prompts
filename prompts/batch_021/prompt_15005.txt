So the other night I got into it with my friend Dan over drinks about, of all things, the continuum hypothesis (we did a math degree together a few years back and apparently still do this to ourselves). It started because he said something like "CH isn't true or false, it's just independent, that's the whole point of Godel and Cohen," and I pushed back that independence from ZFC doesn't settle the philosophical question, it just shows ZFC is too weak to answer it. He said thats exactly backwards: the real lesson of the parallel postulate turning out to be independent of Euclid's other axioms wasn't "go find the missing axiom that reveals the true geometry," it was that there is no true geometry, Euclidean and non-Euclidean are just different, equally legitimate structures, and set theorists still hunting for the "real" answer to CH are making the same mistake people made before that sunk in. I said physical geometry and pure set theory aren't the same game: we went pluralist about geometry because non-Euclidean geometry turned out to model gravity, which is an empirical reason with no analogue for sets, so becoming pluralist about space doesn't obviously mean we should become pluralist about the universe of sets. We went in circles for like an hour and neither of us budged, so afterward I tried to write out the actual state of the argument, partly just to see if my side holds up outside the heat of the moment.

Here's what I've got. Cantor's continuum hypothesis says there's no cardinality strictly between that of the integers and that of the reals. Godel showed in 1940 that CH can't be refuted from ZFC, since it holds in the constructible universe L, and Cohen showed in 1963 that it can't be proved either, using forcing. Everyone agrees on that much; the disagreement starts with what to make of it. Godel himself was a realist about it: he thought CH has a definite truth value in the actual universe of sets, guessed it was false, and hoped new axioms, justified independently on grounds like fruitfulness or intrinsic necessity, would eventually decide it. The awkward part for his own hope is that the most obvious candidates, large cardinal axioms, turned out not to help much: you can have measurables, supercompacts, whatever you like, and still force CH or its negation over the resulting model without disturbing the large cardinals, so that whole hierarchy just sits orthogonal to CH. What has actually moved the needle is forcing axioms instead, like the Proper Forcing Axiom, which pins the continuum down to exactly aleph-2 -- a real "some new axiom decides it" result, just not obviously the kind of self-evident axiom Godel had in mind.

Woodin later tried a more structural route. His Omega-logic work from around 2000 tried to show that, given enough large cardinals, the negation of CH is "generically necessary" in a precise sense while CH isn't, and he took that asymmetry as evidence CH is false at some deep level. The part I find genuinely funny is that Woodin's own more recent Ultimate-L program points the other way: if V really does look like an Ultimate-L type model, you get GCH, CH included, basically for free. So one of the people who's done the most to argue CH is false has also built the best current case that it might be true after all.

Then there's the deflationary move, basically Dan's position, associated with people like Hamkins: on the multiverse view, all of this is exactly what you'd expect if there's simply no fact of the matter beyond "CH holds in these models and fails in those." Forcing gives us such total, symmetric control over CH's truth value that insisting one answer is secretly correct is like insisting the parallel postulate is true or false independent of which geometry you've picked. I don't fully buy this, mostly because it proves too much -- the same "we can force it either way, so there's no fact of the matter" reasoning would apply to statements multiverse people don't actually want to call meaningless, and even Hamkins wants plenty of set-theoretic claims to come out determinate across the whole multiverse. I think the usual dismissal of Freiling's old symmetry argument against CH is instructive here too (the one with two people throwing darts at the real line and using a CH-based well-ordering to generate a supposed paradox): the standard response is that it fails because it smuggles in ordinary probabilistic intuitions that have no business being applied to a wildly non-measurable object like that. Fair enough, but that just shows our intuitions run out at some point, not that the underlying question is empty. So where I land is that the real question isn't "is CH true," it's whether the track record of which new axioms turn out to be fruitful, mutually consistent, and forced on us by considerations independent of CH itself gives us the same kind of reason to expect a determinate answer that fruitfulness already gives us for accepting large cardinals in the first place. I think it does; Dan thinks it obviously doesn't; and at this point I don't think either of us is just repeating talking points.

Would you go through this and flag anywhere I've gotten the set theory wrong or oversimplified it in a misleading way -- especially the Omega-logic and Ultimate-L bits, since that's pretty secondhand knowledge for me? And separately, does my closing argument actually land as a response to the multiverse view, or am I just smuggling in Godel-style realism as a premise and calling it a conclusion? Also curious if you think Dan's side deserves more credit than I'm giving it here.
