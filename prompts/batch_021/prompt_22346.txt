I write a science-and-ideas column that keeps drifting into the history of economics, and the current round of four-day-week pilots and AI-productivity forecasts sent me back to John Maynard Keynes's 1930 essay "Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren," which set its sights on conditions "a hundred years hence" — a horizon we are now, as it happens, closing in on. Next week I'm recording a long interview with an intellectual historian whose new book treats that essay not as a failed forecast about a fifteen-hour work week but as serious moral philosophy: an argument that once scarcity is solved, we can finally set aside what Keynes called "the love of money as a possession," as opposed to money as a means to enjoying life, and get on with the harder problem he thought lay underneath economics — learning to live well. Whenever a critic points out that nearly a century of rising productivity has produced more consumption than contemplative leisure, her standard move is to say that Keynes was describing a psychological tendency rather than issuing a testable prediction, so any shortfall just shows we "aren't there yet." I'd like a set of questions that makes that move harder to repeat.

Could you draft nine or ten interview questions, each substantive enough that she can't simply restate her book's thesis? I want at least one question that uses Keynes's 1938 memoir "My Early Beliefs" — where he admits that his Bloomsbury circle had no idea "civilisation was a thin and precarious crust" — to ask whether the 1930 essay's optimism about the money-motive dissolving rests on the very picture of human nature he later disavowed; one on the apparent tension between that essay's contempt for what Keynes called "purposiveness," the habit of always deferring enjoyment into a receding future, and The General Theory's reliance on exactly that restless, future-directed psychology, "animal spirits," to keep investment and employment from collapsing; one on whether the "good life" he has in mind is a genuinely general ideal or a fairly parochial Cambridge-and-Bloomsbury aesthetic mistaken for a universal one; and one that weighs Keynes's own career as a currency and commodities speculator and enthusiastic art collector against his claim that treating money as an end rather than a means is closer to a psychiatric disorder than an economic error. For each of these, add a short follow-up keyed to the deflection she is most likely to reach for, and close the list with a question that pins her down on exactly what evidence — about leisure, about status-driven consumption, whatever she prefers — would actually count against the thesis rather than simply being filed under "not yet."
