I write a science-and-ideas column for a general-interest magazine, and next month I'm recording a long interview for it with a philosopher of science who has spent her career defending interventionism about causation: the view, built out of James Woodward's manipulability theory and Judea Pearl's causal graphs, that what it is for C to cause E is, roughly, for there to be a possible intervention on C under which E would change. The occasion is a piece I'm writing on how Pearl's "causal revolution" has filtered into fields far from computer science; I've been reading about Mendelian randomization studies, which use naturally occurring genetic variants as stand-ins for a randomized trial that could never actually be run on, say, a lifetime's exposure to LDL cholesterol, and it struck me that the whole technique is really just Woodward's account of causation wearing a lab coat.

I don't want the interview to rehash the textbook contrast between Lewisian counterfactual dependence and the intervention operator, which anyone who has cracked open a philosophy of causation syllabus already knows by heart. Could you draft nine or ten questions for me, each with a short follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give? I need at least one pair on the standard charge that defining causation via "intervention" is circular, since an intervention is itself just a special kind of cause, and on how she resists the conclusion that this makes her theory illuminating rather than genuinely explanatory; one pair that uses the Mendelian randomization case, or something like it, to press her on how the view can license causal claims -- about a lifetime dose of cholesterol, or about a person's sex or race in a piece of social-science research -- where no intervention meeting her own "surgical" conditions could, even in principle, be carried out; and one pair on whether recasting classic preemption cases, the kind where two people throw rocks at a bottle and one throw beats the other to it, in terms of structural equations actually resolves the puzzle or just relocates it into a dispute about which graph is the correct model, given how often the causal-modeling literature has had to patch itself with fixes for switching cases and the like. Please close the sequence with a question asking what finding, or what kind of philosophical argument, would move her back toward the counterfactual theory, or toward suspecting that neither framework captures a mind-independent relation of causation at all.
