Been meaning to write this up for a while. A few months back I went down a Williamson rabbit hole (Knowledge and Its Limits, specifically the anti-luminosity chapter) after seeing it referenced in passing on a LW-adjacent blog talking about whether introspection is trustworthy, and I got kind of obsessed with his argument against the KK principle -- the idea that if you know P you thereby know that you know P. Williamson uses the classic "feeling cold by a dying fire overnight" case to argue that basically no non-trivial mental state is "luminous" (roughly: such that whenever you're in it, you're automatically in a position to know you're in it), and since knowing-that-P is itself a mental state, this sinks KK. I wanted to check if I actually understood the skeleton of the argument or if I was just nodding along and pattern matching, so I tried rebuilding it from scratch with my own example instead of just copying his. Would love some comments on whether I've faithfully reproduced the logical structure, and seperately on whether the argument -- his or mine -- is actually any good.

Here's what I came up with: take the state of "finding a piece of music mildly irritating." Alice listens to a metronome that starts at 40bpm and ticks up by 1bpm every ten seconds until it hits 200bpm, over about half an hour. At 40bpm she isn't irritated at all; by 190bpm she's thoroughly irritated. Her irritation rises continuously in between, and crucially, any two adjacent ten-second samples feel introspectively identical to her -- she can't tell 111bpm-Alice apart from 112bpm-Alice from the inside, even though 40bpm-Alice and 190bpm-Alice obviously feel different.

Now suppose there's some stretch in the middle where Alice is genuinely mildly irritated, and suppose, for contradiction, that "mildly irritated" is luminous, so at every instant in that stretch she knows she's mildly irritated. Knowledge requires a margin for error: a belief only counts as knowledge if it's safe, i.e. still true in the nearest cases the subject can't discriminate from the actual one. But t and t+1 are (stipulated to be) indistinguishable to Alice, and whatever process generates her belief operates identically at both. So if her belief "I'm mildly irritated" is safe enough to be knowledge at t, it has to still be true at t+1, since nothing available to her marks any difference between the two moments. Iterate that step tick by tick all the way to 190bpm and you get the absurd result that Alice is still only mildly irritated even once she's thoroughly irritated. Something has to give, and its the luminosity assumption: at some tick along the way, Alice really is mildly irritated but doesn't know it, because her judgement, though true, isn't safe -- one more imperceptible tick and it would've been false.

That's my reconstruction. What I can't tell is (a) whether I've actually preserved what's doing the work in Williamson's original or just gestured at a generic sorites and called it a day, and (b) whether the argument, his or mine, really threatens KK for knowledge in general, or only for this one family of graded, introspectively-fuzzy states. I keep suspecting there's a real gap between "no sharp cutoff for feeling cold" and "no sharp cutoff for knowing P," and that Williamson needs the second one but his examples only establish the first. Am I onto something there or am I missing why the argument's supposed to generalize? Also happy to hear if the tempo example just doesn't work as an analogy at all, or if I've smuggled in some assumption I shouldn't get for free.
