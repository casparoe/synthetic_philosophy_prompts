I'm a software engineer, and the Church-Turing thesis and the halting problem were settled background for me long before I'd heard of Thomson's lamp. The two turn out to be closely linked: an "accelerating" (or Zeno-style) Turing machine — one that halves the duration of each successive step — is essentially Thomson's lamp with a tape instead of a switch, and some philosophers of physics have argued that such a device (or, using relativistic time dilation instead of literal acceleration, an appropriately arranged Malament-Hogarth spacetime) could deliver to a finite observer a determinate verdict on the halting problem, refuting the physical Church-Turing thesis even if not the purely mathematical one. I want your best reconstruction of how Turing himself would have judged this proposal, reasoning from his actual commitments rather than generic skepticism about infinity: the idealizations already built into his 1936 analysis of mechanical procedure, and his own use of purely abstract oracle machines — introduced in his 1939 paper on ordinal logics to study degrees of unsolvability — which he never claimed were physically buildable. Would he treat the accelerating machine as a legitimate physical realization of something like an oracle, side with something close to Thomson's own move that the machine's final readout, like the lamp's final state, is simply left undefined by the setup, or does his record point somewhere else? Defend the reading you find most persuasive rather than cataloguing every option.
