A friend of mine and I spent an evening arguing about how to read Nāgārjuna's tetralemma (catuṣkoṭi) in the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, and neither of us gave ground. She takes the Garfield–Priest "dialetheist" line: that Nāgārjuna is willing, at least at certain points, to assert genuine, literal contradictions rather than merely gesture at the limits of language. I objected that in the one verse that's usually treated as the clearest case of an affirmed (rather than negated) tetralemma, Garfield's own commentary reads it non-contradictorily, via the two-truths distinction — so nothing there forces us into paraconsistent logic. She replied that the two-truths move just relocates the problem: either "real" is used univocally across the four horns, in which case the contradiction stands, or it isn't, in which case the verse is equivocating rather than teaching anything. We never resolved it.

Here is the passage at issue, Mūlamadhyamakakārikā 18.8–9 (I'm giving the content shared across the standard translations — Garfield, Kalupahana, Siderits and Katsura — rather than quoting any one of them verbatim). Verse 8: everything is real; everything is unreal; everything is both real and unreal; and everything is neither real nor unreal — that, it says, is the Buddha's teaching. Verse 9 immediately follows with a description of "the real" (tattva) that never uses the four-cornered form at all: what is real is not established through dependence on anything else, is at peace, is untouched by conceptual proliferation (prapañca), free of mental differentiation, and without multiplicity of meaning.

For contrast, elsewhere Nāgārjuna uses the same four-cornered structure to deny all four alternatives rather than affirm them — most famously in the treatise's opening verse, where he holds that nothing whatsoever arises from itself, from another, from both, or without a cause.

Could you help me work out what's actually going on, logically, across 18.8 and 18.9? Specifically: does verse 9's run of negations give any textual warrant for cashing out verse 8 via the two truths, so that "neither real nor unreal" just means something like "not capturable by the conventional/ultimate pair at all" — or does verse 9 introduce a further, independent kind of negation that doesn't map onto any of verse 8's four horns, leaving the apparent contradiction untouched? And, taking the negating pattern of the opening verse as the norm for how Nāgārjuna typically uses the tetralemma, is there anything internal to the text that would justify verse 8 functioning differently — affirming rather than denying — or does the shift itself suggest that "the tetralemma" isn't really one stable device across the work?
