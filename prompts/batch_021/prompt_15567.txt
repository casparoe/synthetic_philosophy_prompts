My sister was diagnosed last month with functional neurological disorder, a category clinicians themselves describe as sitting uneasily between "organic" and "psychogenic," and one that has effectively been renamed and re-theorized every few decades since it went under the name hysteria. Watching her move through that diagnostic history in real time has given this term's unit on Hacking a weight it didn't have when I first assigned it. I am TAing the seminar and would like a rigorous, independent grade on the exam answer below before I enter a score of my own.

The question was:

"Using Hacking's discussion of the fin-de-siècle fugue epidemic and the case of Albert Dadas, explain (a) what he means by the 'ecological niche' required for a transient mental illness to become classifiable, and (b) how this case illustrates his more general looping-effects thesis about human kinds. Then assess: does Hacking's treatment of fugue commit him to denying that Dadas was really ill?"

The student's answer, verbatim:

"Fugue is Hacking's central illustration of what he calls an 'interactive kind': unlike an indifferent natural kind such as gold, whose members don't care what we call them, a diagnosis like fugue actively interacts with the people it is applied to. Dadas, a gas-fitter who suffered prolonged bouts of compulsive walking, sometimes lasting months, that he could not afterward remember, became famous once Charcot examined him at the Salpêtrière and made him the template for a wave of similar diagnoses across France. Hacking's point is that this wave was not simply doctors noticing something that had been there all along: publicizing the diagnosis itself generated further cases, because patients and doctors alike now had a script available to them, a process Hacking calls semantic contagion. That is the looping effect in miniature — the classification changes the population it classifies, the changed population prompts revision of the classification, and so on.

For a transient illness like this to get off the ground at all, Hacking argues it needs an 'ecological niche': several conditions that have to hold at once. There must be a cultural polarity that gives the behavior meaning — here, the tension between the new bourgeois prestige of tourism and travel and the older, criminalized category of vagabondage and desertion, so that unexplained wandering could be read either as glamorous or as dangerous. The behavior must be observable, so that doctors can notice and record it. And there must be some 'release' on offer: being classified as sick rather than as a vagrant or a deserter got Dadas out of a considerably worse fate.

Because Hacking shows that the entire category was assembled out of this specific and short-lived set of cultural conditions, and dissolved again once they changed, I think he is committed to holding that fugue was never a real mental illness — Dadas was not sick, he was enacting the only script culturally available for what he was doing, and the doctors who thought they had discovered a genuine disease entity were simply mistaken."
