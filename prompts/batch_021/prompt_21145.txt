I'm currently teaching a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and this week's session on Bayesian confirmation theory ran into a puzzle I'd like a second opinion on before we reconvene. We had just finished setting out the orthodox Bayesian picture of confirmation — evidence E confirms a hypothesis T just in case conditionalizing on E raises one's credence in T — when a student raised Clark Glymour's "problem of old evidence." Her worry was that some of the most celebrated confirmations in the history of science, such as General Relativity's account of the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion, involve evidence that was already known, and hence already assigned probability 1, well before the theory that supposedly earns support from it was even formulated. But if P(E) is already 1, conditionalizing on E cannot budge anyone's credence in T at all, so orthodox conditionalization seems structurally incapable of explaining why we treat such cases as among the strongest evidential support a theory can receive.

A couple of students pushed back with fixes they had run across in a reading group. One brought up the Garber–Jeffrey move of relativizing the framework to agents who are not logically omniscient, so that what actually confirms T is the discovery of a logical fact the agent did not already possess — namely, that T entails E — rather than the discovery of E itself. Another raised counterfactual strategies that try to recover a sensible confirmation relation by asking what the agent's credence in T would have been had she not already known E. Neither proposal struck the class as obviously satisfying on its own terms, and we ran out of time before pinning down why.

Could you help me think this through before next week's class? I'm after an explanation that goes beyond the one-line gloss "the problem is that P(E) = 1" and instead builds up the tension step by step: why orthodox conditionalization really does run into trouble here, what exactly separates a "historical" reading of the problem (about the order in which scientists actually came to believe things) from a "logical" one (about idealized agents' logical omniscience), and whether the Garber–Jeffrey and counterfactual strategies genuinely dissolve the puzzle or just relocate it elsewhere. I'd also value your own view on which approach comes closest to capturing why Mercury's perihelion still strikes working scientists as a genuine triumph for General Relativity, rather than a piece of evidence that GR could not, in principle, have gained any credit from.
