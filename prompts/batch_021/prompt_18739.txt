Alongside my novel-writing, I teach a small continuing-education seminar called "Strategy and Structure," aimed at adults who want just enough formal game theory to recognize it at work in fiction — bargaining scenes, standoffs, the coordination failures between characters who never quite manage to talk to one another. It's a habit left over from an economics MA I abandoned for fiction some years ago, and this term's problem set finally reached the corner of the material I remember finding slipperiest even as a student myself: iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies once you allow the dominating strategy to be a mixed one rather than insisting on a pure alternative. One of my students turned in the answer reproduced below, and before I write anything in the margins myself, I'd like a more reliable second opinion than my own, since this is precisely the part of the course where I trust my instincts least.

The problem as I posed it, followed by the student's submission in full, are reproduced below.

**Problem.** Consider the two-player game below. Player 1 chooses a row (T, M, or B), Player 2 chooses a column (L, C, or R), and entries list (Player 1's payoff, Player 2's payoff).

| | L | C | R |
|---|---|---|---|
| **T** | 3, 1 | 2, 0 | 0, 4 |
| **M** | 1, 0 | 1, 5 | 1, 0 |
| **B** | 0, 4 | 2, 0 | 3, 1 |

(a) Before doing any computation, briefly explain why, in general, checking each strategy against every other *pure* strategy is not sufficient when performing iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies.
(b) Carry out iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies as far as it will go, checking for domination by mixed strategies wherever relevant. At each round, state which strategy is eliminated, by which strategy (pure or mixed) it is dominated, and the payoff comparison establishing this.
(c) Comment on whether the order in which strategies were eliminated could have changed the final outcome, and explain why or why not.

**Student's submission.**

(a) The reason we can't just compare pure strategies is that a strategy can fail to be rational for any belief a player might hold about their opponent even when no single pure strategy beats it in every column — which is exactly the condition defining a *rationalizable* strategy: one that survives iterated elimination of strategies strictly dominated by some pure strategy. Checking mixed strategies as candidate dominators is how we catch these cases.

(b) *Round 1.* Comparing Player 1's rows, T = (3, 2, 0) and B = (0, 2, 3) against (L, C, R). Neither dominates M = (1, 1, 1) alone: T loses to M at R (0 < 1), and B loses to M at L (0 < 1). But take the mixed strategy that plays T and B with probability ½ each: against L it earns ½(3) + ½(0) = 1.5, against C it earns ½(2) + ½(2) = 2, and against R it earns ½(0) + ½(3) = 1.5. Since these all exceed M's payoffs of 1, this mix strictly dominates M, so M is eliminated even though it isn't dominated by any pure strategy.

*Round 2.* With M gone, Player 2's payoff from C is only (0, 0) against (T, B), while L gives (1, 4) and R gives (4, 1). Both L and R now strictly dominate C, which can therefore be eliminated too, even though C was Player 2's best response back when M was still available.

*Result.* We're left with the 2×2 game on {T, B} × {L, R}: T = (3, 0) and B = (0, 3) for Player 1; L = (1, 4) and R = (4, 1) for Player 2. Neither row dominates the other and neither column dominates the other, so no further elimination is possible and this is the final reduced game.

(c) Iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies is well known to be order-independent, so the order chosen above was just a matter of convenience: we would have reached exactly the same 2×2 reduced game had we instead eliminated C in the first round and only removed M afterward.
