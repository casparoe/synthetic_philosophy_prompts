Next month I'm chairing the Q&A after a colloquium talk in our department's speaker series, and the visiting speaker defends a pretty strong anti-individualist line on mental content -- the view that what your thoughts are about is partly constituted by facts outside your head, whether that's your physical environment (Putnam's Twin Earth) or your linguistic community (Burge's arthritis patient), rather than being fixed entirely by what's going on inside your skull. In the paper she circulated, shes pretty breezy about what I think is actually the sharpest problem for the view: if my thought contents depend on environmental facts I have no special access to, and I also supposedly know what I'm thinking just by introspecting, no empirical work required, then it looks like I can sit in my armchair, introspect that I'm thinking "water is wet," and thereby know a priori that my environment contains H2O rather than some twin substance -- which seems absurd, since that's exactly the sort of thing you'd think requires going out and looking. As I understand it, the standard moves are to deny that self-knowledge is really a priori in the relevant sense, to deny that the link between having that thought and the environmental condition is itself knowable a priori, or to just accept the conclusion and argue it's not as absurd as it sounds.

Could you draft me 8-10 Q&A questions that push specifically on this problem rather then re-litigating whether Twin Earth-style externalism is true in the first place? I'd like a couple of questions that pin the argument down precisely enough that she can't wriggle out with "that's not really what my view implies," at least one question aimed at each of the three standard moves above (with a follow-up ready for whichever one she takes), and one question near the end that just asks her to say plainly what, if anything, she thinks we can know about the world from the armchair. No softball "what drew you to this view" questions, please -- there will be grad students in the audience and I'd like to look like I've actually read the paper.
