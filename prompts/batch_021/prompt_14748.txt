I'm a backend engineer, not a philosopher, but a coworker and I have been trading excerpts from Pascal's Pensées back and forth ever since he pointed out that Bitcoin's "longest chain wins" consensus rule is basically a modern restatement of something Pascal wrote centuries earlier. The passage he sent me argues, roughly, that because people can never agree on what is truly just, while everyone can recognize without dispute who is strongest, societies were never able to make justice powerful, so instead they simply dressed up power as justice after the fact. It's a striking line, and it maps disturbingly well onto how a lot of real systems actually resolve disagreement, from how great powers settle disputes down to how blockchain nodes settle on a canonical chain.

Since Pascal is essentially arguing that justice just is whatever the strongest party manages to enforce—the same conclusion Thrasymachus reaches in Plato's Republic, and the one Hobbes builds his entire political theory around—I've started describing him to my coworker as a fellow traveler of theirs: a thinker who, underneath the religious packaging, held a fundamentally cynical, power-is-all-there-is view of political morality. Given that, why is Pascal still routinely taught as a serious moral and religious thinker, rather than being filed away next to Hobbes and the Sophists as just another "might makes right" theorist who happened to dress the idea up more elegantly?
