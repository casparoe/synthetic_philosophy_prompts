I am a retired physicist who has only in the last few years begun reading moral philosophy seriously, and I have just finished Elizabeth Anscombe's 1956 pamphlet "Mr. Truman's Degree," written to oppose Oxford's decision to award Harry Truman an honorary degree. What strikes me most is that her central charge is not that the atomic bombings killed enormous numbers of civilians, but that those deaths were the chosen means of ending the war rather than a foreseen side effect of striking some legitimate target — it is this intention/foresight distinction, later elaborated under the doctrine of double effect, that lets her call the bombing murder without ever having to weigh it against the lives the bombing may have saved.

I would be curious how she might have judged the "signature strike" practice of modern drone warfare, where targets are selected from behavioral patterns rather than positive identification, and where it is a matter of settled policy — though never demonstrably true of any single strike — that a predictable proportion of those killed will be unidentified civilians. Any individual strike can be honestly described as aiming only at a suspected combatant, with civilian deaths merely foreseen rather than intended. Would Anscombe's distinction actually shield this practice, or would she have treated "signature" targeting as just a more bureaucratic version of the very move she accused Truman's Oxford defenders of making — relabeling a policy of killing the innocent as mere foresight? I would value your best reconstruction of her reasoning, including any point at which her own framework might leave the verdict genuinely unsettled.
