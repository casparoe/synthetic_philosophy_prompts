I work on interpretability for large neural networks. A recent disagreement within my team about whether the "feature directions" we isolate in a model's activations genuinely represent anything, rather than being correlational patterns we are anthropomorphizing, sent me back to the teleosemantics literature I last studied during a philosophy minor. Millikan grounds a representation's content in the "Normal explanation" for its producer-consumer history: the conditions under which past tokens of that representation, differentially retained because they benefited the systems using them, actually performed their function. Dretske instead ties content to an internal state-type's having been recruited, over the course of a system's own learning history, to cause some behavior because it reliably correlated with a given condition. Could you compare these two accounts specifically on how readily each could be extended to a network shaped by gradient descent, which has no reproductive lineage in Millikan's sense but does undergo something like Dretske's history of recruitment through training? Please say which view needs less modification to cover such a system, and why. As a further test, consider a version of Davidson's Swampman: a network whose weights are randomly initialized to exactly match those of a fully trained network, with no training ever having taken place. Would either account attribute content to its internal states, and does this case reveal an asymmetry between the two theories that standard biological examples don't?
