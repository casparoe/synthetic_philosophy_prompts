I've been mulling over a question that grew out of a tangent in the comments of an old LessWrong post on the genetic fallacy, which eventually wound its way to a discussion of the diary Gottlob Frege kept for a few weeks in 1924, not long before his death -- a diary that only surfaced among his papers and was published in translation decades later. From what I can gather, the entries are jarring: interleaved with the same terse, exacting prose he used for logic, Frege records vehemently anti-Semitic and anti-parliamentary sentiments, apparent sympathy for a dictator replacing the Weimar Republic, and general hostility to the political left. This reportedly hit Michael Dummett -- the philosopher most responsible for canonizing Frege as the founder of modern logic and analytic philosophy of language in the English-speaking world -- particularly hard, not least because Dummett spent much of his own career as an anti-racist activist.

What I can't quite settle for myself is the more general question underneath this particular case: does material like this have any legitimate bearing on how we assess Frege's actual philosophical output, or is leaning on it just a sophisticated version of the genetic fallacy? The easy answer is that Begriffsschrift and the definition of the cardinal numbers as extensions of concepts don't become any less rigorous because their author turned out to hold repugnant political views forty-odd years later. But I've also seen the connection argued for more seriously in Frege's specific case, on the grounds that so much of his philosophical signature -- his ferocious anti-psychologism, his insistence that logical laws and "Thoughts" inhabit a mind-independent "third realm" (he actually uses the phrase "drittes Reich") untouched by any thinker's perspective or social position, his general horror at anything smacking of relativism about truth -- might spring from the same underlying temperament as a politics organized around imposing one non-negotiable order on a fractious, pluralistic society.

Could you walk me through the strongest version of each side of this, and then give me your own sense of where it lands? I'm especially interested in whether there's a principled way of distinguishing cases where an author's biography is just irrelevant noise from cases where it's genuinely diagnostic of something latent in the work itself -- and where exactly the Frege case falls on that spectrum.
