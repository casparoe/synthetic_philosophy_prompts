I teach a graduate seminar on early modern natural philosophy and religion, and this term's unit on Newton left me dissatisfied with how the secondary literature usually treats the relationship between his physics and his theology. The two are typically handled in separate chapters, as if the antitrinitarian heretic and the author of the Principia happened to share a body but little else — a division that maps rather too neatly onto the manuscripts' own history, since the theological papers were scattered and largely ignored for two centuries until the 1936 Sotheby's sale sent most of them to Abraham Yahuda's collection while the alchemical notebooks went to Keynes. I've drafted the core paragraph of a talk I'm giving to our department's history-and-philosophy-of-science reading group, arguing for a tighter connection than the standard picture allows, and I would like your comments before I develop it further.

Here is the paragraph as it stands: Newton denied that the Son was co-equal and co-eternal with the Father, holding instead that Christ was a created and subordinate being, however supremely exalted, whose divine status depended on the Father's will rather than on any shared necessary nature — a Christology close enough to Arianism that he spent most of his life concealing it, quietly avoiding the ordination his Cambridge fellowship would otherwise have required, and circulating his textual case against the Trinity's proof-texts (he thought both the Johannine comma and the disputed clause in 1 Timothy were later corruptions) among only a handful of trusted readers, Locke included. I want to argue that the same governing idea — that dominion, not shared substance, is what makes something count as fully divine — reappears, transposed, in the General Scholium's insistence that eternity and infinity alone cannot qualify anything as God, since an impersonal Fate or Nature could have those too, and that only lordship over servants does; and again in the Leibniz-Clarke correspondence's defense of a God whose free choices, rather than the principle of sufficient reason, explain otherwise arbitrary-looking facts about the world's size, location, and furniture. On this reading, the voluntarism structuring Newton's natural philosophy and the subordinationism structuring his private theology are not a scientific achievement running alongside an unrelated personal heresy, but two expressions of a single commitment about what divine sovereignty requires.

I would welcome your comments on three things: whether the textual evidence really supports tying the Christology to the physics this tightly, or whether I am forcing together two positions that each already have perfectly good independent explanations; what you take to be the strongest objection to the whole integration thesis, since I suspect there is an obvious one I am not yet stating clearly enough to see past; and whether I have gotten any specific historical or textual detail wrong, given that this is exactly the sort of essay where a small slip — about which passages Newton flagged as corrupted, say, or what the General Scholium actually argues — would be embarrassing in front of colleagues who work on this material directly.
