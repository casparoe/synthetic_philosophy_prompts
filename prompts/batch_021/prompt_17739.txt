A friend from my old rationalist-adjacent reading group, Daniel, is getting married in three weeks, and he's asked me to give the toast. I want to build it around the one idea that has genuinely shaped how he talks about his own relationship, because leaving it out would feel like dodging the most honest material I have. Daniel wrote his dissertation extending David Lewis's account of convention -- on Lewis's picture, a convention is a regularity that solves a recurring coordination problem, sustained not by agreement or design but by everyone's expectation that everyone else will keep conforming, an expectation stabilized by precedent and common knowledge rather than by the regularity being uniquely correct -- with some of the evolutionary game theory that people like Skyrms and Sugden later used to explain how one such regularity gets selected over the alternatives in the first place. He has told me, entirely seriously, that he thinks of the small domestic rituals he and his fiancée Mei have built over six years -- who starts the coffee, the specific phrase that ends an argument, who texts first after a trip apart -- as genuine Lewis-style conventions: solutions to little coordination problems that could easily have gone another way, held in place only by each of them now expecting the other to keep it up, and expecting to be expected to.

The difficulty is the room. Maybe a third of the guests are people from that same reading group, the sort of crowd that has argued about Schelling points at other people's parties, and they will know immediately if I get the arbitrariness of it wrong or fudge what common knowledge actually requires. The rest are family and non-philosopher friends who will only care whether it is funny and warm. There is also a tension I don't want the speech to dodge: Lewis's account is deliberately deflationary, and on it there is nothing about this particular set of rituals -- or, as Daniel and Mei's families keep joking, about the institution of a big white wedding itself rather than eloping -- that had to turn out this way instead of some other equally workable way. Daniel has only asked that it not become an actual philosophy lecture, which I understand, but I would feel worse if that fear pushed me toward saying nothing real at all, or toward a version that quietly pretends the theory doesn't say what it says. Could you write me a five-minute toast, built around the real content of Lewis's account of convention -- coordination, common knowledge, precedent, the fact that some other regularity would have worked just as well -- that gets one genuine laugh out of the reading-group contingent from the substance itself rather than a name-drop, lands emotionally for everyone else, and finds an honest way to say that something can be arbitrary in exactly Lewis's sense and still be the least arbitrary thing in either of their lives?
