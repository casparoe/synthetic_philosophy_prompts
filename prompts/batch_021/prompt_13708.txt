So in my math logic seminar we just went over the Lowenheim-Skolem theorem and ended up at Skolem's paradox, and honestly it's been bugging me way more than it seems to bug anyone else in the room. The setup, roughly: if ZFC is consistent then by the downward Lowenheim-Skolem theorem it has a countable model, but ZFC also proves Cantor's theorem, i.e. that there are uncountable sets like the reals or the power set of omega. So you end up with a model that is countable from the outside, yet contains an element that the model itself thinks is uncountable. When I asked about it, the professor basically just said "it's fine, countability isn't absolute, it's relative to the model" and moved on, but that "resolution" kind of just restates the weird part back to me instead of telling me why I shouldn't find it weird.

What I'm actually trying to get at: what are the most important implications of this for how we should think about set-theoretic notions like cardinality and uncountability? Are they objective features of some independently existing mathematical universe (the kind of thing a platonist wants to say Cantor "discovered"), or does Skolem's paradox show they're inherently relative to whatever model or axiom system you happen to be working in, in a way that should make us more deflationary about the whole cumulative hierarchy of infinities? I also vaguely recall someone in office hours mentioning that Putnam later ran a similar model-theoretic move to argue against metaphysical realism way more broadly, not just about sets, but I don't have a clear picture of how that's suppose to follow from Skolem's original observation, or whether it even should. Would love an actual breakdown of the stakes here rather than just the "relativize and move on" line.
