I cover science and ideas for a general-interest outlet, and on the side I produce a small, limited-run interview podcast on philosophical arguments that are too strange for a news column but too rigorous to wave away. I came across David Lewis's essay "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" while reporting an unrelated piece on the philosophy of punishment, and it has stuck with me: in a few dense pages, Lewis tests the Christian doctrine that Christ was punished in our place against the ordinary logic of debt, where anyone can discharge what's owed regardless of who incurred it, and asks whether punishment can really work the same way, given that it seems to demand that the guilty party specifically be the one who suffers. My guest for the next episode is a philosopher of law who has spent much of her career defending Lewis's tentative way out: the idea that punishment tracks "liability," a status that -- like debt -- can be transferred by agreement or imputation, while "guilt" itself stays fixed to the wrongdoer and never moves. She's extremely practiced at using that guilt/liability split to bat away objections, usually with some version of the line "you're smuggling in a transfer of guilt, and my view doesn't need one" -- and I don't want the episode to consist of her saying that sentence eight different ways.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that make that deflection progressively harder to reach for, each with a short follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give? I'd like at least one question that confronts her with an asymmetry inside ordinary law itself: civil law is full of vicarious liability (employers answering for employees, insurers for the insured), while criminal law almost universally refuses to let a substitute serve someone else's sentence -- so what, on her view, explains that refusal if liability is really as transferable as she claims? I'd like one question built around a case that can't be reduced to money changing hands -- a volunteer serving another person's prison term, rather than paying another person's fine. I'd like another that asks whether the doctrine ultimately needs imputed guilt after all -- Christ treated as guilty, not merely liable -- and whether that quietly reintroduces everything the guilt/liability split was supposed to avoid. And I'd like one that tests whether historical practices like whipping boys and scapegoat rituals actually vindicate her intuition, or simply show how long people tolerated something we would now call an injustice. Close the sequence with a question that asks her to say, concretely, what she would need to see or hear to conclude that penal substitution is not coherent after all.
