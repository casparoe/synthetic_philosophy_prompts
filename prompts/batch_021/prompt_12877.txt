During my career as a field ecologist, I was trained, more or less without question, to look for the adaptive story behind any trait I studied. It is only in retirement, working through the philosophy-of-biology literature, that I have had to confront how contested that habit of mind really is. What I still cannot settle for myself is whether Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique identifies a genuine methodological vice -- the "Panglossian" assumption that every trait is optimally designed -- or attacks a straw man, and whether their preferred alternative, appeal to developmental or historical constraint, is really any more testable than the adaptive stories it was meant to replace.

Please write a dialogue between two philosophers of biology who disagree sharply on this question. Both should be portrayed as equally rigorous: neither is a straw man, and each must engage with the other's actual argument rather than talk past it. Dr. Adler defends what Peter Godfrey-Smith calls methodological adaptationism -- absent specific evidence to the contrary, a trait's contribution to fitness is the right default hypothesis, and the burden of proof lies with anyone who proposes constraint or by-product instead. Dr. Voss holds the Gould-Lewontin line: an adaptive hypothesis requires positive, independent evidence of selective history, not merely evidence of current utility, and defaulting to adaptation without that evidence is no more rigorous than the "just-so stories" the critique was aimed at. Ground the disagreement in a concrete case: Steven Pinker's claim that music is "auditory cheesecake," a by-product of language, auditory scene analysis, and emotional circuitry rather than itself a target of selection. Have Voss begin by defending Pinker's account and Adler press the case that music's cross-cultural universality and developmental regularity are exactly what a selected trait should look like. Neither should concede by the end.
