I'm putting together a piece on mind-bending infinity results for a science newsletter I write — Cantor's diagonal argument, Hilbert's Hotel, that sort of thing — and Skolem's paradox is the one entry where I keep second-guessing my own draft. With the others I've got a clean "aha" I'm confident in, but this one I can walk through the mechanics of without being fully sure I've landed the actual resolution, and my go-to fact-checker (a friend with a math PhD) is off the grid on holiday this week, so you're it.

Here's the paragraph as it stands: "ZFC has only countably many axioms, so by the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem, if its consistent it has a countable model — one where the entire universe of 'sets' can be lined up one-to-one with the natural numbers. But ZFC also proves Cantor's theorem, that no such lining-up exists for the real numbers. So this countable model contains a set that, by its own internal lights, is too big to count. Skolem's conclusion was that 'countable' isn't a property a set just has on its own, it's relative to which model your looking at it from — a bijection between that set and the naturals really does exist, it's just not one of the sets the model itself contains."

Two things I can't tell if I'm getting right: is it fair to say the bijection "really does exist" outside the model, or does that phrasing quietly import more realism than the paradox actually supports? And is Skolem's own moral — that set-theoretic size is relative rather than absolute — still basically the accepted philosophical takeaway, or do people now think that's overclaiming for what's ultimately just a fact about models? Also flag it if any of the mechanics itself is off; I'd rather find out from you than in the comments section.
