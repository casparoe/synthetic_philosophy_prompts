I'm writing a feature on why philosophers of science are so wary of the phrase "closer to the truth," and I keep returning to a particular historical setback. In Conjectures and Refutations, Popper tried to make that phrase precise: he defined a theory's truth content and falsity content as, respectively, the true and false statements it logically entails, and held that one theory has greater verisimilitude than another when its truth content is at least as large and its falsity content strictly smaller (or its truth content strictly larger and its falsity content no larger). David Miller and Pavel Tichý then each independently proved, in 1974, that on these very definitions no false theory can ever come out more verisimilar than any other false theory — which undermines the very comparisons, such as Newton's mechanics versus Einstein's, that the concept was meant to make sense of. Rather than another restatement of that result, I'd like a worked illustration of it: invent two rival false theories about some domain other than physics, specify enough of their true and false logical consequences to pin down each theory's truth content and falsity content, and show precisely where Popper's ordering fails even though most people's intuitive verdict about which theory is closer to the truth is quite clear.
