My grandfather—he was a math professor—passed away a few weeks ago, and at the shiva my uncle brought up something that apparently stuck with him for years: that Kurt Gödel, of all people, genuinely believed in an afterlife, and not just as a comforting hope but as something he thought actually followed from an argument. That kind of floored me, since in my head Gödel is the guy who is maximally careful about what counts as a valid inference, not someone who'd let wishful thinking slide into his reasoning. I did some digging (I vaguely remembered this coming up in an undergrad philosophy of math class, something about Hao Wang's books on Gödel going into his more personal theological views) and as far as I can reconstruct it, the argument he gave in letters to his mother runs roughly like this: the world is rationally ordered top to bottom (he really did think this — it's basically the backbone of his whole worldview); a person's mental and spiritual development takes an entire lifetime and is nowhere near finished at the point where illness or old age cuts it short; a rational world wouldn't be set up so that this development is almost always wasted like that; therefore there has to be some kind of continuation after death in which the development can actually be completed. Someone online pointed out this is basically a cousin of Kant's postulate of immortality from the second Critique, except Gödel apparently treated it as much closer to an actual proof then Kant ever did.

I don't want to just shrug this off as "cute but obviously question-begging," and I also don't want to use it to comfort myself with something I haven't earned. So could you give me the strongest argument you can against this Gödelian line? Please don't just go after the premise that the world is rational — that's exactly the premise he'd hold onto no matter what, so an objection along those lines feels like its not really engaging with him on his own terms. Grant him the rationalism and still show me why the inference to a genuine afterlife doesn't actually go through. Bonus if you can tie it to tensions with his other well-known commitments — e.g. his mathematical platonism, or the mind-vs-machine disjunction from the 1951 Gibbs lecture — rather than just generic objections that would apply to any argument with this shape.
