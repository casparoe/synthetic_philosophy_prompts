I spent my working life as an experimental physicist — feedback loops and control systems, mostly — and it's only in retirement that I've started actually reading philosophy instead of just grumbling about it at dinner parties. My little reading group (mostly retired academics, plus one CS grad student who keeps us honest) has been working through formal epistemology this year, and last month we hit something called the Lobian obstacle that I can't stop turning over. It comes from Lob's theorem, the one that says a formal system can't prove "if I can prove X, then X is true" for some particular X unless it can already prove X outright — its basically a beefier cousin of Godel's second incompleteness result. The upshot, as I understand it, is that a reasoner built entirely on formal proof can never fully bootstrap trust in its own future self, or in a successor it builds, purely from logic — there's always a gap where it has to act on faith rather than proof. It reminded me, oddly, of watching my own kids drive off alone for the first time.

I'd love a short story that treats this bind as the actual engine of the plot rather than as scenery. I've read far too much AI fiction where "the machine became self-aware" does all the narrative work while the reasoning underneath gets hand-waved, and I don't want that here. It doesn't have to involve robots at all, though I wouldn't say no to it — it could just as easily be a scientist handing a life's-work project off to a student, or one version of a mind handing control to the next, whatever setting lets the obstacle really bite. What I want is for whoever's at the center of the story to genuinely hit the point where proof runs out and something else — trust, love, a leap, call it whatever — has to take over, and for the story to earn that moment rather than just assert it. Keep it fairly short, a few pages is plenty, and you don't need to walk the reader through any actual math — just don't let the story cheat on the logic underneath it.
