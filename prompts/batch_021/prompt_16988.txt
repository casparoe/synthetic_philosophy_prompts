I'm a software engineer, and my startup runs an informal bug bounty program: we publicly promise $5,000 to any researcher who privately reports a critical vulnerability instead of exploiting or disclosing it, with nothing in writing beyond that public promise. A researcher recently did exactly that, submitting a detailed report through a one-time anonymous email address, asking for no public credit, and clearly trusting us to pay once the issue was resolved. We've since patched everything, no data was exposed, and because the report was anonymous, there is no legal or reputational pressure of any kind pushing us to actually pay. Part of me thinks that since the danger has already passed, and handing over $5,000 now would change nothing about what already happened, the economically sensible move is to quietly keep the money. That conclusion unsettles me, though, and I can't quite articulate why. Is declining to pay actually the rational choice here, or is my unease pointing to a genuine flaw in that reasoning?
