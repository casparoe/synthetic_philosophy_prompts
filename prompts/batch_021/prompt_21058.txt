I've been rereading David Lewis's Convention after a long-running disagreement with a friend from grad school who now works in evolutionary game theory, and I'd like to see the argument played out properly instead of continuing to re-litigate it over email. The sticking point is this: Lewis defines a convention as a regularity that persists because everyone conforms to it, everyone expects everyone else to conform, everyone prefers to conform given that expectation, and all of this is common knowledge in the population — the same notion of common knowledge that turns up so often in rationalist-blog discussions of coordination problems and Schelling points — with the further, crucial rider that some other regularity would have worked just as well, which is what makes the arrangement arbitrary rather than dictated by anyone's interests diverging. My friend thinks the common-knowledge machinery is doing no real work and is basically a philosopher's just-so story: you can get exactly the same stable, arbitrary, self-perpetuating pattern out of a population of agents, animals, or even dumb reinforcement learners that never represent anything like "I know that you know that I know," as long as some selection process does the work instead. Her favorite example is Maynard Smith's Hawk-Dove game, where "whoever already has it, keeps it" becomes evolutionarily stable purely as a way of avoiding costly fights, with the two parties' interests directly opposed in any given encounter rather than coinciding — which she thinks shows both that common knowledge is unnecessary for a convention to arise and stabilize, and that Lewis's requirement of a shared interest in coordinating is too narrow to cover a lot of real conventions, ownership among them.

Could you write the dialogue we never quite managed to have over email? Have one side defend something close to Lewis's actual picture — common knowledge, coincidence of interest, the alternative-equilibrium condition that supplies the arbitrariness — and the other push the evolutionary alternative, using the ownership/asymmetric case as the main point of pressure. I'd like a real back-and-forth rather than two monologues in sequence: each side should respond to the specific point the other just raised, and in particular the Lewisian shouldn't get to retreat to only the easy, symmetric cases (which side of the road, what time to meet) without saying something about whether that picture can be stretched to cover conventions that aren't pure coordination problems. You can set the scene however you like — I picture two people who keep failing to coordinate on some trivial matter as they talk, but that's just a suggestion. Something like ten to fifteen exchanges feels right, and it needn't end in a tidy resolution if there isn't one.
