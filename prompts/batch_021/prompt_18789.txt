In the literature on the conditional analysis of dispositions, Lewis's reformed analysis — which requires that an object retain its intrinsic causal basis through the process triggered by the stimulus, with the stimulus and the basis jointly constituting an "x-complete cause" of the manifestation — is generally credited with defusing Martin's finkish counterexamples. The received view, following Bird's discussion of antidotes, is that this reform is nonetheless powerless against masking: an antidote can block a poison's lethal manifestation while leaving the poison's causal basis completely intact throughout the process, so Lewis's clause comes out false even though intuitively the disposition is still present. I'm drafting a paper arguing against this received view and want to pressure-test the strongest version of my position before committing to it in print. Please argue, as rigorously as you can, that Lewis's causal-basis analysis survives the antidote objection without simply bolting on an unprincipled ceteris paribus clause — in particular, consider whether "x-complete cause," properly understood on Lewis's own terms, already excludes antidotes as a matter of course rather than by stipulation. Engage with the strongest form of Bird's objection rather than a version that is easy to dismiss.
