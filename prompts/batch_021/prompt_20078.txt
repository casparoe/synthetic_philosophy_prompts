I'm a PhD student in statistics with a side interest in formal epistemology, and I sit in on a cross-departmental reading group where philosophy and statistics students take turns interviewing visiting speakers after their talks. Next month's speaker is a decision theorist who has spent much of her career defending imprecise credences: the view that a fully rational agent's doxastic state, at least given unspecific or symmetric evidence, should be represented not by a single probability function but by a set of them (a "representor"), paired with a permissive decision rule in the neighborhood of Levi's E-admissibility for turning that set into action. Whenever someone in the audience raises Adam Elga's sequential-bets worry from "Subjective Probabilities Should Be Sharp" -- that offering an imprecise agent two bets in succession, each individually acceptable relative to some probability in her representor, can force her into a package that is guaranteed to lose money -- she deflects it with some version of the line "that's an objection to naive, bet-by-bet choice, not to imprecise credence itself; a rational agent plans her whole policy in advance." I'd like our interview to press on that move rather than let her simply repeat it.

Could you draft nine or ten interview questions for this session? I want at least one question on the original motivation for imprecise credences (why unspecific or symmetric evidence should license a set rather than a sharp number, perhaps via an Ellsberg-style case), at least one on the "dilation" problem (cases where conditionalizing a representor on new evidence makes it less precise rather than more, which seems to get the direction of learning backwards), and at least one on Roger White's charge, from "Evidential Symmetry and Mushy Credence," that imprecise agents can get stuck, unable to update appropriately, when the new evidence is itself symmetric between the hypotheses in play. For each of these three, add a follow-up question keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. Then spend three or four questions specifically on the "plan your whole policy in advance" deflection of Elga's worry, sequencing them so that each one makes the deflection harder to reuse -- for instance, by asking about agents who cannot know in advance which future bets they will face, or by asking exactly which decision rule licenses the holistic plan when applying E-admissibility policy-by-policy does not -- and close with a question asking what observation or argument would actually persuade her that credences must be sharp after all.
