I sit on an interdisciplinary panel my university convenes a few times a year -- physics, philosophy, bioethics, and risk management are all represented -- to evaluate research proposals that fall outside what the standing IRB feels equipped to assess on its own. The case before us this month comes from a tenured physicist who wants approval, plus modest equipment funding, to build a version of the "quantum suicide" wager that some philosophers of physics have discussed. His device couples a quantum random-number generator to a mechanism that, on each trial, has some chance of killing him outright, a larger chance of inflicting serious injury, and otherwise does nothing; he intends to trigger it on himself repeatedly over the coming year. His written justification is that, on the many-worlds interpretation, he will never be around to experience the branches in which he dies, so the Born-rule-weighted probability of harm, summed across every branch the device produces -- the very quantity our risk framework is built around -- is simply not what should guide his own decision. He asks us to evaluate his informed consent on that basis, and says he will sign whatever waiver we like.

The panel is stuck along several lines at once. Two members think that if he sincerely and competently holds this view of his own risk, refusing him on paternalistic grounds is not obviously different from overriding any other subject's deeply held convictions about what a given risk means for them, and that our job is to certify that his consent is informed, not to referee his metaphysics. Two others think a review panel answers to people who are not him -- his family, his eventual physicians, the university's own liability -- and so must reason about expected outcomes in the ordinary third-person way regardless of what he privately expects to experience; one of them adds that his own argument, followed through, doesn't obviously deliver the clean immortality he's picturing, since across many repeated trials the branches in which he comes through the year unscathed shrink far faster than the branches in which he comes through it maimed. The fifth member wants to set the philosophy aside and reject the proposal simply because self-experimentation with lethal apparatus falls outside anything the university has ever sanctioned -- though we do, in fact, have a standing, rarely invoked protocol for high-risk self-experimentation that has cleared comparably dangerous physiological studies before, so that move isn't quite the trump card she thinks it is. Please draft the memo I will circulate to the full panel ahead of next week's vote. It needs to reach one concrete recommendation -- approve, reject, or approve with specific, enforceable conditions -- and its reasoning has to actually engage with his argument about which quantity is the rational one for him to track, rather than dismissing it by fiat.
