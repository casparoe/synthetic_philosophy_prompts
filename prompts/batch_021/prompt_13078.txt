Ok so I've been having a running argument with a friend from my rationalist reading group about whether akrasia is a "real" phenomenon of irrationality or just a description we slap onto an otherwise coherent decision procedure. She takes the standard LW-flavored line: hyperbolic discounting makes you dynamically inconsistent, dynamically inconsistent agents are money-pumpable / exploitable by a sufficiently patient trader who offers to swap around your future self's payoffs, and therefore akrasia (giving in to the donut, procrastinating on the paper) is a straightforward instrumental-rationality failure that all the Ainslie-style stuff like bundling and personal rules is just a patch for.

But I distinctly remember reading somewhere -- possibly gwern's page on hyperbolic discounting, or maybe a paper linked from there, could've been Sozou or maybe the Dasgupta-Maskin one, honestly my memory of the citation is mush at this point -- that you can get a hyperbolic-shaped discount curve out of an agent who is a perfectly good exponential discounter moment-to-moment, but who is bayesian-uncertain about the hazard rate governing whether the delayed reward ever actually arrives (they might die, the offer might get pulled, whatever), and who rationally updates toward a lower hazard rate the longer they survive without the reward evaporating. If thats basically right then akrasia that "looks" hyperbolic doesn't have to mean your discounting is broken -- from the outside its indistinguishable from optimal inference over unknown risk.

I'd like you to actually build out the strongest version of this argument for me -- that apparently-hyperbolic discounting, and the akrasia / preference reversals that come with it, need not be a mark of instrumental irrationality, and can instead be the signature of a rational agent updating on hazard-rate uncertainty -- rather than just gesturing at it. And please engage with the obvious holes instead of stepping around them: the classic small-sooner-vs-large-later experiments (people flipping their preference once you add the same delay onto both options) usually stipulate the payoffs as equally certain, so why would hazard-rate uncertainty still be doing any work there; and seperately, Ainslie built his whole picoeconomic apparatus -- bundling, bright-line personal rules, the "successive motivational states bargaining with each other" picture -- to explain stuff like the subjective feeling of temptation and why precommitment devices have the specific value they do, not just to fit a discount curve. Does the rational-hazard-uncertainty story actually compete with that account, or is it secretly just answering a different question and leaving Ainslie's picture fully intact underneath?
