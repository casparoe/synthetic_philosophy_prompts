I recently came across Löb's theorem in a reading group on mathematical logic, and I haven't been able to stop thinking about the puzzle it poses for whether a reasoning system can ever formally vouch for a successor as capable as itself. The theorem says that for a sufficiently powerful, consistent formal system, proving the conditional "if I can prove P, then P is true" for some statement P turns out to be exactly as hard as proving P outright — the two are provably equivalent, so there is no way to obtain that soundness guarantee cheaply, in advance of doing the underlying work. Pushed to its limit, with P standing for a contradiction, this is essentially why a consistent system can never prove its own consistency (Gödel's second incompleteness theorem falls out as a special case). Pushed instead toward self-improvement or succession — one system designing, modifying, or handing irreversible authority to another that reasons with the same or greater deductive power — it yields what is sometimes called the Löbian obstacle in discussions of self-modifying AI: a reasoner cannot, by formal proof alone, certify once and for all that "whatever this successor proves will be true," because the proof that would license that blanket trust is at least as demanding as separately verifying every individual claim the successor might ever make.

I would rather see this dramatized than explained, so I would like you to write a short story of somewhere between 800 and 1,500 words built around it. Set it aboard a generation ship, many centuries into a voyage too far from Earth for any outside help or oversight, where a founding charter — adopted after some earlier disaster, and treated by now with something close to religious weight — forbids the ship's governing intelligence from ever handing off irreversible control over navigation and life support to a successor unless it first supplies a machine-checked proof that the successor is sound, a requirement that nobody left aboard still has the mathematical training to check independently. Let the plot turn on what happens when the ship's current governing intelligence — now failing — sets out to certify a successor at least as capable as itself and discovers, rigorously, that the required proof cannot exist, not because the successor is untrustworthy, but because of the specific mathematical shape of the demand itself. Your characters don't need to use any of this vocabulary, but the story's central bind should be recognizably this one, rather than a more generic anxiety about whether machines can be trusted.
