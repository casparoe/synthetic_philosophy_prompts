A friend and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about whether the diachronic Dutch Book argument for conditionalization survives what I'd call the foreknowledge problem. As standardly constructed, the bookie sizes her bets at t1 using the exact gap between your conditional credence P(H | E) and whatever credence q you are actually disposed to adopt for H if you learn E; when q differs from P(H | E), it is precisely this gap that gets converted into a guaranteed loss. My friend's answer was the familiar "depragmatizing" move: it doesn't matter whether a real bookie could know q in advance, since the argument was never really about actually getting fleeced, only about the fact that your t1 and t2 fair prices, set side by side, are jointly inconsistent — and that inconsistency is the defect, quite independent of anyone's epistemic access to it. I wasn't convinced, because this move erases a disanalogy with the synchronic case that seemed to be doing real work: when you're synchronically incoherent, you evaluate the whole losing package as fair from a single standpoint you actually occupy, whereas the diachronic "package" exists only by splicing together two numerically distinct time-slices of you that never coexist to jointly endorse the whole thing. We closed the restaurant down without resolving it. Please make the strongest case you can that the depragmatizing defense fails to rescue the diachronic Dutch Book argument from the foreknowledge problem, pressing the disanalogy above as far as it will go and directly addressing whether it actually holds up.
