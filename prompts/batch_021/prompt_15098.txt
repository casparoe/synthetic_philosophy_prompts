I'm a novelist, and for a dozen years I've been one of twelve working novelists on the reading panel for a small annual fiction prize: we read the longlist, argue for two days, then vote by secret ballot. The foundation is disbanding the panel this spring in favor of a single celebrity judge, and I've been asked to give the closing toast at our last dinner. One of my fellow jurors has a doctorate in social choice theory and will know exactly what I'm doing if I reach for the Condorcet jury theorem to defend the twelve of us against the one; everyone else will just hear a toast. Please write it. It should honestly explain why a dozen independent, better-than-chance readers can be trusted collectively in a way no single judge can, while admitting that our two days of arguing before the ballot cost us the very independence the theorem depends on -- so the toast can't just borrow the theorem's comforting conclusion without earning it. Keep it to about three minutes, and make sure it reads as a tribute, not a seminar.
