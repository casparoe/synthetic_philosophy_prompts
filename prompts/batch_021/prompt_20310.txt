Here is the question I set: Max Black's "The Identity of Indiscernibles" (1952) asks us to imagine a universe containing nothing but two perfectly similar spheres, alike in every intrinsic respect. (a) Reconstruct the argument this scenario is meant to run against the Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles (PII). (b) State the most influential objection to the argument, the one that appeals to the spheres' relational properties, and explain how Black's dialogue answers it. (c) State precisely what the thought experiment does, and does not, establish about PII.

Here is the student's answer, verbatim: Leibniz's Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles states that no two distinct things can agree in absolutely all of their properties; equivalently, the Indiscernibility of Identicals tells us that if two objects share every property, they are really one and the same object. Black's dialogue attacks this idea by imagining a universe that contains nothing else but two spheres, exactly alike in size, material, temperature, and every other intrinsic respect, and situated in perfectly symmetrical relations to the rest of that otherwise empty space. Because the spheres agree in every qualitative respect and yet are clearly two rather than one, the case is meant to show that qualitative indiscernibility does not guarantee identity, i.e., that PII, taken as a necessary truth about purely qualitative properties, is false.

The most natural response is to insist the spheres are not really indiscernible, since each seems to have a relational property the other lacks: call them Sphere One and Sphere Two; Sphere One has the property of being a certain distance from Sphere Two, and Sphere Two has the property of being that same distance from Sphere One, and these look like two different properties, one had by each sphere alone. Black's dialogue meets this by pointing out that the difference is only apparent: it depends on already having distinguishing labels for the two spheres, which is precisely what could not legitimately be assumed in a case meant to test whether shared qualitative properties suffice for identity. Described without such labels, in fully general terms, neither sphere has any relational property that the other one lacks: each, symmetrically, is simply a sphere that is a certain distance from another sphere exactly like itself. So the appeal to relational properties does not, in the end, discern the spheres; it only presupposes, by smuggling in the labels, the very distinctness it was meant to establish.

Since even this best available reply fails, the thought experiment shows that the Identity of Indiscernibles is false: two distinct things can, after all, share every one of their properties, so no amount of shared properties, whether relational or qualitative, is ever sufficient for identity. Black's spheres therefore refute PII in full generality, and not merely some restricted version of it.
