I spent thirty-six years running an analytical chemistry lab before retiring, and until fairly recently philosophy was territory I had never set foot in — something other people did while I was occupied with mass spectrometers. Since retiring I have been trying to make up for lost time, working through a reading list an old friend from my book club drew up for me, and over the past year I have drifted, rather to my own surprise, into decision theory — partly because my daughter Mara works in AI alignment and I wanted to be able to follow her complaints about work over dinner. Mara is marrying her partner Priya next month, also a researcher in that world, and I have been asked, as father of the bride, to give the toast. A good third of the guests will be people from Mara's professional life, the sort who will silently correct your terminology if you get it wrong, and the rest will be relatives and old friends of ours who have never had occasion to think about any of this.

There is a specific idea from that corner of the literature that I keep returning to, and that I would like the toast to be built around. People there talk about "commitment races": the situation that arises when two bargaining parties can each make a binding, irreversible commitment, and whoever commits first, and to the more extreme and rigid position, tends to come out ahead, because a genuinely fixed commitment forces a rational counterpart to simply optimize around it — this is essentially Schelling's old point about the driver who wins a game of chicken by visibly tearing out his own steering wheel. The trouble is that if both sides understand this, each is pushed to commit earlier, and more inflexibly, than reflection alone would ever recommend, and to do so before learning anything that might have changed their mind, so that the individually winning move can produce the worst outcome for everyone once both sides make it. Some of the writing goes further still, into the idea that a truly rational agent would want to lock in its commitment logically prior to gathering any evidence at all — a level of the argument I will admit I have only half followed — but even the everyday version was enough to stop me short, because it struck me as almost an exact photographic negative of a wedding vow, and I do not want the toast merely to gesture at that contrast; I want it to earn it.

Could you write me that toast? I would like something that runs to about five minutes read aloud, that states the commitment-race idea precisely enough that Mara's colleagues do not hear it as a garbled version of their own work, and that then shows, rather than simply declares, why what she and Priya are doing at the altar is not a move in that kind of race at all — not a bid to bind the other side first, not advantage-seeking dressed up as devotion — while still landing as a genuine and moving account of commitment for my mother, who has never heard the word "equilibrium" used outside of a chemistry classroom. I would rather it contain no jokes at the couple's expense about game theory ruining relationships; I want the idea to be doing real work in the toast, not merely decorating it.
