Here's the question: "Garrabrant and Demski's 'robust delegation' cluster of problems is about an agent trusting a future or more capable version of itself enough to defer to it. Explain why trying to formalize this trust via formal proof runs into the so-called Löbian obstacle, and explain how either logical induction or reflective oracles is supposed to help with it. Then give your own take: is this obstacle a deep problem for embedded agents generally, or a narrow one specific to proof-based approaches?"

And here's the student's answer, lightly trimmed for length:

"Robust delegation is the problem of an agent trusting a version of itself that's smarter, or that exists at a different time, enough to hand off decisions to it -- like trusting your future self, or trusting a subagent, or building a successor AI and trusting it to keep pursuing your goals. The naive approach is to only okay an action (yours or a successor's) if you can formally prove it's safe. But Löb's theorem says that any consistent formal system strong enough to do arithmetic can't prove its own consistency -- so a system can never fully certify that its own proofs are trustworthy, and by extension it can't certify that a successor using the same proof system is trustworthy either, because verifying the successor requires trusting the very same kind of proof the system can't vouch for in itself. This is why Yudkowsky and Herreshoff called it the 'Löbian obstacle' to tiling agents: an agent can't in general prove that 'if I (or my successor) prove X, then X is true,' because a system that could prove that schema for all X could prove anything, including falsehoods, which would make it inconsistent.

Garrabrant et al.'s logical induction gets around this by dropping the requirement of proof altogether and instead having the agent assign calibrated probabilities to logical statements, including statements about its own future beliefs, that converge appropriately over time, so trust becomes a matter of degree rather than an all-or-nothing certificate. Reflective oracles do something similar for game-theoretic settings, letting an agent's probability distribution refer to itself without contradiction. Both are, in effect, ways of replacing 'prove it's safe' with 'assign a reasonable credence that it's safe,' which sidesteps Löb's theorem because the theorem is specifically about provability, not about probabilistic belief.

I think this shows the obstacle is fairly narrow: it's a problem for one specific naive formalization (self-trust via proof in a single fixed system) rather than a fundamental limit on machines trusting other machines, since the probabilistic approaches route around it entirely and, arguably, look more like how humans extend trust to each other anyway."
