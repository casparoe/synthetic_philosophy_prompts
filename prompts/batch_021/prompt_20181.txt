I've been re-reading Locke's chapter "Of Power" (Essay Concerning Human Understanding, Book II, Chapter XXI) for a graduate seminar on early modern theories of freedom, and a passage I expected to serve as a quick warm-up example has instead produced real disagreement in seminar. Locke spends the opening stretch of the chapter arguing that freedom cannot coherently be predicated of the will itself, only of the agent who has a will, on pain of something like a category mistake: "For, it being asked, whether man's will be free, that is as insignificant a question as to ask, whether his sleep be swift, or his virtue square: liberty being as little applicable to the will, as swiftness of motion is to sleep, or squareness to virtue." He then offers what has become his best-known illustration of the resulting distinction between voluntariness and liberty: "Suppose a man be carried, whilst fast asleep, into a room where is a person he longs to see and speak with; and be there locked fast in, beyond his power to get out: he awakes, and is glad to find himself in so desirable company, which he stays willingly in, i.e. prefers his stay to going away. I ask, is not this stay voluntary? I think nobody will doubt it: and yet, being locked fast in, it is evident he is not at liberty not to stay, he has not freedom to be gone. So that liberty is not an idea belonging to volition, or preferring; but to the person having the power of doing, or forbearing to do, according as the mind shall choose or direct."

Read on its own, this looks like a clean, minimal case for prising "willing X" apart from "being free with respect to X," and some commentators treat it as a tidy ancestor of Frankfurt-style cases about responsibility without alternate possibilities. What is dividing my students is what happens once the example is put back into the rest of the chapter. Elsewhere in "Of Power," Locke commits himself to a thoroughly necessitarian story about volition itself: on the view he develops from the second edition of the Essay onward, what a person wills at any moment is fixed by the strength of their present "uneasiness" (his technical term), and this, together with his dismissal of "is the will free?" as an improperly framed question, seems to leave no principled standpoint from which the locked-room man's counterfactual unfreedom could even be assessed. If volition is always necessitated by the balance of uneasiness, why should the presence or absence of an unlocked door be doing any philosophical work, rather than a merely psychological one? Put differently: does the intuitive force of the example depend on smuggling in exactly the sort of leeway, alternate-possibilities notion of freedom that Locke's necessitarianism about the will should leave no room for?

I would be grateful for help thinking through whether this is a genuine tension in Locke's position or one for which he has the resources, explicit or implicit, to dissolve. Relatedly, I would be curious how you would locate the resulting view on the modern compatibilist map: is Locke best read as a leeway compatibilist who has not fully reckoned with his own necessitarianism, an early source compatibilist for whom "the power to do otherwise" is ultimately dispensable rhetoric, or someone whose position does not sit comfortably in either camp?
