I've been reporting a longer feature on the private metaphysical commitments that sometimes surface behind the public work of famous logicians and physicists, and Kurt Gödel has turned out to be the richest case I've found. Beside the incompleteness theorems and the modal ontological proof of God's existence he circulated late in life, Gödel also defended, in his conversations with Hao Wang and, by several scholarly accounts, in correspondence with his mother Marianne, an argument for the existence of an afterlife that owes nothing to revelation and everything to his rationalist conviction that the world is intelligibly and purposively constructed. Charitably reconstructed, it runs roughly like this: the world is "rationally organized" in the Leibnizian sense that nothing happens, or fails to happen, without sufficient reason; human beings develop their intellectual, moral, and even perceptual faculties only gradually and at enormous cost, over decades of experience; in the overwhelming majority of cases this developmental process is cut off by death long before anything like completion; a genuinely rational world could not be one in which such a costly and elaborate process is, as a rule, simply thrown away; therefore some further mode of existence, in which this development continues or is at least not wasted, must obtain.

A philosopher I interviewed for the piece waved this off as "Leibniz with extra steps," not worth engaging seriously, which struck me as too quick given how carefully Gödel usually argued. So I'd like your help giving it a real hearing before I decide how much space it earns in the article. First, set out the argument in its strongest and most valid form, supplying whatever additional premises are needed to close the obvious gaps, and note which of Gödel's other commitments (his mathematical Platonism, his sympathy for the principle of sufficient reason, his rejection of a merely mechanistic universe) are doing the real work. Then argue against the conclusion: show that someone who grants every substantive premise Gödel needs — full rationalism about the world, robust teleology about human development, no brute contingency — is still not thereby committed to a personal afterlife in anything like the sense he wanted, and pin down exactly where and why that further inference fails. Please make the objection work from the argument's own resources rather than smuggling in outside premises, like physicalism about the mind, that Gödel would have rejected out of hand.
