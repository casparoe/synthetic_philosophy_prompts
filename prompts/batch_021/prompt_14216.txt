Longtime readers of the rationalist blogosphere will recognize Newcomb's Problem in its canonical form: a near-infallible predictor, Omega, fills an opaque box with a million dollars if and only if it predicts that you will take only that box and leave a visible thousand-dollar box untouched, and you then choose between the two boxes after the matter is already settled. What has long struck me is how closely Omega's epistemic position resembles God's under Luis de Molina's doctrine of scientia media, on which God knows, at a logical moment prior to any decree of His will, exactly what every possible free creature would do in every possible circumstance — true counterfactuals of freedom whose truth, Molina insists, is neither caused by nor determinative of the creature's eventual choice. Suppose Molina were briefed on Newcomb's Problem, together with the causal, evidential, and functional decision theories that grew out of the rationalist community's long-running argument over it. Working only from the conceptual resources he actually developed in the Concordia — the ordering of natural knowledge, middle knowledge, and free knowledge, and his defense of counterfactuals of freedom against what is now called the grounding objection — how do you think he would account for Omega's predictive success, and would that framework commit him to one-boxing, two-boxing, or to declaring the thought experiment incoherent? I would also value your view on whether his reply to the grounding objection supplies any principled answer to the standard two-boxer's complaint that a choice made after the box is already filled cannot possibly change what is inside it.
