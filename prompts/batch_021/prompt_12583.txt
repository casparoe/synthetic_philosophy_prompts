I've been going down a bit of a rabbit hole with Bernard Williams's opera essays (the posthumous "On Opera" collection) as a total side-quest from my actual dissertation work on his ethics, and there's a move he makes that I can't pin down precisely enough to explain to my advisor without hand-waving, which is a bad sign. Quick background: Williams is basically responding to the old Dr Johnson line that opera is "an exotic and irrational entertainment" -- Johnson meant that specifically about opera seria and its endless da capo arias, where the plot just stalls so someone can vocalize the same feeling for what feels like ten minutes, but the worry obviously generalizes to opera as such: why take an art form seriously when people sing their dying words instead of gasping them out? The boring, standard defense is "it's just a convention, like blank verse in a play -- you get used to it and stop noticing." But in his essay on Puccini (the composer whose Tosca Kerman famously dismissed as a "shabby little shocker"), Williams instead reaches for something he calls "manifest artifice," where the artifice isn't just tolerated in the background but is actively put on display and enjoyed as artifice, apparently on top of whatever the drama itself is doing to you emotionally.

What I want from you: walk me through, as precisely as you can, what extra philosphical work "manifest artifice" is doing that the bog-standard convention-defense doesn't already do. What exactly is the audience getting two servings of, and why would that -- rather than mere habituation to a convention -- be what actually rescues opera from Johnson's charge, if it does at all? I read one secondary source claiming this "excessiveness" ties back to Williams's general allergy to neat theoretical solutions in his ethics (the anti-"morality system" stuff), which would be a really convenient link for my chapter, but I'm suspicious of myself for just wanting it to be true. So also feel free to push back if you think I'm over-extending a nice observation about Puccini specifically into a whole theory of operatic seriousness that Williams never intended.
