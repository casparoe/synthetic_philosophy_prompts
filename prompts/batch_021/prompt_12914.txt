I don't have any background in philosophy — I never went to university and have spent my working life as an electrician — but over the past year I've been slowly working through Kant's *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* on my own, mostly because I kept running into the phrase "categorical imperative" online and wanted to understand it from the source rather than from summaries. I'm using the free Thomas Kingsmill Abbott translation, and most of it, if I read a paragraph two or three times, I can more or less follow. But I've been stuck for weeks on the fourth of the four examples Kant works through when he first applies the universal-law test, and on the paragraph immediately after it, because it seems to me like it might quietly undercut something Kant is very insistent on elsewhere in the same book.

Here is the example itself:

"A fourth, who is in prosperity, while he sees that others have to contend with great wretchedness and that he could help them, thinks: 'What concern is it of mine? Let everyone be as happy as Heaven pleases, or as he can make himself; I will take nothing from him nor even envy him, only I do not wish to contribute anything to his welfare or to his assistance in distress!' Now no doubt if such a mode of thinking were a universal law, the human race might very well subsist and doubtless even better than in a state in which everyone talks of sympathy and good-will, or even takes care occasionally to put it into practice, but, on the other side, also cheats when he can, betrays the rights of men, or otherwise violates them. But although it is possible that a universal law of nature might exist in accordance with that maxim, it is impossible to will that such a principle should have the universal validity of a law of nature. For a will which resolved this would contradict itself, inasmuch as many cases might occur in which one would have need of the love and sympathy of others, and in which, by such a law of nature, sprung from his own will, he would deprive himself of all hope of the aid he desires."

And here is the paragraph right after it, drawing the general moral from all four examples:

"These are a few of the many actual duties, or at least what we regard as such, which obviously fall into two classes on the one principle that we have laid down. We must be able to will that a maxim of our action should be a universal law. This is the canon of the moral appreciation of the action generally. Some actions are of such a character that their maxim cannot without contradiction be even conceived as a universal law of nature, far from it being possible that we should will that it should be so. In others this intrinsic impossibility is not found, but still it is impossible to will that their maxim should be raised to the universality of a law of nature, since such a will would contradict itself. It is easily seen that the former violate strict or rigorous (inflexible) duty; the latter only laxer (meritorious) duty..."

What confuses me is this. Earlier in the book Kant is adamant that an action only has moral worth if it's done from duty and not from any calculation of what's to your own advantage, and he's dismissive of any supposed duty that really just amounts to "do this or things will go badly for you" — he treats that as prudence wearing a disguise, not genuine morality. But when I look at the actual reason he gives for why I can't will the maxim in the fourth example, it's that I myself might someday need other people's sympathy and help, and that willing the maxim would cut off my own hope of getting it. That looks to me exactly like a calculation of personal advantage — the same kind of reasoning he rejects when discussing the false-promise example just before this one, except there the impossibility seemed to be that the very practice of promising would collapse if lying were universal, which feels like a stricter, more logical kind of contradiction than "this would eventually be bad for me." So is the distinction some people describe as "contradiction in conception" versus "contradiction in the will" actually doing the work Kant wants it to do here, or is the fourth example quietly resting on the same self-interested reasoning he spends the rest of the book arguing can never ground a genuine duty? I'd like to know whether there's a way of reading this passage that keeps it honestly a priori, or whether I've stumbled onto a real weak point in the argument.
