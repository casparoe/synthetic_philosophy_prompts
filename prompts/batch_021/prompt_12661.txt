I teach a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of causation, and this week's session was devoted to causation by omission: the puzzle of whether a failure to act — not watering a plant, not pulling a drowning swimmer from the water, not sending a warning — can be a genuine cause of what follows, rather than just a convenient manner of speaking that trades on counterfactual dependence. Two worries dominated the discussion. The first is the old metaphysical complaint that a cause has to be some thing or event, so that strictly speaking "nothing" cannot cause anything, however useful the idiom. The second, more pressing for counterfactual theorists in the tradition of Lewis, is what's sometimes called the profligacy (or promiscuity) of omissive causes: once bare counterfactual dependence is allowed to settle causal status, vast numbers of people who never lifted a finger — not just the negligent gardener, but any distant stranger whose watering would have saved the plant just as well — come out as equally genuine causes, unless some further normative or contextual criterion is smuggled in to privilege one omission over all the rest.

Before we turn to the proposed fixes next week, I'd like to open class with a joke that actually trades on this material, rather than a generic "philosophers overthink everything" joke. Ideally the humor should turn on the profligacy problem, on the "nothing can't cause something" intuition, or on the sheer awkwardness of needing some normative criterion to single out the one "real" omission among all the others that were equally available. Could you write me one?
