For the past couple of months I've been working on a project at my company that uses inverse reinforcement learning to infer what our users actually want from behavioral logs (clicks, dwell time, abandoned carts, that sort of thing), instead of relying on the explicit feedback forms we used before. While trying to figure out why our inferred reward models were so sensitive to seemingly minor modeling choices, I read more deeply into the identifiability problems that plague IRL, and ended up down a rabbit hole into questions that felt more philosophical than technical. I don't have any formal philosophy background beyond a couple of undergrad electives, but I got interested enough to draft a short write-up for our internal engineering blog, and before I post it I'd like a more informed outside opinion, since I suspect I'm either reinventing a well-known argument badly or making a mistake that would be obvious to anyone who actually studies this stuff.

Here's roughly what I wrote:

"IRL starts from the assumption that behavior is (approximately) optimal for some unknown reward function, and tries to work backward from observed trajectories to that reward. The catch, as Ng and Russell already flagged in their original 2000 paper, is that this is badly underdetermined: the all-zero reward makes literally every policy optimal, so the raw problem has no unique answer. The usual fixes — max-margin objectives, or assuming the agent is only noisily rational, as in Ziebart et al.'s maximum-entropy IRL — narrow things down but still leave the reward identifiable only up to transformations (rescaling, shifting, potential-based reshaping) that leave behavior unchanged. And if you also let the agent's degree of rationality itself be unknown, I recall a paper, possibly by Armstrong and Mindermann, showing that no simplicity prior over reward functions saves you: for any story that decomposes a policy into 'reward' plus 'planning/bias,' there's an equally simple rival story that assigns a completely different, sometimes reversed, reward.

That last result is what got me, because it stopped feeling like an engineering nuisance and started feeling like a formal cousin of something I vaguely remember from a philosophy of language course — Quine's point that translating someone's utterances leaves you permanently underdetermined between rival translation manuals that fit all the evidence equally well, so that asking which one is 'really' correct is a confused question. If IRL has a structurally similar problem, maybe that suggests 'preference' isn't a hidden fact sitting inside a person waiting to be recovered by a good enough algorithm, any more than the 'true schema' of an undocumented legacy database is something you uncover rather than choose. On that view, a reward model is closer to a useful fiction than a discovery, which seems like a much stronger and stranger claim than the usual disclaimer economists attach to revealed-preference theory."

I'd like your comments on three things in particular: whether I've got the IRL side right or I'm overstating how severe the identifiability problem actually is; whether the move from "behavior underdetermines reward" to "there may be no fact of the matter about what someone prefers" is a legitimate philosophical inference or whether I'm quietly sliding between an epistemic claim and a metaphysical one; and whether this connects to some specific existing position or debate in the philosophy of mind or philosophy of action that I should just go read instead of rederiving badly on my own.
