"Frankfurt's target is the Principle of Alternate Possibilities, which says that someone is only blameworthy or praiseworthy for an action if, at the moment of choice, they were free to choose differently. To refute this, Frankfurt describes a case in which a neurologist, Black, wants a man named Jones to perform some action -- say, voting for a particular candidate. Black has implanted a device in Jones's brain that allows him to monitor Jones's neural activity and predict what Jones is about to decide. If the device detects that Jones is about to decide not to vote for the candidate, it will intervene and cause Jones to vote for the candidate anyway. But if it detects that Jones is going to vote for the candidate on his own, it does nothing, and Jones votes for the candidate entirely under his own steam. As it happens, Jones decides on his own to vote for the candidate, so the device never intervenes. Frankfurt claims that Jones is morally responsible for voting for the candidate even though, given the device, Jones could not have done otherwise. This is supposed to show that moral responsibility does not require alternate possibilities, and thus that determinism is compatible with moral responsibility after all.

Defenders of PAP have tried to resist this conclusion with the 'flicker of freedom' strategy. According to this response, first proposed by Widerker and Ginet, there actually is an alternate possibility available to Jones: the 'sign' that triggers Black's device -- Jones's incipient decision against the candidate -- must either be causally sufficient to produce Jones's eventual choice or it must not be. If it is sufficient, the example just presupposes determinism and therefore begs the question against a libertarian defender of PAP. But if it is not sufficient, then Jones really could have followed through on the sign and decided otherwise, so PAP survives after all. Either way, Jones retains at least a flicker of freedom that Frankfurt's story cannot eliminate.

The philosopher John Martin Fischer responds on Frankfurt's behalf by arguing that whatever alternative possibilities remain in the story -- such as Jones's having a certain brain state just prior to his decision -- are too thin and insufficiently under Jones's control to be the kind of thing that could ground responsibility or its absence; a flicker of this sort is not 'robust' in the relevant sense. Since these flickers cannot do the work PAP needs them to do, Fischer concludes that appealing to them does not save the principle. What actually grounds Jones's responsibility, on Fischer's view, is that Jones's decision issues from his own reasons-responsive mechanism in the actual sequence of events -- that is, he has guidance control over his action -- regardless of whether he also had regulative control, i.e., the ability to do otherwise."
