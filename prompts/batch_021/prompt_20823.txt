My friend Dan and I got into a whole thing last night about whether morality is "real" in any mind-independant sense, and somehow it turned into a full-on argument about 17th century theology, because he pulled up this bit from Leibniz's Discourse on Metaphysics (section II) to back up his point. Its Leibniz going after people who think good and just things are only good because God arbitrarily decreed them that way — he doesn't name names here but everyone reads it as aimed at Descartes' idea that God even freely creates the eternal truths. Here's the exact passage that kicked off the argument:

"In saying, therefore, that things are not good according to any standard of goodness, but simply by the will of God, it seems to me that one destroys, without realizing it, all the love of God and all his glory; for why praise him for what he has done, if he would be equally praiseworthy in doing the contrary? Where will be his justice and his wisdom if he has only a certain despotic power, if arbitrary will takes the place of reasonableness, and if in accord with the definition of tyrants, justice consists in that which is pleasing to the most powerful? Besides it seems that every act of willing supposes some reason for the willing and this reason, of course, must precede the act. This is why, accordingly, I find so strange those expressions of certain philosophers who say that the eternal truths of metaphysics and Geometry, and consequently the principles of goodness, of justice, and of perfection, are effects only of the will of God. To me it seems that all these follow from his understanding, which does not depend upon his will any more than does his essence."

Dans take is that this basically makes Leibniz a moral realist in the modern sense — goodness and justice are just true, full stop, the same way math is, whether or not God even exists. I pushed back, because Leibniz keeps tying everything back to God's understanding instead of letting it float completely free of God altogether, and I vaguely remembered that elsewhere (the Monadology, the bit about God's understanding being the "region" of the eternal truths) he says that without God there wouldn't even be any possibles, let alone moral ones. So it seems less like "morality is mind-independent" and more like "morality depends on God, just not on his will," which feels like a genuinely weird third option — not standard divine command theory, but not secular realism either. Neither of us could actually settle it, so can you help me think through it properly: does grounding goodness in the divine understanding rather than the divine will actually succeed in making it non-arbitrary, the way Leibniz clearly wants it to, or does it just push the arbitrariness back a level (why does God's understanding contain these standards of goodness rather than others)? And is "God wills it because it's good" versus "it's good because it follows necessarily from God's understanding/essence" actually a meaningful difference here, given that Leibniz doesn't think God chose his own essence either?
