The question, exactly as she set it, concerns the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) and the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA) as they bear on the "presumptuous philosopher" and the Doomsday Argument:

"Model S holds that the relevant reference class of observers contains exactly 10 members, in total, across all of time. Model L holds that it contains exactly 1,000,000 members. Before any observations are taken into account, a physicist assigns each model a prior probability of 0.5.

(a) Using the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA), and taking into account only the fact that you exist as some member of the reference class (with no further information about which member you are), compute your posterior probability for Model L.

(b) Explain why, if instead you rely on the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) on its own, unsupplemented by SIA, this same fact — that you exist as some member of the reference class — leaves your credence in Model L exactly where the prior left it, at 0.5.

(c) The gap between your answers to (a) and (b) is essentially Bostrom's 'presumptuous philosopher' scenario. Explain, in your own words, what work this thought experiment is meant to do in the debate between SSA and SIA.

(d) Now suppose you additionally learn that you are the very first member of the reference class to have existed, in temporal order. Using SSA, compute how this new piece of information affects your posterior probability for Model L relative to Model S, and explain how this piece of reasoning relates to the Doomsday Argument."

And here, in full, is the student's submitted answer:

"(a) By SIA we weight each model by its prior times its population: P(L | I exist) ∝ P(L)·N_L and P(S | I exist) ∝ P(S)·N_S. So P(L | I exist) = (0.5 × 1,000,000)/(0.5×10 + 0.5×1,000,000) = 1,000,000/1,000,010 ≈ 0.99999. Taking into account only my own existence, I should already be almost certain that Model L is correct, purely from the armchair.

(b) SSA tells us how likely it is that we occupy any particular slot in the reference class, given a model — each of the N slots is equally likely, so any specific person has probability 1/N of being 'me' under that model. But merely knowing that I exist as some unspecified member of the class doesn't favour either model, since both models already guarantee that at least one observer has exactly the experiences I'm having; SSA has no extra step that rewards a model for having more slots, the way SIA does. So the observation 'I exist' has likelihood 1 under both models, and Bayesian updating leaves the 0.5/0.5 prior untouched.

(c) The presumptuous philosopher example asks us to imagine physicists on the verge of running an expensive experiment that would settle whether Model S or Model L is correct, when a philosopher, reasoning as in part (a), interrupts to say the experiment is a waste of money, since we can already be all but certain the answer is L. Bostrom uses this to refute SIA: it is absurd that mere existence should let armchair reasoning out-perform a costly, dedicated experiment, so the assumption that generates this consequence has to be rejected outright, which leaves SSA as the correct principle of anthropic reasoning.

(d) If I am the very first member of the reference class, then under any model, my probability of having that particular rank is 1/N, since SSA treats every rank as equally likely a priori. So P(rank 1 | S) = 1/10 = 0.1 and P(rank 1 | L) = 1/1,000,000 = 0.000001. Updating on this, P(S | rank 1) = (0.5×0.1)/(0.5×0.1 + 0.5×0.000001) ≈ 0.99999, so my posterior for Model L collapses to about 0.00001. This is exactly the logic of the Doomsday Argument: just as SIA in part (a) told us that mere existence is stronger evidence for whichever model posits more observers, this same self-indication reasoning tells us that being an early-ranked observer is stronger evidence for whichever model posits fewer of them, since an early rank is a larger fraction of a small population than of a large one. The Doomsday Argument simply applies this SIA-style thinking to our own rank within the human species, and concludes that we should raise our credence that humanity's total population will end up on the smaller side — that is, that extinction is nearer than we might naively think."
