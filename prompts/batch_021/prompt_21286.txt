I'm taking a seminar on classical Islamic philosophy this term, and this week's session was built around a close reading of Avicenna's flying-man argument in the Shifa and the Isharat. Our instructor kept pressing a distinction that split the room: showing that the self can be known without any reference to the body is one thing; showing that the self really is a substance distinct from the body is another, and several of us doubt Avicenna is entitled to the second, stronger claim. Please write a dialogue between two classmates of mine who kept arguing about it after class — one who thinks the flying man, properly read, does establish full substance dualism, and one, a committed Aristotelian hylomorphist, who thinks it shows at most that we can fail to attend to our own body, never that we could exist apart from it. They should also work through why Avicenna insists the flying man is created whole in a single instant, with no prior sensory history, and whether that stipulation actually blocks the suspicion that his "pure" self-awareness is quietly smuggling in bodily information via proprioception or memory. Have them engage each other's real arguments rather than restate their own positions, and end with them trying to pin down whether their disagreement is about the thought experiment itself or about a deeper commitment that neither one is willing to give up.
