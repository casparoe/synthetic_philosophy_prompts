I'm several drafts into a novel whose central character is a disillusioned military historian who, while researching a book on the ethics of statecraft, becomes fixated on Hegel's writings on war -- he can't decide whether the philosopher has been unfairly flattened into a caricature of a war-glorifying reactionary, or whether the caricature is closer to the truth than his sympathetic sources admit. To get the philosophy right before I put words in this character's mouth, I've been sitting in on a small Hegel reading group, and at the last session, while we were working through the material on sovereignty and international relations in the Philosophy of Right, two of the regulars -- I'll call them Marcus and Ines -- fell into an argument I haven't been able to stop turning over.

Marcus opened by claiming that Hegel's position is stronger than a mere observation that war sometimes happens: Hegel says outright that a guaranteed, prolonged peace would breed corruption in a people, comparing it to a pool of water gone foul for want of wind, which means he treats periodic war as functionally necessary to a state's ethical vitality rather than as a regrettable contingency. Ines answered that the real engine of the argument is structural, not evaluative: sovereign states, unlike individuals within a state, have no common judge standing over them, so a genuine clash of particular wills between states can, failing negotiation, only be settled by force -- a claim about the absence of any authority above the state, not a case for war being good. Marcus granted that the "no common judge" point is certainly part of the picture, but argued it can't absorb the wind-and-water language: Hegel is making the further, independent claim that even a Kantian league of perpetually peaceful states would be bad for a people, since without the occasional demand that citizens risk everything for it, they subside into the private self-interest of civil society and lose their orientation to the universal. Ines conceded that this is indeed a separate and more ambitious strand of the argument, but held it still falls well short of glorification: Hegel insists that as much of ordinary ethical life as possible -- family, private property, non-combatants -- be preserved even in war, and nowhere argues that wars of conquest, or simply more of them, would therefore be good. Marcus pressed further, asking whether this generous reading survives contact with Hegel's philosophy of history more broadly, where he calls history a "slaughter-bench," has reason work through the passions of conquerors like Napoleon, and treats world-historical change as arriving mostly through violent upheaval -- doesn't that suggest war specifically, and not just negativity in general, is close to being progress's preferred instrument? Ines replied that this last move conflates two different registers of Hegel's thought: the philosophy of history's talk of reason's cunning and tragic sacrifice is a general claim about the gap between individual intention and rational outcome that covers revolutions and economic upheavals as readily as it covers war, whereas the Philosophy of Right's argument is narrowly about sovereignty; collapsing the two, she said, reintroduces exactly the caricature that more careful readings of Hegel are trying to correct.

Neither would give ground, and I ended up filling a notebook rather than taking a side. Could you number the six moves -- Marcus's opening claim, Ines's reply, and on through the rest -- and say who has the better of it at each step and why, rather than just handing down an overall winner? I'd also value your stepping back at the end to say what you think Marcus and Ines are, between them, still missing about Hegel's actual view of war.
