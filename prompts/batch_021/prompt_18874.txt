I'm a novelist, and for the past few months I've been keeping a series of essays on my own website about the ethics of storytelling—less craft advice than an attempt to work out, largely for my own benefit, what writers of fiction actually owe their readers, with half an eye toward eventually gathering the stronger pieces into a collection. The one I'm currently drafting turns on a contradiction that has bothered me since a graduate seminar years ago, and I would value an outside reader's judgment before I commit much further to the argument.

The essay's central claim, as it currently stands, runs roughly as follows. In the Lettre à d'Alembert, Rousseau condemns the theater almost without reservation: actors are professional dissimulators paid to counterfeit passions they do not feel, audiences are flattered into a comfortable, self-regarding pity that substitutes for any real exercise of virtue, and even the most well-intentioned tragedy tends to mirror back the vices of the very audience whose patronage sustains it. Yet only a few years later, the same man publishes Julie, ou la Nouvelle Héloïse, a novel so effective at manufacturing exactly that sort of overwhelming, tearful identification that readers wrote to him in droves insisting Julie must have been a real woman and begging for details of her life. My essay argues that Rousseau's own prefatory dialogue defending the novel—which distinguishes solitary, domestic reading from public spectacle, and leans heavily on the fact that the story's ultimate lesson rewards renunciation rather than indulgence—does not actually rescue him from the charge his theater arguments level at everyone else: a private and even more absorbing fantasy life is, on his own terms, at least as capable of producing inert, self-congratulatory feeling as anything staged in a theater.

Before I build the rest of the piece on top of this, I would like your comments on two separate matters. First, whether the contradiction as I have described it actually survives contact with what Rousseau wrote, or whether I am collapsing a distinction he was entitled to make. Second, and quite apart from the question of accuracy, whether this reads as a genuinely interesting way into the ethics of fiction for a reader with no particular investment in eighteenth-century France, or as the kind of narrow scholarly point that will need a much stronger contemporary hook before it earns a place in a collection meant for general readers.
