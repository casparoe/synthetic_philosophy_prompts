"A textualist judge is only interested in the semantic meaning of the enacted words -- what an ordinary, competent reader of English would have understood the text to mean at the time it was passed -- and treats things like committee reports and floor statements as, at best, irrelevant and at worst illegitimate evidence of a 'legislative intent' that was never actually voted into law. Because the whole point is fidelity to the text, a textualist reads statutory language as narrowly and literally as the words allow, even when that produces a result Congress plainly never anticipated, since fixing bad or outdated statutes is Congress's job, not the courts'. A purposivist, by contrast, treats the words mainly as evidence of a deeper legislative purpose -- the mischief the statute was enacted to cure -- and will read even fairly clear text in light of that purpose, drawing on legislative history where it's available.

Holy Trinity Church is the textbook illustration of the purposivist move. The statute made it unlawful to prepay the transportation of any alien under contract to perform 'labor or service of any kind' in the US, and the Court leaned on that phrase's generality to ask what kind of labor Congress actually had in mind, rather than just applying it as written. Since the title of the act and the committee reports made clear Congress was worried about cheap manual and unskilled labor undercutting American workers, Brewer read the statute as not covering a church's contract with an English minister, on the theory that a case can be within the letter of a law and still fall outside its spirit. A committed textualist rejects this method regardless of how sympathetic the result is: once you concede that 'labor or service of any kind' covers what the church did, patching over that with Congress's unstated purpose is exactly the move textualism exists to rule out.

Bostock is the hard case for the usual political story because Gorsuch, a textualist in the Scalia mold, reaches a result -- that firing someone for being gay or transgender is necessarily discrimination 'because of sex' -- that looks progressive, using a method that's supposed to be conservative. He explicitly refuses to ask what the Congress that passed Title VII in 1964 expected the statute to do, insisting that once you fix the ordinary meaning of 'because of sex' and apply ordinary but-for causation, it doesn't matter whether that Congress ever imagined this particular application. Alito's dissent argues this is really purposivism smuggled in under a textualist label -- that the majority is quietly updating the statute to match contemporary norms while calling it textualism -- but the majority opinion itself never appeals to purpose or changing values; it claims to be doing nothing more than applying the text's original meaning to facts the drafters didn't foresee, which isn't the same thing as purposivism even if the two can look alike from a distance."
