I'm several drafts into a novel whose central device is a woman who, once a year, on the same night, is able to sit across a table from someone who is—or claims to be—the version of her from a life in which she made one different choice at twenty-two. The manuscript never uses words like "possible world" or "modal," but the emotional weight of the reunion scenes rests entirely on a question I cannot settle for myself: is the woman across the table actually *her*—a single person somehow present in two lives at once—or is she someone else entirely, a distinct individual who merely resembles my protagonist down to the last freckle and regret?

In trying to get this straight, I went looking into how philosophers who work on possible worlds would carve up the question, and came away with two camps and a genuine muddle. David Lewis's counterpart theory, as I understand it, denies that anything exists in more than one world at all: what we're tempted to call "the same person living another possible life" is really just a distinct individual, related to the original only by resemblance. The rival view, which I've seen attached to both Kripke and Plantinga, treats that as too cheap a way out—insisting that ordinary claims like "she could have stayed married" are claims about one and the same woman, genuinely present, in some sense, in more than one possible course of events. What I can't work out is how the two sides actually argue past each other. I've seen it said that Kripke's objection to Lewis turns on a man named Humphrey who lost an election, and would take no comfort in learning that a mere counterpart of his—someone else, however similar—won in some other world instead, since that isn't a fact about him at all. But I've also seen Lewis's side of the ledger: that treating one person as literally present in two worlds runs into trouble, because that person would then have to carry flatly incompatible properties—sitting in one world, standing in another—as though they belonged to a single thing rather than two, which is exactly the difficulty counterpart theory was designed to dissolve. I cannot tell whether these two objections actually collide, whether one simply outranks the other, or whether the two camps are quietly answering different questions and mistaking it for a disagreement.

Could you walk me through this properly: not the two positions set out side by side, which I already have, but the actual back-and-forth, so that I can feel why capable philosophers still divide over it instead of treating one side as having delivered a knockout blow? I'd like it pitched the way you'd explain it to someone who has to dramatize the distinction rather than formalize it—I need the intuition underneath the apparatus more than the apparatus itself. And once that's laid out, I would be grateful for your judgment, as a matter of the philosophy rather than of taste: if I want my reunion scenes to read as an encounter with a genuine continuation of my protagonist's own life, rather than a meeting with a stranger who happens to share her memories, is that a choice the literature actually lets me earn honestly, or would I be siding with the view that has the harder time saying what "she" refers to in the first place?
