Ok so this got weirdly personal for me. My boyfriend and I have this recurring fight about whether our landlord is trying to screw us over on the lease renewal, and after like the fifth round of the exact same argument he pulled up Aumann's agreement theorem on his phone and said something like "two rational Bayesians who have common knowledge of each others opinions literally cannot agree to disagree, so one of us is being irrational right now." He's one of those Robin-Hanson-pilled rationalist types who thinks persistent disagreement is basically a sign someone's not being honest with themselves. I hate that it stuck with me, but it did, and when I actually read up on the theorem it turns out the whole thing quietly leans on something called the common prior assumption: the idea that any two rational agents must have started out, before any evidence came in, with the exact same prior probabilities over everything, so all their disagreement has to be traceable to different information rather then to different starting points.

Now I can't decide if that's actually a requirement of rationality (in which case he's got a point and I should just update already) or if it's more like a convenient rigging of the game-theory setup that doesn't actually tell you anything about disagreements between real people who might reasonably start from different priors without anyone being irrational. Could you write me an essay that actually settles this instead of hedging? Walk through what work the common prior assumption is doing in Aumann's theorem and the related no-trade results, give the strongest version of the case for treating it as a genuine constraint on rational agents (the Harsanyi-doctrine-ish argument that any belief difference has to bottom out in an evidence difference), then the strongest case against that (priors are supposed to be the pre-evidential, subjective part of your credence, so demanding that everyone converge on the same one seems to either beg the question or ask something rationality just can't require). I want an actual verdict at the end, not a "well, both sides have some good points" -- something like a real position on whether sincere, informed disagreement between two people who each think the other is rational is even possible, and what if anything that should mean for arguments like the lease one.
