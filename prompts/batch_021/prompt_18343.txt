A friend of mine and I, both winding down a seminar on philosophy of mind, spent most of a long dinner arguing about panpsychism — specifically, about whether any of the going responses to the combination problem actually earn their keep, rather than just restating the problem in fancier language. She kept insisting that "the" combination problem is really several distinct problems traveling under one name: getting many micro-subjects to add up to a single macro-subject, getting simple micro-qualities to add up to the rich structure of macro-experience, and explaining why some aggregates (brains) yield a unified subject while arbitrary ones (say, my left shoe plus the Eiffel Tower) don't. Her complaint was that most proposed "solutions" only ever target one of these strands while quietly hoping the others come along for free. I was more willing to give partial credit — I argued that Goff's phenomenal bonding relation and Mørch's fusion view, whatever their flaws, are at least honest attempts to explain subject-unification rather than definitional dodges — whereas she thought Coleman's panqualityism just relabels the problem by denying there were ever any micro-subjects to combine in the first place, and that cosmopsychist "decombination" (Nagasawa and Wager's version, or Shani's) simply runs the same unexplained trick in the opposite direction, from cosmos down to us. Neither of us could agree on what should even count as solving, rather than merely dissolving, the problem, so the evening ended in a draw.
