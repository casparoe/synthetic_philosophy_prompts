Last night I got into a long argument with a friend, Priya, about epistocracy versus democracy — specifically, whether any scheme for giving better-informed or more "competent" citizens greater weight in political decisions could ever be more than elitism dressed up in respectable language. She was defending a fairly mild version of the idea, something like extra votes for people who pass a basic civics test, and at some point she sent me a passage from Mill's *Considerations on Representative Government* to show that even Mill, of all people — the great nineteenth-century champion of individual liberty — thought a graduated franchise was not merely permissible but demanded by justice. I know *On Liberty* far better than I know the *Considerations*, so this was new territory for me, and although I can follow the general shape of the argument, one stretch of it has left me stuck, and I'd like help working through it properly rather than just nodding along.

Here is the passage, from Chapter VIII, "Of the Extension of the Suffrage":

"But though every one ought to have a voice—that every one should have an equal voice is a totally different proposition. When two persons who have a joint interest in any business differ in opinion, does justice require that both opinions should be held of exactly equal value? If, with equal virtue, one is superior to the other in knowledge and intelligence—or if, with equal intelligence, one excels the other in virtue—the opinion, the judgment, of the higher moral or intellectual being is worth more than that of the inferior: and if the institutions of the country virtually assert that they are of the same value, they assert a thing which is not. One of the two, as the wiser or better man, has a claim to superior weight: the difficulty is in ascertaining which of the two it is; a thing impossible as between individuals, but, taking men in bodies and in numbers, it can be done with a certain approach to accuracy. There would be no pretence for applying this doctrine to any case which could with reason be considered as one of individual and private right. In an affair which concerns only one of two persons, that one is entitled to follow his own opinion, however much wiser the other may be than himself. But we are speaking of things which equally concern them both; where, if the more ignorant does not yield his share of the matter to the guidance of the wiser man, the wiser man must resign his to that of the more ignorant. ... Now, national affairs are exactly such a joint concern, with the difference, that no one needs ever be called upon for a complete sacrifice of his own opinion. It can always be taken into the calculation, and counted at a certain figure, a higher figure being assigned to the suffrages of those whose opinion is entitled to greater weight. There is not, in this arrangement, anything necessarily invidious to those to whom it assigns the lower degrees of influence. Entire exclusion from a voice in the common concerns is one thing: the concession to others of a more potential voice, on the ground of greater capacity for the management of the joint interests, is another. The two things are not merely different, they are incommensurable."

What I can't pin down is what exactly is doing the justificatory work in the comparison to "two persons who have a joint interest in any business." At some points Mill seems to be making an epistemic argument — that weighting votes by competence will make collective decisions more likely to be correct, and that this is why the wiser person's judgment is "worth more." At other points he seems to be making a claim about desert or fairness that doesn't obviously depend on outcomes at all — that superior wisdom or virtue simply has "a claim to superior weight," full stop, whether or not it improves what the community decides. These look to me like two different arguments, resting on different kinds of evidence, and I can't tell which one Mill thinks is actually carrying the weight, or whether he assumes they just coincide. I'd also like help with the claim, a few lines later, that outright exclusion from the suffrage and merely unequal weighting within it are "not merely different" but "incommensurable" — is that a genuine conceptual distinction, or a way of dodging an uncomfortable question (since a sufficiently lopsided scheme of weighting — say, giving the "wise" ninety-nine votes to everyone else's one — would surely amount to exclusion in substance)? This sits oddly next to a line just a few pages earlier in the same chapter, where Mill insists that "there ought to be no pariahs in a full-grown and civilised nation; no persons disqualified, except through their own default." Could you help me reconstruct the argument premise by premise and tell me whether, on Mill's own terms, it actually holds together?
