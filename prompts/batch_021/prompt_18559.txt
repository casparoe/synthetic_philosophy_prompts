I serve on my university's Honor Council, where five-member panels of students and faculty decide academic-integrity cases by simple majority, returning a finding of "responsible" or "not responsible." I'm also taking a seminar on social epistemology that just spent a week on the Condorcet Jury Theorem. A 3-2 finding is currently on appeal, and the Council's governance subcommittee, on which I sit, is weighing a reform proposed in response: expand hearing panels to nine members and require at least seven votes to return a finding of responsibility, on the reasoning that more voters, deciding under a higher threshold, should yield fewer mistaken findings. Proponents invoke the theorem by name; skeptics on the subcommittee point out that our panelists are all trained by the same office and shown the same case file before they vote, that nobody has ever checked whether panels are reliably better than chance to begin with, and that raising the threshold changes what a panelist has an incentive to do with a private doubt. Please draft the short memo I need to circulate before Tuesday's meeting: it should give the subcommittee one concrete recommendation -- adopt, reject, or a modified version -- with a stated point for revisiting the decision, and it should take the case for the reform seriously on the theorem's own terms rather than treating "more voters, higher bar" as automatically safer.
