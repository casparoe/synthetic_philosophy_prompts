I am currently at work on a novel whose protagonist is a molecular biologist who spends much of the book deciding whether to have her own embryo tested for a hereditary condition that runs in her family, and I have been leaning on two friends with very different backgrounds to keep her interior monologue about genetics honest rather than merely novelistic. One of them, Elena, runs a lab studying gene regulatory networks; the other, Marcus, trained as a philosopher of biology before moving into science journalism. At a dinner I hosted a few weeks ago, the two of them got sidetracked into an argument I have not been able to stop turning over since: whether it is literally true, or merely a convenient figure of speech, to say that DNA is a code.

(1) Elena opened by insisting the code talk is not decoration: the mapping from each three-letter codon to its corresponding amino acid is arbitrary in exactly the sense that matters for codehood — the way the sound of a word bears no necessary relation to its meaning — just implemented in molecules rather than in ink or sound. (2) Marcus answered that arbitrariness of correlation is cheap and does not by itself get you a code. Tree rings correlate "arbitrarily" with past rainfall, and nobody calls the tree a cipher; whatever turns a mere correlation into a code — an interpreter, a convention, some further grounding — DNA needs to be shown to have it, and until then "the ribosome reads the mRNA" is exactly as figurative as "the thermostat reads the temperature." (3) Elena replied that natural selection supplies precisely that further grounding: the codon assignments were shaped by selection for producing workable proteins, which is the very sort of history philosophers of mind invoke to explain how a vervet monkey's alarm call can be about a leopard without anyone doing the interpreting, so genetic information-talk earns its keep the same way. (4) Marcus shot back that this proves too much, since a bird's wing and the four chambers of a human heart were shaped by selection for a function too, and nobody wants to call those "coded" — so either codehood becomes so elastic that it explains nothing, or Elena owes him some further feature that the genetic case has and the wing does not. (5) Elena's rejoinder was that the genetic case does have that further feature. The codon table is shared, give or take a handful of known exceptions, across organisms whose lineages have been evolving separately for billions of years — exactly what you would expect from an inherited, arbitrary, combinatorial convention rather than a one-off local adaptation like a wing — and when the table was worked out in the 1960s, the scientists themselves described what they were doing as cracking a cipher, not as measuring a correlation. (6) Marcus's closing shot was that the vocabulary scientists reach for while making a discovery does not settle what they discovered. A cipher, properly speaking, presupposes a sender and a receiver who mean something by it, and DNA has neither; the fact that cryptographic thinking was heuristically indispensable for cracking the biochemistry no more shows that DNA really is a code than the usefulness of saying "the brain computes" shows that the brain is literally running software.

Neither of them budged, and I later realized I had quietly written a version of their argument into the novel without being sure I had been fair to both sides. Could you go through these six moves one at a time and say whether the person making each one was right, mistaken, or somewhere in between, and why, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I would also value your sense, standing back from the individual moves, of what Elena and Marcus are each missing, since I suspect I am too close to my own manuscript to see it clearly.
