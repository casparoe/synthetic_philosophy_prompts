The question was: "Explain the difference between dialetheism and paraconsistent logic as theses (one is about truth, the other about logical consequence). Then explain why a dialetheist who wants to avoid trivialism needs a paraconsistent logic, and, using Graham Priest's Logic of Paradox (LP) and the liar sentence as your example, say specifically which classically valid principle LP gives up in order to block explosion (ex contradictione quodlibet) -- and whether the law of excluded middle survives in LP." Its worth 10 points.

Here's the student's answer, copied verbatim: "Dialetheism is the semantic thesis that some contradictions -- statements of the form A & ¬A -- are actually true, not merely assertable or useful fictions. The classic example is the liar sentence ('This sentence is false'), which Priest argues is both true and false at once. Paraconsistent logic, developed by Priest in the 1970s, is a different kind of claim: it's a property of a logic's consequence relation, namely that not everything follows from a contradiction, i.e. that explosion fails as a valid rule. The two theses are independent of each other -- you could adopt a paraconsistent logic for purely practical reasons (e.g. reasoning sensibly about an inconsistent database) without thinking any contradiction is literally true. But a working dialetheist does need a paraconsistent logic, because if the liar really is both true and false and you reason about it with a fully classical, explosive logic, you can derive that literally any sentence you like is true, which is the trivialism Priest is desperate to avoid. Priest's own logic, LP, blocks this by giving up the law of excluded middle: because the liar is neither cleanly true nor cleanly false but 'both', 'A or not-A' is no longer guaranteed to hold, so the classical derivation of explosion no longer goes through, and Priest gets to keep his one glut without the rest of the theory collapsing into everything being true."
