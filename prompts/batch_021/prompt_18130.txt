I spent my career as an experimental physicist, and it was only after I retired five years ago that I began reading philosophy in earnest, largely to fill the hours the laboratory used to occupy. I came to Émilie du Châtelet by the route most scientists do, through her annotated translation of Newton's Principia and her contribution to the vis viva dispute, and was pleasantly surprised to learn she had also left behind an unpublished essay, the Discourse on Happiness, arguing that a genuinely happy life depends not only on virtue, health, and well-chosen passions, but on retaining a certain protected capacity for illusion -- and that stripping away someone's harmless illusions, however well-meant, can leave them worse off rather than better.

I still sit on my hospital's clinical ethics committee, which recruited me years ago as its token outside scientist and never quite let me go after retirement. A case from our affiliated memory-care unit has been on my mind precisely because of du Châtelet. A patient with advanced dementia asks for her late husband several times a day; for over a year, nursing staff have answered with small comforting fictions ("he's at work, he'll be home for supper") rather than telling her, truthfully, that he died three years ago and letting her relive the grief on each telling. Her son, who holds her power of attorney, endorses the practice as the only thing that reliably calms her. Her daughter, a hospice nurse, is appalled: she calls it institutionalized dishonesty toward someone who cannot consent to being deceived, and wants a written policy requiring staff to redirect or sit with her distress without asserting falsehoods. Because the same disagreement keeps recurring with other memory-care patients, the medical director has asked the committee to settle on general guidance rather than deciding case by case, and I've been asked to circulate a first-draft opinion before we meet next Tuesday.

Could you draft that opinion for me? I would like a concrete recommendation with a timeframe for review, one that engages seriously with the daughter's objection rather than dismissing it as an excess of scruple, and that explicitly works through whether du Châtelet's defense of illusion -- offered, so far as I can tell, on behalf of people who can still examine and revise their own beliefs -- actually extends to a case where the illusion is manufactured and sustained entirely by others, for someone who no longer can.
