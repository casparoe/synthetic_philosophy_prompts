I've noticed that discussions of "utility functions" in the rationalist blogosphere have a habit of sliding between two different things: a bare ordering that rationalizes someone's choices, and something with genuine welfare content. Write a dialogue between two economists debating this after a seminar on consumer theory. One is a committed revealed-preference purist in the tradition of Samuelson and Houthakker, who holds that a person's utility function is nothing more than whichever ranking rationalizes their observed choices in accordance with the Weak and Strong Axioms of Revealed Preference, and denies that there is any further psychological fact it must answer to. The other is a welfarist who invokes the gap between decision utility and experienced utility — citing Kahneman's peak-end-rule findings and cases of hyperbolic-discounting precommitment, where people pay to bind their own future choices — to argue that choices can misrepresent a person's own good. Have them press each other on their strongest objections: the purist should raise the worry that letting "true" preferences diverge from revealed choice makes the theory unfalsifiable and covertly paternalistic, while the welfarist should ask whether a single stable utility function can even rationalize one agent's conflicting choices across time, as the precommitment cases suggest it cannot.
