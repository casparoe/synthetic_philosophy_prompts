Okay, this is going to sound like a weirdly specific ask, but bear with me. I teach a grad seminar on formal epistemology and decision theory, and this week's session is on Kelly betting -- the old result that if your repeatedly wagering on favorable-odds bets, the bankroll fraction that maximizes your long-run *geometric* growth rate is exactly the fraction that maximizes expected *log* wealth per bet, rather than the fraction that maximizes plain expected value (which basically means betting everything every time, and going broke with probability 1 the moment a long enough losing streak shows up). Some people call the log-growth view "geometric rationality," to distinguish it from ordinary arithmetic/expected-value rationality. What I can't settle in my own head -- and what started an annoyingly smug email thread with a colleague in econ -- is whether any of the standard arguments for treating geometric rationality as uniquely correct actually work, or whether it really is, as he insists, just one arbitrary utility function (log utility) among the infinitely many that von Neumann-Morgenstern allows, with zero special normative status.

Below are seven considerations from this literature that keep coming up, each with a short gloss so you have enough to go on without needing to look anything up. A devastating objection should score near 0, not land in the middle just for being "on topic." Ties in score are fine, but I still want a strict linear order. using these seven short names, ordered most to least decisive:

- "asymptotic dominance": the Breiman / Algoet-Cover theorems showing a Kelly bettor's wealth eventually overtakes any other fixed strategy's, with probability 1, in the long run.
- "ergodicity argument": Ole Peters' claim that ordinary expected-value reasoning covertly relies on an ensemble average that isn't the quantity an individual repeatedly-betting agent actually lives through; time-average log growth is offered as the properly "ergodic" quantity instead.
- "it's-just-log-utility": the deflationary objection that nothing special is going on here -- Kelly betting is simply what falls out of assuming logarithmic utility, an arbitrary point in utility-function space like any other.
- "Samuelson's fallacy charge": Samuelson's objection (delivered, memorably, entirely in one-syllable words) that "almost certainly ahead eventually" is being illegitimately conflated with "expected-utility superior at any actual finite horizon."
- "fractional-Kelly practice": the fact that working gamblers and investors (Thorp very much included) overwhelmingly bet half-Kelly or less in practice, read by some as tacit admission that full log-growth maximization isn't what real-world rationality demands once drawdowns matter.
- "scoring-rule parallel": the fact that the log score used to justify Bayesian belief-updating in accuracy-first epistemology is, mathematically, the same object as the Kelly growth rate.
- "bounded-ruin property": the fact that Kelly betting, being a fixed fraction of current wealth every time, structurally guarantees you can never be wiped out by any single bet.
