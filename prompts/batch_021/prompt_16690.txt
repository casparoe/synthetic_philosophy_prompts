I'm a philosophy professor, and this year I hold my department's seat on the university's Student Conduct Board, which meets next week to decide a case that has split us down the middle.

Two months ago the trustees voted to end, effective immediately, a "no-loan" aid guarantee that a cohort of current students had been promised at enrollment, adding roughly $9,000 a year to their debt for the remainder of their time here. After a petition that gathered over 2,000 signatures, two meetings with the provost, and a denied request for a public forum, about 35 students occupied the lobby of the administration building for 40 hours, blocking staff access and forcing the annual scholarship gala to relocate. They issued signed statements naming themselves and their demands, remained nonviolent throughout, and left once campus police issued a final warning under the disruption policy—before anyone was arrested.

One faction on the Board wants real sanctions (probation, a transcript notation), arguing that the disruption policy has to bind regardless of how sympathetic the cause is, and that students who left before arrest and now ask us to waive even our internal consequences are claiming the credit of civil disobedience without its cost. Another faction wants the charges dropped, arguing that this was civil disobedience in the tradition of Thoreau and King, that the trustees broke a promise to people who had already arranged their finances around it, and that punishing the response would mean punishing conscience for a wrong the university itself created.

Please draft the short memo I will circulate before the meeting. It should recommend one concrete disposition of the case, with a timeframe for carrying it out, and it should engage seriously with the strongest argument on whichever side it does not ultimately adopt, rather than just gesturing at it.
