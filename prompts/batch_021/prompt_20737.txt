I am a retired physicist, and since closing my lab for the last time three years ago I have spent a good part of my retirement finally reading philosophy properly, starting, as so many scientists apparently do, with the free will debate and working outward from there. I mention this only because it explains why I have ended up needing a bit of help with something rather specific. My closest friend of more than forty years, Warren, a cognitive neuroscientist who spent much of his career on readiness-potential experiments in the tradition of Libet, died three weeks ago, and his widow has asked me to say a few words at the memorial gathering in his department's common room next month. The room will be split fairly evenly between family members who have never read a word of philosophy and former colleagues and students who will notice immediately if I get this wrong.

Warren was, by his own cheerful insistence, a thoroughgoing hard determinist who regarded moral responsibility in any deep sense as a leftover superstition; his standing joke, delivered completely deadpan at every award and retirement dinner he was ever cornered into attending, was some version of "don't thank me, thank my neurons." And yet, without any contradiction he ever seemed to notice, he was also the most reliably grateful and quickest-to-forgive person I have known: he mentored two generations of students with real tenderness, and I never once saw him hold a grudge, not even against the colleague who tried to scoop him in the late nineties. Since his death I have been reading, and re-reading, "Freedom and Resentment" (checking each time that I have the right Strawson — the father, not Galen, who has built much of his own career arguing that nobody is ever truly responsible for anything), because it seems to me the one piece of philosophy that actually speaks to who Warren was. As I understand the argument, our reactive attitudes — resentment, gratitude, love, forgiveness — were never really hostage to the metaphysical question in the first place, so a hard determinist loses no entitlement to them at all. If I have gotten that slightly wrong, please trust the essay over my summary of it.

What I cannot manage on my own is turning this into five or six minutes that the grieving relatives will simply experience as a loving portrait of the man, while the colleagues who understood exactly what his joke meant recognize that I have taken his view seriously rather than quietly setting it aside at the one moment it mattered most. Would you write that eulogy for me? I would like it built around the joke and somehow resolved by the end, without ever curdling into a philosophy lecture, without putting a citation in a dead man's mouth, and without having him say, even implicitly, anything he would have flatly rejected while he was alive.
