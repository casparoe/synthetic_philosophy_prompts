So this happened at game night a couple weekends ago and I still can't stop thinking about it. Near the end someone proposed a dumb bet as a joke: flip a coin over and over until it lands tails, and if that happens on the nth flip you win 2^n dollars -- 2 bucks if its tails right away, 4 if it takes two flips, 8 if it takes three, doubling forever. Somebody pointed out this is basically the St. Petersburg paradox (the "fair price" for the bet is technically infinite since the expected payout diverges, yet obviously nobody would pay more than like $20 to play), and that set off a genuine 40-minute argument between two friends of mine -- Nadia, who's a quant, and Colin, who's ABD in philosophy and spends way too much time on decision theory blogs.

Nadia's opening move: there's no real paradox here, you're just computing the expected value of the wrong quantity. Swap "expected dollars" for "expected utility," admit money has diminishing marginal value (log utility, say), and the fair price comes out finite and totally sane -- this is literally Bernoulli's 18th century fix and it works fine. Colin said that's the textbook answer but it doesnt actually generalize: he claimed there's an old result (from the 1930s, I want to say the guy's name was Menger but don't hold me to it) showing that for ANY utility function that grows without bound, no matter how slowly, you can cook up a new version of the game with faster-growing prizes that reproduces the exact same paradox, just measured in utils instead of dollars -- so log utility doesn't save expected utility theory, it just kicks the can down the road. Nadia's comeback: fine, so the actual fix is bounded utility, which she said seems independently plausible anyway -- surely no amount of stuff makes a life infinitely good, there's presumably some ceiling -- and once utility is bounded, no version of any such game can ever have infinite expected value, period. Colin's reply: bounded utility isn't a free lunch either, because it means that once you're close enough to the ceiling, huge differences in stakes basically stop registering -- you become weirdly insensitive to small-probability shots at enormous goods or catastrophes, which just relocates the paradox's weirdness rather than removing it. At that point Nadia said maybe the actual lesson is that expected utility maximization just isn't the right rule for these extreme, unbounded cases, and it's fine to trust our gut on the coin game without giving up EU theory for ordinary, everyday choices. Colin's closing shot was that this move is suspicious: once "the theory doesn't apply here" becomes available any time the math gets uncomfortable, EU theory stops doing real work and just becomes a fancy way of restating whatever we already believed.
