In my high school's philosophy elective, we recently finished a unit on death that included a chapter from Shelly Kagan's book *Death*, covering Epicurus's argument that death cannot be bad for the person who dies, since harm requires an existing subject, and by the time death actually occurs, that subject no longer exists to be harmed. Our teacher then walked us through what she called the standard response, the "deprivation account": even though being dead can't feel bad the way a headache does, death is still bad for you because it deprives you of all the good things you would otherwise have experienced, in the same way someone can be harmed by a friend secretly badmouthing them behind their back, even if they never find out about it. Since the deprivation account seems to explain perfectly well how death can be bad for someone without requiring the dead to consciously suffer, it seems to me that this basically resolves the puzzle and shows that Epicurus was simply wrong.

Given how neatly the deprivation account appears to settle the issue, I was surprised that our textbook still describes the badness of death as "one of the most contested questions in contemporary philosophy" and spends several more pages on further objections, as if Epicurus's challenge were still very much alive. A couple of my classmates think the textbook is just being needlessly thorough about a problem that's already basically solved, but I'm not so sure. Could you help me understand whether that's right? If the deprivation account really does face unresolved problems of its own, I would love to know what they are and why philosophers have not simply been able to patch them up by now.
