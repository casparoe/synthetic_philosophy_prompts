The question she'd set reads: "Explain how Ruth Millikan's teleosemantic theory of mental content attempts to solve Jerry Fodor's 'disjunction problem,' using the example of a frog that snaps at both flies and BB pellets. Your answer should explain what Millikan means by the 'proper function' of a mechanism, and why this notion is supposed to do better than mere reliable causal covariation at fixing determinate content." Here, in full, is what the student wrote:

"Fodor's disjunction problem is the worry that if we try to fix the content of a mental representation just by looking at what it reliably co-varies with, we get the wrong answer whenever the representation is triggered by more than one kind of thing. The standard case is a frog, which snaps its tongue not only at flies but also at BB pellets shot past it in a lab. If content were just a matter of reliable causation, the internal state that causes the snap would seem to mean 'fly or BB pellet' (or 'small dark moving thing'), since both types of object reliably cause it. But intuitively we want to say the state means fly, and that the frog misrepresents the BB pellet as a fly, rather than saying flies and BBs are both part of what the state means.

Millikan's teleosemantics tries to fix this by looking at etiology instead of current causal powers. On her account, a mechanism has a 'proper function' F if past tokens of that very mechanism performed F, and it is because they performed F that the mechanism was reproduced and now exists in its current form; proper functions are therefore fixed by natural selection, not by what the mechanism actually does most often today. The frog's snap-inducing mechanism has the proper function of catching flies, because it was ancestral, fly-catching tokens of the mechanism, not BB-catching ones, that were selected for and explain why frogs today have the mechanism at all. This is why Millikan says content is fixed by 'Normal conditions' for the mechanism's operation: the Normal condition for the frog's mechanism is that a fly is present, because that is the condition that usually obtained when the mechanism operated in the frog's evolutionary past, and that made the behavior successful often enough for selection to favor it. A BB pellet triggers the response without satisfying this Normal condition, so the frog misrepresents it, and the disjunction is broken because only one of the two conditions is written into the mechanism's evolutionary story.

One attractive feature of this view is how it handles Swampman, the creature who coalesces by freak accident into a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of an existing person. Because Swampman's cognitive mechanisms are structurally identical, down to the last molecule, to the mechanisms of the person he replaces, and those mechanisms only have the structure they do because that structure was selected for performing certain functions, Swampman's mechanisms count as having those same proper functions too, so teleosemantics ends up assigning him exactly the mental contents we would pre-theoretically want him to have. This is a point in the theory's favor: an adequate theory of content should agree with our intuitive judgment that Swampman, being just like an ordinary person in every observable respect, believes and desires just as an ordinary person does."
