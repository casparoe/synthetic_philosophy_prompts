I'm a software engineer, but for the past couple of years I've also sat on the small internal review committee responsible for signing off on research reports before our foundation, which funds and publishes work on catastrophic and existential risks, puts them out under its name. I ended up on the committee mainly because we're a small organization where everyone senior takes on extra roles, not because I have any training in philosophy, and the case in front of us for next Tuesday's meeting sits well outside my usual territory.

One of our funded researchers has submitted a report whose headline claim is that humanity has roughly a 5% chance of surviving the next two centuries. The core of the argument is a version of the Carter-Leslie "Doomsday" reasoning: if you treat yourself as a random sample from everyone who will ever be born -- the self-sampling assumption -- your own comparatively early birth rank becomes mild evidence against there being vastly many more humans still to come. A footnote concedes that the rival self-indication assumption, which weights hypotheses by how many observers they predict, would cancel this update entirely, but argues SIA should be rejected anyway because it commits you to Bostrom's "presumptuous philosopher," who claims from his armchair to out-argue physicists about the size of the universe, and because SIA arguably stops being well-defined once you allow the sort of unbounded or infinite future populations that some of our own AI-related reports take seriously. About half the committee wants to publish the report as submitted, since the reasoning is honestly disclosed and clearly caveated; the other half wants the 5% figure removed or heavily qualified before anything goes near a policymaker's inbox.

Could you draft the short memo I'll circulate to the rest of the committee before the meeting? I need it to land on one concrete recommendation -- publish as is, publish with specific changes, or hold it back entirely -- and to take the strongest version of whichever side it doesn't endorse seriously, rather than treating that position as an easy mistake to dismiss. Since none of us are philosophers by training, it would also help if the memo explained plainly why this particular disagreement has no tidy resolution in the literature, since that is the part our discussions keep getting stuck on.
