I never studied philosophy formally, but I've been turning over a question for a couple of years now, ever since a friend and I argued about whether it's really praiseworthy to do the right thing "just because you care" rather than because you know it's your duty. That argument sent me down a rabbit hole that started with something I read about Kant: he thought that a person who helps others simply because he's warm-hearted and enjoys it hasn't yet shown real moral worth, since his kindness is really just another pleasant inclination, no different in kind from wanting your dinner. To sharpen the point, Kant imagines that same person's warmth curdling under the weight of his own grief, until he feels nothing for anyone else — and says that if he goes on helping people anyway, from a bare sense that it's owed rather than from any remaining feeling, that is where moral worth, as opposed to mere temperament, finally shows itself.

What really got me stuck is the case people use against this: Huckleberry Finn, who decides not to turn in his friend Jim even though his conscience — shaped by the only moral education he'd ever gotten — keeps telling him the whole time that helping a runaway slave is theft and a sin. He never once thinks about duty or what's owed; he just can't bring himself to do it to Jim. Some of what I've read treats this as basically fatal to Kant's picture, since Huck looks more admirable for going against his own principles on a feeling, not less. But then I found a reply that says someone who's only ever moved by care for one particular friend, and never by anything like a commitment to people as such, might just have gotten lucky that his feelings pointed the right way this one time, and couldn't be counted on in a case where he didn't already love the person in trouble.

That's about where I got stuck, and I don't trust myself to referee it fairly. Could you write me a dialogue between two equally sharp characters: one who holds that an act only has genuine moral worth if something like duty is doing real work in producing it, even if that work is mostly happening in the background rather than in the agent's conscious thoughts at the moment of acting, and one who thinks the Huck Finn case shows this whole picture is backwards, so that moral worth is really a matter of being moved by the actual person and situation in front of you. I'd like two objections to actually come up in their exchange and get a real response rather than a wave-off: against the "feeling, not duty" side, the worry above that it can't explain why a good person's goodness should be reliable rather than lucky; and against the "duty" side, the worry that treating duty itself as the object of your concern, instead of caring about the people duty is supposed to protect, is a strange, almost fetishistic thing to want. Feel free to give them whatever names or labels make the exchange read most naturally — I just don't want either one to fold early or get the last word for free.
