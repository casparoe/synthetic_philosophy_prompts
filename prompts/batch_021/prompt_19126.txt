Question: "Nozick illustrates his objection to patterned and end-state principles of distributive justice with the Wilt Chamberlain example. Reconstruct the argument in your own words, and explain what it does and doesn't establish about Rawls's difference principle."

Student's answer: "Nozick's entitlement theory says a distribution is just if it arose through the three principles of acquisition, transfer, and rectification -- rectification being the principle that checks that the initial holdings in a distribution were acquired without force or fraud, so that we can call some starting distribution D1 just. The Wilt Chamberlain example asks us to grant that D1 is just, by whatever patterned principle we like, say strict equality. Wilt Chamberlain then signs a contract where every fan who comes to watch him play pays an extra 25 cents of the ticket price directly to him. A million people attend over the season because they want to see him play, and he ends up with $250,000, far more than anyone else has, so the resulting distribution D2 no longer fits the original pattern. But every single transfer from D1 to D2 was voluntary. Nozick's point is that no pattern can be maintained over time without continually interfering with people's choices about their own justly-held holdings -- as he puts it, liberty upsets patterns. Since Rawls's difference principle is exactly this kind of patterned, end-state principle, the example shows that maintaining it would require constant redistribution, and therefore that any distribution reached through free exchange, whatever the starting point, is one nobody can legitimately object to."
