So this is a bit of a weird ask but here's the backstory: I spent most of this past fall canvassing for a local city council candidate, and it kind of broke my brain a little how much energy inside the campaign went into internal party positioning (who's loyal to who, which faction you're seen as aligned with) versus the actual policy stuff the candidate ran on. Afterward someone from my political theory reading group sent me Simone Weil's essay on abolishing political parties (I think she wrote it near the end of WW2, while working for the Free French government-in-exile, basically as a proposal for how France should be run after liberation), and a lot of it landed way more than I expected -- especially her idea that a party isn't just a group of people with shared goals but a kind of machine for manufacturing collective passion, one whose real, unstated purpose ends up being its own unlimited growth, so that truth and justice get treated as mere means to that end instead of the other way around.

Naturally I brought this up with my roommate, who worked on the Hill for two years and is a big believer in the "responsible party government" line -- parties are what actually let a mass of scattered individual opinions turn into a governing coalition, they give voters a legible brand to hold accountable, they solve coordination problems that would otherwise paralyze any large legislature. I did not do well defending Weil's side, mostly because I dont think I understand either position well enough yet to get past "parties bad" vs. "but organization tho."

What I'd love is a dialogue between the two positions (doesn't have to literally be me and her, just two people who actually know what they're talking about) where each one has to engage with the other's strongest point instead of just restating their own -- so Weil's side should have to answer whether a world without formal parties just produces informal factions with none of the accountability, and the pro-party side should have to actually sit with the claim that party membership structurally pressures people into subordinating honest judgment to party growth. If it helps, set it in a car on the long drive back from a canvass shift, since that's basically where the real version of this argument happened.
