So I went down a bit of a rabbit hole this week that started with rereading old LessWrong threads on the St Petersburg paradox and ended with a friend sending me a paper about something called the "Pasadena paradox" (Hájek and I think a guy named Nover, early-to-mid 2000s? correct me if im wrong on that). The setup is basically the St Petersburg coin-flipping game again, except the payoffs alternate in sign and shrink a bit faster, so that instead of the expectation just diverging off to infinity like the usual case, it apparently doesn't converge to any value at all — not infinity, not any real number, nothing. Something about the series only being conditionally convergent instead of absolutely convergent, which means you can rearrange the terms Riemann-style and get literally any sum you want, or send it off to plus or minus infinity, just by adding up the exact same set of outcomes in a different order.

I can follow the math ok (conditional convergence rang a bell from an old real analysis class) but I don't have a great intuition for why this counts as a genuine philosophical problem for expected value rather than just a fun fact about infinite series. Like, isn't there an obvious privileged order here — just add the terms up in the order the coin flips actually happen, n = 1, 2, 3...? Why doesn't that resolve it? Can you walk me through why decision theorists treat the rearrangement business as actually damning for expected utility as a concept, rather than something you can just stipulate your way around? And as a bonus, if you've got a take on it: does this kind of pathology actually bite on the more familiar "fanaticism" worries around Pascal's mugging and tiny-probability-of-astronomical-payoff arguments that show up on the EA forum, or is Pasadena more of a mathematician's curiosity that doesn't really transfer to real decision problems?
