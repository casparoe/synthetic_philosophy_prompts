So I've fallen down a bit of a rabbit hole reading decision theory stuff on rationalist blogs and forums (never studied any of this in school, it's just become a weird hobby of mine), and there's this "updateless decision theory" idea that I cannot make peace with, specifically the "counterfactual mugging" scenario everyone uses to explain it. Setup as I understand it: a perfect predictor called Omega flips a fair coin. If it lands tails, Omega will hand you $10,000, but only if Omega had already predicted that you would hand over $100 in the scenario where the coin landed heads instead. If it actually lands heads, Omega just walks up and asks you for the $100, full stop, no $10,000 coming your way in this run no matter what, the tails-branch is just gone. The "updateless" answer everyone gives is that you should pay, because a policy of "pay if heads" has a better expected payout averaged over both sides of the coin than a policy of "don't pay", so a truly rational agent commits to that policy up front and then actually follows through even once its the heads-branch.

Here's my issue, and I was arguing about this with a friend over text last night and neither of us could really nail it down: once your standing there and Omega's literally asking for the $100, you know with total certainty the coin was heads. It's not a 50/50 thing anymore from where you're stood, its a done fact. So how does an average over a coinflip that, from your current totally-certain vantage point, didn't even land the other way, still get to boss around what's rational for you to do right now? Averaging only seems to make sense while you're genuinely unsure which branch you're in. Whenever I bring this up people just fire back with "well if you're the type of agent that wouldn't pay, Omega would've predicted that and you'd never get offered the good deal on the tails side in the first place" -- but that feels like it's dodging the actual question, since in THIS run, heads already happened, there's no tails-branch left for you to protect by paying, so that comeback just seems to be restating the policy argument rather than explaining why a certain, present fact should bow to reasoning that was built for the uncertain case. Could you write me a real, rigorous argument for the position that paying up is NOT rational once you're actually in the heads-branch and know it for sure -- and specifically take that "you wouldn't have been offered the deal" comeback apart piece by piece rather than just gesturing at it the way everyone online seems to?
