I sit on my state's parole board. Every inmate's file now arrives with an actuarial score, produced by a risk-assessment instrument the board adopted last year, that estimates his probability of reoffending within three years by locating him within a comparison group drawn from historical release data. The case forcing us to revisit our policy on these scores involves an inmate at whose hearing his attorney and his victim's family each submitted a different breakdown of that same underlying dataset: the attorney's version narrows the comparison group by offense type, age, and program completion to arrive at a 6 percent risk (though that group contains only seventeen people); the family's version uses the full statewide release cohort for his offense category to arrive at 38 percent; and the instrument's own default output, based on a mid-level grouping, says 24 percent. All three figures were computed correctly from the same real data; they simply disagree, and the hearing panel had no principled basis for treating any one of them as "the" inmate's actual risk rather than the others. The board is now split three ways: defer always to whichever comparison group is narrowest, on the theory that more specific is more accurate; abandon these scores altogether and return to the panel's own clinical impression of the man in the room (though one member points out — rightly, I think — that unstructured clinical judgment has its own long and well-documented record of doing worse than actuarial tools at exactly this kind of prediction); or find some third approach.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before we vote on the 14th. It should recommend, with reasons, how this inmate's case ought to be resolved given the conflicting figures, and it should propose a standing policy for how the board is to handle risk scores whenever a case admits more than one defensible comparison group, since this dispute is certain to recur. I would like the memo to take seriously the possibility that there may be no principled way, even in principle, to single out the "correct" comparison group for a given individual, rather than treating this as a data-quality problem that a bigger dataset would eventually fix, while still arriving at a recommendation the board can actually act on this month.
