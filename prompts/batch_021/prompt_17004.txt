My younger sister, Ingrid, is getting married next month, and I've been asked to give the toast at the reception, largely, I suspect, because I'm the sibling who writes about ideas for a living and can be trusted not to embarrass her in front of her entire philosophy department. Ingrid has built her career on Wittgenstein's On Certainty, and specifically on "hinge propositions" — those bedrock certainties (that the earth existed long before I was born, that this is my hand, that memory is broadly reliable) which Wittgenstein argued are never actually known or evidentially justified in the ordinary sense, but simply have to stand fast, unquestioned, before any doubting or reasoning can get going at all. I've absorbed a fair amount of this secondhand, from a decade of family dinners and from covering the politics of expertise and public trust for my own beat, which overlaps with hinge epistemology rather more than I would have guessed.

Her fiancé, Sam, is a structural engineer, and in structural engineering a hinge is itself a precise technical term for a joint that permits rotation while still carrying load, which means every person in her cohort has already made the obvious joke about a woman who studies hinges marrying a man who designs them. I would like to retire that joke rather than repeat it. What I actually need is a toast, four or five minutes long, that uses her real philosophical commitments as its structural logic without ever tipping into an actual philosophy lecture: roughly a third of the room — her advisor, her co-authors, the graduate students she supervises — will know this material cold and wince at a wrong move, while the rest, including our parents, need it to work as a warm, legible tribute with no background required. The one move I cannot afford, because she would correct me on the spot, is describing her certainty about Sam as a very well-justified belief ("I've never been more sure of anything") or as a leap of faith taken once the evidence ran out — both quietly misstate her own position, which is that some certainties are not graded conclusions awaiting more support, and are not acts of faith either, but the ground on which the marriage's ordinary, everyday doubts and disagreements get to stand. Could you write it?
