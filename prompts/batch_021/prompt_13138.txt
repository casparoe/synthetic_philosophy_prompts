Last weekend an old graduate-school friend of mine, now finishing a PhD in philosophy of psychiatry, and I spent nearly two hours arguing about a proposal that is currently in front of the clinical policy committee I sit on for my hospital's outpatient bereavement and loss program. The committee must decide whether to adopt formal screening and diagnosis for Prolonged Grief Disorder (PGD) -- the category added to the DSM-5-TR in 2022 -- as a routine part of intake, using a brief validated instrument such as the PG-13-Revised. Under the proposal, a patient who reports intense yearning for or preoccupation with the deceased on most days, together with at least three of the eight accompanying symptoms (identity disruption, a sense that life is meaningless, and so on), persisting well beyond the twelve-month threshold the DSM specifies for adults, would receive a formal diagnosis and be referred into structured, billable treatment such as Complicated Grief Treatment. I should mention, since it came up in the argument, that ICD-11 sets the same threshold at six months rather than twelve -- a discrepancy my friend thinks is itself revealing.

Her objection was not the familiar "grief is not an illness" complaint. She argued instead from Ian Hacking's account of looping effects and interactive kinds: a diagnostic category like this does not simply pick out a population that was already there, and once clinicians, insurers, popular writing, and patients themselves start organizing experience around the label, the grief itself starts to be lived and narrated through the checklist, new borderline cases proliferate, and the category keeps moving in response -- which she takes the DSM/ICD-11 disagreement to be a symptom of, not a coincidence. I pushed back that plenty of our patients are severely impaired for years and currently get neither insurance coverage nor clinical attention for lack of a billing code, and that a looping effect is not automatically corrosive -- it can equally be how real suffering gets taken seriously instead of dismissed as "normal" grief. Neither of us gave much ground.

Could you draft the short memo I need to circulate to the committee ahead of our meeting in two weeks? It should end in a concrete recommendation -- adopt, reject, or adopt with specific conditions -- with a timeframe attached, and it should treat my friend's looping-effects argument as a serious constraint on the proposal rather than something to be dispatched with a paragraph about impairment statistics before moving on.
