I spent most of my career doing statistical mechanics and quantum theory (cold atom stuff toward the end), so "indistinguishable particles" were just a fact of life for me long before I ever thought about them philosophically - you symmetrize the wavefunction for bosons, antisymmetrize for fermions, and get on with the calculation. Now that I'm retired I've been going to a little philosophy reading group at the community center, and last month we did Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles plus Max Black's thought experiment with the two perfectly similar spheres floating alone in an otherwise symmetric universe, which everyone kind of treated as the last word on why the principle fails. My friend Deb (a retired chemist) said afterward that quantum mechanics "already proved Leibniz wrong decades ago" with real indistinguishable particles, and that comment has been bugging me since, because I'm not convinced the two cases are doing the same philosophical work.

So here's what I'd like help sorting through: could you compare Black's two-spheres case with the case of quantum mechanically indistinguishable particles (say, two electrons in a spin-singlet state, or a bunch of bosons condensed into the same mode) as challenges to the identity of indiscernibles? I recall reading, maybe in a footnote somewhere, about a distinction between particles that can only be "weakly" discerned via some relation even though no ordinary property tells them apart, versus ones that apparently can't even be discerned that way - I think fermions and bosons come out differently on this, but I'm definately fuzzy on the details. Does that quantum wrinkle track anything actually present in Black's classical example, or is the physics case a genuinely harder (or maybe easier) problem for Leibniz's principle than two spheres in an imaginary universe ever were? I'd like an honest verdict on which case poses the bigger threat to the principle, not just a tidy survey of both.
