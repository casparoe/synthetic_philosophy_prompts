I attend a Jesuit Catholic high school, and I've been asked to give the student address at this year's baccalaureate service, the faith-focused ceremony held the evening before commencement. For my senior seminar capstone I worked on Rawls's writing on public reason, and I want the address to turn on two of his ideas: the "proviso," which allows comprehensive religious reasons into public political debate as long as reasons that don't depend on any particular faith eventually get offered too, and the overlapping consensus, where citizens who hold different comprehensive doctrines each endorse the same political conception of justice, but for their own separate reasons. Our graduating class is genuinely mixed: some of us are devout, some lapsed, a few openly atheist, while the school's own mission is unapologetically Catholic, and whoever gives this address always closes with some version of the old Jesuit charge to go out and "set the world on fire." I don't want to just repeat that line. Please draft a five-minute address that actually uses Rawls's distinctions to explain how we can carry a comprehensive faith, or its absence, into a pluralist democracy without either treating it as merely private or expecting public life to run on it, in a way that would satisfy the theology faculty as much as the families in the pews.
