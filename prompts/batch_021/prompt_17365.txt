Since retiring after thirty-five years as an experimental physicist, I have been spending my mornings working through the foundations of decision theory, partly out of curiosity about how much of welfare economics rests on axiomatic derivations that feel structurally similar to representation theorems in physics. The result that has occupied me for the last few weeks, ever since a friend from my old department pointed me toward it, is Harsanyi's 1955 aggregation theorem. As I understand it, the theorem states that if each individual's preferences over lotteries satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, if society's preferences over those same lotteries satisfy the axioms too, and if society is indifferent between any two lotteries that every individual is indifferent between, then the social utility function must be expressible as a weighted sum of the individual utility functions plus a constant, with the weights turning out non-negative once one adds the mild further condition that unanimous individual preference implies social preference. Harsanyi presented this as a proof that utilitarianism follows from premises that anyone who already accepts expected-utility theory as the correct account of rational choice under uncertainty should find very hard to reject.

Two objections keep resurfacing in the secondary literature I have been working through, and I cannot yet decide how much weight they deserve. The first, due to Amartya Sen, holds that the theorem trades on an equivocation between two senses of "utility": the purely formal, choice-theoretic sense fixed by the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, which is a number unique only up to a positive affine transformation for each individual and carries no interpersonal content whatsoever, and the substantive, Bentham-style notion of welfare that classical utilitarianism actually needs to sum across people. On Sen's reading, all the ethically interesting work is being done by an interpersonal-comparability assumption that the theorem itself never licenses, so what appears to be an unconditional proof of utilitarianism is, at best, a conditional one. The second objection, due to Peter Diamond's 1967 comment on Harsanyi's original paper, uses a simple example — two people with identical claims to a single indivisible good, compared under a policy of handing it to one of them outright versus a policy of assigning it by a fair coin toss — to show that any rule built purely from expected utilities computed ex ante, as Harsanyi's Pareto condition requires, is necessarily indifferent between the two policies, even though most people's fairness intuitions are not.

I would like you to write an essay that takes a definite side on the following question: does Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, despite these two objections, still give us good reason to accept some form of utilitarianism as the correct rule for aggregating individual interests into a social ordering? Please reconstruct the theorem's argument carefully enough to show exactly where each objection bites, or fails to bite, engage with at least one serious attempt in the literature to rescue Harsanyi's conclusion against them, and close with an explicit, argued verdict rather than a survey of considerations on both sides — I have read enough philosophy papers concluding that "the matter is more complicated than it first appears" and would like this one to actually commit to a position.
