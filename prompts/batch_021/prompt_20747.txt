I spent about thirty-five years running an analytical chemistry lab before I retired, and now that I've got time on my hands I've fallen down the philosophy of mind rabbit hole -- mostly perception and philosophy of science stuff. One thing that's been rattling around in my head for weeks is this "cognitive penetration of perception" debate. When I was working I always took it as basically obvious that observation is the neutral bedrock you build theories on top of -- sure, Hanson and Kuhn taught us observation is "theory-laden" in some loose sense, but I figured that was mostly about interpretation and attention, not that your beliefs and concepts could literally reach in and repaint the raw sensory feed itself. Now I'm reading Susanna Siegel and some of the empirical literature, and apparently there's a real live fight about whether that "repainting" happens at the level of perceptual experience itself, not just in judgments made downstream of it.

What's got me hooked is the empirical back-and-forth specifically. On one side you've got things like the old Delk & Fillenbaum "characteristic color" result, the banana/memory-colour matching study (Hansen, Olkkonen, Walter and Gegenfurtner, the one where the "achromatic" setting for a banana-shaped patch turns out to be slightly blue rather than grey), and the Levin & Banaji study where an identical grey face gets matched as darker when its categorized as Black rather than White -- all offered up as real cases of concepts or memory changing perception itself. On the other side there's Firestone and Scholl's big 2016 BBS target article arguing that pretty much every one of these effects can be explained away as eye movements, response bias, or "cognitive contamination" of the judgment/report rather than any real change in the percept, and that vision is a lot more modular, in Fodor's sense, than the old "New Look" tradition wants it to be. I mentioned this to an old colleague who's still active in vision research and she just laughed and said Firestone and Scholl basically reinvented Fodor's modularity thesis as a methodology paper.

So here's what I'd like: write me an essay that actually adjudicates this fight rather than just surveying it. Lay out the strongest version of the case for genuine cognitive penetration and the strongest version of the Firestone/Scholl-style skeptical case -- including whatever criteria you think are actually the right ones for telling "the experience changed" apart from "the judgment about the experience changed" -- and then come down on a side and defend it. I'll accept a qualified verdict, like "penetration is real for effects of kind X but the kind Y cases are all judgment artifacts," as long as its a real, falsifiable-ish claim and not just "well, it's complicated." If you can, say something too about what's actually at stake epistemologically if cognition really does turn out to penetrate perception -- does that threaten the old idea of perception as an independent check on belief, or not really.
