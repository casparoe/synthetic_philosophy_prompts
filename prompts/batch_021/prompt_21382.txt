Here's the question we gave them:

"In 'Freedom and Resentment,' Strawson argues that the truth or falsity of determinism is irrelevant to whether reactive attitudes like resentment and gratitude are ever appropriate. Reconstruct his argument. Be sure to explain (a) the participant/objective attitude distinction, and (b) how Strawson's own position differs from the 'optimists' and 'pessimists' he criticizes."

And here's the answer one of my students gave:

"Strawson separates the personal reactive attitudes -- resentment, gratitude, hurt feelings, and their self-directed cousins like guilt -- from the objective attitude, which is the stance we take toward someone we see as an object to be managed, treated, or handled rather than a person to hold responsible, e.g. small children or the severely mentally ill. The optimists think reactive attitudes and practices like punishment are justified by their good consequences, like deterrence and moral education, so for them determinism changes nothing. The pessimists reply that if determinism is true, consequences alone can't justify resentment or blame -- you need actual desert, and determinism rules that out. Strawson's own position is basically a more careful version of the optimist one: reactive attitudes are ultimately justified by the valuable social role they play in upholding moral norms, which is exactly why it wouldn't matter if determinism turned out to be true. He actually pushes this further than the optimists do: he points out that even setting determinism aside, real desert would require being the ultimate author of your own character, causa sui, which nothing can be, so the only notion of desert we're entitled to is the thin, socially useful one, not a metaphysically loaded one."
