In a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind, we spent a session on the Lucas–Penrose argument: the claim, originating with J. R. Lucas and developed further by Roger Penrose, that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show the human mind cannot be equivalent to, or fully captured by, any fixed algorithmic formal system. Afterward, two other students, Owen and Ines, kept arguing about it over coffee while I mostly listened; I wrote down the exchange as faithfully as I could once I got home, since neither of them is a professional logician but both clearly know the material well and were making real points rather than talking past each other. I'd like an outside read on who is right about what, since I don't trust my own judgment on the logic here.

The exchange went roughly like this. (1) Owen opened with the standard argument: for any consistent formal system F strong enough to represent arithmetic, Gödel gives us a sentence G(F) — equivalent to "F cannot prove this sentence" — which is true if F is consistent but which F cannot prove. A human who understands the construction and grants that F is consistent can simply see that G(F) is true, yet F cannot output G(F) as a theorem. Since this works for any F we might propose as a model of human mathematical reasoning, no such F is the whole story, so the mind can't just be an algorithm. (2) Ines objected that "seeing G(F) is true" is only as good as the assumption that F is consistent, and nobody has shown that any system actually proposed as a cognitive model is consistent, nor that human reasoning itself is consistent; if Owen is allowed to simply help himself to F's consistency, that isn't a display of reasoning power beyond F, it's just an extra assumption smuggled in that F was never permitted to use about itself. (3) Owen replied that granting F's consistency isn't smuggling anything in: if F is proposed as a model of a reliable mathematician's output, consistency is already built into that hypothesis, since an inconsistent F would sometimes assert falsehoods. Granting the hypothesis and then deriving G(F) is legitimate, not question-begging. (4) Ines answered that this doesn't help, because what F itself can prove is the conditional "if F is consistent, then G(F)" — essentially just the arithmetized incompleteness theorem, provable by F as readily as Owen can state it. The gap would only appear in the unconditional, categorical assertion of G(F), and producing that requires independently certifying F's consistency rather than just granting it for the sake of argument — which is exactly what's unearned. (5) Owen then reformulated the argument to be self-directed: suppose someone actually hands you a specific system F* and claims it's a complete model of your own mathematical reasoning. If you trust F* enough to rely on its outputs the way a competent mathematician would, you're thereby committed to trusting its consistency, and that commitment lets you derive G(F*), which F* cannot do — so F* wasn't complete after all. If instead you don't trust F*, then F* still isn't a complete model of your judgment, since refusing to trust it was the correct call. Either way, being shown a specific candidate seems to defeat it, sidestepping the abstract worry about unverified systems in general. (6) Ines granted this version was more interesting, but argued it trades a logical proof for a psychological wager: nothing forces the "trust" branch. Faced with an actual, humanly unsurveyable F*, the rational reaction might just be suspended judgment, which by Owen's own admission establishes nothing — so the whole argument now hinges on a speculative claim about how a mathematician would react to a system that has never actually been produced, which is a much shakier foundation for an anti-mechanist conclusion than a genuine proof.
