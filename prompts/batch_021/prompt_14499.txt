This semester I'm taking a seminar on classical and contemporary arguments for the existence of God, and we've just spent two sessions reconstructing Kurt Gödel's modal ontological argument from the version that became widely known through Dana Scott's notes. I can follow the derivation as far as its headline result: given axioms governing "positive" properties — that they are closed under strict implication, that exactly one of a property and its negation is positive, and that both being God-like and enjoying necessary existence count as positive — together with the S5 principle that whatever is possibly necessary is necessary, one can derive that a God-like being necessarily exists.

What I can't reconstruct on my own is the objection my professor raised only in passing: that J. Howard Sobel showed these very same axioms entail a full-blown "modal collapse," on which every actually true proposition turns out to be necessarily true — a result generally treated as fatal to the argument as Gödel left it, even though, I gather, there is some scholarly dispute over whether Gödel's own metaphysical sympathies would have left him less troubled by that consequence than most of his later readers have been.

Could you reconstruct, at the level of intuition rather than full formal notation, exactly where the contingency gets squeezed out of the system — which axiom, or combination of axioms, is doing the damage, rather than simply asserting that it happens? I would like the same treatment for C. Anthony Anderson's 1990 emendation, which, as I understand it, drops the half of Gödel's positivity axiom that made positivity exhaustive while strengthening the definition of "God-like" into a biconditional over necessary properties, so as to block Sobel's derivation without sacrificing the necessary-existence conclusion. Once you've walked me through both moves, I would also value your own assessment: does Anderson's revised argument still trade on whatever intuitive motivation Gödel's positive-property framework was supposed to have, or is it more honestly described as a technical patch that avoids modal collapse at the cost of whatever made the original argument philosophically interesting?
