The question was: Compare the Buddhist doctrine of anatta (no-self) with Derek Parfit's reductionist account of personal identity. Address (a) how each view treats the idea of a persisting self, (b) at least one argument or thought experiment each position relies on, and (c) one significant way the two views diverge despite their apparent similarity.

The response I'm grading:

"Both the Buddhist doctrine of anatta and Derek Parfit's reductionist account of personal identity deny that a person is a single, unchanging thing that persists through time. In Buddhist thought, what we call a 'person' is really just a name for five ever-changing aggregates, or skandhas (form, sensation, perception, mental formations, and consciousness), arising and passing away together through the process of dependent origination. The Buddha illustrated the point with the simile of the chariot, telling his disciples that a chariot is nothing more than a label we apply to wheels, axle, and frame arranged in a certain way, with no separate 'chariot' hiding behind the parts; a person, similarly, is only a label for a certain arrangement of aggregates. Because it denies both an eternal soul and the idea that persons simply do not exist at all, the doctrine is often described as a middle way between two extremes.

Parfit reaches a strikingly similar conclusion using very different tools. Drawing on thought experiments involving teleportation and the splitting of a person's brain between two bodies, he argues that personal identity itself just consists in psychological continuity and connectedness—what he calls Relation R. On his view, even in the more dramatic fission cases, the original person survives as both resulting people, since Relation R holds equally in each direction; identity is simply Relation R holding, however many times over. Parfit noted that reaching this reductionist conclusion left him with a new sense of kinship with Buddhist philosophy, since the Buddha appeared to have reached a similar conclusion some twenty-five centuries earlier.

The two views differ most clearly in emphasis: anatta functions primarily as a soteriological teaching, embedded in a broader path aimed at liberation from suffering, whereas Parfit arrives at his reductionist conclusion primarily as a metaphysical thesis about what personal identity consists in, only afterward drawing out its implications for how we might think about death and self-interest."
