So I've been chewing on Parfit's hitchhiker case from Reasons and Persons for a reading group -- you know, the guy dying of thirst in the desert who has to convince a driver he'll pay $100 once he's safe in town, even though once he's actually safe there's no self-interested reason left to hand the money over. Parfit uses this to motivate what he calls "rational irrationality": it can be rational to turn yourself into the kind of agent who will do something later that, when the moment comes, won't actually be rational by ordinary self-interest standards. Someone in the group insisted this is basically just Gauthier's constrained maximization from Morals by Agreement wearing a different hat -- the idea that you should dispose yourself to comply with agreements (be a "constrained maximizer") even when defecting is locally utility-maximizing, because having that disposition is what gets you included in mutually beneficial deals among agents who's dispositions are somewhat detectable ("translucent") rather than fully hidden from one another.

Can you actually lay out how these two ideas relate rather than just asserting they're the same thing? Like, is rational irrationality just constrained maximization shrunk down to a single-agent, single-shot case, or is there a real structural difference between what Parfit needs for the hitchhiker case to work and what Gauthier needs to make constrained maximization a stable disposition across a whole population of agents? And once you've hashed that out, tell me which of the two you think gives the more convincing account of why it can be rational to bind your future self to something that self won't (at the time) want to do.
