I've been down a bit of a rabbit hole lately reading about really old logic puzzles and paradoxes - Zeno's arrow, the Liar, that kind of thing - and it eventually led me to this ancient Chinese guy called Gongsun Long, who was part of a group called the School of Names, something like 2300 years ago. Everyone who talks about him brings up the same bit from his "white horse" dialogue, the "a white horse is not a horse" line, where the trick is supposedly that "horse" names a shape and "white" names a color, so they can't be the same thing. Ok fine, I sort of get that one, it just seems like a cute point about what a word is pointing at.

But then I dug up a longer version of the text with a second argument stapled onto the same dialogue, and this one I genuinely can't get my head around. Translated roughly, it goes something like this: "If you are looking for a horse, a yellow one or a black one will do. But if you are looking for a white horse, a yellow one or a black one will not do. Now suppose a white horse really were the same as a horse - then what is being looked for in both cases would be the same thing, and 'white' would add nothing to it. Yet a yellow or black horse counts as an answer in one case and not in the other, and 'counts as an answer' and 'does not count as an answer' can't both be true of the same thing. So a yellow horse and a black horse are alike in that both can count as 'there is a horse' but neither can count as 'there is a white horse.' Therefore it is clear that a white horse is not a horse."

I brought this up with a friend of mine who actually reads philosophy properly, and she said this second bit is doing something logically different from the shape/color argument - something about confusing classes with individual things, not just a point about what "white" and "horse" refer to. My own instinct is that its just a trick about how the verb "look for" works, like, of course you need different things to satisfy different requests, that doesn't automatically mean the requests are about two unrelated categories of thing. But I don't really trust my own instinct here since I've never studied logic formally. Could you walk me through step by step what's actually happening logically in this "seeking a horse" bit? Specifically: is it really a separate argument from the shape/color one, or basically the same move wearing a different coat? Does my "it's just about the word look for" objection actually hold up, or is it missing something? And is my friend onto something real with this classes-vs-individuals idea, and if so where exactly does that mix-up happen in the argument? I'd like to actually understand the mechanics here rather than just get told that ancient Chinese logicians liked being tricky.
