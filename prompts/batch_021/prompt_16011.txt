So I'm a few years into a PhD in history and philosophy of logic, and something that happened at reading group a couple weeks ago has been rattling around in my head since. Quick background in case it's not familiar: near the end of his life (I think it's from 1924, so Frege would've been about 76) he kept a private diary that, once it finally got published decades after his death, turned out to be pretty ugly -- virulently anti-semitic, hostile to the Weimar constitution and to parliamentary democracy generally, wanting some kind of strongman rule instead. Apparently it's not just one bad aside either, there's a fair bit of it. Even Dummett, who basically built his career on rehabilitating and explicating Frege, has said on record that finding out about it definately disturbed him. Anyway, two people in my cohort, Lena and Owen, got into a real argument at the pub afterward about whether any of this should matter for how we assess Frege's actual philosophy -- the logicism, sense and reference, the anti-psychologism stuff -- and I keep replaying it and can't settle who's right.

(1) Lena: obviously irrelevant. The context principle is either defensible or it isn't, the sense/reference distinction either earns its keep or it doesn't, and none of that shifts one inch depending on what horrible stuff the man privately believed in his 70s. Judging the Grundgesetze by the diary is a textbook genetic fallacy, like refusing to use a theorem because you hate the mathematician who proved it.
(2) Owen: he's not saying the logic gets invalidated, but "bears on his philosophy" doesn't only mean "changes the truth value of some proposition." Frege's whole case against psychologism is built on a picture of logic as a rigid, objective order that has to be defended against contamination by messy, contingent, individual human psychology -- and it's at least worth noticing that this is a guy who also wanted a rigid social order defended against "contamination" by the wrong people getting a political say. That doesn't refute the logic, but historians of philosophy lean on biography like this constantly to explain a thinker's emphases and blind spots.
(3) Lena: that "rhyme" is cheap and runs in any direction you like -- if Frege had been a committed egalitarian instead, you'd just as happily say his belief in universal, impersonal logical laws rhymed with a belief in everyone's equal rational standing. You can gin up a resonance between a theory and any set of political commitments after the fact, so noticing one particular resonance isn't evidence of anything in particular.
(4) Owen: fine, the rhyme alone doesn't show the logic is wrong, but he wasn't only gesturing at a vibe -- the actual language Frege reaches for against psychologism (purity, corruption, defending the objective against the subjective) is the same language that shows up in the diary, just aimed at people instead of propositions. That's a textual link, not merely an analogy, and it should at least change how charitably we read some of the more heated anti-psychologism passages, even while the formal results stand untouched.
