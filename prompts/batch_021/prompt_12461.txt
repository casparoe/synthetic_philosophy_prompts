so etiological (selected-effects) theories say a trait's function is whatever effect it was selected for. the big selling point of this over Cummins-style causal-role theories is supposed to be that causal-role functions are hopelessly liberal -- pick whatever system and capacity you like and pretty much anything counts as a function (on a causal-role story the heart's "function" could just as well be making thumping noises). but etiological theory has its own classic headache: traits that get co-opted for a new use partway through their evolutionary history. insect wings are the standard example -- the exaptation story says they started out small and mainly helped with thermoregulation, only becoming big enough to matter for flight later, at which point flight became the actual reason they stuck around. Godfrey-Smith's patch for this ('modern history theory') is to say function tracks an organism's *recent* selective history rather than a trait's ultimate origin, so wings get to count as being for flying now, even though thermoregulation is what got them started. can you argue that this patch doesn't actually save etiological theory from the arbitrariness charge it loves to press against Cummins -- i.e. that it just relocates the arbitrariness into 'how far back does recent have to go,' which seems no more principled than any choice of system/capacity a causal-role theorist has to make.
