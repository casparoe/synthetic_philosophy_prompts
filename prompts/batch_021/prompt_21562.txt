I sit on my law school's classroom climate committee, and we've got a genuinely annoying case to resolve before next week's faculty meeting. One of our employment discrimination profs was teaching a hostile-work-environment opinion, the kind where the court spells out, letter for letter, the exact slur a supervisor used on the plaintiff, because the whole point of the opinion is that courts have sometimes treated a single use of that particular word by a supervisor as severe enough on its own to establish liability, when a milder insult would have to be repeated a bunch of times before it added up to the same thing. He read the quoted passage out loud exactly as written. A student complained afterward that hearing it said in class reproduced the harm no matter what he "meant" by it, and asked that instructors be required to say "the n-word" instead whenever they hit one of these passages. The prof's response is that euphemizing it guts the actual legal point (that this word is doing distinct, heavier work than an ordinary epithet), and that reading a quotation into the record just isn't the same speech act as using a slur on someone.

This is basically what my friend Mara and I were arguing about til like 1am on Sunday, and neither of us budged an inch. She kept insisting a slur's offensiveness doesn't come from what the speaker is asserting or implying at all, that the word is more or less a banned item in the language, so saying it out loud is what keeps it doing damage, even when you're citing it to condemn the underlying conduct, not despite the citing. I kept coming back to reclaimed use as the counterexample: if the word were just radioactive no matter who says it or why, a Black comedian using it about himself should land exactly as bad as a supervisor screaming it at an employee, and it obviously doesn't, so something about who's speaking and for what purpose has to be doing semantic or pragmatic work after all. We never settled where "quoting a court record for evidentiary purposes" actually sits between those two poles, and honestly that's the real question our committee has to answer, not just "is this guy racist."

Our handbook just says instructors should use "professional judgement" with offensive material and doesnt say what that means, which is exactly the gap this fell into. Could you draft the committee's actual decision memo? I need a real recommendation, not a survey of the debate -- something like: never read these words aloud and always substitute "the n-word," allow it but only under specific conditions (advance notice, an opt-out, whatever), or leave it to instructor discretion with some kind of guardrail attached. Give it a timeframe for when it'd kick in, and whichever way you land, actually engage with the strongest argument on the losing side instead of just gesturing at it -- that's the part our last draft completely botched.
