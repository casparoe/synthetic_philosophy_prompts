After a career as a research chemist, I have spent much of my retirement reading the major philosophers directly rather than relying on the potted summaries I had picked up over the years. One discovery surprised me: George Berkeley, whom I had known only for his doctrine that objects exist solely in being perceived, devoted three pamphlets collectively titled The Querist to monetary policy, where he argued that money is essentially a conventional measure of credit and exchange rather than a store of intrinsic value in precious metal, and advocated a national bank that would issue paper currency for a cash-starved Ireland. Given that his own monetary theory already treated money as closer to a mutually sustained idea than to a material substance, how do you think Berkeley would have assessed a currency such as Bitcoin, which dispenses with any national bank or sovereign guarantor in favor of a fixed, algorithmically enforced supply? Please ground your answer in the specific economic and civic concerns he actually raises in The Querist, rather than treating Bitcoin as simply the logical endpoint of his immaterialism.
