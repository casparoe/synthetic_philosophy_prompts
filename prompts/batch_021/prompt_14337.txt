My younger brother has bipolar I disorder, and after his third manic-episode hospitalization this year, he asked me to help him draft what's sometimes called a "Ulysses contract": a psychiatric advance directive in which he, while stable, pre-authorizes his doctors to override any refusal of treatment his manic self might later voice. Working through it with him sent me back to Jefferson's 1789 letter to Madison, where he argues that "the earth belongs in usufruct to the living" and that no generation has the standing to bind its successors to debts or laws they never agreed to — he even calculates, from mortality tables, that a generation's natural span is something like nineteen years. I can't stop relating that argument to what my brother just did to himself, since this isn't one generation binding the next but one self binding a future self who will, when it matters most, sincerely want out of the very arrangement his earlier self designed. Would Jefferson see those as the same problem in different guise, or is there a principled reason his usufruct argument against intergenerational precommitment wouldn't touch a person's right to pre-bind their own future will? I'd like your best reconstruction of how he would actually reason it through, from his premises rather than his conclusions, since this is exactly the sort of case he never had to face.
