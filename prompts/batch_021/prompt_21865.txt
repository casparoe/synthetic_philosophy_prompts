I sit on the small committee that administers my philosophy department's endowed public lecture in logic and philosophy of language, named decades ago the Frege Lecture. At our last meeting a colleague circulated passages from a diary Frege kept in 1924 and never published in his lifetime; it only became widely known within philosophy decades later, not least through Michael Dummett's own dismayed reaction to it. In it, Frege argues that Jews should be stripped of full political rights, heaps scorn on parliamentary democracy, and blames Germany's postwar troubles on Jews, Catholics, and the political left. That evening I had a long argument with a friend who thinks this settles the matter and the name should go. I found myself defending the opposite view — that the sense/reference distinction and the Begriffsschrift stand on their own logical merits, untouched by their author's politics — until she pointed out that Frege's decades-long campaign against psychologism rested precisely on treating logical laws as objective and binding on every thinker regardless of psychology or circumstance, which sits oddly beside a diary insisting some citizens' standing should depend on ancestry. The committee is weighing three options: keep the name, retire it quietly, or keep it alongside a permanent framing statement read before each lecture. Please draft the short memo I'll circulate before we vote in three weeks, recommending one option with reasons and engaging honestly with the strongest case for whichever option it sets aside.
