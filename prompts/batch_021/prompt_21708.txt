Question 3 (20 points). The dogmatism paradox, in the form Kripke originally presented it and Harman later discussed in print, can be set out as follows:

(1) S knows that p.
(2) If S knows that p, then S knows that any evidence e that would apparently disconfirm p is misleading evidence.
(3) If S knows in advance that some piece of evidence, should it arise, would be misleading, then S is entitled to disregard that evidence, without examining it, when it arises.
(4) Therefore, S is entitled, in advance, to disregard any future evidence against p without examining it.

(a) Which principles about the nature of knowledge — factivity, epistemic closure, the KK thesis — does this argument actually need in order to go through, and which merely resemble principles at work in other, superficially similar epistemic puzzles, but are not required here? (8 points)

(b) The defeasibility analysis of knowledge (Lehrer and Paxson; Klein) is often presented as a response to this paradox. State the analysis and explain precisely which step above it blocks, and why. (8 points)

(c) In two or three sentences, illustrate with an example from empirical inquiry why conclusion (4) should worry us even though each individual step looks plausible on its own. (4 points)

Student's answer:

The argument trades on the factivity of knowledge: since S knows p, p must be true, so genuine evidence against a truth cannot exist, and any apparent counter-evidence is therefore misleading. It also needs a closure principle, since S must be able to move from knowing p to knowing the further, distinct proposition that any counter-evidence is misleading — the same kind of single-premise closure that does the work in ordinary sceptical arguments about brains in vats. Crucially, the argument also depends on the KK thesis, that if S knows p then S knows that S knows p, since without iterating knowledge in this way S is never entitled to treat her own epistemic position as secure enough to license ignoring future evidence sight-unseen. This is why most contemporary treatments, following Williamson's rejection of KK in Knowledge and Its Limits, simply deny KK and take the paradox to dissolve.

The defeasibility analysis offers a different diagnosis. On this view, S knows p just in case S has a true, justified belief that p and there is some future item of evidence against p that would, were it to arise, defeat that justification — so that knowledge is inherently provisional, held only until better evidence turns up. This blocks step (2), because it shows that "knowing" p never really licenses treating future counter-evidence as merely apparent: since a defeater might always be coming, S's justification is never more than temporary, and step (2) illegitimately treats it as settled.

A working example: a researcher convinced her hypothesis is correct might, on this argument, feel entitled to write off anomalous data as noise before even looking at it — exactly the complacency that blind-analysis protocols in experimental physics exist to rule out.
