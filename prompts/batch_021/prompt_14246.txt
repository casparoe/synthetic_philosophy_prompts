(a) Explain the core claim of Rosenthal's higher-order-thought (HOT) theory of consciousness: what, on this view, makes a mental state a conscious state, and why does the theory locate a state's phenomenal character in the content of the higher-order thought rather than in the first-order state itself?

(b) Higher-order thoughts can misrepresent their targets: a subject might token a HOT to the effect that she is in pain when no pain state is actually present. Explain why misrepresenting, "targetless" HOTs of this kind are thought to pose a problem for Rosenthal's theory, and explain how Rosenthal responds to the problem.

(c) Rosenthal's theory is standardly classified as an "actualist" HOT theory and contrasted with Peter Carruthers's "dispositionalist" HOT theory. Explain this contrast, and explain how Rosenthal's view is supposed to block the threat of an infinite regress of ever-higher-order conscious states.

And here, in full, is the student's answer:

(a) On Rosenthal's view, a mental state is a conscious state just in case the subject has, at roughly the same time, a higher-order thought to the effect that she is in that very state, where the thought is not arrived at by any conscious inference or observation -- it simply seems to the subject, immediately, that she is in the state. Because the qualitative character we associate with an experience is just how the state is represented as being by the HOT, two subjects could be in physically identical first-order states while only one of them is having a conscious experience, if only one of them has the relevant HOT; conversely, an unconscious pain is still a pain in every first-order respect, it is simply not a state anyone is aware of being in, so there is nothing it is like to have it.

(b) If a subject tokens a HOT representing her as being in pain but there is no actual pain state for the HOT to be about, Rosenthal's theory seems forced to say that the subject is having a conscious experience of pain even though there is no pain there to be conscious of, which sounds incoherent, since it seems to posit a conscious quality dangling free of anything that has that quality. Rosenthal's response is that this is no more paradoxical than any other case of misrepresentation: perceptual systems routinely represent things that are not there, as in afterimages or phantom-limb pain, and from the inside such a misrepresentation is exactly as vivid as an accurate representation would be. Since the conscious quality of an experience is, on this theory, constituted by how the HOT characterizes things rather than by some further fact about the first-order state, a targetless HOT will make it seem to the subject exactly as if she is in pain, and that seeming is all "conscious pain" was ever supposed to consist in.

(c) Rosenthal's theory is best described as dispositionalist: what makes a first-order state conscious is not that a HOT actually occurs, but that the subject is disposed to token one -- a HOT would occur if her attention were suitably directed at the state, even if none occurs at the moment. This dispositional structure is exactly what lets Rosenthal stop the regress of higher and higher HOTs: since we do not, as a rule, introspect our own thoughts, the second-order HOT is itself never an actually occurring conscious state, only a standing disposition, so there is no need for a third-order thought to make it conscious in turn, and the hierarchy terminates automatically after a single step. Carruthers, by contrast, holds the actualist view, on which the relevant HOT must actually be tokened, and not merely be available, for the target state to count as conscious -- which is why he is committed to the higher-order machinery running constantly in the background even when we are not introspecting anything.
