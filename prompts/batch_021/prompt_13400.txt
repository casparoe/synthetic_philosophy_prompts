For my seminar on the metaphysics of space and time, we just spent two weeks on the Leibniz–Clarke correspondence read alongside Newton's bucket argument from the Scholium to the Principia. What stuck with me most was Leibniz's "shift argument": his claim that if the entire material universe were reflected east-to-west, or bodily shifted a few feet in some direction, or the whole of creation had simply been started a few years later, while every relation among bodies (and every relation among events) stayed exactly the same, nothing would be observably different. By the principle of sufficient reason and the identity of indiscernibles, Leibniz concludes, these cannot count as genuinely distinct possibilities — so space and time cannot be the independent, substantival containers that Clarke (channeling Newton) needed them to be in order to make sense of the bucket's rotating water.

Here's what I didn't appreciate until the following lecture: essentially the same argument resurfaces, dressed in differential geometry, as Earman and Norton's "hole argument" against substantivalism about the spacetime of general relativity — and the standard way of blocking its threatened indeterminism is literally called "Leibniz equivalence" in the philosophy of physics literature, i.e., the stipulation that two models related by a diffeomorphism (which agree everywhere except on how fields are smeared across some "hole") represent one and the same physical possibility. Substantivalists who don't want to fold so quickly turn to what's come to be called "sophisticated substantivalism" (associated above all with Butterfield and Brighouse), which tries to grant Leibniz equivalence while still denying that spacetime points reduce to the matter located at them. A friend in my seminar insists that once you show Leibniz the actual mathematics, he'd basically just become a sophisticated substantivalist, and that the label "Leibniz equivalence" would embarrass him more than flatter him; I'm not so sure, and it's been bothering me since.

So I'd like your help settling it: if you sat Leibniz down and carefully explained the hole argument, general relativity's spacetime manifold, and the sophisticated-substantivalist rejoinders to it, would he recognize "Leibniz equivalence" as simply his own shift argument coming home, and embrace it without reservation — or would other commitments of his system (the identity of indiscernibles applied to "thin," qualitatively bare manifold points; his insistence that spatial and temporal relations must ultimately be grounded in the perceptions of actual monads rather than in free-floating geometric structure; his lifelong ambivalence about treating merely mathematical continua, like infinitesimals, as fully real) pull him toward a more qualified verdict, or even toward rejecting the whole hole-argument framework as posing the wrong kind of question? Please don't hedge with something like "he'd probably sympathize in spirit" — reconstruct the specific argumentative pressure points and commit to where you think he would actually land.
