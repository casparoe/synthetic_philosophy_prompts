For years, in my philosophy courses, I have calibrated incoming teaching assistants by having them grade a shared stack of old practice essays alongside me, telling them essay by essay whether their grade matched mine -- a method that seemed like a perfectly sound way to pass on good judgment about argumentative quality. Grading on their own now, several of my most successful former TAs -- the ones who matched me most closely during that process, and who are fully confident they are applying exactly what I taught them -- have converged on the same tell: they reward any essay that anticipates an objection with the stock phrase "one might object that...", even when nothing substantive follows it, and mark down essays that handle objections without using that phrase. It turns out the strong essays in my original stack did use that phrase constantly, simply because strong essays tend to anticipate objections; the correlation was genuinely there, I just never meant to be teaching anyone to detect the phrase itself rather than the underlying skill. Was my criterion simply underspecified, such that a clearer rubric would have headed this off, or could something like this happen even with a perfectly good criterion? And if it's the latter, given that I can't know in advance which incidental features of any given practice stack will turn out to be misleading, what would actually correcting it require?
