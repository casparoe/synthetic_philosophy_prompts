Within the literature on moral responsibility, a blamer's "standing to blame" is usually treated as a condition that can fail independently of whether the blame is actually warranted on the merits -- that is, independently of whether the target really did something wrong. The paradigm case is hypocrisy: if I reproach you for cutting in line while routinely doing the same myself, most treatments of the topic agree that something has gone wrong with my blaming you, even though my accusation may be perfectly accurate. G. A. Cohen's discussion of who is entitled to "throw the first stone" and R. Jay Wallace's account of hypocrisy as a violation of the equal standing of persons both try to explain this phenomenon, but, like most of the literature that follows them, they tend to treat hypocrisy -- together, perhaps, with complicity in the very wrong being condemned -- as more or less the whole story about why standing gets lost.

I'd like to test how much of the phenomenon that leaves out. Construct a single, detailed scenario involving a critic who (a) has never done, and would never do, the thing they are condemning, and (b) is not complicit in, and does not benefit from, that particular wrong -- and yet where the intuition that the critic lacks standing to voice the blame still feels robust, driven by some further fact about the critic's relationship (or lack of one) to the wrongdoer or to the person wronged. Spell out exactly which feature of the scenario is doing the work of stripping the critic of standing despite (a) and (b) holding, and then say what your example shows about whether "lacking standing to blame" can be reduced to a consistency-with-one's-own-conduct requirement at all, or whether it names a more heterogeneous cluster of conditions that hypocrisy-centered accounts don't obviously capture.
