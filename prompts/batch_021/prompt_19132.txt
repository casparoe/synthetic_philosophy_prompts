So this is a weirdly specific thing to want philosophy help with, but here goes. I got engaged in October, and ever since, my fiancé's brother — who's doing a PhD in behavioral ecology, something to do with fiddler crab claws — has made it his whole personality to justify literally every expensive wedding decision with "the handicap principle." Ring too expensive? "That's the point, the cost is what makes it an honest signal, Zahavi basically proved this." Venue, photographer, even the guest welcome bags, same line everytime, and now his mom has started parroting it back at me too like its settled science. I got annoyed enough that I spent a couple of late nights actually reading up on Zahavi and Grafen and the ESS models instead of just saying "that's not how weddings work, Kevin."

And I think I've found the actual hole in his reasoning, I just can't get it into proper argument shape on my own. My understanding of Grafen's formal version is that what keeps a signal honest is a difference in marginal cost across quality types (a single-crossing type condition), not high absolute cost — and that at equilibrium the honest signaler, especially a high-quality one, can end up paying very little, because it's the cost a low-quality faker would have to pay that does the deterring, not any cost actually incurred on the path of play. If I've got that right, "the whole point is that it's expensive" gets Zahavi almost exactly backwards. Could you build me the strongest version of that argument — something that actually works through the ESS/marginal-cost logic instead of just asserting it, and that also deals with whether human status spending is even the right kind of case for this (an actual handicap, versus something closer to one of Maynard Smith and Harper's "conventional signals," propped up by social sanction rather than intrinsic cost)? I want it sharp enough that next time Kevin says "handicap principle" at Sunday dinner I've got more than a shrug.
