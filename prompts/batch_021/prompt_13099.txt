**Question 3.** Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument purports to show that non-reductive physicalism about the mind is an unstable position: unless mental properties are reduced to physical properties, mental causation turns out to be illusory.

(a) [10 points] Lay out the argument's key premises — the causal closure of the physical domain, the causal exclusion (non-overdetermination) principle, and the claim that mental properties are both distinct from and dependent on (supervenient on) physical properties — and show step by step how they generate the dilemma non-reductive physicalists face.

(b) [8 points] Multiple realizability was originally invoked by Putnam and Fodor to motivate exactly the kind of non-reductive, autonomous mental properties Kim's argument targets. Explain that original motivation, and then explain why Kim thinks multiple realizability fails to secure the irreducibility it was supposed to guarantee.

(c) [7 points] Kim's own preferred way of softening the dilemma is what he calls functional reduction. Explain how this proposal is supposed to preserve mental causation, and identify the one class of mental property that Kim thinks resists this treatment, and why.

Here is the student's answer, verbatim:

(a) The argument rests on three ingredients. First, causal closure: every physical event that has a cause at all has a sufficient physical cause at that time, so physics never needs to look outside itself for causes. Second, the exclusion principle: barring genuine overdetermination (like a firing squad where every bullet is individually lethal), no event has two distinct sufficient causes at once. Third, non-reductive physicalism's own commitments: mental properties are not identical to physical properties (that's the "non-reductive" part), but every instance of a mental property is had in virtue of, or supervenes on, some physical property (that's the "physicalism" part) — so whenever a mental property M is instantiated, there is automatically some physical property P instantiated too, sufficient by closure to produce whatever physical effects follow. Now suppose M causes a physical event, such as pain causing a wince. The wince already has a sufficient physical cause, the physical realizer P of the pain. Since M and P are numerically distinct properties (premise three) and this is obviously not a case of two independent causes luckily converging the way a firing squad is, the exclusion principle says only one of them gets to count as the cause of the wince, and given that physics is closed, physical goings-on don't need any help from a separate mental cause. So the mental property is squeezed out: either it turns out to be nothing over and above the physical property after all (reductionism), or, if it's really distinct, then it is epiphenomenal, causally inert — and Kim's argument thereby shows that eliminativists like the Churchlands were right all along that there are no such things as beliefs, desires, and pains, just as it turned out there were no such things as phlogiston or witches.

(b) Putnam and later Fodor pointed out that a single mental kind like pain can be realized by all sorts of different physical substrates — C-fibers in humans, whatever the analogous structure is in an octopus's nervous system, and in principle silicon circuits in a robot — so pain can't be identical to any one of these physical kinds without arbitrarily privileging one realizer over the others. This multiple realizability was supposed to show that psychology is "autonomous": its kinds cut across physical kinds and can't be reduced to them, which is exactly the non-reductive, dependent-but-distinct picture Kim's argument is aimed at. Kim's response is that multiple realizability actually cuts the other way: if pain in humans, octopuses, and robots is realized by physically very different properties, the most that is warranted is a series of local reductions — human pain to the human realizer, octopus pain to the octopus realizer, and so on — because the disjunction of all these heterogeneous realizers isn't the kind of unified, projectible property that could figure in causal laws. So rather than blocking reduction, multiple realizability just means we should expect reduction species-by-species or structure-by-structure instead of one grand psychophysical law.

(c) Functional reduction works by first defining a mental property in causal-role terms — pain, say, is whatever occupies the role of being typically caused by tissue damage and typically causing withdrawal, guarding, and reports of hurting — and then identifying that role-property, in a given system, with whatever physical property actually occupies the role there. Once this move is made, the mental property's causal powers just are the physical realizer's causal powers, so there are no longer two competing sufficient causes to exclude; there is one cause, describable at two levels. Kim takes this to rehabilitate mental causation quite generally, and he extends it even to phenomenal, qualitative states — the felt, subjective quality of pain, and not just its causal role — since these too, he argues, can be defined by their typical causes and effects and then paired off with whichever neural state realizes them in us, closing off any residual gap between the physical and the phenomenal.
