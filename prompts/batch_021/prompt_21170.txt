On rationalist-adjacent forums, Kelly betting has attained something like doctrinal status: in threads about prediction-market wagers, EA grant sizing, or personal bankroll management, someone can be relied upon to insist that the Kelly fraction is simply "the" rational bet size, full stop, as though this followed from probability theory alone rather than from any assumption about what the bettor wants. I recall a LessWrong post, or perhaps a comment thread, arguing that this instinct is basically right — that Kelly betting doesn't actually presuppose log utility — which surprised me, because the textbook derivation I had always seen simply shows that maximizing expected log wealth reproduces the Kelly formula, which makes Kelly betting look like ordinary expected-utility maximization for an agent who happens to have logarithmic utility: a narrow and, if you credit Samuelson's decades-long campaign against the "geometric mean" camp (he was irritated enough to write at least one rebuttal almost entirely in one-syllable words), rather arbitrary assumption to elevate into "the" rational choice. The ergodicity-economics literature associated with Ole Peters tries to recover something closer to Kelly's own original, utility-free framing by redescribing the issue as one about ergodicity and time-averages in multiplicative wealth processes rather than about risk preferences — a move now sometimes discussed under the banner of "geometric rationality," as though it amounted to a distinct, more physically grounded notion of what rational betting even is.

I would like you to compare these two ways of underwriting Kelly-style behavior: the orthodox story, on which Kelly betting is nothing more than expected-utility maximization under log utility and is therefore only as compelling as that utility assumption; and the ergodicity-economics story, on which time-average growth-rate maximization is forced on any rational agent by the dynamics of the wealth process itself, regardless of their utility function's curvature. Are these genuinely distinct normative theories, capable of issuing conflicting advice in some real decision, or is the second just the first wearing a physicist's coat? If you think a real divergence exists, construct the specific bet, portfolio, or insurance decision in which the two would disagree, and say which one gets it right; if you think no divergence exists, explain what — if anything — is actually purchased by redescribing the problem in terms of ergodicity rather than utility curvature.
