I've been circulating the opening chapter of my new novel through my writing group, and a disagreement broke out over a device I use there. The chapter follows a woman named Marta through an unremarkable morning, and partway through, the narration steps outside the scene to say: "She did not yet know that the argument she was about to have with her brother would end their partnership for good." Later in the same chapter, in real time, Marta agonizes for pages over what to say to him, evidently free to choose her words.

Ines argued that the flash-forward and the agonizing scene can't both be doing honest work: if the narrator can already truly say the fight "would end their partnership for good," that's settled before Marta opens her mouth, which makes her later deliberation decorative rather than real. Marcus replied that this is the fatalist's classic slip: from "it's true the fight will end things" all you can validly infer is that it will, in fact, end things, not that it's already unavoidable that it will — and that's perfectly compatible with the fight ending things precisely because of what she freely chooses to say. Ines answered that this isn't a report made after the fact, which is the whole appeal of the device: it's asserted at a point where, by the story's own chronology, nothing has happened yet, and that's just Aristotle's old sea-battle worry — if it's already true today that there will be a battle tomorrow, doesn't today already have to contain whatever makes that so? Marcus said that only follows if truth needs something sitting in the present to make it true; on a four-dimensionalist view of time, which every novel already assumes by existing whole on the shelf before anyone reaches chapter one, the future event can simply be its own truth-maker. Ines pointed out that, on one long-standing reading, Aristotle took this worry seriously enough that he gave up treating every future-tense statement as simply true or false, rather than concede that the battle was already settled a day early. Marcus answered that most logicians since have kept that principle intact and blocked the argument at the earlier move instead, from "will" to "must," since giving it up causes worse problems than it solves.

Neither budged, and I don't know the material well enough to referee it myself. Could you go through the exchange move by move, say who has the better of it at each point, and flag anything you think both of them are missing?
