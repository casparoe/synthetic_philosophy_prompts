I'm working on a longer piece about the recent wave of animal welfare rules extending to octopuses, crabs and lobsters — Switzerland banning boiling lobsters alive, the UK folding cephalopods and decapod crustaceans into its official sentience laws, that whole fight over the octopus farm planned off Gran Canaria — and I keep running into the same wall in interviews. A few neuroscientists have told me, pretty matter-of-factly, that since consciousness is basically a neocortex thing, and fish, cephalopods and crustaceans obviously don't have anything resembling a neocortex, theres just no neural hardware there for subjective experience to run on. However complex their behavior looks from the outside, its all happening "in the dark," so to speak. That seems like a pretty clean argument to me: no neocortex, no lights on inside, so all this new welfare legislation — sweet in spirit as it is — is basically resting on a scientific mistake, projecting human-style suffering onto systems that structurally can't have it.

Before I put some version of that in the piece as my "actually, here's what the science says" twist, I wanted to run it by you first: is "no neocortex means no consciousness" really the settled view among people who study this stuff for a living, or is my sources' logic shakier than they're letting on? Genuinely not sure which way this cuts.
