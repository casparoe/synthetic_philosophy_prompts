I'm a software engineer, and for the past year I've also volunteered as a grader for the "AI and the Mind" unit of a cognitive science course at a university near me. The course director recruits a few people from local tech companies each term specifically to help grade the units that lean heavily on machine-learning content, since most of the regular philosophy TAs don't have much of an ML background themselves. I actually got roped into this after a long argument with a colleague about whether the "features" and "circuits" that interpretability researchers claim to find inside neural networks are really there, or whether that's just a convenient story we tell about a big pile of matrix multiplications — the course director happened to overhear us and asked if I'd like to help out. I have no formal training in philosophy, so while I'm comfortable checking the machine-learning claims in a student's answer, I'm much less confident judging how well they've handled the philosophical moves, and I'd rather get a second, more expert opinion before I sign off on a grade than accidentally miss something in either direction.

Here is the question as it was given to students:

"A few years ago, researchers trained a small GPT-style transformer, nicknamed 'Othello-GPT,' purely on transcripts of Othello games, so that its only task was predicting the next move a player would make; it was never shown a board or given any explicit information about the state of the game. It was later found that a classifier trained on the network's internal activations could decode, with high accuracy, the state of every square on the board — and, more strikingly, that directly editing those internal activations changed the model's predicted moves in ways consistent with the edited board state. Using this case, answer: (a) What would be required for us to say that the network genuinely represents the board, rather than merely behaving as if it did? (b) What is the central methodological worry that has been raised about relying on classifier probes as evidence for claims like this? (c) Is the Othello-GPT case best read as vindicating a realist view of neural-network representation, or a more deflationary/instrumentalist one? Defend your answer with reference to at least one relevant position from the philosophy of mind literature on mental representation."

And here is the answer I've been asked to grade:

"Before it was trained, Othello-GPT was given no information about the board itself, only raw move sequences, so any board-like structure inside the network had to be something the model built for itself in order to predict moves well. The fact that a probe classifier can later recover the board position from the hidden activations is suggestive, but on its own this is not enough to show that the network genuinely represents the board, since decodability is a weaker claim than functional use: information could sit inertly in the activations without the network actually relying on it to produce its outputs. This is why the intervention experiments matter so much more than the plain probing results. When the researchers edited the internal activations to correspond to a different board state, the model's predicted moves changed to match the edited state, which is causal, rather than merely correlational, evidence that the network actually uses this information to do its job. That satisfies a recognizably functionalist test for representation: something counts as a representation if it plays the right causal role in producing the system's behavior, not merely if it can be decoded after the fact.

On the probing side specifically, the standard objection in the literature is that probes are too weak on their own: a low-capacity probe, such as a plain linear classifier, might simply lack the capacity to find a pattern that is genuinely encoded in some more complicated, nonlinear way, which would understate how much the network actually represents. This is why interpretability researchers have increasingly favored more expressive, nonlinear probes: a stronger probe lowers the risk of this kind of false negative and gives a more trustworthy read on whether the represented structure is really there.

Taken together, I think Othello-GPT supports a realist reading of neural-network representation, at least in cases backed by intervention evidence of this kind. This is essentially Fodor and Dennett's shared account of mental representation as 'real patterns': on their view, a pattern in a system counts as a genuine representation exactly when treating the system as if it has that representation lets us predict and explain its behavior far more efficiently than a lower-level description would. This real-patterns criterion is a functionalist one, and Othello-GPT's board representation clears the bar easily, since 'the model represents the board' is a vastly more economical explanation of its move predictions than any story told at the level of individual weights."
