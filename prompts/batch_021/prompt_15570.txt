For a piece on heart-disease prevention, I ran my own numbers — age, cholesterol, blood pressure, smoking status — through three cardiovascular risk calculators, each endorsed by a different major medical association and built on a different long-term population study. The estimates didn't just differ at the margins: one put my ten-year risk low enough that a doctor wouldn't mention medication, while another placed me squarely in statin territory. When I asked the researchers behind each tool which figure was actually right for me, each defended their own model as the best-validated option for "a patient like me," with no one able to explain why an equally reputable model landed somewhere so different. It feels like there should be one true fact about my own risk, but the closer I look, the more that number seems to depend on which group of strangers I've been statistically lumped in with. Is one of these calculators simply wrong, or is my assumption that I have a single, well-defined personal risk level itself the mistake?
