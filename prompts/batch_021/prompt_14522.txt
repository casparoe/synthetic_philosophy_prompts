I'm about two-thirds into my current novel and I've hit a scene I don't trust myself to draft straight, so I want to farm out a rough version and then work it into my own voice after. My narrator, Dana, has to give the eulogy for her old grad school advisor, Marcus, a cognitive scientist who spent his whole career as one of the go-to defenders of the extended mind thesis -- the Clark and Chalmers stuff, Otto and his notebook, the argument that your mind doesn't stop at your skull if something outside it (a notebook, a phone, another person) is coupled into your thinking reliably enough. He was always fussy about distinguishing this from the older Putnam-style point about meanings depending on the world -- for Marcus it was never about content, it was about the process of thinking itself being partly made of stuff outside your head. His line, the one all his students quote at parties, was that where the mind "actually" ends is a boring fact about biology, not some sacred boundary worth defending.

The scene is hard because the funeral has two audiences and Dana can feel them listening differently. Half the pews are department colleagues, including a few who spent their whole careers telling Marcus that being causally hooked up to something isn't the same as that thing being part of your mind, and they'll absolutely notice if she reaches for something the theory can't actually support. The other half is his sister and a pile of ex-undergrads who just want to hear that some part of him is still around. The easy line -- "he lives on in his notebooks, his lectures, the lab he built" -- is exactly the sentence that'll make the colleagues wince, since on Marcus's own account a notebook that nobody's brain is actively trusting and reading anymore isn't part of a mind at all, its just paper. My one beta reader called my first attempt at this speech "a nice card any dead professor could've gotten," which, fair.

Could you write the actual eulogy Dana delivers? Something that could plausibly be read aloud in about four or five minutes, and that earns whatever comfort it offers honestly out of the theory itself, rather than quietly setting extended mind aside for the length of the speech and picking it back up again at the reception.
