Between novels, I've been sitting in on a friend's graduate seminar on social ontology while I research a book whose plot turns on a sovereign currency collapse, and I offered, in exchange, to help her grade a stack of make-up essays from students who missed the session on Searle, Haslanger, and Hacking. I did the assigned reading myself, but it has been a long time since I thought seriously about status functions and ameliorative analysis, and I would rather not hand back a mark I can't defend if she asks me about it over dinner.

The question the students were given reads as follows: "Money, gender, and race have all been described by philosophers as 'socially constructed.' Explain, with reference to at least one specific philosophical framework, what it means for a kind to be socially constructed in a way that is compatible with that kind being perfectly real rather than illusory or arbitrary. Then argue for whether money, gender, and race are socially constructed in essentially the same way, or whether that label conceals important differences between them." It is worth 20 points toward the seminar grade. Below, unedited, is the answer submitted by one student:

"Philosophers who say that money, gender, and race are 'socially constructed' do not mean that these categories are illusions or fictions we could simply stop believing in tomorrow. John Searle's *The Construction of Social Reality* gives the clearest model for why not: certain facts, which he calls institutional facts, exist only because we collectively accept that some object or person has a certain status. His formula is 'X counts as Y in context C' — a green piece of paper counts as a five-dollar bill in the United States because we collectively accept it as such. Because that collective acceptance is stable and widespread, money is completely real: it can be borrowed, taxed, and stolen, even though its existence depends on our attitudes in a way that a rock's does not.

Sally Haslanger extends this same idea to gender and race. On her view, to be a woman just is to occupy the social position that counts as female in a given context — gender is a status function imposed on bodies the way legal-tender status is imposed on paper. She calls her method 'ameliorative' because she is not simply describing how we already use the word 'woman' but recommending the definition that will do the most useful work for feminist politics: once we see that being a woman is a matter of collective recognition rather than biology, we can also see that the category could in principle be revised by changing what we collectively accept. Race works by the same mechanism, substituting perceived ancestry for perceived sex.

The upshot is that money, gender, and race are socially constructed in essentially the same way: all three are Searlean institutional facts, kinds that exist because, and only because, we collectively treat certain markers — paper, bodies — as carrying a socially significant status. The one real difference is a difference of degree rather than kind: the rules that create money are written into statutes and enforced by central banks, whereas the rules that create gender and race are informal and unconscious, so that most participants in the practice do not realize they are engaged in one. But in both cases, take away the collective attitudes and the kind disappears; there would be no more women or men, in Haslanger's sense, than there would be dollars, if everyone simultaneously stopped treating bodies, or paper, that way."

Could you grade this out of 20 and give me point-by-point comments I can actually pass along, rather than a general impression?
