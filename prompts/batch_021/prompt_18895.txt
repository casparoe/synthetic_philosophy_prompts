In my graduate seminar on democratic theory, we discussed a stylized case: the town of Rivermouth is holding a referendum on whether to double the capacity of its small regional airport. Only registered Rivermouth voters may vote, but the new flight paths would route almost all the added noise and pollution over Fenwick, a separate, smaller town next door whose residents have no vote in the referendum and never will, since the airport sits entirely within Rivermouth's boundaries. Two of my students kept arguing about it after class.

(1) Elena said the franchise is drawn in the wrong place: since Fenwick's residents will bear most of the real costs of the decision, they have at least as much claim to a vote as Rivermouth's own residents do, and restricting the referendum to people inside an administrative line that predates the airport is arbitrary. The demos, she said, should track whoever's interests are substantially affected by a decision, not a line on a map.

(2) Marcus objected that this proves too much: trace the causal effects of almost any local decision far enough — property values, contractor bids, downstream traffic — and you can find non-trivial effects on people well outside the jurisdiction, so "affected interests" alone gives no way to stop enfranchising ever-wider, ill-defined populations for ever more decisions. He proposed that the relevant test is instead whose conduct the town's authority actually binds: Fenwick's residents are not subject to Rivermouth's ordinances, so whatever they suffer as a side-effect, they have no standing to demand a vote in Rivermouth's elections.

(3) Elena replied that this gets the moral order backwards: coercion matters only because it is one way that people's interests get overridden without their say, so interests are still doing the fundamental work. She added that Fenwick's residents are coerced here in a perfectly literal sense too — nuisance and property law will forcibly bar them from doing anything about the noise — so the decision is in fact enforced on them by the same coercive apparatus Marcus wants to reserve for Rivermouth's own citizens.

(4) Marcus answered that if being legally barred from suing over a side-effect counts as being coerced by the decision that caused it, then everyone anywhere protected by ordinary property and tort law is "coerced" by every externality anyone generates, and his criterion collapses right back into Elena's boundary-less one. Still, he insisted that jurisdiction is at least a determinate, all-or-nothing fact where "how affected is affected enough" is not — so if anything can fix a boundary non-arbitrarily, it will look more like his criterion than hers, even though he conceded he could not yet say why subjection, rather than interest, should count as the more basic notion.

They asked me who was right, and I genuinely could not settle it. Could you go through the exchange move by move — saying who has the better of it at each of the four numbered moves, since it needn't come out even — and then flag anything important you think both of them are overlooking about whether the boundary of the demos can be fixed in a principled way at all?
