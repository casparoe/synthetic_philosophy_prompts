I'm teaching a graduate seminar on death and personhood in early Chinese philosophy this term, and we just spent a whole session on the handful of Zhuangzi passages that deal with death head-on: Huizi showing up to console him after his wife dies and finding him sitting there with his legs sprawled out, drumming on a basin and singing; the skull he meets on the road to Chu, who, when offered a chance to be brought back to life, knits its brow and says it would rather keep its kingly happiness than take on the troubles of the living again; and the dying Master Lai in the "Great and Venerable Teacher" chapter, whose friend jokes that the Creator might turn him into a rat's liver or a bug's arm, and who himself says the Great Clod that burdens him with a body and labors him with life is the very same one that eases him in old age and rests him in death, so insisting on staying human forever would be like a lump of metal on the forge screaming at the smith that it refuses to become anything but a famous sword. Half-jokingly, one of my students asked what Zhuangzi would make of Bryan Johnson, and I haven't been able to drop the question since.

In case you don't know the reference: Johnson is the tech millionaire who reportedly spends something like $2 million a year and employs a team of 30-odd doctors trying to reverse his own biological aging, under the banner "Don't Die," on the premise that people alive today might be the first generation who don't have to grow old and die at all. He's the flashiest edge of a broader "longevity" movement that also includes more sober-sounding scientists like Aubrey de Grey, whose SENS project (Strategies for Engineered Negligible Senescence) has spent decades framing aging as an engineering problem to be solved rather than a natural fact to be accepted. My first instinct is that Zhuangzi would see the whole enterprise as exactly the "nothing but a man!" mistake the smith would call inauspicious. But I suspect that's too easy, and maybe too convenient a way of using Zhuangzi to dunk on people who are, when you get down to it, just frightened of dying -- which Huizi's reaction suggests is a pretty ordinary thing to be. Zhuangzi isn't a philosopher of pure passivity either: there's real craft and skill prized in the book too (Cook Ding keeps the same blade sharp for nineteen years precisely by working with the grain of the ox rather than forcing it), so it's not obvious that "intervening" is automatically the sin. Couldn't a committed Zhuangzian say gene therapy and rapamycin are themselves just more transformations thrown up by the same process that produces old age, no less natural then decline, and that what actually earns the "inauspicious metal" charge isn't intervention as such but a specific anxious, gripping refusal to stop if and when it doesn't work?

Could you actually work through what you think Zhuangzi's real position would be here, engaging with the specific passages rather than the generic "Daoism just means go with the flow" version? I want you to land on an actual view rather than a survey of considerations on both sides, and to take the strongest form of the objection to your own answer seriously before you do.
