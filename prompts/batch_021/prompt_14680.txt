I'm a retired experimental physicist, and in retirement I've fallen into a habit of reading philosophy I never had time for during my career. My old book club (mostly other retired scientists — we used to argue about grant funding, now we argue about Descartes) picked Penrose's *Shadows of the Mind* this month, on the theory that it'd be "relevant to all this AI stuff." Back when it came out I only really cared about the quantum-gravity-and-microtubules chapters and skimmed straight past the logic, so this time I forced myself to actually sit down with the Gödel argument in Part I, and now I'm stuck.

As far as I can tell, the bare-bones Lucas/Penrose move goes: for any consistent formal system F strong enough to encode arithmetic, Gödel's theorem hands you a sentence G(F) that's true but unprovable inside F; a human mathematician can apparently see that G(F) is true (because they can see that F's consistency entails it), even though F itself can't prove it; so human mathematical understanding outruns any single F, and minds aren't algorithms. The obvious objection, which I came up with myself before I even got to the part of the book where Penrose addresses it, is: how does the mathematician know that F — i.e, their own reasoning — is consistent in the first place, given that Gödel's second theorem says a consistent F can't prove its own consistency? Looks like the arguement just helps itself to the very thing in question.

My (possibly faulty) memory is that Penrose knew this objection was coming and tried to route around it by upgrading the requirement from mere "consistency" to "soundness," plus some bootstrapping move where a human merely coming to believe their own system is sound is enough, in principle, to generate output that system couldn't have produced on its own. I also recall this patched version getting fairly thoroughly taken apart — I want to say by Solomon Feferman, and maybe David Chalmers too, in a journal symposium that followed the book — but for the life of me I can't reconstruct their counter-argument anymore. Could you spell out fairly precisely (a) what work the move from consistency to soundness is actually supposed to do for Penrose that plain consistency couldn't, and (b) what the strongest version of the standard rebuttal is, i.e. why most logicians still don't think the patched-up argument shows that minds outrun machines? I'd like to walk away actually understanding the crux of it, rather than just filing it under "smart people disagree."
