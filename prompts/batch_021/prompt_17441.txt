And here's the classmate's answer, word for word:

"The Ellsberg urn has 90 balls, 30 of which are red and known, and the other 60 are either black or yellow but we're never told the proportion. Subjects choose between betting on red (bet 1) or black (bet 2), and then, separately, between betting on 'red or yellow' (bet 3) or 'black or yellow' (bet 4). Most people prefer bet 1 to bet 2, since the odds on red are pinned down at 1/3 while black could be anywhere from 0 to 2/3. But when yellow is added to both remaining options, the same people tend to flip and prefer bet 4 to bet 3, even though red-or-yellow should feel just as vague as black-or-yellow. If you try to find a single probability p for black that rationalizes both choices, the first preference requires p < 1/3 and the second requires p > 1/3, which is impossible. So there's no subjective probability that makes the pattern consistent, and it violates the independence axiom of von Neumann-Morgenstern's expected utility theorem, which says preferences between two options shouldn't be affected by a common outcome that's added to both of them equally.

One well-known formal response is Gilboa and Schmeidler's maxmin expected utility model, where instead of holding a single prior, an agent holds a whole set of priors and evaluates each act by its expected utility under the worst-case prior in that set. Betting on black gets evaluated pessimistically, so it looks worse than the known 1/3 chance on red, and the same logic flips things around for the second pair of bets. This is essentially the same mechanism as the 'certainty effect' from prospect theory, where people overweight outcomes that are certain relative to outcomes that are merely probable — both are really just saying that people don't treat probabilistic and certain payoffs on the same linear scale.

As for whether this is irrational: since the pattern can't be represented by any single probability-utility pair, it violates Savage's axioms, and by the standard definition of rationality used in decision theory, that makes it irrational. Most economists treat Ellsberg's result as showing that people depart from the rational-agent model in a predictable, systematic way rather than through random noise."
