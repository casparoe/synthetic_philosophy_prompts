I've spent the last couple of weeks going back and forth between Daniel Dennett's 1991 paper "Real Patterns" and the interpretability write-ups that keep making the rounds on the Alignment Forum, and I keep suspecting the two are converging on the same question without anyone quite saying so. As I understand Dennett, his target in that paper is to carve out a middle position between industrial-strength realism about beliefs and desires (roughly, Fodor's view that they are discrete, language-like structures actually inscribed somewhere in the brain) and Churchland-style eliminativism (folk psychology as a convenient but strictly false story, no more real than the sun "rising"). His way out is to argue that a pattern in some body of data is real just to the extent that there exists a description of that data which is more efficient than the raw bit-map and which buys genuine predictive leverage; the "gliders" that show up in Conway's Game of Life are his go-to illustration of a pattern that is objectively there, and recoverable by any sufficiently good observer, without being some further thing over and above the underlying cellular automaton.

What I can't stop noticing is how much this sounds like the argument, among people who do mechanistic interpretability on neural networks, over whether the "features" and "circuits" they extract are real constituents of what the network computes, or merely one convenient redescription among many equally serviceable ones. The standard motivating story is that individual neurons are often "polysemantic" (each one lighting up for several unrelated concepts at once) because the network is packing more sparse features into its activation space than it has dimensions for, a phenomenon interpretability researchers call superposition; sparse-autoencoder-based dictionary learning is then used to unpack the activations into a larger set of more "monosemantic" features. The trouble is that this decomposition isn't unique: different dictionary sizes and sparsity penalties yield different, non-nested feature sets, and features are known to keep "splitting" into ever-finer sub-features as the dictionary grows, with no principled place to stop.

So here is the comparison I'd like worked through properly, rather than just gestured at the way most things I've read do: does Dennett's compressibility criterion actually license the realism that interpretability researchers seem to want when they call a discovered feature "real," or does the non-uniqueness and endless splitting show that the neural-network case is disanalogous to his Game-of-Life examples in some way that breaks the comparison? Relatedly, is that non-uniqueness even a problem for "real patterns" realism specifically, or is it simply the sort of observer- and purpose-relativity that Dennett's own pluralism about levels (physical, design, and intentional stances all counting as "real" for different predictive purposes) was already designed to absorb?
