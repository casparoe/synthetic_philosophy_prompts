Been elbow-deep in the dispositions literature for my lit review chapter and its honestly more fun than it has any right to be. Quick version for context: the simple conditional analysis says something like "the glass is fragile iff it would break if struck," then Martin's electro-fink wrecks that — a dead wire hooked to a device that senses the instant a conductor touches it and flips the wire live right then, so the counterfactual comes out true even though the wire wasn't live a second earlier. Lewis tries to patch this with his reformed conditional analysis (tie the disposition to an intrinsic causal basis that has to persist through the test), which handles finks fine, but then Johnston's masks show up: cases where the causal basis is totally untouched, nothing about the object changed, but some extrinsic thing blocks the manifestation anyway (his go-to case is a poison that's still lethal, just masked by an antidote taken alongside it). Two completely different ways for the counterfactual test to mislead you, and I don't think most people who toss around "finks and masks" as a pair actually keep the difference straight.

So: can you write me a short story, any genre, ~700 words, where the ending genuinely doesn't work unless the reader has correctly figured out — for some character or object in it — whether whats going on is a fink or a mask? Please don't just reskin the wire or the poison/antidote case, invent your own setup. Feel free to tack a couple sentences onto the end spelling out which was which, so I can check my read against yours.
