So this is going to sound like a weird thing to be loosing sleep over, but I got a surprise bonus at work this year and decided to actually be serious about donating a chunk of it instead of just picking whatever charity ad I saw last, so I fell down the effective altruism rabbit hole trying to figure out cause prioritization. Somewhere in there I hit this "cluelessness" objection to consequentialism and now I can't stop chewing on it. The basic idea, as far as I understand it, is that consequentialism says an act is right insofar as it produces the best consequences, but literally any action ripples outward in totally unpredictable ways forever -- like, funding bednets in some village changes who has kids with who and when, which changes which specific kid gets conceived, which cascades into basically all of human history from that point on, and there's no way for me to know if that ripple is net good or bad. So how am I supposed to know my donation is actually "right" in the sense consequentialism cares about?

I brought this up with a friend from book club who did a philosophy MA and she was way less rattled by it than me -- she said something about how a lot of that "downstream noise" just washes out in expectation, so you can basically bracket it and reason using the evidence you do have, and mentioned some distinction (Greaves, I think?) between that "washing out" kind of cluelessness and a nastier "complex" version, where you have real evidence pointing in conflicting directions (like, growth-focused aid might be great for welfare but bad for the climate) that doesn't just cancel out. I think the original "no, this is a genuinely huge problem" version of the argument is from someone called Lenman, but I'm fuzzy on details and we mostly ended up talking past each other rather than actually resolving anything.

Could you write this out properly as a dialogue between two people who really know this literature: one who thinks cluelessness is a serious, maybe even fatal, problem for using consequentialism as an actual decision procedure, and one who thinks the expected-value / "washing out" move basically defuses it for most practical purposes. I want them to genuinely engage with each other's strongest moves rather than just restate their own side -- so the "it's fatal" person needs a real response to the washing-out argument instead of ignoring it, and the "it washes out" person has to actually grapple with the harder complex-cluelessness cases instead of just insisting everything cancels out eventually. If it fits naturally, I'd love the tension between near-term and long-termist EA giving to come up at some point, since that's honestly the practical question I'm trying to work out for myself.
