A friend and I spent much of a long evening arguing about Bradley's regress without ever converging, so I would like a more disinterested verdict than either of us could offer alone. We agreed that any account of what unifies a particular and a universal, or a subject and a predicate, into a genuine state of affairs or proposition, rather than leaving us with a mere list of constituents, has to say something about what actually does the unifying, and that this is exactly where the regress threatens to bite. Where we disagreed was on which of the standard replies actually earns its keep, rather than merely relabeling the problem or trading one obscurity for another.

Please rank the following replies to Bradley's regress and the associated problem of the unity of the proposition, from most to least successful by that standard:

1. Bergmann's nonrelational "nexus," a primitive tie connecting particular and universal directly, without itself being a further relatable term.
2. Frege's unsaturated concepts, whose built-in incompleteness lets them combine with objects without any further connector.
3. Armstrong's and Olson's "brute fact" states of affairs: non-mereological unities, metaphysically prior to rather than composed out of their constituents.
4. Armstrong's and Orilia's "benign infinitism," on which the further relations generated by the regress supervene on the first and add nothing further to reality, much as with the truth-regress.
5. Vallicella's external unifier, an agent-like operator credited with "the power of contingent self-determination," that brings states of affairs into existence.
6. Weiland, Betti, and Maurin's relata-specific relations, individuated so tightly to their particular relata that they could not possibly relate anything else.
7. Russell's early distinction, drawn in his 1903 Principles of Mathematics, between a relation occurring merely "as term" and a relation occurring "as verb," i.e., actually relating.
8. Russell's later multiple-relation theory of judgment, which relocates the unity of a judgment away from an independent proposition and into a many-place relation involving the judging mind.
