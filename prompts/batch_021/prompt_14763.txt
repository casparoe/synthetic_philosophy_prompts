I work with causal inference professionally — mainly Judea Pearl's structural causal models and do-calculus — and this has made me curious how philosophers analyze causation more carefully. As I understand it, David Lewis's counterfactual theory treats causation as counterfactual dependence, evaluated via similarity between possible worlds, whereas Woodward's interventionist theory, which is much closer to the causal-graph framework I use, cashes out causal claims in terms of what would happen under a well-defined intervention on a variable. Since "what would happen under an intervention" is itself a counterfactual, I'm not sure how sharp this distinction really is. Please compare the two theories, and assess whether interventionism counts as a genuinely distinct theory of causation or is essentially a refinement that swaps Lewis's similarity metric for a more precise, structural notion of dependence. Ground your answer in how each approach handles one specific hard case for the classic counterfactual account, such as a scenario of late preemption or a "switching" case where an intervention changes the causal route to an outcome without changing whether the outcome occurs.
