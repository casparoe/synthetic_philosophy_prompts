I spent thirty-five years as an experimental particle physicist before retiring, and for the last two years I have been trying to fill the resulting gap with philosophy, mainly through a reading group attached to my old university. The question that keeps pulling me back in is the oldest one on the books: why is there something rather than nothing? My first instinct, as a physicist, was that this had essentially been dissolved by physical cosmology, so I went back to Lawrence Krauss's "A Universe from Nothing: Why There Is Something Rather Than Nothing" expecting it to hold up better than I remembered. It did not, at least not against David Albert's review in the New York Times, which struck me as basically right that a quantum vacuum, or even the deeper law-like structure that allows such a vacuum to fluctuate into particles, is a highly specific something rather than the "nothing" the question is actually asking about.

Going further into the philosophical literature has not settled things for me, though. Adolf Grünbaum argues that the whole question rests on an unearned assumption: that nothingness is somehow the default, "expected" state of affairs against which the existence of anything at all stands in special need of explanation, and that once this assumption is dropped the apparent mystery evaporates. Peter van Inwagen tries to dissolve the question from the opposite end, arguing that there are vastly more ways for something to exist than there are for there to be nothing, so that on any reasonable way of counting possibilities, something was always overwhelmingly the likelier outcome. And Derek Parfit, in his essay "Why Anything? Why This?", spends a great many pages hunting for some principle that could non-arbitrarily select our world, or any world, as the one that gets to be actual, and seems to conclude that no candidate principle is fully satisfying.

Rather than another survey of this territory, of which I have now read several, I would like an essay from you that actually commits to a position on two linked questions. First: is physical cosmology -- "universe from nothing" proposals of the kind Krauss offers included -- even the right sort of theory to make progress on why there is something rather than nothing, or does it inevitably answer a different, easier question by trading on an equivocal sense of "nothing"? Second: is the original question a genuine one that could in principle admit of an answer, or does some dissolution strategy along Grünbaum's or van Inwagen's lines (or a better one of your own devising) show that it rests on a mistake? I am less interested in a balanced tour of the options than in your own argued verdict on each question, defended rather than hedged -- though I do want you to state the strongest objection to the position you end up taking, and explain why it ultimately fails to move you.
