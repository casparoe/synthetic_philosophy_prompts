I spent thirty-five years running a comparative physiology lab before I retired, and I have since been using some of the free time to read philosophy of biology properly for the first time -- Sober and Godfrey-Smith initially, and then, chasing the footnotes back, the older papers by Larry Wright and Robert Cummins that everyone still seems to be arguing with when they try to say what a biological "function" actually is. That reading proved useful, in an odd way, at a reunion dinner for my old department last spring, where two former colleagues of mine -- I will call them Helen, a comparative anatomist, and Peter, an evolutionary biologist -- spent most of the evening disputing whether the human vermiform appendix has a function, and I still cannot work out which of them had the better of it.

Helen opened with the "safe house" hypothesis: the appendix, she said, shelters commensal gut bacteria as biofilms so that they can recolonize the colon after something like a severe bout of diarrhea wipes it out, and comparative studies have since found that the structure evolved independently something like thirty separate times across mammals over roughly eighty million years -- far too often, on her view, to be an accident, and grounds enough to call immune support its function. Peter replied that all of this shows what the appendix currently does, and how often blind lymphoid pouches have arisen in mammalian guts, but nothing about why the modern human version is still here; he reminded her that Darwin himself, in 1871, had already dismissed the human appendix as a shrunken remnant of the much larger cecum our plant-fermenting ancestors relied on, and suggested the safe-house story might simply be what any leftover pouch of lymphoid tissue would incidentally do, whether or not that is what has kept it in the population. Helen countered that Peter's standard would leave most of physiology unable to say much of anything: nobody insists on a demonstrated, ongoing selection regime before saying the pancreas's function is insulin secretion, and functional morphologists routinely, and legitimately, assign functions on the basis of current causal contribution alone. Peter conceded the point about the pancreas, but said that was exactly why it was a poor test case -- there, current role and selective history happen to agree, so no one is ever forced to choose between them -- whereas the appendix, like the coccyx or the wisdom tooth, is precisely the kind of case the historical requirement was invented for, and waiving it here simply settles the dispute in Helen's favor by assumption. Helen's rejoinder was that this makes the historical requirement unfalsifiable in practice: unless Peter can specify how much selective maintenance, sustained over how long, is enough to upgrade "does X" to "is for X," then "selected effect" is no more rigorous than "does something useful" -- it only sounds more rigorous, dressed as it is in evolutionary language that cannot actually be checked in most real cases, this one included.

Neither of them budged, and the rest of the table was no help. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say, at each step, who had the stronger position and why, rather than simply handing down an overall winner, noting anything you think both of them are overlooking? I would also value your view on a question the argument left unresolved: is Helen and Peter's disagreement at bottom empirical (about what, if anything, is currently keeping the human appendix in the population), conceptual (about what "function" ought to mean in biology), or some mixture of the two -- and if it is a mixture, how would you go about separating the two questions, so that a point scored on one is not mistaken for a point scored on the other?
