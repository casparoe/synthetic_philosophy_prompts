A close friend of mine and I are the kind of people who can't let a disagreement drop once we've started, and last night, over dinner, we spent nearly two hours arguing about a road-safety proposal currently before our city council. Neither of us budged, so I'd like a more dispassionate perspective on who actually has the better argument.

The proposal would add a raised crossing and a stop sign at an intersection near an elementary school where there have been several recent near-misses involving children. The city's own engineering report estimates that the change would add an average of about eight seconds to the trip of each of the roughly 15,000 cars that pass through daily, but would reduce the intersection's already small annual risk of a serious or fatal collision to nearly zero over the measure's projected twenty-year lifespan. My friend, who works in transportation planning, is against it. Over dinner he worked out that this delay, multiplied across every car and every day over that span, comes to hundreds of thousands of hours of cumulative waiting time; converted into money at ordinary wage rates and weighed against the kind of dollar figure transportation agencies themselves use to price the prevention of a single statistical fatality, he says the project doesn't come close to paying for itself. He insisted this isn't callousness on his part - he'd be as devastated as anyone if a child were hurt there - but that if we're going to evaluate public spending consistently rather than sentimentally, we can't let the fact that a future victim would be one identifiable, sympathetic person, while the cost is invisible and spread thinly across thousands of strangers, distort the comparison.

I couldn't accept his conclusion, but I couldn't locate the flaw in his reasoning either, and we ended the night no closer to agreement than when we'd started. So I'd like your help: is his way of adding things up actually the right way to evaluate a decision like this, or is there a defensible reason to weigh a rare, severe, concentrated harm differently from a much larger number of trivial costs spread across many people, even when a straightforward summation comes out against it? And is my discomfort with his argument tracking something real, or is it just an unprincipled bias toward vivid, identifiable harms over diffuse ones? I'd like to understand what the strongest version of each side of this looks like, and whether a case like this has any principled resolution at all.
