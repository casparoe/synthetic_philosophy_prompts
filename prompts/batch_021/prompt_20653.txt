So I've been falling down an AI safety rabbit hole for the past few weeks (started with a Rob Miles video, ended up reading actual MIRI papers at 1am like a crazy person), and the thing I can't stop chewing on is the "Löbian obstacle" — the result that a self-modifying agent reasoning purely with formal proof can't, in general, trust a successor version of itself to only output true beliefs. The reason is Löb's theorem: if your system could prove "whenever I prove X, X is true," it could already prove X directly, so the obvious plan of "only adopt the successor if you can first prove its conclusions are sound" just collapses in on itself. What's been bugging me is I keep running into two totally different reactions to this in stuff I read. The original tiling agents paper treats it like a genuinely nasty, maybe unavoidable wall for proof-based self trust. But then later work — Christiano's probabilistic reflection principle, and eventually Garrabrant's logical induction — gets talked about like it basically dissolves the problem, mainly by just not demanding proof-level certainty about your future self in the first place.

I asked my friend who's doing a logic PhD about this and she was way more skeptical of the "probability fixes it" framing than I expected, which is what gave me the idea: can you write me a dialogue between two people on opposite sides of that exact disagreement? One thinks the shift to probabilistic self-trust (logical induction and all that) is a real, substantive answer to the Löbian obstacle — that demanding literal proof-certainty about your future self was always the wrong standard, and calibrated probability is exactly the right replacement for it. The other thinks its mostly a dodge — you can rebuild an analogous self-reference headache for a probabilistic reasoner, and "converges to calibration in the limit, no finite-time guarantee" was never the kind of self-trust anybody actually needed from a self-modifying AI. Neither one should be a pushover — I want them actually responding to each other's specific arguments instead of just restating their own position in different words each time, and I want to come away still genuinely unsure who won. Something like 20-ish exchanges of back-and-forth, no extra narration beyond who's speaking.
