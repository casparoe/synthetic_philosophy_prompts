I'm working on a Cold-War-era espionage novel, and one chapter turns on a scene in which my protagonist, an intelligence analyst, has to update her beliefs about a defector's location from a single garbled radio intercept. I wanted the reasoning in that scene to actually hold up, not just sound plausible, so I ran a simplified version of it past two friends -- Nadia, who works as a statistician, and Callum, who has a philosophy PhD in formal epistemology -- and the two of them ended up arguing about it long after I'd gotten what I needed for the book.

Here is the setup I gave them. The defector is equally likely to be hiding on the Left Bank or the Right Bank of the city, fifty-fifty. Independently, on either bank, he is equally likely to be in the Old Quarter or the Docks, fifty-fifty within each bank -- so all four combinations start out equally likely, at 25 percent apiece. My analyst then intercepts a single partial message: 'If he's on the Left Bank, the odds are 3 to 1 that he's in the Old Quarter.' Nothing else comes through. The question is how she should now revise her probabilities for all four combinations, not just the two the message actually mentions.

Callum recognized this as a version of a known puzzle in formal epistemology, the Judy Benjamin problem, which did nothing to stop them disagreeing about the answer. Roughly, in order:

1. Nadia said the principled move is to find the new distribution over all four combinations that satisfies the 3:1 constraint while diverging as little as possible, in the standard information-theoretic sense, from the original distribution, which gave all four combinations equal weight. She'd actually run the numbers: doing this doesn't just rearrange the Left Bank -- it also raises the overall probability that he's on the Right Bank at all, from 50 percent to about 53 percent, even though the message never once compared the two banks.

2. Callum said that has to be a mistake: the message is entirely about the internal split of the Left Bank and gives no evidence whatsoever about Left versus Right, so the only defensible update leaves the Left/Right split at fifty-fifty and pours the Left Bank's own half into a 3:1 split between the Old Quarter and the Docks.

3. Nadia replied that Callum's rule isn't some neutral default either: run her same minimization but add his 'don't touch the bank split' condition as an extra constraint, and you get his exact numbers back -- so his supposedly assumption-free rule is really just her rule with one more assumption bolted on, not an alternative to making assumptions at all. And since the message is a claim about a conditional probability, not about any actual event, there was never a plain 'condition on what you learned and leave everything else alone' option on the table to begin with.

4. Callum answered that the formal equivalence doesn't erase the asymmetry: his extra constraint is earned by what the message actually says -- nothing at all about the Right Bank -- while Nadia's rule needs no such license for letting the Right Bank move; it just falls out of the general formula when nobody tells it to leave anything alone. Being able to redescribe his answer as her method plus a constraint doesn't make his constraint arbitrary, he said -- it makes it the one constraint in this whole affair that's actually justified.

Neither of them budged, and I still don't know whose instincts I should be borrowing for my own scene. Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each point? I'd also like to know whether there's an assumption both of them are quietly relying on that neither has noticed. I'm not fishing for 'the' textbook answer, if one even exists -- I want the four moves judged on their merits.
