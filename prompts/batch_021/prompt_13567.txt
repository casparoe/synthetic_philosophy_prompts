Ok so this happened at a dinner thing like two weekends ago and I still can't stop thinking about it. Backstory: our friend Jesse had promised another friend, Mira, that he'd feed her cat while she was away at a wedding. Two days before she left, he got invited on a last-minute camping trip with his brother (who's only in town for like a week) and just... didn't do it. Turned out totally fine because Mira's downstairs neighbor noticed the cat crying at the window and fed it without ever knowing Jesse was even supposed to. When Jesse told this story at the table, kind of pleased with how it worked out, two other people there, Nadia and Ben, ended up arguing about whether he'd actually done anything wrong, and it went on forever. Roughly reconstructed, it went something like this:

(1) Ben said Jesse obviously did nothing wrong, since Mira was never actually let down and never even knew there was a gap — no harm came to anyone, so there's no wrong to speak of.
(2) Nadia said that's backwards: Jesse wronged Mira the moment he decided not to follow through, because what he owed her was the actual thing (showing up), and whether it happened to work out afterward is just luck — you don't get moral credit for being bailed out by luck.
(3) Ben came back with: if the wrong is really independent of outcome like that, it should be exactly as bad to break a promise that changes nothing as one that leaves someone stranded, but obviously the second is way worse, so outcome has to matter, which means Nadia's "it's-wrong-the-second-you-decide" story can't be the whole picture.
(4) Nadia said outcome can affect how bad the wrong is without affecting whether it's a wrong at all in the first place — same as how lying to someone is still wrong even if they never find out and it changes nothing for them, it's just a smaller-scale wrong than a lie that ruins something.
(5) Ben then said okay, but if it's not really about Mira being harmed, then whatever obligation is left just seems to be Jesse and Mira both buying into some general social convention around promise-keeping — which means the real wrong, if there is one, is against the practice everyone relies on, not against Mira specifically. Which felt off to him, like it was letting the convention take the blame that should land on a person.

Nobody at the table settled it (I just sat there switching allegiances every two minutes) and I've kept chewing on it since. Could you go through moves 1 through 5 and say who's more right at each step, not just who wins overall? Especially curious whether Ben's move in (5) is an actual problem for Nadia's view or if theres a way out of it that neither of them saw. Also flag anything you think both of them are just missing entirely, and end with a one-line verdict on who ended up closer to right overall.
