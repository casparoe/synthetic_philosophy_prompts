I was listening to a philosophy podcast on my drive in the other day (I'm a backend engineer, zero formal philosophy training, so bear with me) and they went deep on Harry Frankfurt's old counterexample to what they called the "principle of alternative possibilities." The setup: there's a neuroscientist who's rigged up a chip in Jones's head that will force him to vote for Smith if Jones shows any sign of going to vote for someone else, but Jones ends up choosing Smith entirely on his own, so the chip never actually fires. The point being that Jones literally could not have done otherwise (the chip guarantees the outcome no matter what), yet basically everyone's gut reaction is that he's still fully responsible for that vote. To me that seems like a clean proof that "could have done otherwise" is just irrelevant to free will and moral responsibility, full stop, kind of like how a watchdog process that never actually has to kill and restart your service doesn't change anything about whether your code ran correctly on its own.

So here's my confusion: if Frankfurt nailed that down back in 1969, why is the free will vs. determinism debate still such a massive, active thing in philosophy departments today? It feels like this one thought experiment should've closed the book in favor of compatibilism, since the main weapon incompatibilists always lean on (determinism removes your ability to do otherwise, therefore no one's really to blame for anything) just got taken away for good. I mentioned this to a friend who's in a philosophy PhD program and she said "it's way more complicated than that" but had to run before explaining. Is the thought experiment actually not as airtight as it sounds, or is there some other chunk of the free will debate that this argument just doesn't touch at all, even if we grant that Frankfurt is completely right about the chip case? I feel like I'm missing something structural about how this fits into the bigger picture and would love to actually understand it instead of just repeating the "gotcha" at parties.
