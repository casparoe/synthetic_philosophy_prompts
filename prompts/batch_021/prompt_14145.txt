I've followed the recurring infinite-ethics threads on LessWrong and Overcoming Bias for some time now, and I keep returning to Bostrom's paper 'Infinite Ethics' and the problem he calls 'infinitarian paralysis': once you allow that the universe (or the total stock of value) might be infinite, standard aggregative consequentialism seems to lose all ability to rank outcomes, since the relevant sums either fail to converge or converge to ties regardless of how different the underlying arrangements of good and bad look up close. The patches on offer include importing the overtaking and catching-up criteria from infinite-horizon growth economics, falling back on Pareto-style dominance orderings that only ever produce a partial ranking, valuing outcomes with hyperreal (non-Archimedean) numbers instead of real ones, and simply restricting moral evaluation to the finite causal difference an action makes rather than to the state of the universe as a whole.

Write an essay that weighs these proposals -- and any others you think are more promising -- against each other and against the option of abandoning full aggregation over infinite domains altogether. Reach a genuine verdict: something like 'no proposed fix actually works, so consequentialists should fall back on local/causal evaluation' would count as an answer, and so would defending a specific fix, but 'the matter remains unresolved' would not.
