Here's the question as it was posed on the exam: "Explain the puzzle of material constitution using the statue and the clay. State the modal ('Leibniz's Law') argument for thinking the statue and the lump of clay that constitutes it are numerically distinct objects, making clear what work Allan Gibbard's Lumpl-and-Goliath case does in that argument. Then explain how EITHER the perdurantist (four-dimensionalist) OR the constitution theorist would respond, in order to avoid an unpalatable proliferation of coincident objects, and say which response you find more convincing and why."

And here's the student's answer, in full:

"The puzzle starts from three claims that each look true on their own but can't all be true together: (1) the statue and the lump of clay occupy exactly the same place at the same time and are made of all the same matter; (2) they seem to have different modal and historical properties; and (3) Leibniz's Law (the Indiscernibility of Identicals) says that if two things are truly identical, they must share every property, without exception. For instance, the lump of clay could survive being squashed into a ball, but the statue could not - so the clay has the property 'can survive squashing' while the statue lacks it. Since they differ in at least one property, Leibniz's Law tells us they can't be identical, so the statue and the lump must actually be two numerically distinct objects that merely happen to coincide.

Allan Gibbard's Lumpl-and-Goliath case is the classic way of sharpening this argument. Lumpl (the lump) and Goliath (the statue) come into existence at the same instant out of previously formless clay, and in the case as originally given, they're destroyed together too, so across their entire actual careers they are perfectly coincident in matter, space, and time. Gibbard uses this case to argue that Lumpl and Goliath must nevertheless be distinct objects, since the clay could instead have been squashed back into a ball rather than fired, or the statue could have been sculpted a moment later out of slightly different clay - which shows the two have different persistence conditions and modal profiles even in the case where they happen to coincide completely as things actually stand.

The perdurantist dissolves the puzzle by denying there was ever really one thing there to begin with: objects are four-dimensional 'space-time worms' composed of temporal parts, and the statue-worm and the lump-worm are simply two distinct, if heavily overlapping, aggregates of temporal parts - rather like two roads that run together for a few miles before parting ways. Because Lumpl and Goliath were never strictly identical in the first place, Leibniz's Law was never actually under threat, and there's no need to posit some extra, mysterious 'constitution' relation to explain how two things can be almost, but not quite, the same thing.

Overall I find the perdurantist response more convincing than the constitution theorist's, because it actually gets rid of the coincidence instead of just giving it a fancy name and asserting that it isn't identity."
