A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about Simone Weil's 1943 note to the Free French leadership in London calling for the outright abolition of political parties, and neither of us managed to persuade the other. Weil's charge is structural, not partisan: whatever ideals a party professes, it is, in her words, "a machine to generate collective passions," built to pressure the judgment of its own members, and oriented ultimately toward nothing but its own unlimited growth, so that truth and justice get subordinated to party interest almost by design. What struck me afterward is that E. E. Schattschneider made essentially the opposite argument at almost the same historical moment, insisting flatly that "modern democracy is unthinkable save in terms of parties." Write a dialogue between a philosopher defending Weil's case for suppressing parties entirely and one defending Schattschneider's view that parties are indispensable once politics moves beyond a small, face-to-face community. The Weilian should press the claim that this corruption follows from a party's very structure, regardless of its ideology or its members' sincerity; the other should press Weil on whether her alternative — decisions reached through individual conscience alone, with passions canceling out through honest public deliberation — can actually function at the scale of a modern electorate, or whether something functionally equivalent to a party would simply reappear under another name. Each side should engage the other's actual argument in turn, and the dialogue should not end in an easy concession from either one.
