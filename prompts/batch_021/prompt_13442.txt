The following is a question from a graduate seminar on Marx, together with a student's answer to it. Grade the answer and justify the grade with point-by-point comments rather than a general impression.

Question: In Capital, Marx argues that capitalist exploitation does not depend on workers being cheated in the wage exchange: even when workers are paid the full value of their labor-power, exploitation still occurs. Explain the theoretical apparatus behind this claim, paying particular attention to the distinction between labor and labor-power. Then explain how this account relates to the theory of alienation developed in the 1844 Manuscripts: are exploitation and alienation two names for a single underlying wrong, or do they diagnose distinct problems with wage labor?

Student's answer: Marx's key move is to separate labor-power — the worker's capacity to work, which is what is actually sold on the market — from labor itself, which is what the capitalist directs once the sale has gone through. Labor-power is a commodity like any other, and Marx assumes it exchanges at its value: a value fixed by the cost of the goods needed to reproduce the worker from one day to the next (food, housing, and so on). On this picture, the wage contract itself involves no cheating; the capitalist pays exactly what the worker's labor-power is worth.

The exploitation shows up only once the worker is actually put to work. Over the course of a full day, the value embodied in what the worker produces exceeds the wage the capitalist paid for that day's labor-power; the difference is surplus value, which is the source of profit. Because this gap opens up even though the wage transaction is fair by its own standards, Marx can locate exploitation in the structure of the wage relation itself, rather than in any individual employer's dishonesty.

This is essentially the same verdict Marx reaches, by a different route, in the 1844 Manuscripts' account of alienation: estranged labor separates the worker from the product of his labor, from the activity of laboring, from his own species-being, and from other people. Capital simply re-derives these same four estrangements more rigorously, this time from the mechanics of value rather than from a philosophical account of human nature.
