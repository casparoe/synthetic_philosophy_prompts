I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for a long time (LessWrong, the old Overcoming Bias archives, Scott Alexander's various blogs), and a few years ago several of us turned that into a standing habit: a Tuesday-night group that does calibration practice and occasionally works through papers from the epistemology-of-disagreement literature -- Christensen, Elga, that general cluster on higher-order evidence. Partly as a defense against the failure mode Scott Alexander describes in "Epistemic Learned Helplessness" (the idea that you can be rational to distrust an argument you can't refute, if arguments you can't refute have a track record of leading you astray), we've kept a shared spreadsheet for two years logging everyone's stated confidence on multi-step estimates against how things actually turned out.

Two weeks ago this stopped being an abstract exercise. One member, Dev, spent an evening deriving an estimate for one of the group's standing forecasting questions (something about a pending regulatory decision, if I remember right), checked the derivation twice, and landed on 92% confidence in his final figure. Someone pulled up the spreadsheet and pointed out that Dev, specifically on multi-step quantitative estimates where he reports confidence above 90%, has historically been correct only about 55% of the time -- a gap that has held up across enough predictions for long enough that nobody in the group thinks it's noise. Dev reread his derivation twice more afterward and still could not locate anything wrong with it.

This is what set off two other members, Renata and Owen, neither of whom has backed down since. (1) Renata said Dev has to revise substantially downward from 92%, full stop: the spreadsheet is exactly the sort of evidence a Bayesian is obligated to condition on, and refusing to move just because the derivation still feels right is the inside view refusing to update on the outside view. (2) Owen replied that if Dev does revise down to, say, 65%, he still hasn't found a flaw and every step of the derivation still strikes him as sound -- so he'd now believe the argument works while believing he shouldn't trust his own sense that it works, which is epistemic akrasia, not humility; better to locate the actual error or trust the derivation, not hold both attitudes at once. (3) Renata answered that this is only incoherent if first-order and higher-order evidence must be collapsed into a single verdict before Dev is allowed to act on anything -- it's perfectly ordinary to say "on the object-level considerations alone, 92%, but all-things-considered, lower," and that isn't two beliefs in tension but one belief that has already incorporated a further piece of evidence, namely the track record itself. (4) Owen said that's fine only if the track record actually bears on this derivation, which is exactly what he doubts: "instances where Dev felt over 90% confident on some multi-step estimate" is a reference class, not this argument, and if the reasoning holds up on a recheck, downgrading purely because of a pattern about past instances of himself amounts to grading his work without engaging with it -- taken to the limit, the policy means Dev can never trust a derivation that surprises him, which seems like a strange way to ever improve at estimation.

I can see genuine force in both positions, and I don't think either of them is being dense or disingenuous -- I just can't work out who actually has the better of it, or exactly where the other one's move fails. Could you adjudicate move by move: for each of Renata's and Owen's four moves, say whether it succeeds, partially succeeds, or fails on its own terms, and why? Then close with what, if anything, you think both of them are missing about what Dev is actually rationally required to believe, and say which overall position comes closer to being right.
