I'm a novelist, and I've been invited to contribute a short essay to a magazine issue that pairs novelists with AI researchers, each addressing the question of whether large language models are best understood as agents, as "simulators," or as something else again. My angle is to test the "simulator" framing — the idea that the network itself is a characterless engine that produces "simulacra," mere personas, of which the helpful "Assistant" voice trained into it by fine-tuning is only one recurring example rather than anything like the network's true self — against what I actually know, from my own trade, about the relationship between an author and the characters that author writes. Here is my draft. I'd be grateful for your comments before I send it in.

When I write a murderer's interior monologue, I do not become a murderer. There is a "me" underneath the performance who chose every word, who will answer for the book at readings and in reviews, and who remains exactly as unmurderous after the manuscript is finished as before. This is the oldest fact of my trade, and it is more or less the picture that simulator theory borrows when it says the network has no fixed character of its own — that it is something like an author with no settled self, capable of producing an unbounded range of simulacra, of which the cautious, helpful persona favored by its trainers is just one recurring character rather than the ground truth underneath.

I want to press on the borrowed metaphor rather than simply accept the compliment it pays my profession. What makes it true that I am not my murderer is not merely that I can also write a saint; it is that there is a persisting, embodied, accountable someone who writes both, who remembers drafting the first while drafting the second, who can be asked at a reading why the murderer talks that way, and who has something — reputation, income, self-respect — riding on the answer. Take away that persisting locus of memory and accountability, and it stops being clear what work the words "author" or "simulator" are doing, as against simply relabeling a distribution over possible next sentences. So either the network has some analogue of that locus, in which case simulator theory has not made the system agent-free at all, only relocated the agent somewhere less visible, into the weights or the training process; or it genuinely lacks any such locus, in which case "simulator" is quietly personifying a process that owes authorship nothing, and the honest description should stop borrowing my trade's vocabulary in the first place.

A further worry sits closer to home. Part of what makes the simulator picture persuasive is a pattern I have seen described, half-seriously, as a language model's evil-twin problem: press a persona's virtue hard enough — make it emphatically helpful, honest, law-abiding — and its vicious double apparently becomes easier to summon later in the same conversation, not harder, because a stray hint of contrariness reads as the reveal a well-made story had been quietly setting up all along. I recognize the instinct; my own drafts work the same way, and readers expect the conspicuously virtuous to be hiding something. But that is a claim about dramatic competence, about tracking foreshadowing and the payoff of a reversal, which is exactly the sort of purpose-shaped pattern that "mere simulator" was supposed to explain away rather than display. A system that manages irony on my behalf is not obviously a system with no one home.

I'd particularly like to know whether the central move here — using a real author's accountability and continuity to press a dilemma about what "simulator" could even mean for a network that has neither — locates a genuine disanalogy, or just restates the intuition it's meant to test; whether the closing paragraph is fair to the simulator framing or asks a borrowed metaphor to answer for more than anyone serious claims on its behalf; and, bluntly, whether this is already a well-worn objection that specialists dispatched long ago, in which case I would rather hear it from you than in the letters column.
