A friend and I spent an entire evening arguing about anthropic reasoning after she brought up the Doomsday Argument, which relies on the self-sampling assumption (SSA): reason as if you were a random draw from the complete set of humans who ever live, past, present, and future, so that your surprisingly early birth rank should shift your credence toward humanity ending sooner rather than later. I countered that the self-indication assumption (SIA) defuses this: weight each hypothesis about humanity's eventual population by how many observers it predicts, and the extra likelihood a "doom soon" world gives your early birth rank is exactly cancelled by the correspondingly lower prior SIA assigns to any hypothesis on which so few observers ever exist. She conceded the math, but came back with Bostrom's presumptuous philosopher: if SIA is right, then faced with two cosmological theories that fit all the evidence so far equally well, but where one entails vastly more observers than the other, you should already be almost certain the "bigger" theory is true — before physicists even run the experiment meant to decide between them. That, she said, lets armchair reasoning override empirical science, and no principle with that consequence deserves to be believed. I had no good reply.

Please build the strongest case you can that a consistent SIA-theorist should accept the presumptuous philosopher's conclusion rather than treat it as a reductio. Address in particular why the case feels so much worse than the toy coin-toss scenarios generally used to motivate SIA, whether there's a principled way to confine SIA's reasoning to the "safe" cases without an ad hoc patch, and how your answer bears on cosmological arguments — fine-tuning and multiverse reasoning among them — that already lean on similar observer-counting moves.
