I'm one of two student representatives on my high school's curriculum committee. For years, our school has run an optional semester away in which small groups of juniors do wilderness travel and community-based service instead of regular classes. Nearly everyone who completes it says the experience reshaped what they care about and that they're glad it happened, yet also admits, when pressed, that an honest preview beforehand would not have persuaded their earlier self to enroll. The administration is now considering making the program mandatory for all juniors rather than an elective that students apply for. Half the committee argues this is obviously right: only alumni are in any position to judge the program's worth, and it's paternalistic to let an untransformed fifteen-year-old veto something almost everyone ends up grateful for. The other half argues this gets things backwards: you cannot consent to a change in the very values you'd use to judge it, and alumni testimony comes only from people the program has already remade. Could you draft the short memo I'll bring to our next meeting? It should recommend a concrete policy with a rollout plan for next year's juniors, and explain why more disclosure alone can't settle the dispute, rather than treating this as a simple contest between paternalism and autonomy.
