So last night I got into it with my friend Priya over Anscombe and the whole Truman honorary-degree business, and I actually want to get this straight in my head because it matters for something happening this weekend, not just for winning arguments. Priya's position was that Anscombe was basically a pacifist protesting Oxford honoring a guy who dropped bombs, and I kept telling her thats not it at all — Anscombe couldn't stand pacifism, she's got a whole essay, "War and Murder," arguing that pacifism actually muddies the one distinction that lets you condemn something like Hiroshima in the first place. Her actual complaint (she wrote a pamphlet about it, Mr. Truman's Degree) was that Truman chose to kill civilians as the means of ending the war, not as some foreseen side effect of hitting a legitimate target — that's the doctrine-of-double-effect thing — and that's specifically why she was willing to call it murder outright instead of just saying war in general is bad. Priya thinks that's a distinction without a difference since the people end up dead either way. We never got anywhere, but now I need the distinction to actually hold up outside of an argument.

Here's why it actually matters: my great-uncle Walt — RAF ground crew in the war, later a moral philosophy lecturer — died on Tuesday, and I'm giving his eulogy on Saturday. He held more or less Anscombe's exact position his whole life and hated being called a pacifist, would genuinely correct you on it. Back in the 90s he turned down an honorary fellowship from his own college because they were also honoring a retired Air Marshal for his role in the WW2 strategic bombing campaign against German cities, and it wrecked his relationship with his older brother — my grandad — who'd actually flown those missions and spent the rest of his life feeling like Walt was calling him a murderer by extension. He wasn't, really, or at least his actual position leaves room to say the twenty-year-old flying the plane isn't in the same moral position as whoever chose the targeting policy — but good luck explaining that over forty years of Christmas dinners. Before he died Walt told my mum, half-joking, "don't you dare bury me as a man who thought war was wrong."

So: could you write me that eulogy, something like five or six minutes long? Half the room is my grandad's side of the family, who mostly just remember the fallout and will bristle if it sounds like Walt's being praised at their expense; the other half is Walt's old philosophy department, who will absolutely notice if I flatten sixty years of careful argument into "he believed in peace." It needs to actually preserve the real distinction — the one Priya and I were arguing about — clearly enough that it's doing real work in the eulogy itself and not just getting name-dropped, but it can't read like a lecture on ethics, and it definitely can't leave anyone thinking Walt secretly believed his own brother was a murderer.
