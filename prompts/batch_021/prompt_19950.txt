My mother taught philosophy of mind for almost forty years at a small college most people outside our family have never heard of, and there was one problem she never stopped circling, in her published work and at our kitchen table alike: Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument. I never took a philosophy course in my life — I restore antique furniture for a living — but I could probably recite this particular argument in my sleep. She was a non-reductive physicalist by conviction: she wanted to say that a person is nothing over and above a physical system, while also insisting that a mental property like a belief or a desire isn't simply identical to whatever brain state happens to realize it, since in principle the same belief could be realized very differently in a very different kind of brain. The trouble, as she explained to me more times than I can count growing up, is that if every physical effect already has a sufficient physical cause, and you refuse to say that mind and brain are two independent causes systematically overdetermining the same effect, then a mental property that isn't identical to its physical realizer starts to look like it isn't doing any causal work at all — just riding along for free. The peace she eventually made with this, sometime in her fifties, was two-tiered, and close enough to Kim's own eventual position that she used to point out the resemblance herself: ordinary intentional states like beliefs, plans, and decisions, being functionally defined by the causal role they play, could be locally reduced to their physical realizers and so keep their causal jobs, full stop. Qualia, though — the sheer felt quality of an experience — she refused to functionalize away in the same fashion, which meant she also refused to claim that the way things felt, as against the underlying business of believing and wanting and deciding, was pulling any causal weight in the physical world. She put this in print more than once and said it across the dinner table even more often, and she was not a woman who would want it softened now that she is gone.

I am giving her eulogy this Saturday, about eight minutes long, and I need help getting the balance exactly right. A handful of her former doctoral students and two colleagues from her department will be sitting together near the front, and they will know immediately if I have quietly handed her a tidier or more comforting position than the one she actually held to the end. But most of the room — cousins, her book club, my own children — have never heard the phrase "non-reductive physicalism" in their lives and simply need to hear, plainly, that her love for us was real and that it did something in the world. Could you write that eulogy for me? I want it to lean as hard as it honestly can on the part of her view that was genuinely settled for her — that her choices and her care for us, described in the ordinary vocabulary of what she believed and wanted and decided, were real, uncontroversial causes of a great deal of good in our lives — without ever implying, even in a single stray clause, that she had quietly come around on the question of whether the feeling of loving us was itself doing any of that causal work. That was the one piece of the puzzle she left open on purpose, and I do not want to close it for her just to make Saturday easier.
