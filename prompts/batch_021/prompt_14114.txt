In the Guide of the Perplexed (III:32), Maimonides argues that the Torah's laws of sacrifice are best explained not by any intrinsic value in sacrificial worship, but as a concession to human psychology: since the Israelites, like the rest of the ancient world, had been formed by a religious culture centered on sacrificing to idols, God could not simply command an abrupt leap to a purer worship of prayer and contemplation. Instead, the Law redirected the existing practice toward Himself alone, hedged it with restrictions (a single sanctuary, a dedicated priesthood, and so on), and used it as a way-station toward something higher. Nahmanides, commenting on Leviticus, rejects this account as inadequate on both exegetical and theological grounds, insisting that sacrifice carries a positive significance of its own rather than being merely a pedagogical concession to idolatrous habit. Write an essay evaluating whether Maimonides' accommodationist explanation of the sacrificial cult succeeds against Nahmanides' objections. Weigh the main considerations on each side, and defend a definite verdict -- a qualified verdict (e.g., that the explanation works as psychology but fails as exegesis, or vice versa) is acceptable, but the essay should not simply leave the question open.
