I'm a novelist a few drafts into a book with a scene, set in eleventh-century Isfahan, where a physician-philosopher modeled loosely on Avicenna leads a skeptical student through the "flying man": the thought experiment in which a man created whole and suspended in mid-air, sensing nothing, not even his own limbs, would still know that he exists. My philosopher uses this to convince the student that the soul is not the body. Before committing to how the scene argues its case, I sent the draft to two friends, Noor, who teaches medieval Islamic philosophy, and Callum, who works on philosophy of mind, and when the three of us got on a call to talk it through, they ended up arguing with each other more than with me.

Noor said the scene is fair to Avicenna: (1) strip away every sensation and the man would still know "I exist," which shows immediately that the self isn't the body, so my philosopher is entitled to treat the experiment as settling the matter. Callum objected that (2) the experiment alone shows only that self-awareness doesn't depend on awareness of the body; inferring that the self therefore isn't the body is the same move as inferring you can't be the masked man because you don't recognize him. Noor answered that (3) Avicenna uses the experiment as one part of a larger case for the soul's immateriality made elsewhere in his work, so whatever it shows in isolation, its job there is to establish dualism. Callum replied that (4) a premise's role in a larger case isn't the same as its entailing that case's conclusion on its own, and that blurring the two is exactly what makes "Avicenna's cogito" a misleading label, since Descartes built his cogito to stand alone while this is one plank among several.

I couldn't settle it myself, and I'd rather not put a bad argument in a serious character's mouth. Could you go through the exchange move by move, say who has the better of it at each step, and then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing about how the flying man argument actually works?
