so for my "Material Objects" seminar this semester (an upper-level metaphysics elective that's basically one whole term on van Inwagen's Material Beings) I have to turn in a short critical paper responding to his answer to the special composition question, and I think I've got a decent angle but want a sanity check before I actually write it up properly. Quick recap so the ask makes sense: his answer to the SCQ is that some simples compose something iff thier activity constitutes a Life, so organisms end up being basically the only composite objects that exist - everything else (chairs, stars, my laptop) is just simples "arranged x-wise" with no further object actually there. He knows this needs to handle vagueness, so theres a whole chapter arguing you can get vagueness in the number of things that exist without any vagueness in what there is, and he leans pretty heavily on cases like a single-celled organism undergoing fission (one Life ends, two begin, more or less at the moment it splits) to show composition can start and stop without anything metaphysically spooky going on.

My idea for the paper is that cellular slime molds (Dictyostelium, the "social amoeba" everyone studies for the cooperation/cheating stuff) are a nastier case than fission, and I don't think he obviously has the resources for it. Normally these things live as totally seperate, independent amoebae, each presumably its own Life. When food runs out they stream toward each other using cAMP signalling over several hours, pile up into a mound, form a migrating "slug" that moves toward light as one coordinated unit, and eventually differentiate into stalk cells (which die) vs spores (which survive) - full division of labor, plausibly counts as a Life by the end. So composition switches ON for a bunch of previously uncomposed simples. My claim is that's actually worse for him than fission, because fission has one fairly clean transition point, while aggregation is gradual along several independent axes at once (spatial integration, chemical coordination, loss of the ability to survive alone, developmental commitment to a fate) that don't cross their thresholds together, so theres no single moment to even localize the vagueness the way there is with fission. (There's also this extra wrinkle where the amoebae that aggregate aren't always genetically identical, so you get actual "cheater" cells inside the slug - not sure if that's worth getting into or just a distraction from the main point.)

Does this actually land as a real problem for organicism, or is there some resource in the book (or in responses to him i haven't gotten to yet) that handles gradual aggregation just as well as it handles fission? Also honestly not sure if this is a novel enough angle for a term paper or just an obvious move once you know a bit of developmental biology - would love blunt feedback on both, plus whether the cheater-cell aside is worth keeping or if I should just cut it and build the whole paper around the gradualism point instead.
