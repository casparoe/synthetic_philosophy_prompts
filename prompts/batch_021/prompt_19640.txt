Over drinks last winter, four friends and I decided we'd enter a marathon relay together this spring. We split the 26.2 miles into legs according to our strengths, built our long training runs around one another's paces and weekend availability, and pooled money for a single, non-refundable team entry fee. Nobody signed anything or extracted a promise from anyone; we simply talked it through one evening and each said we were in.

About six weeks ago, one teammate told the rest of us she's pulling out. Nothing dramatic prompted it: a new job now claims her weekends, and, as she put it, her situation changed, so her plan changed. She's even offered to cover whatever it costs to find and register a substitute, so none of us stands to lose money over this. And yet two of the other four of us feel oddly wronged by the decision, in a way that seems to outlast any practical inconvenience we could be compensated for. Her reasoning seems impeccable on its own terms — people revise plans all the time as circumstances change, and if she'd simply abandoned a solo race nobody would think twice — but something about having worked out this particular plan together, rather than alone, seems to change what she owed the rest of us once she said she was in.

I write about science and ideas for a living, and part of me suspects this maps onto some existing body of thought — something about what changes, if anything, once people commit to a course of action jointly rather than each on their own, and what any one of them is or isn't entitled to do unilaterally afterward. Is our teammate right that this is really no different from any other change of personal plan, just one that happens to inconvenience four people instead of none? Or is there a meaningful sense in which the four of us who remain are owed something here that a solo runner backing out of her own race would never owe anyone? I'd be grateful for help thinking through what's actually going on beneath this disagreement.
