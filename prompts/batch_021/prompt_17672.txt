In my high school philosophy elective this term, we spent a few weeks on George Berkeley, and I feel like I have a reasonably solid grip on the core of his immaterialism: the argument that "matter," understood as a mind-independent substratum underlying sensible qualities, is an incoherent notion, and that for an ordinary object to exist is just for it to be perceived, or at least perceivable by some mind. What I hadn't expected, and can't find much accessible discussion of, is that the very same Berkeley spent a great deal of energy in the 1730s writing The Querist, a long series of rhetorical questions on Irish economic policy, in which he suggests that money is not itself wealth but something more like a ticket or counter recording a claim on goods and labor, and that a country could in principle prosper through the "industry" of its people even with comparatively little actual gold or silver in circulation.

My teacher mentioned in passing that some scholars read this monetary view as more than an unrelated hobby of Berkeley's — as though his refusal to let money's value rest on being "backed" by some intrinsically valuable metal is, in some real philosophical sense, the economic cousin of his refusal to let bodies rest on an intrinsically existing material substance underneath their qualities. I would like to know whether there is an actual, defensible line of reasoning connecting these two views, rather than just a coincidental pun on the word "substance." Could you walk me through what, specifically, in Berkeley's metaphysics would have to do the work of supporting his claims about money, and also point out where you think the analogy is weakest, or might break down entirely?
