I am preparing a graduate seminar on the ethics of testimony and deception, and this week's session turns to one of Augustine's least-discussed works, the letter Contra Mendacium ("Against Lying"), written around 420 to the Spanish bishop Consentius. Consentius had written to Augustine describing, and apparently endorsing, a scheme by which trustworthy Catholics would infiltrate the underground Priscillianist sect by posing as converts to it, the better to expose and correct its members from within. Augustine's reply is a sustained argument that this would be wrong, whatever good end it served. I have been working through H. Browne's nineteenth-century translation, still the standard one reprinted in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers series, and while I can follow Augustine's overall verdict, one stretch of his reasoning has left me stuck, partly on account of the syntax and partly because I cannot reconstruct the principle that is doing the work.

Here is the passage, from sections 4 and 5 of the letter:

"...if, in order to attain to the discovery of them, we falsely give ourselves out for Priscillianists, because we shall praise their dogmas as though they were our own, whoso shall believe the same, will either be confirmed among them, or will be transferred to them in the meantime straightway... Whence it is gathered, that it is more pernicious, or to speak more mildly, that it is more perilous for Catholics to lie that they may catch heretics, than for heretics to lie that they may not be found out by Catholics.

Well then, let us set before our eyes a cunning spy as he makes up to the person whom he has already perceived to be a Priscillianist; he begins with Dictinius the bishop, and lyingly bepraises either his life, if he knew him, or his fame, if he knew him not; this is more tolerable thus far, because Dictinius is accounted to have been a Catholic, and to have been corrected of that error. Then, passing on to Priscillian (for this comes next in the art of lying), he shall make reverend mention of him, of an impious and detestable person, condemned for his nefarious wickedness and crimes! In which reverend mention, if haply the person for whom this sort of net is spread had not been a firm Priscillianist, by this preaching of him he will be confirmed... truly, this astuteness of him who tells the lie slays the soul of him who believes it, or, that being slain already, doth in the same death sink, and hold it down."

What I cannot pin down is the exact shape of the asymmetry Augustine wants to establish. He holds that it is worse for the Catholic spy to lie in order to catch a Priscillianist than for the Priscillianist to lie in order to escape detection, yet the asymmetry cannot simply consist in one side deceiving and the other not, since on Augustine's own account both parties are equally lying and equally intend to deceive. My best guess is that he is quietly relying on some further principle about the harm done by the content of what is falsely asserted, over and above the bare fact of assertion-with-intent-to-deceive that he treats elsewhere as the very essence of a lie: a false profession of heresy is worse than a false profession of orthodoxy because of what a bystander might come to believe as a result, and orthodox belief cannot damn while heretical belief can. Is that the right way to reconstruct the argument, and if so, how well does it sit with the more austere, intention-centered definition of lying he is otherwise committed to? I would also welcome help simply parsing the "cunning spy" illustration in section 5: I can follow the sequence of events well enough, but I am not sure which feature of the spy's conduct is meant to be doing the actual moral work, as against merely coloring the example.
