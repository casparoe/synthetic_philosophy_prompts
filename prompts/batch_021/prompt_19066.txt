I'm TAing an undergraduate seminar on Roman philosophical prose, and this term we spent two sessions on Cicero's *Cato Maior de Senectute*. For the take-home midterm I set an essay question about the dialogue's argumentative structure, and I now have a stack of answers to grade. Since I'd like my grading to be as consistent and well-justified as possible across the whole stack, I want a rigorous, independent check on one representative submission before I finalize scores — something more careful than the kind of quick, holistic read a busy grader might otherwise give it.

Here is the question as I posed it: "In the *Cato Maior de Senectute*, Cato is made to answer four standard complaints against old age. For each of the four, briefly reconstruct his rebuttal, citing at least one concrete example or anecdote he uses along the way. Then, in a closing paragraph, assess whether Cato's answer to the fourth complaint (the nearness of death) argues in the same register and with the same kind of confidence as his answers to the first three, or whether something changes."

And here, verbatim, is the student's answer:

"Cato identifies four charges typically leveled against old age: that it withdraws us from active life, that it weakens the body, that it takes away nearly all our pleasures, and that it brings us close to death.

Against the first charge, Cato argues that the affairs suited to old age are not those requiring physical vigor but those requiring judgment, authority, and experience: the deliberations of statesmen and the counsel of the Senate, whose very name he traces to *senex*. He points to Fabius Maximus, whose patient strategy against Hannibal succeeded because it relied on seasoned judgment rather than youthful impetuosity.

Against the second charge, Cato concedes that strength declines, but insists that the old should use whatever strength remains rather than demand the strength of youth, and that moderate exercise can preserve a good deal of vigor besides. He holds up Milo of Croton, the famous wrestler, as an example worth imitating here: watching younger athletes train and weeping over his own withered arms, Milo shows exactly the honest, undefended reckoning with physical decline that Cato wants an old man to have, in place of a false bravado about strength he no longer possesses.

Against the third charge, Cato turns the alleged loss into a gain: bodily pleasures are, on his account, actively dangerous, since they cloud judgment and drive people to crimes and betrayals, a point he supports by relating a speech of Archytas of Tarentum on the perils of pleasure, passed down to him by his old host Nearchus. In pleasure's place, old age offers the pleasures of conversation and, above all, of agriculture, which Cato praises at length from his own experience on his farm.

Against the fourth charge, Cato argues that death should not be feared, since either the soul is immortal, in which case death is a welcome release to a better existence among the great souls of the past, or the soul perishes along with the body, in which case there is simply nothing left to fear. Cato treats this disjunction as effectively closing the question: since Stoic physics holds that the soul is a fragment of the same rational fire that pervades and governs the whole cosmos, its survival after the body follows more or less automatically, and this confidence is continuous with the confidence Cato has shown throughout the dialogue. Old age, on his telling, has a ready and complete answer for every complaint brought against it, right down to the last one.

On the whole, the dialogue's argumentative strategy is consistent from beginning to end: each of the four charges receives a specific, well-evidenced rebuttal, and the treatment of death is simply the last and most complete instance of the same method."
