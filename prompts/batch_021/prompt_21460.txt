I am a junior at a Catholic high school, and this semester I was elected as a student member of our Honor Board, the body that hears cases when someone is accused of violating the "truthfulness" clause of our code of conduct. Coincidentally, in theology class we just spent two weeks on Augustine's treatises "On Lying" and "Against Lying," and our teacher kept stressing how absolute his position actually is: he sorts lies onto a scale from most to least grave depending on who is harmed and who benefits, yet he still refuses to call even the mildest, best-intentioned lie permissible. He even works through the case of the Hebrew midwives who lied to Pharaoh to save Israelite infants, and argues that God rewarded them for their mercy, not for the lie itself, which he insists was still a sin. I did not think any of this would matter outside of a quiz, but now I am on a board that actually has to decide something like it.

Here is the case: a sophomore named Maya was asked directly by our dean whether she had seen her lab partner, Jordan, looking at a phone during the chemistry final. She said no, even though she had, because our handbook calls for an automatic one-semester suspension for any cheating offense with "no exceptions for context," and she knew Jordan had been up all night dealing with a family emergency and was not thinking clearly. A proctor reported Jordan independently, so Jordan's case is being handled separately; what is actually in front of the Board is only Maya's lie to the dean. Our handbook says students must "answer questions from staff truthfully," but it says nothing about whether motive, or the seriousness of what is being covered up, should change how a lie is treated, and the Board is split between members who think Maya's lie was basically harmless and members who think lying to an administrator during an official investigation is automatically the more serious kind of violation.

Could you draft the short memo I will bring to our meeting this Thursday? It should give a clear recommendation for Maya's case specifically (dismissal, warning, probation, or whatever else our handbook allows, with reasoning a skeptical board member could actually push back on), and it should work through how Augustine's own framework would treat her case, including why her good motive would not get her off the hook for him even though it obviously matters to some degree, and what, if anything, would distinguish her lie from a more self-interested one. Please also take the strongest argument for going easy on her seriously, rather than mentioning it only to set it aside.
