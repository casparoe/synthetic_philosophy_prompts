QUESTION: The cognitive-penetrability debate asks whether the content of perceptual experience -- not just where attention happens to be directed -- can be influenced by a perceiver's beliefs, desires, or expectations.
(a) Explain Fodor's argument, from the persistence of illusions like the Müller-Lyer, for the claim that perception is "informationally encapsulated."
(b) Give one empirical example that has been offered as evidence for genuine cognitive penetration, and explain why Firestone and Scholl think this kind of evidence falls short of establishing it.
(c) Does the fact that perception integrates information across modalities -- e.g., the McGurk effect, where what you hear is altered by watching the speaker's lips -- count as an instance of cognitive penetration? Explain your answer.

STUDENT ANSWER:
(a) Fodor's argument starts from the fact that illusions like the Müller-Lyer persist even once we know, and fully believe, that the two lines are the same length. If background knowledge could freely seep into perception, the illusion should dissolve the moment we learn the truth, but it doesn't. Fodor concludes that cognition in general is informationally encapsulated: our beliefs and the mechanisms that fix perceptual content are walled off from one another, so what we believe cannot literally change what we see.
(b) A good example is Bhalla and Proffitt's finding that hills look steeper to people who are tired, elderly, or wearing a heavy backpack, which suggests perceived slant is influenced by the anticipated energetic cost of climbing. Firestone and Scholl argue this isn't decisive because the slant judgments in these studies are usually explicit verbal or numerical estimates rather than the perceptual experience itself, so the effect may be showing up at the level of judgment or response bias rather than showing that the visual experience of the hill actually changed.
(c) Yes -- the McGurk effect is a clean case of cognitive penetration, since what you consciously hear changes depending on purely visual information from the speaker's mouth. That's a cognitive state (the visual perception of the lips) reaching in and altering the content of another perceptual modality, which is exactly the kind of top-down influence the penetrability camp is describing.
