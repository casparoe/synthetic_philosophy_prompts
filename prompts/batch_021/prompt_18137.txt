In my IB Theory of Knowledge class, when we reached the unit on History as an Area of Knowledge, our teacher gave us a handout summarizing Carl Hempel's argument that historical explanations, underneath their surface, work the same way scientific explanations do: by showing that the event to be explained follows from some general law together with the relevant initial conditions, even when historians never state the law outright. A few of us argued about this after class, because it seems to clash badly with how historians actually write. When someone explains why the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand led to the outbreak of the First World War, they don't seem to be quietly invoking a law of history the way a physicist invokes a law of motion — they're telling a story in which the assassination only becomes "the spark that started the war" retrospectively, in light of everything that followed it. One of my classmates said this is essentially the objection that philosophers like William Dray, Arthur Danto, and Hayden White raised against Hempel, and that there is a whole literature on whether narrative is a distinct, non-reducible form of historical understanding rather than a dressed-up covering-law explanation — but neither of us understood the arguments well enough to know who had the better of it.

Could you write an essay that works through this debate properly? I would like it to first explain what Hempel's covering-law model actually claims, and why he rejected the older idea that history is a fundamentally different, "idiographic" kind of inquiry not in the business of finding laws at all; then lay out the strongest versions of the narrativist objections — Dray's point that historians typically explain actions by showing they were the reasonable thing to do given the agent's own situation and beliefs, rather than by citing a law linking situation-types to action-types, and the further, more radical worries that Danto and White raise about narrative form itself doing irreducible interpretive work. Rather than just presenting both sides and stopping there, please take a position — whether historical explanation basically is covering-law explanation in disguise, is something essentially narrative and non-law-like, or is some specific combination of the two — and defend it, since I am seriously considering this as a topic for my extended essay and want to understand the real shape of the disagreement before I commit to it.
