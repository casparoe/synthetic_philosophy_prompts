I'm about two-thirds through a first draft of a heist novel—five strangers, one impossible job, the whole ensemble-cast setup—and I've been trying to make the "we're actually doing this together" moments between my characters feel like more than just vibes. So a few weeks back I went down a rabbit hole reading about "collective intentionality" and joint action, thinking it'd help me write the group's decision-making so it actually holds together, and now I've got three different accounts rattling around in my head that I can't cleanly seperate from each other.

From what I can tell: Bratman wants shared intention to reduce to each person's own individual intentions plus common knowledge and meshing sub-plans—no group-level anything required. Searle agrees there's no "group mind" but thinks that's not enough, and says each individual has a primitive "we intend" state in their own head that can't be broken down into an "I intend" plus beliefs about the others. Then Gilbert goes further and argues that when people jointly commit to something, a "plural subject" comes into being and generates real obligations between the members, ones that don't reduce to anybody's personal intentions at all. Could you actually spell out what's substantively different between these three views—not just summarize each one back to back? And to make it concrete: in the scene I'm drafting, one of my five crew members wants to bail two minutes before the job starts. Does it actually matter, philosophically, which of these three accounts is correct, for whether the other four are owed something by her, or is she just breaking an ordinary promise like any other? I'd like to get the metaphysics at least defensible rather than hand-waving it the way most thrillers do.
