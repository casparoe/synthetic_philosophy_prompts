I've been working through David Lewis's papers on objective chance for a graduate reading group on the metaphysics of probability, and I keep getting stuck on the problem he dubs the "Big Bad Bug" in "Humean Supervenience Debugged." I follow the basic shape of the difficulty and of Lewis's own fix, but I'm not convinced the fix earns its keep, and I'd like to see the skeptical case made out rigorously rather than piece it together myself from secondary summaries.

Here is the setup as I understand it. The Principal Principle says that your credence in a proposition A, conditional on learning that the objective chance of A is x together with any admissible evidence, should equal x, where admissible evidence is roughly evidence whose bearing on A runs entirely through its bearing on the chance of A (historical information up to the relevant time counts; "crystal ball" information about how things actually turn out does not). Lewis was also committed to Humean supervenience about chance: chances at a time must supervene on the pattern of occurrent, non-modal fact spread across the whole of history, including everything that happens afterward, since the true chances are whatever "best system" analysis delivers when fitted to the entire actual course of events, past and future. This has the awkward consequence that a chance hypothesis can assign positive chance to its own undermining: if a coin has an objective chance of one-half of landing heads on each of a long series of independent tosses, there is some small but positive chance, according to that very hypothesis, that the coin lands heads on every single toss in the series—a run that would make a quite different, and much less flattering, hypothesis the best system, and so would make the original hypothesis false. Lewis argued that this is not merely an uncomfortable coincidence but a genuine formal inconsistency in the original Principal Principle, and he responded by adopting the "New Principle," which he credits to Ned Hall and Michael Thau: instead of conditionalizing on the proposition that some target proposition has chance x, you conditionalize on a proposition that specifies the entire chance function of the world, and the value you defer to is that function's chance of A conditional on your admissible evidence, rather than its bare unconditional chance of A.

Please argue for the position that this fix is philosophically inadequate: that whatever it does to restore formal consistency, the New Principle fails to preserve the rationale that made deference to chance compelling in the first place, so that the Big Bad Bug is best read as a real cost of combining Humean supervenience with any recognizable norm of chance-credence deference, rather than as a puzzle that has actually been laid to rest.
