At work I recently used a model checker whose semantics are built on so-called "Kripke structures," and looking into the name pulled me into a corner of modal metaphysics I hadn't expected: the dispute between David Lewis's counterpart theory and the older transworld-identity view of how a person relates to other possible worlds. As I understand it, Lewis holds that every concrete individual is confined to a single world, so that when we say someone could have lived differently, we're strictly talking about a distinct-but-similar counterpart doing so in another world, never about that very person; transworld-identity theorists deny this, insisting that one and the same individual, picked out directly rather than matched by resemblance, can genuinely be part of more than one possible world. Kripke himself pressed a pointed objection against Lewis using a candidate named Humphrey who lost an election: Humphrey would get no comfort from learning that a merely similar person wins in some other world, because what he cared about was himself winning, not a stand-in. I'd like to see this dispute dramatized rather than summarized. Please write me a short story, roughly 1,000-1,500 words, in which a character is reunited with a version of someone they lost, arrived somehow from a nearby possible world, and let the emotional and practical climax turn on a concrete stake -- a shared debt, a marriage, a child's custody, anything with real consequences -- that only makes sense if the returned person is truly, numerically the one who left, rather than a stranger who merely resembles them down to the last detail. Don't let any character stop to explain the two positions to the reader; the plot and the choices people make under pressure should carry the philosophical weight, and it should be clear by the end which metaphysical picture the story has quietly committed to.
