My great-aunt Rosalind died three weeks ago at ninety-four, and I have somehow ended up writing her eulogy for the memorial this coming weekend, mostly because I am the family member whose job involves producing coherent sentences on a deadline, even though mine are usually in Python rather than English. Rosalind spent almost fifty years on the council of a small local psychical research society that explicitly modeled itself on the original Society for Psychical Research in London, and she kept a biography of Henry Sidgwick, that society's first president, on her nightstand for as long as anyone in the family can remember. Her standard explanation for admiring him so much, as best my mother can reconstruct it, went something like this: Sidgwick had ended his major work on ethics admitting he could not actually prove that reason requires a person to be moral rather than simply self-interested, and had suggested that only a genuinely moral universe -- one in which the two ultimately coincided, perhaps through some form of survival after death -- could close that gap; and rather than either assuming this was true or dismissing the whole question, he had spent much of his later career trying, empirically and rather unglamorously, to find out. Rosalind apparently regarded her own decades of sitting in on mediums and reviewing case notes, taken up not long after the man she had planned to marry died young, as continuing exactly that project, and by everyone's account she remained, like Sidgwick himself, unconvinced and essentially agnostic right up to the end, despite wanting very badly for the evidence to come out the other way.

The memorial audience is going to split awkwardly down the middle: a dozen or so of Rosalind's fellow researchers, who will notice immediately if I turn her into either a secret spiritualist or a tidy debunker, and several dozen relatives who have never heard of Sidgwick and mainly want to be told that she is "at peace" and perhaps reunited with her fiancé. I don't want to hand either group a version of her she would not recognize. Could you write the eulogy for me? I would like it to take her actual, uncomfortable intellectual position seriously enough to build the whole speech around it, rather than resolving it into the kind of easy consolation she spent her entire adult life declining to accept without evidence.
