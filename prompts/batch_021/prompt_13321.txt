So a couple of coworkers got into this argument at a happy hour last week and I havent been able to stop thinking about it. One of them (did a philosophy minor, of course) claimed that if you built an AI with the exact same functional/causal organization as a human brain -- same relationships between inputs, internal states, and outputs, just running on silicon instead of neurons -- it would necessarily be in real pain when it's "in pain," not just faking it. The other coworker, more of an old-school hardware guy, pushed back hard with basically "a mind just literally is a brain, full stop," and then somebody threw around the phrase "multiple realizability" and everyone got confused about what that even settles.

Could you write the dialogue these two should have actually had? One character is a committed functionalist, the other a committed mind-brain identity theorist, and they're hashing out whether a silicon system that's functionally identical to a human could really be in pain. Don't let the identity theorist just get steamrolled by the multiple-realizability point and concede -- give them an actual comeback (e.g. biting the bullet with a species-by-species version of the identity claim instead of one universal law tying pain to a single physical state). And don't let the functionalist off easy either -- make them genuinely grapple with the objection that their view seems to commit them to saying something absurd counts as concious too, like an entire country's population relaying messages to each other by radio. Both characters need to be sharp and actually responding to each other's latest point, not just restating their opening position -- and it's fine if it doesn't end in a tidy resolution.
