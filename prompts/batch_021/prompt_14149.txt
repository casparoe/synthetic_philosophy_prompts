I spent most of my career as an evolutionary biologist modelling the population dynamics of cooperative behaviour, so the Prisoner's Dilemma and its iterated cousin were old friends of mine long before I retired. Since retiring I have had the leisure to read more widely in game theory and decision theory proper, and one strand of that literature has lately caught my attention in a way the biological material never quite prepared me for: so-called "program equilibrium," sometimes discussed under the heading "open-source game theory." The basic idea, which I gather traces back to a 1988 paper by J. V. Howard and was put on firmer footing by Moshe Tennenholtz around 2004, is that if players submit programs rather than actions — programs that can inspect one another's source code before deciding what to do — then mutual cooperation in a genuinely one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma can be an equilibrium outcome rather than a fluke of repeated play or reputation effects. What really stopped me in my tracks, though, was a later refinement, associated with people working around the Machine Intelligence Research Institute, that uses Löb's theorem from provability logic to construct a program (I believe called "FairBot") that can be formally proven to cooperate with any other program that itself provably cooperates with it, apparently without collapsing into the infinite regress of "I cooperate if it cooperates if it cooperates if..." one would naively expect. I recall there is a whole small menagerie of these constructions — a "PrudentBot," for instance, that cooperates with FairBot and with itself but still defects against a program that simply cooperates unconditionally, so as not to be exploited — and this only sharpened my puzzlement about what exactly is being demonstrated.

I can follow the technical machinery well enough — self-reference and fixed-point constructions are not wholly unfamiliar to someone who has read a bit of mathematical logic — but I keep going back and forth on its philosophical import. On one reading, this seems to show something substantive: that rational, self-interested agents can secure the cooperative outcome without appeal to repetition, reputation, or third-party enforcement, purely through the internal structure of their reasoning. On another reading, I suspect all the real work has simply been relocated into the assumption that each program has certain, verified access to the other's exact source code — an assumption that, examined squarely, seems to smuggle back in precisely the kind of guaranteed mutual transparency that made cooperation trivial to secure in the first place, so that the "solution" is really only a restatement of the problem in more technical dress. I would like your help settling this. Please write an essay that lays out the strongest case for each side — that program equilibrium, including its Löbian refinements, constitutes a genuine solution to the problem of securing rational cooperation without external enforcement, versus the view that it merely relocates that same problem into an idealized assumption about source-code transparency without resolving anything deeper about trust or commitment — and then commit to a definite conclusion. A qualified verdict is acceptable (something like "it is a genuine formal result, but one whose philosophical significance is much smaller than its proponents suggest, for such-and-such reason" would count), but the essay should not simply leave the two readings balanced against one another.
