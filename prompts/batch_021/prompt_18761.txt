For a graduate seminar on the logic of obligation, I'd like a rigorous dialogue on whether genuine moral dilemmas — cases where an agent is obligated to do A and obligated to do B, yet cannot do both — are so much as possible. One speaker, in the spirit of Bernard Williams and Ruth Barcan Marcus, should defend their reality by appeal to the aptness of residual guilt or regret toward the option not taken, on the grounds that this is best explained by the forgone obligation having been genuine rather than merely apparent. The other, in the spirit of Alan Donagan and Earl Conee, should deny that any dilemma could be genuine, holding onto both the agglomeration principle (if one ought to do A and ought to do B, one ought to do both) and the thesis that obligation entails possibility, and explaining away apparent dilemmas as artifacts of prior wrongdoing or of conflating prima facie duties with all-things-considered ones. Have them work through one case in depth, then close by pinning down exactly which of those two principles — if either — the first speaker is willing to give up in order to preserve the reality of the conflict.
