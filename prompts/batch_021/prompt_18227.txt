I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but I've been falling down a rabbit hole of old LessWrong and Effective Altruism Forum posts on decision theory as it applies to AI safety, and I keep running into a pair of ideas I can't quite get a stable grip on: "acausal threats" and the proposed defense of adopting "surrogate goals." As far as I can piece together, the worry is that a sufficiently advanced agent could be coerced by another agent with which it has no causal contact whatsoever — no messages, no shared history, nothing — purely because each can predict the other's behavior well enough (through simulation, or logical inference about what a rational agent in that position would do) for a threat to functionally "land" even though it's never causally transmitted, or in some framings, never actually carried out at all. The proposed fix, surrogate goals, has the agent deliberately commit to caring about some cheap, substitute objective exactly as fiercely as it cares about its real values, so that a purely instrumental threatener — one who only wants compliance, not harm for its own sake — gets redirected into "threatening" the worthless substitute instead, leaving the agent's actual values untouched.

Something about this strikes me as both ingenious and a little too good to be true, and I'd like to see the strongest version of the disagreement laid out rather than just the pitch. Could you write me a dialogue between two decision theorists, having it out over coffee after an AI safety workshop — one of whom has come to believe that deliberately cultivating a surrogate goal is a rational and stable way to defang acausal threats, and the other who thinks it's a trick that never really solves the underlying commitment problem and may even introduce new vulnerabilities? I don't want either character to be a pushover: the skeptic should press on things like whether a sufficiently sophisticated threatener can simply see through or route around the substitution, and whether being "the kind of agent who manufactures fake values under pressure" is even coherent or stable in the first place, while the advocate should have real, specific responses rather than just restating the original proposal. I'd like them to actually shift the state of the argument as they go, rather than talking past each other.
