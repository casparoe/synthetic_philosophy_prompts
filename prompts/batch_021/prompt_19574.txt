I'm the student representative on my university's Therapeutic Use Exemption Panel, the body that decides whether student-athletes can keep taking medications on our conference's banned-substances list when they have a genuine medical need for them. This term I'm also taking a seminar on philosophy of medicine, where we've spent several weeks on the therapy/enhancement distinction: Boorse's biostatistical account of disease as a statistical deviation from species-typical functioning, Daniels' use of that baseline to argue that justice requires treating impairments but creates no comparable obligation to enhance people beyond "normal," and the standard worries about whether that baseline can really do the work it's asked to do.

A case in front of the panel right now feels like a real-world stress test of exactly that worry, rather than a textbook illustration of it. One of our sprinters was diagnosed with adult ADHD three weeks before conference championships, after a single session with a telehealth psychiatrist she'd found online, and was prescribed Adderall; she's asking us to approve an exemption so she can keep taking it through the rest of the season. About half the panel wants to approve it more or less automatically, on the grounds that we defer to a treating clinician's diagnosis and shouldn't be in the business of practicing medicine ourselves. The other half points out that attention and impulsivity don't come with a natural cutoff separating "disordered" from "normal but inattentive," that stimulants reliably improve reaction time and delay fatigue in people who don't have ADHD at all, and that a diagnosis obtained this quickly and this close to championships is at least as consistent with performance-seeking as with treatment-seeking. Our bylaws instruct the panel to approve exemptions for "legitimate treatment of a diagnosed condition" but not for anything that "confers a competitive advantage beyond restoring normal function" — language that simply assumes there's a fact of the matter about where her normal function lies.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate to the panel before we vote this Thursday? I need it to land on one recommendation — approve, deny, or approve with specific conditions (a second, independent evaluation; a delayed effective date; a non-stimulant alternative; whatever you think is best justified) — and to give reasoning that coaches and administrators without philosophical training could actually follow and defend to the athlete and to her competitors. Please don't let the memo quietly borrow the bylaws' assumption that "restoring normal function" picks out a clean baseline; if that assumption is doing less work than it looks like it's doing, say so, and show how you're deciding the case anyway. And whichever way you come down, engage honestly with the strongest thing that can be said for the other side: the cost of denying or delaying treatment to a student who may well have a genuine, if recently diagnosed, disorder, or the cost to her competitors of letting the exemption stand.
