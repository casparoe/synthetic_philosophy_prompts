So I'm in a political philosophy seminar this semester and we just spent like three weeks slogging through Rawls's political liberalism stuff -- public reason, overlapping consensus, the "burdens of judgment," all of it. Our professor mentioned in passing that Rawls's whole apparatus was basically built for a fairly polite, mid-century kind of religious pluralism (mainline Protestants, Catholics, Jews, all disagreeing about deep stuff but agreeing on the constitutional basics) and that he never really had to grapple with a comprehensive doctrine that just flat out rejects the premise of church-state seperation and wants to use ordinary democratic politics to get rid of it. Which got me thinking about the current wave of "Christian nationalism" in the US -- politicians openly saying the country was founded as a Christian nation and the constitution should reflect that, states passing laws requiring the Ten Commandments to be posted in every public school classroom (Louisiana did this in 2024, I think a couple other states are trying to copy it now), that kind of thing. Rawls died in 2002, so he obviously never weighed in on any of this directly.

What I'm actually curious about is less "would Rawls have approved of this" (obviously not) and more: does his own theory give him a principled way to rule a movement like this out as an "unreasonable" comprehensive doctrine, or does he basically just have to stipulate it away -- which would kind of prove the critics right that "reasonable" secretly just means "already agrees with me"? And separately, is there anything actually left of "overlapping consensus" as a stabilizing force once a decent chunk of citizens openly reject the premise the whole thing's built on, or does his theory just go quiet once that happens? I'd love to hear how you'd reconstruct his likely response using the actual moving parts of his view rather than just "Rawls liked secular government and tolerance."
