So I'm about 40k words into a novel that's loosely based on my grandfather, and I've hit a snag that's turned into a whole family argument rather than just a plot problem. He made a handful of big choices across his life — turned down a job that would've meant moving the whole family across the country, stayed in a marriage that was visibly rocky for a solid decade in the middle, and near the end quietly gave a big chunk of his savings to a cousin instead of splitting it evenly between his kids. My sister is 100% convinced this proves he valued duty and loyalty above his own happiness. My brother thinks it just proves he hated conflict and change more than he valued anything else. I've privately always suspected it was guilt over something none of us were ever told about. I brought this up at my writing group last week half-jokingly and it turned into a whole debate, which is how I ended up down this rabbit hole — three totally different "theories of him," and the exact same pile of facts backs up all three equally well.

For the book I obviously need to land on something true-feeling for the character, but its got me stuck on the bigger question too: if all you ever have access to is what someone actually did — no diary, no deathbed confession, nothing — is there an actual principled way to figure out what they really valued, or will the same set of choices always support more than one story about a persons motives, no matter how hard you dig? Part of me thinks you should be able to break the tie by just "thinking about it more," but part of me suspects the ambiguity might just be built into the situation itself, and no amount of extra thinking fixes that. Curious what you think, both as a "how do I write this guy honestly" question and as a "is this actually just unknowable, or am I missing some trick" question.
