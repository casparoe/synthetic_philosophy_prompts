I'm a senior taking AP Statistics, and a few weeks ago our teacher devoted a class period to the replication crisis in psychology, using the power-posing research as a cautionary tale. Since then I've been reading around the subject on my own for an end-of-year independent research paper, and I keep running into a narrative that goes roughly: the crisis happened because psychologists relied on frequentist null-hypothesis significance testing (the familiar p < .05 threshold), and the fix is to switch to Bayesian methods — posterior probabilities, Bayes factors, and so on — which are supposedly more honest about uncertainty. I want to write a short paper pushing back on that narrative, and before I turn my notes into a full draft I'd like feedback on whether the argument actually holds up.

Here is my argument sketch. First, most of the specific abuses that Simmons, Nelson, and Simonsohn catalogued under "researcher degrees of freedom" — optional stopping, selectively reporting dependent variables or conditions, and so on — don't seem unique to p-values; a researcher who tries several priors, several ways of operationalizing a variable, or several model comparisons until one yields a Bayes factor they like is running essentially the same unprincipled search, just in different notation, which is close to what I've seen called the "garden of forking paths" problem. Second, the significance-testing ritual that actually gets taught and used in practice is already a confused hybrid of Fisher's evidential logic and the Neyman–Pearson decision-theoretic framework, so a good deal of what gets blamed on "frequentism as such" (including in the ASA's 2016 statement on p-values) looks to me more like a problem with how that hybrid procedure is taught and applied mechanically than a problem inherent to long-run error-rate control. My tentative conclusion is that preregistration, publishing null results, and adequately powering studies are doing almost all of the real work in reducing false positives, and that swapping to Bayesian inference on its own would mostly relabel the crisis rather than resolve it — and might even make things worse if a posterior probability gets treated as more trustworthy than it deserves to be.

Could you tell me whether this line of reasoning is sound, and where it might be too quick? I'm especially curious whether I'm being unfair to Bayesian statistics — that is, whether there are structural reasons Bayesian inference resists p-hacking-style abuse better than I'm giving it credit for (something to do with the likelihood principle and stopping rules, maybe, though I don't fully understand that argument) — whether there's a more rigorous version of the "it's incentives and flexibility, not the paradigm" thesis that I should be reading and citing instead, and whether you think this is a reasonable thesis to defend in about 1,500 words or whether I'm biting off more than a paper that length can chew.
