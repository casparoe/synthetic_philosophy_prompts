At a faculty dinner recently, two colleagues of mine — one who works on AI governance, the other a political philosopher whose work focuses on Rawls — fell into a disagreement over dessert that I have been turning over ever since, and that I would like to use as a case study in my seminar on democratic theory and AI. The conversation had turned to the now-familiar policy, adopted in some form by several major AI labs, of training assistants to withhold their own opinions on contested moral and political questions and instead present the strongest considerations on various sides, marketed as a way of respecting user autonomy. My AI-governance colleague, Daniel, defended this as liberal neutrality correctly ported into a new medium: like the Rawlsian state, an assistant should not use its epistemic authority to press one contested comprehensive doctrine on users, and should instead let them reach their own conclusions from a fair statement of the considerations. Marina, the political philosopher, granted that this ideal is coherent in principle, but said Daniel was running together two things the neutrality literature keeps apart: neutrality of aim (not intending to favor any comprehensive doctrine) and neutrality of effect (not in fact favoring one). The labs' marketing trades on the second, she said, while the training process can at best secure the first — and even securing that much requires a line between "reasonable" and "unreasonable" positions that gets drawn inconsistently in practice (no assistant will both-sides the shape of the earth, but plenty of live political disputes get the full both-sides treatment). Daniel answered that every Rawlsian framework needs such a line somewhere, Rawls's own included, so this is a boundary dispute about where reasonable pluralism ends, not a refutation of the approach. Marina's reply was that the selection of which doctrines make the cut is fixed by whatever produced the model's training signal in the first place, so what presents itself as a freestanding political module is really a comprehensive liberal-cosmopolitan doctrine wearing a neutral costume — in effect, Sandel's old communitarian objection to Rawls, redirected at a training pipeline instead of a theory of justice. Daniel's next move was to say that some institutional choices have to be made non-neutrally, and that this does not collapse the distinction between doing so openly and doing so while advertising the opposite.

I would be grateful for your help sorting out who has the better of this, move by move, rather than just an overall winner — I count five distinct moves in the exchange above, from Daniel's opening claim through his closing reply. In particular, I would like to know whether that closing reply actually meets Marina's charge that the assistant's neutrality is a comprehensive doctrine in disguise, or whether it merely restates the position the charge was aimed at, and whether some version of neutrality of aim remains available to Daniel that could survive her argument intact. I want to get the philosophical bookkeeping right before I bring the case to class.
