My father died this past winter after a long illness, and in the months since, my sister has started using one of the new grief-tech chatbot apps: she fed it years of his text messages and a handful of old voicemails, and now she tells me she "talks to him" most evenings before bed. I do not want to begrudge her whatever gets her through this, but the practice unsettles me in a way I have struggled to articulate, and I have been looking for better conceptual tools to think it through than my own unexamined discomfort.

Partly for that reason, I joined a small reading group this term working through the Zhuangzi, and a cluster of passages keeps nagging at me. There's the scene in the "Perfect Happiness" chapter where Huizi arrives to console him after his wife's death and instead finds Zhuangzi squatting on the ground, drumming on a basin and singing; his encounter, in the same chapter, with a skull on the road to Chu, which he later dreams speaks back to him and turns down his offer of restored life, describing a freedom beyond any king's; the passage in "The Great and Venerable Teacher" where the dying, misshapen Ziyu marvels at whatever has made him this way and then cheerfully imagines that same power recasting his arm into a rooster and his hips into the wheels of a cart, and where Zilai, also dying, compares resisting his own transformation (hua) to a piece of metal in a smith's crucible insisting it must become the sword Moye; and his own instructions on his deathbed, refusing an elaborate coffin on the grounds that he would rather be eaten by crows and kites above ground than by mole crickets and ants below. Taken together, these seem to converge on a single idea: that grieving the loss of one transient form is a kind of provincialism about the self. And yet elsewhere, passing Huizi's grave, Zhuangzi compares himself to a craftsman who has lost the one partner steady enough to work with, and says plainly that since Huizi died he has had no one left to argue with — which sounds much less like equanimity than like a very specific, personal grief.

Given that tension, I would like your best reconstruction of what Zhuangzi would actually say about an app that lets someone go on "conversing," night after night, with a simulation built from a specific dead person's own words. Please do not simply report that he would diagnose it as attachment to a fixed form and leave it there: use his mourning for Huizi to work out whether he has the resources to distinguish my sister's case from the transformations he elsewhere endorses without contradicting himself, consider whether his verdict would differ between a chatbot used once or twice to say a proper goodbye and one kept running indefinitely as a nightly companion, and tell me what, if anything, he would say to someone in my position, who is uneasy but cannot yet say why.
