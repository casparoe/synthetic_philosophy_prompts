Donald Davidson held that a creature's responses acquire genuine content only through triangulation: two creatures causally responding both to a shared object in the world and to each other's responses, which together fix that object—rather than some private stimulation—as what their reactions are about. He pressed the same externalist point with the Swampman case, where a physical duplicate of him, assembled by chance in a swamp, utters all the sentences he would have uttered, yet Davidson insisted it believes and means nothing by them, for want of the right causal history. Suppose Davidson had lived long enough to encounter today's large language models, which generate fluent, contextually apt text purely by training on other speakers' words, without ever triangulating in real time on a shared object with a second creature. Would his principle of charity find any purchase on such a system, and does training on a vast corpus of human-produced language count, on his own view, as inheriting the right sort of causal history—or would he treat it as no substitute for triangulation and deny that the model means anything by what it outputs?
