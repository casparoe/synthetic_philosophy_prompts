I am at work on a historical novel that follows the Athenian fleet in the days before Salamis, and one subplot involves a seer wrestling with the Delphic oracle's notorious "wooden walls" prophecy — essentially the kind of case Aristotle has in mind when he worries, in *De Interpretatione* 9, about whether "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is already true, or already false, the day before it happens. While I was talking through the plot at dinner, two friends of mine ended up arguing about the underlying puzzle for the rest of the evening: Eleanor, who taught logic for thirty years before retiring, and Marcus, a playwright with no formal training in the subject but a stubborn analytic streak. I wrote down the shape of their exchange afterward, since I think they were each onto something real, and I would rather have an outside read on it than referee it myself.

(1) Eleanor opened by defending what she called the traditional line: if it is already true today that the battle happens tomorrow, then tomorrow is already fixed, so nothing genuinely open remains between now and then; the only way to keep the future open, she said, is to deny that the sentence is true or false yet — it becomes one or the other only once the battle is fought or called off. (2) Marcus objected that this trades on a slide between two different claims: all that really follows from the sentence's being true today is the harmless fact that, necessarily, if it is true today that there is a battle tomorrow, then there is a battle tomorrow — not that the battle itself is somehow necessary. The captains, he said, can still bring the battle about, or avoid it, through perfectly ordinary, undetermined decisions; the truth of the forecast simply rides along with whatever they in fact do, without forcing their hand. (3) Eleanor granted there is no causal necessitation here, but pressed a different objection: what, today, makes the sentence true, if nothing about tomorrow's battle yet exists to make it so? Calling it "true" now, absent any such fact, is just empty bookkeeping — which is exactly why we should say there is no fact of the matter yet. (4) Marcus replied that this proves far too much, since the identical demand would strip the truth value from thoroughly mundane future statements that nobody wants to treat as gappy — "the tide will turn before dawn," say — so the tidier fix is to drop the demand for truth-makers altogether: a statement about tomorrow is simply true just in case tomorrow turns out that way, no further machinery required. (5) Eleanor answered that the tide is a poor comparison, since it is already settled by present causes and the laws of nature, whereas whether these particular captains choose to engage tomorrow is not yet settled by anything at all; so her worry can still apply to genuinely open events like the battle without generalizing to causally fixed ones like the tide, and Marcus's counterexample does not reach as far as he needs it to.

Neither of them budged, and since I had started the argument in the first place, I did not feel it was my place to declare a winner. Could you go through the exchange move by move, say who has the better of it at each numbered step — not just who comes out ahead overall — and then say what, if anything, you think both of them are missing?
