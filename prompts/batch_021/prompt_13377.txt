I sit on the clinical ethics committee at the rehab hospital where I work (I'm a hospitalist, not a neuro person, which matters for why I need help drafting this). Our neuro team wants us to approve using a TMS-EEG complexity measure -- I'm pretty sure it's called the perturbational complexity index, PCI -- as part of how we evaluate patients in vegetative state/unresponsive wakefulness syndrome and minimally conscious state. The basic idea, which comes out of Tononi's integrated information theory, is you zap the cortex with a magnetic pulse, run the EEG response through a compression algorithm, and a high enough score on that complexity measure is supposed to mean there's "something it's like" to be that brain right now, even in patients who show literally zero behavioral response on the bedside exam. There's apparently real data behind it too: some behaviorally unresponsive patients score above the cutoff (they get labeled PCI+ or something like that), and that's apparently correlated with better odds of later recovery in at least some cohort studies.

I got into a whole thing about this with my friend Priya, who's finishing up a neuroscience PhD, over dinner last week, and it got way more heated than I expected for a Wednesday. She thinks refusing to use the best available tool just because "the theory behind it is contested" is basically negligence with extra steps. I keep getting stuck on the fact that IIT itself is a genuinely disputed theory of consciousness -- there's Scott Aaronson's old objection that IIT's math implies wildly unconscious-seeming systems should have sky-high phi, there was that whole letter a while back from a big chunk of the consciousness-science field calling IIT close to unfalsifiable, and even PCI's own defenders admit it doesn't compute anything like real integrated information, it's just "inspired" by the theory. So "PCI predicts who recovers" and "PCI measures whether this patient is conscious right now" are two seperate claims, and I don't think our committee is being careful about which one we're actually endorsing when we vote on this. That distinction isn't academic for us either -- the neuro team wants the result to help decide things like whether to keep putting a patient through an exhausting communication-device trial, how aggressively to treat probable pain, and eventually whether to support a family that wants to withdraw care.

Could you draft the memo I'd actually bring to Thursday's meeting? I need a real recommendation -- adopt PCI into our protocol, adopt it only under specific conditions, or hold off for now -- not a "more research is needed" shrug. Lay out the reasoning in a way the neuro team could actually push back on point by point, and please take whichever side ends up "losing" seriously instead of strawmanning it; Priya's negligence worry deserves a real answer, not just a passing mention.
