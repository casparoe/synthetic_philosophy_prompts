I'm a political science PhD student, and this term I chaired the small committee that selects the keynote speaker for our department's graduate conference. With four finalists under consideration, we replaced simple plurality with a point-based ranking system, in which each member ranks all four candidates and higher-ranked candidates earn more points. After the vote, one member admitted that she had ranked her genuine second choice above her actual favorite, having calculated that doing so would prevent her least-preferred finalist from winning. What struck me more than her admission was what happened when I subsequently tried to redesign the scheme: every alternative I sketched, including procedures with elimination rounds, still admitted some pattern of preferences under which a similar move would pay off for somebody. Is this simply a sign that we haven't yet found the right counting method, or is there a deeper reason why no ranking-based procedure over more than two options can be made immune to this kind of maneuvering?
