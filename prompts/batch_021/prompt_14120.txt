Ok so I've been on a bit of an SEP tab-hoarding binge this week on theories of truth (as you do), and it dredged up something that's bugged me since I first read what I think was Eliezer's old "Useful Idea of Truth" post, the one where he leans pretty hard on Tarski's disquotation schema ("snow is white" is true iff snow is white) to wave away most of the standard philosophical fussing about truth. That move always felt like it works great for the boring cases and just quietly steps around the one case that's actually hard, i.e. the Liar sentence itself.

So now I've got the "real" answers to that rattling around in my head and I can't settle on one. There's Tarski's own hierarchy move (no language can contain its own truth predicate without contradiction, so you stack object-language/metalanguage levels forever). There's Kripke's fixed-point construction, where you build truth up from the "grounded" sentences via strong Kleene valuations and just let the Liar sit there ungrounded, neither true nor false. There's Gupta and Belnaps revision theory, where truth is a rule you keep re-applying to your own hypothesis about its extension, and the Liar doesn't come out gappy so much as "unstable," it just flips forever across revision stages and never settles. And then theres Graham Priest's dialetheism, which just bites the bullet, says the Liar is both true and false, and reaches for a paraconsistent logic so that one true contradiction doesn't blow up the rest of the system.

Rather than have you walk me through these again, I want to see them in action. Could you write me a short story, maybe 1000-1500 words, set in some small self-contained institution (a monastery of logicians, a generation ship's legal code, an AI's internal deliberation module, whatever setting you think fits best) that has officially committed to exactly one of these four theories as its way of handling paradoxical self-reference in its law, scripture, or source code, and have some crisis in the plot turn on a moment where the specific mechanics of that choice (gaps vs. hierarchy vs. instability vs. gluts) genuinely matters to how things get resolved? I don't want the cop-out version where a computer just short-circuits and dies on the classic Liar sentence, I've read that one too many times. Ideally someone who already knows their Kripke from their Priest could tell which theory the institution picked just from watching the crisis unfold, but it should still work as a good story for someone who's never heard of the Liar paradox at all.
