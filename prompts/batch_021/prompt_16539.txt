I spent my working life as an experimental physicist, so Kurt Gödel was always a bit of a hero to me on the incompleteness side of things, and I loved the story of him and Einstein walking home together from the Institute every day -- Einstein apparently said near the end that his own work didn't excite him much anymore and he mostly kept coming into the office for the walk with Gödel. Since retiring I've fallen down a philosophy rabbit hole and picked up a biography, and the bit that's been stuck in my head for weeks now is how strange the private Gödel was compared to the icon of pure logic everyone remembers. Apparently he wrote letters to his elderly mother back in Vienna trying to convince her, on more or less rational grounds, that some kind of personal survival after death was likely -- something like: the world is rational, every person has this enormous capacity for development that almost never gets close to finished in one lifetime, so a genuinely "rational" world wouldn't just switch someone off before that development is done. And this is the exact same man who, years later, starved himself to death because he'd become convinced his food was being poisoned and would only eat what his wife Adele prepared -- so when she herself ended up in the hospital for months, he basically stopped eating and wasted away.

That contrast has been rattling around in my head ever since -- a careful, almost mathematical case for why a person's existence has to continue, coming from a mind that couldn't survive its own irrational terror of a dinner plate. Could you write me a short story built around that contradiction, set in roughly his last year or so? I don't need a biography lesson and you don't have to get every date exactly right, its fiction and you can take some license with scenes and dialogue, but I'd like his actual reasoning about the afterlife -- why he thought it followed from the world being "rational" -- to be treated as real, load-bearing content in the story and not just background color, maybe with a beat or two involving Adele or the memory of Einstein (who'd already died by then). Mostly I'm just curious what it might've felt like from the inside, holding both of those convictions in one head at the same time.
