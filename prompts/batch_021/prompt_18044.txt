And here's the student's answer in full: "The Knowability Principle (KP) says every truth is knowable: for all p, if p is true then its possible for someone to know that p, symbolized ∀p(p → ◊Kp). Non-Omniscience (NO) is the modest-sounding claim that there's at least one truth nobody actually knows: ∃p(p ∧ ¬Kp).

The trick is to plug the whole conjunction 'p ∧ ¬Kp' into KP in place of p. Since NO says this conjunction is true for some p, KP tells us it must be possible to know it: ◊K(p ∧ ¬Kp). But that can't be right. If someone could know 'p ∧ ¬Kp', then by the KK thesis - the idea that knowing something requires also knowing that you know it - we can split the known conjunction into its two known parts, Kp and K¬Kp. Since knowledge is factive (Kp → p), K¬Kp gives us ¬Kp. So we'd have both Kp and ¬Kp, a flat contradiction, and since that's impossible no matter what, it's necessarily impossible: ¬◊K(p ∧ ¬Kp). That directly contradicts what KP forced us to say two steps back. So KP and NO can't both be true, and since NO is just the modest claim that ignorance exists somewhere, anyone who wants to hold onto KP is stuck admitting every truth is actually known by somebody.

Historically this result is Fitch's Paradox, and Fitch worked out the proof in the early 1960s as a direct rebuttal of Michael Dummett's semantic anti-realism, the view that truth just consists in verifiability. The paradox shows that this kind of anti-realism, despite sounding modest, secretly collapses into a naive idealism on which nothing is ever true-but-unknown. Some anti-realists, like Timothy Williamson, have tried to escape by moving to intuitionistic logic, since without double-negation elimination you can't quite get all the way from 'there's no unknown truth' to 'every truth is known' - though even then you're stuck with the weaker but still uncomfortable ¬¬Kp for every truth p."
