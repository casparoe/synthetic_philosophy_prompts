I'm a software engineer, not a policy person, but I somehow ended up on my town's water resources advisory board -- I got voluntold onto it two years ago after I made a spreadsheet at a city council meeting about our HOA's water bill, and apparently that makes you "infrastructure material" now. We're about to vote on which 50-year water plan to recommend to city council, and the board is genuinely split. Option A is the conservation plan: float a bond now, rates go up about 15% for a decade, and we fund aquifer recharge and leak replacement so the water table basically holds steady indefinitely. Option B is the growth-as-usual plan: rates stay low, we keep permitting new housing at the current pace, we keep pumping the same wells -- except our own hydrology model says that around 2075 the aquifer hits crisis levels, and whoever's living here by then is looking at rationing or paying a fortune for piped-in water.

Here's the thing that's derailed our last two meetings. A woman doing philosophy grad work spoke at public comment and said something that's stuck with me since. She said whichever plan we pick doesn't just change how good or bad life is for "future residents" -- it changes who those future residents even are. Housing costs, when people can afford to move here, when couples decide to have kids -- all of that shifts depending which plan we choose, so the actual people living here in 2075 under Option B are, strictly speaking, different individuals than the ones who'd be living here in 2075 under Option A. And if that's right, you can't really say Option B "harms" any specific future resident, because the alternative for that exact person isn't a better life under Option A -- it's never existing at all, since someone else entirely would exist in their place. She called it "the nonidentity problem" and said its a real thing people argue about in climate policy and population ethics, not something she made up for us. I looked it up for five minutes and mostly just got more confused. Half the board now thinks this is a clever way to dodge our obligations to whoever comes after us; the other half thinks it's exposed a real hole in the "think of future generations" framing everyone, me included, had been using without question.

I need to bring a short written memo to next Thursday's vote, and I want it to actually grapple with this instead of dodging it. It should recommend one option -- A, B, or some honest hybrid -- with a real timeline attached, and the reasoning has to take the nonidentity argument seriously enough that even the board members who found it persuasive would recognize their own position in it, not just feel like I explained their view and then quietly set it aside. Can you draft that memo for me?
