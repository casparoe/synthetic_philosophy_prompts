I sit on my university's newly formed cybersecurity-incident committee, convened after a peer institution paid a seven-figure ransom to recover encrypted patient and research data -- I suspect I was added because I teach decision theory. We must recommend a standing ransomware policy, and the committee is split: half want a binding public commitment never to pay, since only a credible refusal keeps us from being marked as an easy target; half want case-by-case discretion, since refusing once our systems are already encrypted looks like pure loss for a principle. This feels to me like Parfit's hitchhiker played out at institutional scale: the disposition that keeps you from being chosen as a target in the first place is not the choice that looks rational once you're already staring at the ransom note. Please draft the two-page memo I'll circulate before the vote. It should recommend a specific policy, including whether and how narrowly any exception process should run, and it should take seriously the strongest objection to whichever side it favors -- especially the worry that binding an institution to "resoluteness" is not the same as binding a single person to it, since the costs land on patients, researchers, and students who never agreed to bear them.
