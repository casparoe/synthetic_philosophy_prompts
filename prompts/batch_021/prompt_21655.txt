I'm a cognitive science PhD student trying to turn a seminar paper into an actual dissertation chapter on split-brain cases and unity of consciousness, and I want a sanity check on the central move before I bring it to my advisor, who already thinks its too cute by half.

Quick recap of where the literature stands, more or less as I have it in my notes: Nagel's old point that neither "one subject" nor "two subjects" fits a split-brain patient without remainder, so our ordinary way of counting minds might just run out on us in this case. Puccetti's harder line that there were always two people per skull and the callosotomy just unmasks the split rather than creating it. Then various people, Bayne included, trying to rescue something like a single stream with switch-models or partial-unity stories. And then there's the 2017 Pinto et al. paper that reran the classic lateralized-stimulus tasks on the handful of complete-commissurotomy patients still around to test, and found more cross-field integration than the textbook "two independent hemispheres" story predicts - patients registering stuff on both sides in ways they're not supposed to be able to. I gather this got a lot of pushback, partly on eye-movement/fixation grounds, but the basic finding seems to have stuck.

My move, which I like way more than I trust myself about: the "one mind or two" question is badly posed, because unity isn't one relation, it's a bundle of separable criteria (shared attention, shared memory access, one locus of verbal report, one locus of agency) that can each be satisfied in degrees and can come apart from each other. So the split-brain case isn't a hard case for an otherwise clean concept, its evidence the concept was never determinate to begin with - and the 2017 findings fit that picture nicely, since partial integration on some dimensions and not others is exactly what a "degrees, not a count" view predicts, and a two-Cartesian-egos view has to explain away.

What I actually want comments on: (1) whether this is just Nagel '71 with extra steps and I'm kidding myself that it's a further contribution, (2) whether "degrees of integration" is doing real philosophical work or is a redescription that quietly ducks the question of what unifies a subject of experience in the first place, (3) whether I'm overselling what Pinto et al. actually showed, since that's the bit I'm least confident about.
