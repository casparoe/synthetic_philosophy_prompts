"Consider a three-member appellate panel that must decide whether a defendant is liable for breach of contract. Liability holds just in case (P) there was a valid contract and (Q) the defendant breached its terms. The judges' individual views are: Judge 1 accepts P and Q (and so liable); Judge 2 accepts P and not-Q (and so not liable); Judge 3 accepts not-P and Q (and so not liable).

(a) [4 pts] Explain what happens if the panel decides the case by majority voting on P and Q separately, versus by majority voting directly on the liability question, and explain why this is philosophically significant.
(b) [4 pts] List and Pettit (2002) show that this kind of conflict is an instance of a general impossibility result for judgment aggregation. State the four conditions on an aggregation rule that they prove cannot jointly be satisfied once the agenda is rich enough, with a one-sentence gloss on each.
(c) [2 pts] Briefly explain how the premise-based procedure and the conclusion-based procedure each resolve the dilemma in this example, and which of the four conditions each is naturally understood as giving up."

And here is the student's answer, verbatim:

"(a) If the panel votes on P and Q separately, a majority (Judges 1 and 2) accepts P and a separate majority (Judges 1 and 3) accepts Q, so under the panel's own rule that liability follows whenever P and Q both hold, this premise-based procedure delivers 'liable.' But if the panel instead just polls each judge's bottom-line vote on liability, only Judge 1 says liable, so a majority (Judges 2 and 3) votes 'not liable' — the conclusion-based procedure gives the opposite verdict. This matters because every judge's own views are individually consistent (no one accepts P, Q, and not-liable together), yet two equally natural ways of aggregating those consistent views into a group verdict pull apart. Group rationality doesn't come for free just because every question was decided by a 'fair' procedure like majority rule. This tension was first noticed by List and Pettit in a 1986 paper on collegial courts, where they called it the doctrinal paradox; they revisited it in the 2002 paper, generalizing it to any set of logically connected propositions and renaming the general phenomenon the discursive dilemma.

(b) In the 2002 paper, List and Pettit prove that once the agenda contains propositions connected by a logical rule (like P, Q, and P&Q) rather than just a list of unrelated yes/no questions, no aggregation rule can satisfy all of the following:
- Universal domain: the rule must accept any possible combination of fully consistent, complete individual judgment sets as input, not just some restricted subset of profiles.
- Unanimity: if every individual accepts a given proposition, the collective judgment must agree with them on it.
- Systematicity: the collective judgment on each proposition is generated by a single rule, applied the same way to every proposition, using only individual judgments on that same proposition — so the group's stance on the conclusion can't be read off how people voted on the premises, and different propositions can't be aggregated by different rules.
- Collective rationality: the group's resulting set of judgments has to itself be logically consistent (and, on the standard version, complete) — the panel can't simultaneously come out accepting P, accepting Q, and rejecting 'P and Q.'

(c) The premise-based procedure resolves the dilemma by majority-voting only on P and Q and mechanically reading the verdict off those results, which amounts to giving up systematicity: the collective judgment on liability is no longer a function of how people voted on liability, but of how they voted on other propositions entirely. The conclusion-based procedure resolves it the opposite way, majority-voting directly on liability and not letting the premise votes constrain the outcome, which likewise abandons systematicity, just by privileging the conclusion instead of the premises. So both procedures escape the impossibility result the same way, by treating the agenda asymmetrically rather than applying one neutral, independent rule to every proposition; they just disagree about which propositions get to count as basic."
