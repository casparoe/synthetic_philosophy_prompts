My aunt Marion is retiring after something like 38 years as a lawyer who almost exclusively defended protesters -- anti-war blockades, environmental sit-ins, that sort of thing -- and I got roped into giving a toast at her retirement dinner next month, which is stressing me out because I've never done anything like this before. The crowd's going to be split down the middle: half old co-counsel and activist friends of hers, a couple of whom are actual political philosophy professors, and half family who mostly just know her as "the aunt who occasionally got arrested along with her own clients." She's got this one belief that the first half of the room will recognize instantly, which I only understand because she's explained it to me a hundred times over the years at Thanksgiving: she thinks accepting the punishment isn't just the price you pay for breaking a law to make a point, its actually the whole point of civil disobedience -- going to jail on purpose is what proves you still respect the legal system even while you're breaking one law inside it, instead of just being some guy who thinks he's above the rules. Apparently this is a real position, straight out of Rawls, not something she invented at a dinner party. It made her sort of famously hardcore -- the family joke is that she's the only defense lawyer anyone's ever met who'd get visibly annoyed when a judge offered her client a plea deal for less time, because on her own theory, taking the deal kind of defeats the purpose.

Except there's a complication the activist half of the room will also remember: a few years back, in a case I don't know all the details of, she apparently pushed a client pretty hard to take a deal and dodge a genuinely brutal sentence, and a couple of her oldest friends have never fully let her live that down. I don't want to stand up and pretend that never happened, but I also don't want the toast to turn into a seminar on the tensions in Aunt Marion's legal philosophy. Could you write me something like a five-minute toast that's built around that one idea -- jail as the point, not the price -- so that it actually lands for the philosophy-and-law crowd, reads as pure affection to everyone else who has no idea what any of that means, and doesn't just quietly skip over the plea deal thing?
