I'm a PhD student in the history of political thought, currently drafting a chapter of my dissertation on the trajectory of J.S. Mill's views on property and cooperation after 1848. The occasion for this is an argument that came up in my reading group: a colleague insisted that Mill's late sympathy for socialism was little more than a rhetorical flourish grafted onto an otherwise unreconstructed liberal individualism -- mostly Harriet Taylor's influence showing through -- and that even the standard textbook gloss ("Mill flirted with socialism in his later years") already concedes too much. I think this is wrong, but making the case has forced me to be far more precise about the primary texts than the reading-group argument itself ever demanded.

Below are my working notes toward the chapter's central section; I haven't turned this into full prose yet, and I'd rather get feedback now than after a draft is sunk-cost. My core claim is that there is more continuity in Mill's thought here than either "Mill converted to socialism" or "Mill's socialism was empty rhetoric" allows, and that the continuity runs through his treatment of private property as an institution justified purely by expediency rather than natural right -- a position already fully in place in the "Of Property" chapter of the 1848 Principles, where he explicitly leaves open the possibility that some form of collective arrangement might eventually prove itself superior. On this reading, three things that look like they're in tension are not: (1) the increasingly enthusiastic treatment of voluntary worker cooperatives that Mill added to "On the Probable Futurity of the Labouring Classes" across later editions of the Principles, including his preference there for Fourierist decentralization over St-Simonian centralism; (2) the more guarded, competition-defending tone of the unfinished Chapters on Socialism, which strikes many readers as a retreat; (3) the Autobiography's self-description, from roughly the same late period, as ranging himself and Harriet under the general label of Socialists. What changes across these texts, on my view, is not Mill's underlying commitment but his estimate of how promising the actually-existing socialist proposals are once tested against his own consequentialist standard -- so that the Chapters on Socialism material is best read not as backpedaling but as Mill doing exactly what his own principles always required of him: holding contemporary socialist schemes to the same skeptical, evidence-demanding scrutiny he had always applied to existing arrangements, private property included.

I'd welcome comments of three kinds. First, accuracy: have I got the arc across the editions of the Principles right, and am I being fair to what Chapters on Socialism actually argues, or am I flattening a real shift that a more careful reading would preserve? Second, dialectical strength: is there a stronger version of the "real change of mind" position than the one I'm arguing against above, one I might be underrating simply because I'm already committed to the continuity reading? Third, I'd be grateful for pointers to specific secondary literature, old or recent, that anticipates or directly challenges this interpretation -- I would much rather find out now than at my defense that this is a well-worn position I've simply failed to credit.
