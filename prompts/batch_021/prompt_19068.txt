A friend and I spent much of last night arguing about the surprise examination paradox, in a debate that now threatens to unravel a surprise party the two of us are planning together. We had told the guest of honor, half in jest, that "something surprising is being arranged for you on one of the next three Saturdays." My friend insists this announcement is structurally identical to the schoolteacher's in the classic paradox, and that the same backward-induction reasoning which supposedly eliminates every candidate day for the exam now shows the guest of honor can reason his way to the conclusion that the party cannot occur, as a genuine surprise, on any of the three Saturdays — meaning we have already spoiled things merely by speaking. I suspect this move is too quick, but I cannot say precisely why our case escapes the argument, or whether it instead reveals something the classic version obscures. Please set out the most important implications of the surprise examination paradox, and of its leading proposed solutions (such as Quine's objection to the students' very first inferential step, or Sorensen-style epistemic "blindspots"), for whether our announcement has actually undermined the surprise — and tell me which of us is arguing more carefully.
