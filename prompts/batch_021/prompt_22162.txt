I'm working on a piece about diachronic rationality — the idea that rationality imposes requirements on our choices over time, not just at a single moment — and money-pump arguments keep coming up as though they were a decisive proof that any agent with intransitive preferences, human or artificial, can have an unlimited amount of money extracted from it by a sufficiently patient trading partner, no matter how it reasons about the situation. If that's right, it seems to settle, by pure logic, that transitive preferences are a non-negotiable requirement of rationality. So why do philosophers working on incommensurability, such as Ruth Chang and Larry Temkin, still treat the rational permissibility of intransitive or 'on a par' preferences as an open question rather than something these arguments have already foreclosed?
