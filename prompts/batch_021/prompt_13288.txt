Question (100 points total): The St. Petersburg paradox shows that a simple, well-defined gamble can have infinite expected value. Nover and Hájek's Pasadena paradox is often said to pose a strictly more serious problem for expected utility theory than that. (a) [20 points] Set up the Pasadena game and compute its expected value as an infinite series. (b) [30 points] Explain precisely why decision theorists take this to be a worse problem than the St. Petersburg case. (c) [25 points] Use the neighboring "Altadena" game to sharpen the point. (d) [25 points] Briefly note one response to the paradox from the subsequent literature.

Student's answer:

(a) In the St. Petersburg game, a fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads; if that happens on toss n, the player receives $2^n. Since P(heads first on toss n) = 1/2^n, every term of the expected-value sum is (1/2^n)(2^n) = 1, and summing infinitely many 1's diverges to +∞. The Pasadena game uses the same coin-tossing setup, but pays (-1)^(n+1)·2^n/n dollars on toss n — positive when n is odd, negative when n is even. Weighting by the probability 1/2^n gives a term of (-1)^(n+1)/n, so the expected value is the series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ..., the alternating harmonic series, which converges to ln 2 ≈ 0.693.

(b) At first that looks like an improvement on St. Petersburg — a tidy finite number instead of infinity. But the alternating harmonic series is only conditionally convergent, not absolutely convergent, since the series of absolute values (1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + ...) is the ordinary harmonic series, which diverges. Whenever a series fails to converge absolutely, the Riemann rearrangement theorem guarantees that its terms can be reordered to add up to any sum whatsoever, including +∞ or −∞, just by changing the sequence in which the payoffs are added — and reordering the payoffs doesn't change the gamble itself, since a gamble is just a set of payoffs and probabilities, not an order in which to list them. So where St. Petersburg gives an unambiguous, if unpalatable, verdict, Pasadena gives no verdict at all: the expected value isn't a big number, it's not a number at all. That, rather than mere infinitude, is what Nover and Hájek think decision theory cannot digest.

(c) The Altadena game, which pays exactly one dollar more than Pasadena on every single outcome, sharpens this: any reasonable theory of choice ought to rank Altadena strictly above Pasadena, since it dominates it in every possible state of the world. Yet Altadena's expectation is exactly as undefined as Pasadena's, by the same rearrangement argument, so expected utility theory can't even underwrite this minimal dominance judgment — it can't say Altadena is better than, worse than, or equal to Pasadena.

(d) Nover and Hájek's own proposed fix, refined in their subsequent work, is to replace the classical ("strong") notion of expectation with a "weak expectation": a gamble is assigned whatever value its running average of payoffs approaches with probability approaching one as the number of plays grows, on the model of the weak law of large numbers rather than the strong one. This rehabilitates ln 2 as the value of the Pasadena game without reopening the rearrangement problem, and is now widely accepted as resolving the paradox.
