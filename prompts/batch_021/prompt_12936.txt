A few of us from the local Astral Codex Ten meetup started a slow reading group last year, working through the metaethics debates that Yudkowsky's old "Metaethics Sequence" gestures at without ever really engaging the technical literature. Like a lot of people who come to this from the rationalist side rather than a philosophy department, I'd absorbed the impression that non-cognitivism was basically the "boo/hurrah" theory of emotivism and that Geach had dispatched it decades ago in a couple of paragraphs. Once we actually sat down with Blackburn and Gibbard directly (using Schroeder's *Noncognitivism in Ethics* as a crib, since the primary texts are rough going), it became clear that the embedding problem is a lot more load-bearing, and a lot less settled, than the folk version suggests. I wrote up the notes below to make sure I actually understood the two main responses before our next meeting, and I ended up with what I think is a real worry about the Gibbard side of things — though I can't tell whether it's a genuine problem or something I've half-remembered wrong from a paper I read a while ago.

Here are the notes. The problem in bare form: an expressivist says "lying is wrong" expresses disapproval of lying rather than a belief with truth conditions. But the identical string turns up unasserted inside "if lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong," where the speaker obviously isn't expressing disapproval of lying — and yet the modus ponens from that conditional plus "lying is wrong" to "getting your little brother to lie is wrong" is valid, which requires "wrong" to mean the same thing in both premises. Blackburn's response is to build a logic of attitudes: validity becomes the impossibility of holding all the attitudes the premises express while rejecting the attitude the conclusion expresses, with conditionals expressing some higher-order attitude toward combinations of first-order ones. (I believe there's a specific tree-structured notation for this in *Spreading the Word*, but I no longer trust my memory of the mechanics.) Gibbard's response in *Thinking How to Live* looks tidier on its face: he pairs ordinary possible worlds with "hyperplans" — maximally specific plans covering every contingency an agent could face — and lets normative sentences be true or false at a world-hyperplan pair, which more or less imports classical logic off the shelf, just with a fatter index. My worry is that this solves Frege-Geach by quietly ceasing to be expressivism in any metaphysically interesting sense: once "wrong" has a genuine truth-conditional semantic value relative to an index, all the actual philosophical work — explaining the practical, motivation-involving character of moral judgment without positing mind-independent moral facts — has to be done by a separate, non-semantic story about what hyperplans are and why we have them, so the puzzle looks relocated rather than solved. This feels like a cousin of the "creeping minimalism" complaint Dreier raises against quasi-realism generally, but applied to a target — Gibbard's possible-worlds machinery — that I'm not sure it was meant for, and I can't tell if that's a real extension of the point or a conflation of two separate objections.

I'd appreciate comments of three kinds: whether I've represented Blackburn and Gibbard fairly enough that this would survive contact with someone who has actually read them closely; whether the worry about Gibbard is a recognized problem for plan-expressivism or whether I've reinvented (or misapplied) Dreier's point; and whether the notes as they stand would be intelligible to a sharp friend who knows the rough shape of the fact/value distinction but has never heard of Frege-Geach specifically. If there's a cleaner way for an expressivist to block the worry in the last paragraph, I would rather hear the strongest version of that reply than have the objection simply conceded.
