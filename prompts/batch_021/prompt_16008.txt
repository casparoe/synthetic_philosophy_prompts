I'm on the small committee at my company that reviews and signs off on postmortems for our worst ("Sev-1") incidents before they go out to the rest of engineering, and a write-up we're finalizing this week has turned into a bigger disagreement than I expected. Three weeks ago, a refactor removed a validation check on one field in our checkout flow; when that field arrived null from a client we don't fully control, a downstream pricing service threw an unhandled exception and took the whole checkout path down for about fifty minutes. The draft postmortem, written by the incident commander, names the missing check (and the missing regression test that would have caught its removal) as the "root cause," and recommends a coaching conversation with the engineer who did the refactor.

Half the committee thinks that's reasonable; the other half points out that at least three other omissions were just as necessary for the outage to happen: the reviewer approved the pull request without flagging that the change had no new test coverage, a ticket asking for broader edge-case tests on exactly that code path had been sitting deprioritized in the backlog for eight months, and the alert that would have caught the malformed requests within two minutes had been muted after a false-positive spree the previous quarter, with the ticket to re-tune it left untouched since. If any single one of those four things had gone differently, we don't have an outage — so strictly by that test we have four root causes, or none, and "root cause" stops doing any real work. One of our staff engineers, who reads philosophy outside of work, told me this is basically an old and apparently unresolved problem philosophers call "causation by omission," which didn't leave me feeling much better about Thursday.

Could you draft the memo I'll bring into Thursday's meeting? It needs to do two concrete things: recommend how the postmortem should describe causation in this specific case — keep the engineer's omission as the named root cause, list several contributing causes with no ranking among them, retire "root cause" language for this class of incident altogether, or something else you think fits better — and state a rule the committee could actually reuse the next time several omissions all pass the "it wouldn't have happened otherwise" test, so we're not relitigating this from scratch every quarter. Whatever you recommend, take seriously the strongest argument for keeping a single named cause — that spreading responsibility across four different omissions means no one in particular is on the hook to fix anything — rather than dismissing it as just blame culture. And keep it something the committee could act on inside our one-hour meeting, not a philosophy seminar.
