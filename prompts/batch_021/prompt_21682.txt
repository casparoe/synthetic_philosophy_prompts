After forty years directing an experimental immunology laboratory, I have spent much of my retirement finally reading the moral philosophy I never had time for as a working scientist, and a small discussion group I joined last year has become the highlight of my week. An episode from early in my career has stayed with me since our last meeting, where we argued about it for the better part of an hour without reaching any agreement, and I would value an outside opinion.

At the time, I was supervising two postdoctoral fellows who were each running a small, independent trial of an experimental vaccine adjuvant in mice, using a protocol I had approved for both of them. Unknown to me, each fellow, under pressure to finish before the same conference deadline, made an identical private judgment call: they skipped one of two required mid-trial health checks on the animals, reasoning that the compound had produced no adverse signs during earlier dosing and that the extra check was largely a formality. Neither told me or our institution's animal-welfare committee. As it happened, one fellow's batch of adjuvant behaved exactly as expected; his trial finished cleanly, his paper became a minor landmark in the field, and his career took off from there. The other fellow's batch, because of an unrelated manufacturing problem that had nothing to do with either of their conduct, had picked up a trace contaminant, and without the skipped check to catch it, a number of animals suffered badly before anyone noticed. When the full story eventually came out, she was formally reprimanded, stripped of her supervisory duties, and never really recovered her standing in the field, even though she had done nothing that her colleague had not also done, for identical reasons, under identical pressure.

What I have never been able to settle, even after all this reading, is whether my long-standing sense that she was treated unfairly is actually defensible, or whether I am just refusing to accept that carelessness which happens to cause real harm deserves a different response from carelessness that, through no credit of the agent involved, happens not to. Was the field right to let their two careers diverge so sharply on the strength of one contaminated batch, or is there a genuine mistake being made here that I haven't been able to put my finger on?
