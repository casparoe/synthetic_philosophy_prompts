I am working on a novel, in the tradition of philosophically minded campus fiction like Rebecca Goldstein's, whose protagonist, Marta, is a philosophy graduate student quietly unraveling as she finishes a dissertation chapter that nobody on her committee has read closely enough to challenge. The chapter attacks the "act-based" theories of the proposition developed in recent years by Scott Soames and Peter Hanks: the idea that a proposition such as *that Obama is tall* is unified not because some further relation glues Obama to tallness, but because predicating is something a thinker does — an act — and acts, unlike inert lists of ingredients, do not need external glue to hold together. Marta's central claim, meant to be the intellectual spine of the book's middle section, is that this move does not dissolve Bradley's regress so much as relocate it: instead of asking what unifies a subject and a predicate into one proposition, the act theorist now owes us an account of what unifies the sub-act of entertaining Obama and the sub-act of predicating tallness into a single act of judgment — and that question looks exactly as pressing as the one it was meant to replace.

The trouble is that when I read the scene aloud to a friend who works in philosophy of language, she told me that Marta's argument, as drafted, was a strawman that any competent Soames or Hanks defender would dispatch within the first minute of a Q&A, and I would rather not send my protagonist into her job talk — at a small college where this is realistically her last shot at a tenure-track line — armed with a strawman. Could you help me build the strongest defensible version of Marta's argument? Please first state briefly why the act theorist takes herself to have escaped the regress in the first place, then press the relocation objection as hard as it will genuinely go, and, crucially, anticipate and answer on the act theorist's behalf the most natural reply available to her: that a mind or an agent is already a unity in a way that bare propositional constituents are not, so that an act of judgment does not stand in need of any further unifier at all. I would like the result to run a few paragraphs, written plainly enough that I can fold it into dialogue, but without sanding down whatever is genuinely difficult about the philosophical content.
