I'm preparing a graduate seminar on medieval resistance theory and want to push my students past the flattened textbook claim that 'the Scholastics justified tyrannicide.' Aquinas's own position, developed across De Regimine Principum and the Summa's treatment of sedition, turns on a distinction between a ruler who is a tyrant in title alone—a usurper—and one who was legitimately installed but rules tyrannically in practice; John of Salisbury, in the Policraticus, grounds his considerably more unqualified defense of tyrannicide instead in whether the would-be killer is bound to the ruler by an oath of fealty. Compare these two distinctions and the work each is doing: once you take each thinker's qualifications seriously, whose position actually licenses more in the way of private, unilateral tyrannicide, and whose framework had the greater afterlife in early modern resistance theory? Justify your answer.
