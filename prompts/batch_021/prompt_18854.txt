Since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have been working my way through the history of philosophy, and I have just spent a long stretch with Leibniz. What strikes me is how closely his account of pre-established harmony resembles, on its face, something quite recent: the idea of "acausal trade" discussed in contemporary decision theory. Leibniz held that monads are causally "windowless" — nothing can pass between them — yet their internal states unfold in perfect mutual correspondence because God coordinated the whole system once and for all at creation, rather than continuously intervening (as the occasionalists thought) or letting substances literally act on one another. Acausal trade describes agents who likewise cannot causally interact — separated by unbridgeable distance, time, or even branches of reality — but who can still benefit one another by each correctly predicting the other's disposition and acting on that prediction, with no signal ever passing between them and no coordinator arranging things in advance. Do you think Leibniz would recognize acausal trade as essentially his own doctrine transposed into a secular, agent-centric register, or would he insist there is a fundamental disanalogy? Please reconstruct his likely response using the actual resources of his system — the principle of sufficient reason, the identity of indiscernibles, his views on causation among finite substances, and his commitment to this being the best of all possible worlds — and tell me where, by our own lights, his reasoning would hold up and where it would fail him.
