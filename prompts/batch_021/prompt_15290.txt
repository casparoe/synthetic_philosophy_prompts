I recently got rotated onto my college's Appointments and Community Standards Committee (mostly because nobody else wanted the slot), and on literally my second meeting we've landed a genuinely nasty case I can't stop chewing on. Our philosophy department extended an offer to a strong hire, Professor Iris Kaplan, for a newly funded position, the Latham Chair in Practical Ethics. The catch is that the chair comes bundled with directing "Foundations," our required first-year seminar/mentoring program where 18-year-olds work through questions about integrity, relationships, and how to live. Last spring Kaplan published a widely-read essay that basically re-runs Russell's argument from Marriage and Morals: that treating a spouse's infidelity as an unforgivable betrayal owes more to old property-and-paternity anxieties than to any defensible principle, that jealousy is trained rather than some fixed moral compass, and that what's actually blameworthy is dishonesty and carelessness toward a partner (and any kids involved), not non-monogamy as such. A bloc of trustees, egged on by a handful of furious parents, is now invoking a founding-era bylaw requiring chairholders to show "exemplary character in matters of marriage and family," and wants the offer pulled, or at minimum wants Kaplan kept on as a researcher but removed from directing Foundations.

Half the committee thinks this is moral panic dressed up as bylaw-literalism and we should just proceed as planned; the other half wont engage with anything Kaplan actually wrote and keeps saying some version of "she's pro-cheating, obviously she can't run a mentoring seminar for teenagers." I dont think either side is being quite fair, and I say that as someone who'd literally pulled my old copy of Marriage and Morals off the shelf two weeks earlier after a dinner argument with friends about whether jealousy is "natural" — so I've actually read the thing Kaplan is drawing on.

I need to send the Provost's office a short memo before we reconvene next Tuesday, and I'd like your help drafting it. It has to land on one actual recommendation — proceed with the appointment as offered, proceed but reassign the Foundations directorship to someone else, or rescind the offer — backed by reasoning that goes beyond just waving at "academic freedom" or "moral fitness." And it needs to take the trustees' and parents' worry about the mentorship role seriously rather than just dismissing it as moral panic, even if that's not where the recommendation ends up landing.
