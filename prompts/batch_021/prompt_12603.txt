I have no formal training in philosophy or economics, but for the past year I've been working through decision theory as a hobby, and recently spent a few weeks on Lara Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility theory. I'm part of a small online reading group for people in the same position as me, and two of the other members, Dana and Marcus, got into a real back-and-forth about it that I haven't been able to settle in my own head.

For context: in the Allais paradox, most people prefer a certain $1 million over a 10% chance of $5 million, 89% chance of $1 million, and 1% chance of nothing, yet those same people tend to prefer a 10% chance of $5 million (otherwise nothing) over an 11% chance of $1 million (otherwise nothing). Ordinary expected-utility theory can't accommodate both preferences at once, since the trade-off between the two prizes on offer is structurally the same in both cases.

(1) Dana said this pattern isn't a mistake at all: on Buchak's view, a person can have one stable attitude toward risk, formalized as a function that reweights the probability of ending up with at least a given outcome, and it's this attitude, not any confusion about money or chances, that flips the preference between the two problems. (2) Marcus replied that you can always cook up such a function after the fact to match whatever someone actually chose, so this isn't a defense of the choices, it's a relabeling of them; ordinary expected utility already lets a curved utility function capture risk-aversion, so bolting on a second free-floating function just buys more room to fit any data, not a reason to think the fit tracks something rational. (3) Dana answered that the very same complaint could be leveled at expected utility's own use of a curved money-utility function, which nobody treats as mere curve-fitting; the point of the second function is that how much a guarantee is worth to you, over and above its expected utility, is a further, independent thing that two people who value money identically can still rationally disagree about. (4) Marcus said the cost of that extra freedom shows up elsewhere: to get it, Buchak's theory has to give up the independence axiom, so it becomes sensitive to how a gamble is packaged or staged rather than only to what happens in each state of the world, which in principle opens the door to a sequence of trades that leaves someone worse off in every state even though each step looked like an improvement to them at the time. Neither of them budged after that.
