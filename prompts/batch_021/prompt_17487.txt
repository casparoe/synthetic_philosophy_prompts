Okay, this has been eating at me for about two weeks now, so I could use an outside perspective. I started a new job in the fall as a data scientist at a small personal-loan company, and part of my job is running fairness checks on our default-prediction model before it goes to compliance for sign-off. When I first ran the numbers I was pretty pleased with myself: within every racial group we tested, the model's predicted default probabilities lined up almost exactly with actual outcomes, so a "15% risk" score really did mean a 15% chance of default no matter who you were. Then our outside fair-lending consultant ran her own analysis and pointed out that among applicants who never actually defaulted, Black applicants were being denied at a noticeably higher rate than white applicants, while among applicants who did default, white applicants were more often the ones who'd been waved through as low-risk. She's told my manager this needs fixing before we launch, full stop.

Here's the part that's actually breaking my brain: I've spent the last week trying to close that second gap without messing up the first one, and it's like squeezing a balloon — nudge the thresholds per group and the scores stop meaning the same thing across groups again, throw in more features and a whole extra year of data thinking it's just a sample-size fluke, and the exact same trade-off just pops back up somewhere else. My manager keeps saying I just need a "smarter model," and a friend doing her stats PhD said something like "oh yeah, that's a known thing with risk scores" before getting dragged into a meeting, which did not help my anxiety at all. I vaguely remember some big public fight a few years back over a risk-scoring tool used in the courts that ran into exactly this kind of problem, though I never read up on how, or whether, it actually got resolved. For what it's worth, our overall default rate is meaningfully higher for Black applicants than white applicants in this data, though I honestly don't know if that's even relevant or just a depressing coincidence.

So: is there some deeper, structural reason these two things — the score meaning the same thing across groups, and the mistakes landing on people at the same rate across groups — genuinely can't both hold at once here, no matter how good the model gets? And if that's true, is there an actual principled answer for which one we should be prioritizing, or is "compliance" just going to end up picking a side and calling it a technical decision instead of the judgment call it definately is?
