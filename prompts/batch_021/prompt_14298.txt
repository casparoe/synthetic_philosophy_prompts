A small philosophy-of-biology reading group I've belonged to for a few years (it grew out of an old rationalist-adjacent meetup) spent last month on the units-of-selection debate, and two members have kept relitigating one thread of it in our group chat ever since. I can't referee it myself and would like an outside adjudication.

(1) Owen opened by pointing out that any multilevel-selection model can be re-expressed as an equivalent kin-selection model: the Price equation lets the same fitness covariance be partitioned either into "within-group" and "between-group" components or into a relatedness-discounted cost and benefit, and the predicted change in gene frequency comes out identical either way. Since the two frameworks never disagree on a single number, he concluded that the decades-old fight between "gene's-eye-view" and group-selectionist camps is a dispute about bookkeeping, not biology, and that anyone still insisting group selection is a distinct causal process is mistaking notation for substance.

(2) Leah answered that formal equivalence of two partitions doesn't make them explanatorily equivalent, any more than the empirical equivalence of Newtonian and Lagrangian mechanics shows there's no real question about which quantities are doing the causal work. Which decomposition tracks the actual population structure is a substantive claim, not a free choice of coordinates.

(3) Owen replied that if no observation could ever distinguish the two hypotheses, then insisting one partition is "really" correct is exactly the kind of unfalsifiable dispute we ought to dissolve rather than adjudicate — and that Leah's own physics analogy cuts against her, since most physicists treat rival formalisms the same way.

(4) Leah's rejoinder was that the equivalence result itself only goes through under idealizing assumptions (additive fitness effects, weak selection, particular population structures); outside them the two decompositions can come apart and yield different predictions. More importantly, which framework you default to determines which idealizations you're willing to accept and which empirical questions — say, whether to go looking for group-level adaptations — you think to ask in the first place. So "dissolve the question" is premature.

Neither will budge.
