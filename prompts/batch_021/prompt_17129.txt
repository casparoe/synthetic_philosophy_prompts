Sharon Street's evolutionary debunking argument against moral realism keeps coming up in a metaethics reading group I joined after working through Yudkowsky's old metaethics sequence on LessWrong, and I've noticed we mostly keep rehearsing the same surface-level version of the argument: realists are stuck between claiming that natural selection somehow tracks independent moral truths (an implausible coincidence, since selection pressure is sensitive to reproductive fitness, not truth) and denying any such tracking relation (in which case our moral beliefs look like the accidental byproduct of a process indifferent to their accuracy, which should worry us). What we haven't really done justice to is the family of "third-factor" replies open to the realist — the idea that some further fact, such as that cooperating with kin or reciprocating favors is both fitness-enhancing and, independently, genuinely good, could explain why selection pressure and moral truth happen to line up without either one causing the other, so that the apparent coincidence dissolves without the realist needing selection itself to be truth-tracking.

I'd like you to write a fairly substantial dialogue between two characters — a committed Street-style debunker and an equally sharp non-naturalist realist who presses the third-factor reply — that works through this specific move rather than restating the debate in general terms. Give the realist's third-factor argument in its strongest form, but also give the debunker real, specific counters to deploy: for instance, pressing on whether the posited third factor is doing any independent explanatory work or is just a redescription of the correlation, and raising the "companions in guilt" worry that a structurally similar debunking argument threatens our confidence in basic mathematical or logical beliefs, which the realist presumably isn't willing to give up either. I'd like the realist to have something substantive to say in response to that worry too, rather than letting the debunker land the last blow by default — in short, neither side should be a pushover, and the exchange should move through several rounds of genuine claim and rebuttal rather than each side simply restating its opening position.
