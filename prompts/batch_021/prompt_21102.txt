For nearly thirty years I worked as an experimental physicist on rare-event searches, first in neutrino detection and later in direct dark matter experiments. A good deal of the practical craft in that line of work consists in appreciating how a detector's false positive and false negative rates interact with the prior probability, or base rate, of the signal one is looking for — an interaction that is notoriously easy to misjudge by intuition alone. Since retiring, I have taken up philosophy in earnest through a small discussion group that meets monthly, and we recently spent a session on the algorithmic fairness literature that grew out of the 2016 ProPublica investigation into the COMPAS recidivism-risk tool. What struck me most was the subsequent theoretical work — by Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan, and separately by Chouldechova — establishing that a risk score cannot in general be both calibrated within each racial group and equalized in its false positive and false negative rates across groups, except in the special cases where the groups happen to share the same base rate of reoffending, or where the score is a perfect predictor.

My instinct is that this is essentially the base-rate fallacy familiar from diagnostic testing, reasserting itself at the level of group comparisons, but I would rather not simply rest on that analogy. Could you explain, at an intuitive rather than a fully algebraic level, why calibration and equalized error rates are placed in tension whenever base rates diverge across groups? I am particularly interested in what each property is really demanding of the score, and why those demands come apart. I would also value your judgment on whether this impossibility result shows that "fairness" genuinely fractures into mutually exclusive conceptions once base rates differ, or whether that is too strong a moral to draw from what is, after all, an unavoidable statistical fact about how these quantities relate to one another.
