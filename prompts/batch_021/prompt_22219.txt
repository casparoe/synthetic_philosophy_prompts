I'm a backend engineer, and a few of us at work run an informal lunchtime "science club" where someone picks a pop-science book, article, or documentary each month for the group to pick apart — usually a much calmer affair than our recurring tabs-versus-spaces arguments. This month's pick was a documentary segment on quantum entanglement, and it set off a real argument between two of my colleagues, who I'll call Elena and Marcus, that spilled over into the rest of the week and that nobody else in the group could settle. I spend my actual working hours thinking about "consistency" and "locality" in the far more mundane sense of distributed databases, so the terminology hooked me even before the argument got interesting, even though I have no formal background in physics or philosophy beyond one undergrad course.

The documentary's headline claim was that Bell's theorem proves the universe is "nonlocal." (1) Elena took this at face value: she pointed out that Bell inequality violations have now been confirmed in loophole-free experiments, that Aspect, Clauser, and Zeilinger shared the 2022 Nobel Prize for exactly this line of work, and that it's therefore settled physics, not interpretation, that measuring one particle of an entangled pair really does instantaneously affect its distant partner. (2) Marcus objected that this overreaches: the "no-signaling theorem" is just as settled, and it guarantees that nothing you do to your particle produces any detectable difference on your partner's end, however far away — so if there's no way, even in principle, to detect the supposed "effect" locally, calling it a genuine physical influence rather than a very strange correlation is a rhetorical choice, not an experimental result. (3) Elena replied that Marcus was conflating two different results: no-signaling only tells you that you can't use entanglement to send messages, while Bell's theorem is the separate, stronger claim that no theory in which each particle carries its own pre-assigned local properties can reproduce the statistics we actually observe; something has to give — either some real nonlocal connection, or particles that simply don't have definite properties before measurement — and no-signaling makes neither horn go away. (4) Marcus, who mentioned he'd been down a YouTube rabbit hole of physicist Sabine Hossenfelder's videos on the subject, came back with the point that "something has to give" quietly assumes that experimenters' choice of what to measure is statistically independent of whatever hidden properties the particles carry; deny that one assumption instead (a position called "superdeterminism") and you can build a fully local, fully deterministic model that reproduces every observed correlation exactly. So, he concluded, Bell's theorem doesn't force nonlocality on anyone — it forces a choice between nonlocality and superdeterminism, and rejecting superdeterminism is a physical judgment call, not a mathematical necessity. Elena's closing line was that superdeterminism is "a cop-out on the level of solipsism," at which point everyone else just wanted to get back to their sandwiches.

Now that a week has passed and everyone's cooled off, I'd like a more rigorous take than we managed over lunch. Could you go through moves (1) through (4) and, for each, give a verdict on how right or wrong it is, as a JSON object of the form {"1": <score>, "2": <score>, "3": <score>, "4": <score>}, where each score is an integer from -2 (clearly mistaken) to 2 (clearly right), followed by a couple of sentences justifying each score? I'd also genuinely like to know whether "nonlocality or superdeterminism" is really the exhaustive choice the two of them made it out to be, since having it collapse into such a clean dilemma made me suspicious that something was being left out.
