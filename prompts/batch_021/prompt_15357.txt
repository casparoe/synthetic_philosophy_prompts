Question: "State the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem, including its key assumptions, and explain in your own words why it implies that no reasonable voting rule can be fully strategy-proof. Illustrate your answer with a short worked example, and comment on whether ranked-choice (instant-runoff) voting escapes the theorem's conclusion, as some of its advocates claim."

His answer: "A voting rule, or 'social choice function,' takes everyone's ranked ballots and produces a single winner. The Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem says that if such a rule (1) has to produce a result no matter what preferences people report, (2) is capable of making any candidate the winner under some set of ballots, and (3) isn't a flat-out dictatorship where one voter's favorite always wins no matter what anyone else thinks, then, as long as there are at least three voters choosing among the candidates, somebody will always be able to get a better outcome for themselves by reporting insincere preferences instead of their real ones. In other words, 'non-dictatorial' and 'strategy-proof' can't coexist.

You can see this with the Borda count. Suppose three voters rank candidates A, B, and C, giving 2 points to their first choice, 1 to their second, and 0 to their third, with ties broken alphabetically. Voter 1 sincerely ranks A > B > C, and voters 2 and 3 both sincerely rank B > A > C. Tallied honestly, B ends up with 5 points and A with 4, so B wins. But voter 1 actually prefers A to B. If voter 1 instead reports A > C > B — burying their true second choice, B, beneath C, whom they like even less — A still gets 4 points, but B drops to 4 as well, creating a tie that goes to A alphabetically. So voter 1 comes out ahead by misrepresenting where B and C rank relative to each other.

This is also part of why instant-runoff voting has become the preferred reform among people trying to get rid of strategic voting: because IRV eliminates candidates round by round instead of tallying every ballot's points at once, a voter's incentive to rank anyone but their sincere favorite first mostly disappears, so IRV sidesteps the problem the theorem describes for rules like Borda count or plain plurality."
