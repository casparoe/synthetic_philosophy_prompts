Ok this is gonna sound like a weirdly specific question but its been stuck in my head since Tuesday. I just started a two-year term on my university's Academic Integrity Board (I'm untenured faculty, still not totally sure why they picked me), and our policy says a student is only 'responsible' for a violation if two seperate things are both true: (a) unauthorized collaboration actually happened, and (b) the student intended to deceive the instructor about whose work it was -- genuine confusion about the rules is supposed to get you off the hook even if (a) is true. We're a five-person panel, and at our last hearing we voted issue by issue, (a) first and then (b). Each one passed 3-2, so mechanically the student came back 'responsible' on both elements and got a suspension.

Except afterward, comparing notes over coffee, we realized that if we'd just been asked 'responsible, yes or no' straight up instead of splitting it into two elements, only two of us would've said yes. The 3-2 majority on (a) and the 3-2 majority on (b) only overlap in two people -- the other 'yes' votes on each element are two different people, and each of them is privately convinced the *other* element wasn't actually met. So three of the five of us, if you'd asked us directly, don't personally think she should've been found responsible at all, even though that's exactly the result our own votes produced once you run them through the rule. Nobody gamed anything, it just fell out of how the sub-votes happened to break. I mentioned the whole thing to a friend who does formal epistemology and she got way more excited about it than a plagiarism case really warrants, said something about 'the discursive whatchamacallit' and people not wanting to end up a 'dictator', none of which was especially actionable for me.

We've got a structurally identical case coming up in three weeks, and the handbook just says the board 'determines responsibility by majority vote' without saying whether that means voting on the elements or voting on the bottom line. Could you draft the short memo I'd circulate before that meeting, recommending which of those two procedures we should actually adopt going forward, and saying what -- if anything -- that implies about the finding we already issued? A couple of people on the board will have genuinely good reasons for preferring whichever procedure you don't pick, so don't just assert your answer's obviously right -- actually take on the strongest version of the other side's objection.
