This week's question concerned Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals:

Question (15 points). David Lewis's 1976 argument is often summarized as showing that the probability of a conditional is not, in general, the corresponding conditional probability.
(a) [5 points] State the Equation that Lewis's argument targets, and explain what additional assumption about the probability functions involved is needed to turn it into a genuine triviality result, rather than a constraint that could easily be satisfied by some single, one-off probability function.
(b) [5 points] In your own words, sketch why assuming the Equation holds under that additional assumption leads to an unacceptable conclusion.
(c) [5 points] Give two distinct strategies philosophers have used to resist the triviality result, and state which assumption of the argument each one rejects.

And here is my classmate's answer, which I've been asked to grade against the instructor's rubric:

"The Equation at issue is the claim that P(A > B) = P(B | A) whenever P(A) > 0, where '>' denotes the indicative conditional. Stalnaker had already suggested something like this on the basis of his possible-worlds semantics, since on that semantics the chance that 'if A, B' is true should, in simple enough cases, just track how likely B is once we move to the nearest A-world. Lewis's point is that the Equation is not yet a substantive, refutable claim if it only has to hold of our actual credence function P; it becomes one once we additionally require it to hold of every probability function obtainable by conditionalizing P on some proposition of positive probability — that is, of every credal state a rational agent could reach by updating on new evidence.

Given that stronger requirement, the argument runs roughly as follows. Suppose P(A&B) and P(A&¬B) are both positive. Conditionalizing on A&B makes both A and B certain, so the conditional probability of B given A becomes 1, and the Equation then forces P(A>B) to be 1 in that updated state as well. Conditionalizing on A&¬B similarly drives the conditional probability of B given A to 0, forcing P(A>B) down to 0. But the unconditional Equation also fixes P(A>B) at P(B|A) to begin with. Recombining these updated values with the law of total probability shows that P(B|A) can only keep the value the Equation assigns it if it stops being sensitive to A at all, so A and B collapse into probabilistic independence. Since A and B were arbitrary, the same reasoning would apply to almost any two propositions, which is absurd — and this is really a version of the same complaint that has always dogged the material conditional, namely that a conditional analysis can be forced to relate two propositions that have nothing to do with one another.

Philosophers have resisted this in at least two ways. The first, due to Adams and developed further by Edgington, denies that indicative conditionals express propositions with truth conditions at all: 'if A, B' has no truth value, so there is no proposition for a probability function to attach to, and the Equation survives as a claim about assertability rather than truth. This rejects the assumption that 'A>B' is truth-apt. The second is Stalnaker's own response to Lewis, which in the end takes the same route: Stalnaker also concluded that conditionals lack truth conditions and adopted broadly the Adams picture, on which the Equation holds only because a truth-conditional semantics is no longer being asked to satisfy it. So both replies really give up the same assumption, that conditionals are truth-apt, rather than the requirement that the Equation hold across every conditionalization of P."
