Okay so a couple weeks ago my grandpa finally got his cataract surgery after basically being unable to see anything but blurry light and shapes for like 8 years. The surgeon told us something that kind of blew my mind: even once the bandages come off, his brain might not immediately "know how" to see properly, like he might not be able to tell what shape something is just by looking at it, until his brain sort of relearns it. Which has turned out to be weirdly true and it's been a strange thing to watch happen in real time.

It reminded me of an old reading from a philosophy class i took years ago, so i dug up my notes. It's from Locke's Essay Concerning Human Understanding, book 2 chapter 9, and it's basically the source of that famous blind-man-cube-and-sphere thought experiment. Here's the actual passage:

"But we having, by use, been accustomed to perceive what kind of appearance convex bodies are wont to make in us; what alterations are made in the reflections of light by the difference of the sensible figures of bodies; the judgment presently, by an habitual custom, alters the appearances into their causes. So that from that which is truly variety of shadow or colour, collecting the figure, it makes it pass for a mark of figure, and frames to itself the perception of a convex figure and an uniform colour; when the idea we receive from thence is only a plane variously coloured, as is evident in painting.

To which purpose I shall here insert a problem of that very ingenious and studious promoter of real knowledge, the learned and worthy Mr. Molyneux... 'Suppose a man born blind, and now adult, and taught by his touch to distinguish between a cube and a sphere of the same metal, and nighly of the same bigness, so as to tell, when he felt one and the other, which is the cube, which the sphere. Suppose then the cube and sphere placed on a table, and the blind man to be made to see: quaere, whether by his sight, before he touched them, he could now distinguish and tell which is the globe, which the cube?'

I agree with this thinking gentleman... and am of opinion that the blind man, at first sight, would not be able with certainty to say which was the globe, which the cube, whilst he only saw them; though he could unerringly name them by his touch, and certainly distinguish them by the difference of their figures felt."

I get the general gist of the cube/sphere bit, everyone knows that part. What i'm actually stuck on is the first paragraph, the stuff about "judgment" turning "appearances into their causes" and it getting mistaken for a "mark of figure." Like what is Locke claiming actually happens in your head when you look at a round object, and why does that specific claim end up being the reason he sides with "no" on the blind man question? Is he basically saying we don't ever directly "see" shape at all? Want to actually understand the logic here instead of just nodding along like i did back in undergrad.
