I'm working on a longform piece about the private lives of great logicians and mathematicians, and one case has been nagging at me ever since I stumbled on it in a footnote to a history of early analytic philosophy. In the last full year of his life, Gottlob Frege kept a private political diary that stayed out of view until it surfaced among his papers decades after his death. It sits oddly with his public image as the austere founder of modern quantificational logic: the entries voice contempt for the Weimar Republic's parliamentary system, hostility toward, among others, socialists, Catholics, and the French, and an open antisemitism that includes the view that Jews should be denied full political rights in Germany. From what I've read, the diary's emergence came as a genuine shock even to the philosophers who had spent much of the twentieth century rehabilitating Frege's reputation and building analytic philosophy of language largely on his foundations.

What keeps me interested in the case, beyond the obvious "brilliant man, ugly views" angle, is that Frege's own philosophy staked everything on separating the subjective from the objective. He attacked psychologism relentlessly, arguing that logical laws and the thoughts expressed by sentences belong to a mind-independent domain he explicitly called a "third realm" (in his own German, ein drittes Reich), sealed off from both the physical world and the private inner world of individual mental life. More than one commentator has noted the eerie coincidence of that particular label, given what the German word for "realm" would come to designate within less than a decade of Frege's death, but I'm more interested in the substantive question the coincidence dramatizes: there's something almost too on the nose about a philosopher who insisted that truth floats free of any individual point of view turning out, in his own private notebook, to be so thoroughly captured by the crudest prejudices of his own point of view.

For the piece, I'd like to go beyond the easy "separate the art from the artist" framing and get at something more precise. Could you lay out what you take to be the most important implications of the political diary for how we should evaluate Frege's contributions to logic and philosophy of language? In particular, I'm curious whether Frege's own doctrine of anti-psychologism gives us any special, principled purchase on when a thinker's biography is genuinely relevant to assessing their work, as opposed to the many cases where bringing up biography is really just an ad hominem wearing the costume of intellectual history.
