Frank Ramsey's essay "Truth and Probability," composed in 1926 and published posthumously in 1931, is usually treated as the founding document of subjective Bayesianism: it grounds an agent's degrees of belief in coherent betting behavior and derives the probability axioms from what is now called a Dutch Book argument. Less often discussed is how careful Ramsey was to separate mere formal consistency among degrees of belief from the further, more demanding question of which habits of belief-formation actually count as reasonable for a human being to have; this concern surfaces in his brief but suggestive closing remarks on induction, where "being reasonable" is treated as something a good calculus of consistency does not, by itself, guarantee. I've been rereading the essay for a seminar on Bayesian epistemology, and it strikes me that this particular strand of Ramsey's thought bears directly, if obliquely, on a puzzle that wasn't formulated until decades after he died in 1930.

The puzzle is the so-called problem of old evidence, pressed most influentially by Clark Glymour in 1980. On the textbook Bayesian story, evidence E confirms hypothesis H just when P(H|E) is greater than the prior P(H). But if E was already assigned credence 1 well before H was even proposed, then P(H|E) simply equals P(H) by the probability calculus, so E cannot confirm H at all -- however impressive the fit between them turns out to be. This clashes with actual scientific practice: Mercury's anomalous perihelion precession was a known, unexplained fact for decades before Einstein showed that general relativity entailed exactly that value, and this retrodiction is still treated as one of the theory's strongest confirmations. The most influential rescue, due to Garber and pursued in different ways by Jeffrey and others, denies that ordinary believers are logically omniscient: since nobody had actually worked out, prior to Einstein, that GR entails the observed precession, discovering that entailment is itself a piece of news that can rationally move credences, even though the precession itself was old news.

What I would like from you is a reconstruction, argued for rather than merely surveyed, of how Ramsey himself -- reasoning strictly from the commitments he actually defends in "Truth and Probability," not from Garber's or anyone else's later formal apparatus -- would most plausibly have diagnosed the problem of old evidence and responded to it. Does his explicit unease about treating logical and deductive omniscience as built into rational belief mean he essentially anticipated the Garber-style resolution before the problem even existed? Or does his more thoroughgoing psychologism about degrees of belief push him toward dissolving the problem rather than solving it -- toward denying that there is any general "problem" here at all, as opposed to a set of particular facts about how some believer's confidence happened, as a matter of psychological history, to shift? Defend one reading over the other, and be explicit throughout about exactly which claims or passages in Ramsey's actual view are doing the work.
