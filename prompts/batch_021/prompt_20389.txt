Before retiring, I spent three decades studying collective decision-making in ant and bee colonies; since then I have finally had time to read philosophy in earnest. I remain an emeritus member of my university's long-range capital-planning committee, where an economist colleague has proposed something livelier than our usual vote: a pilot of Robin Hanson's futarchy for the choice between funding a new interdisciplinary research building and expanding graduate housing. Under her scheme, the committee would first vote on a single composite metric of institutional welfare (research output, grant income, graduate retention and satisfaction), then open two conditional prediction markets to any faculty, staff, or student who cares to trade -- one paying out on that metric a year after the building is funded, the other a year after the housing is funded -- with whichever market prices higher deciding which project is built. Some members call this a rigorous corrective to a process normally driven by seniority and lobbying; others say it launders a genuine disagreement about what the university is for as though it were a mere forecasting problem, and warn that a thin market of self-interested bettors will manipulate the numbers rather than reveal them, citing the U.S. government's brief, notorious 2003 experiment with a Pentagon-funded futures market on Middle Eastern political instability, killed within days of becoming public. As the newest member, I have been asked to write the short memo the committee will discuss next week, recommending whether to run the pilot and, if so, on what terms and over what time frame. Please draft that memo, with a clear recommendation and a fair hearing for the strongest objection on the losing side.
