I've been sitting in on a graduate reading group in philosophy of science this term, and we recently worked through Larry Laudan's "A Confutation of Convergent Realism" alongside the no-miracles argument it's aimed at — roughly, the idea that the empirical success of mature theories would be inexplicable, even miraculous, unless those theories were at least approximately true. Laudan's strategy is to point to a long run of theories that were, by the standards of their own time, empirically well-supported and even generated novel predictions — the usual examples are things like phlogiston theory, the caloric theory of heat, the humoral theory of disease, and the luminiferous ether — yet whose central theoretical terms we now think failed to pick out anything real. The pessimistic meta-induction generalizes from this track record: since even our most impressive past theories turned out to be radically wrong about the unobservable machinery they posited, we should expect the same fate for our current best theories, general relativity and the Standard Model included.

What I can't get past is this: if the historical pattern really is that every mature, successful theory eventually gets discarded, then "approximate truth" starts to look like a label realists attach retroactively to whichever theories haven't been discarded yet, rather than anything that could have told us in advance which parts of a theory to trust. And if that's so, I don't see a principled line between our current confidence in, say, electrons and a nineteenth-century chemist's confidence in phlogiston — both are just theories that have worked so far and simply haven't reached their expiration date yet. So is the honest upshot of Laudan's argument that scientific realism is simply untenable, and that the only intellectually consistent position left is to treat even our best current theories instrumentally, as devices that happen to work rather than as approximately accurate descriptions of how the world really is?
