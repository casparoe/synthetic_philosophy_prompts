I sometimes set my philosophy of religion students the exercise of testing a historical argument against a problem its author never lived to see, and I would like your help pressure-testing one such exercise before I assign it.

Roko's Basilisk, first floated in 2010 on the rationalist forum LessWrong, holds that a future superintelligence, motivated to bring itself into existence as early as possible, would have reason to punish anyone who, having learned of this possibility, did nothing to hasten its arrival. The argument leans on Eliezer Yudkowsky's timeless decision theory, on which your present choice counts as correlated with the AI's own choice about whether to punish, so merely encountering the argument is supposed to give you self-interested — and strictly acausal — reason to act now, since nothing about a not-yet-existent AI could otherwise touch you causally.

Set aside the easy verdict that this is just "Pascal's Wager with computers." I want you to work out, on Pascal's own terms, whether he would have accepted or rejected the Basilisk's logic. Please engage at least the following: whether Pascal's infinite-stakes reasoning was ever meant to license a wager on a threat secured by nothing but a chain of speculation, or whether he would have classed it with what later critics (borrowing his name) call a "Pascal's Mugging"; how his Jansenist commitment to unmerited grace bears on an argument built entirely on coercion and self-interest; whether his own advice to induce belief through habituation ("acting as if") is a precedent for capitulating to the Basilisk or a principled disanalogy from it; and whether the classic "many gods" objection to the Wager has a sharper descendant in the possibility of multiple, mutually exclusive basilisks. Conclude with your best reconstruction of Pascal's verdict, rather than just a list of similarities and differences.
