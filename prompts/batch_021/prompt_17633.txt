I'm grading final exams for a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of relations and would like an independent check on one response before I record a mark. Below are the question and the student's answer.

Question (20 points): "Explain how Bradley's regress arises once a relation is treated as a further term standing alongside the things it relates. Then explain why Russell took this regress to bear on the problem of the unity of the proposition, and assess whether his distinction between a relation 'actually relating' its terms and that same entity considered merely as a term secures a solution, or only relocates the difficulty."

Student's answer: "Bradley's regress starts from the common-sense picture in which a relation, such as 'loves,' is just one more item in the world standing alongside the two things it connects. If the relation is itself a further term, we need some other relation to tie it to the original two terms; that new relation will then need a further relation to tie it to its own terms, and so on without end, so a relation, on this picture, can never actually succeed in relating anything. This is essentially the same difficulty as Plato's Third Man regress, where positing a further Form to explain a resemblance just pushes the question back a step; in both cases, treating a connection as one more thing that itself needs connecting guarantees that the connecting never gets done, which is why Bradley concludes that the distinctions relational thought draws must ultimately dissolve into a single, undifferentiated Absolute.

Russell inherited this worry as the problem of the unity of the proposition: what makes 'A loves B' a single claim rather than a mere list {A, loves, B}? His answer was that the relation must be unsaturated: 'loves' is inherently incomplete, like a function awaiting its arguments, and only when A and B fill its argument-places does a genuine unity result rather than an inert aggregate. This blocks the regress, since the relation was never a self-standing term to begin with, so no further relation is needed to attach it to A and B. The cost is that the very same entity can never be named or treated as a logical subject without losing the unifying power that makes it a relation in the first place — an odd result, but not an incoherent one."
