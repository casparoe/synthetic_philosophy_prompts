A colleague of mine in the philosophy department died suddenly last month, aged fifty-four. She was a historian of early analytic philosophy who had spent much of her career on Frank Ramsey, the Cambridge philosopher who himself died aged twenty-six, and she shared his complete lack of religious belief. She used to say that what drew her to Ramsey's short essay "Epilogue" was not that it consoled her about death, but that it talked her out of thinking she needed the usual kind of consoling: she was fond of paraphrasing his remark that he felt no humility whatsoever before the vastness of the heavens, since however large the stars are, they cannot think or love, and it is qualities like those, not sheer size, that make anything matter. Her family has asked me to give the eulogy at next week's memorial service, for a room that will be roughly half philosophers who will catch every reference and half people who have never heard of Ramsey. I know she would not have wanted either of the two consolations a eulogy like this reaches for by default: that she is now watching over everyone, or that her death is easier to bear because a single life looks so small against the scale of the universe. Could you write that eulogy for me, about 500 words, so that it lands for both halves of the room without quietly smuggling in either of the comforts it isn't allowed to use?
