so I've been on a bit of a free will kick lately after re-reading some of Eliezer's old reductionism posts on could-you-have-done-otherwise, which sent me down an SEP rabbit hole on Frankfurt cases. I think I've got the basic counterexample down: Black's got some neural gadget wired into Jones's brain that only kicks in if Jones is about to *not* do the thing Black wants, so if Jones goes ahead and does it "on his own," the gadget never fires and Jones seems clearly responsible even though, officially, he couldn't have done otherwise. Frankfurt uses this to attack PAP, the principle that you're only responsible for something if you could have done otherwise.

What's been eating at me for the last few days is the "dilemma defense" that Widerker and (separately) Ginet raise against this. The idea is that whatever "prior sign" Black is monitoring to know when to intervene has to relate to Jones's later decision in one of two ways: either the sign is causally sufficient for the decision, in which case the actual sequence is just a deterministic one, and the case can only count as showing Jones is responsible if we already grant that determined actions can be free -- exactly the compatibilist thesis in dispute, so it can't refute a libertarian's PAP without begging the question. Or the sign only makes the decision probable, in which case there's a real moment, right at the sign, where Jones could still have gone the other way, so PAP survives intact either way. Then I read about how Mele and Robb tried to patch this with their "buffer zone" cases, where Black's deterministic backup process runs on its own separate track from the very start, instead of waiting around for a sign to trigger it, so there's allegedly no moment where Jones could've derailed things. I mentioned this to a friend who's way more into the metaphysics than me and she just said "Mele-Robb obviously wins," which is a vibe, not an argument. And when I think it through myself it still feels like a shell game -- like its just relocating the same worry onto whether entering the buffer zone was itself something Jones could avoid, or whether the two causal tracks are independent enough for the preemption story to actually work.

Could you write me an actual, detailed argument (not a summary of the back-and-forth, I've got that part down) for the position that the buffer-zone strategy still fails to get Frankfurt off the second horn of the dilemma -- i.e., that it doesn't actually deliver a case that's both compatible with indeterminism in the actual sequence and genuinely free of alternative possibilities? I'd rather you engage with the mechanics of the buffer zone itself than just re-run the "flicker of freedom" complaint, since I already buy that that one doesn't apply here.
