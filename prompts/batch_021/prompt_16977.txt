During my working years as an experimental physicist I relied constantly on numerical algorithms, yet I confess I never looked closely at the theoretical bedrock beneath the word "computable" until I retired and started attending a philosophy discussion group at the local university. I was struck by how, within the space of a couple of years in the mid-1930s, Turing's machines, Church's lambda calculus, the Herbrand-Gödel general recursive functions, and Post's combinatorial production systems were all shown to pick out exactly the same class of functions, despite arising from quite different intuitions about what a "mechanical procedure" even is. It is even said that Gödel himself was not fully persuaded that Church's proposal had captured the right notion until he saw Turing's analysis in terms of a human computer working mechanically through a calculation. To my mind, this remarkable convergence of independently motivated formalisms looks like about as strong a form of mathematical proof as one could ask for: surely the fact that every reasonable attempt to formalize "effectively calculable" lands on the identical class of functions is itself a rigorous demonstration that the Church-Turing thesis is true, rather than a conjecture that has merely survived without a counterexample for the better part of a century.

If that is right, however, I am puzzled by something else I came across in my reading: proposals for so-called hypercomputation, for instance using Malament-Hogarth spacetimes, in which a suitably placed observer could in principle witness the completion of infinitely many computational steps within their own finite proper time, thereby deciding predicates that no Turing machine can decide. If the Church-Turing thesis has already been proved in the way I describe above, wouldn't any such physical scheme be chasing a mathematical impossibility, in much the same way that a compass-and-straightedge trisection of an arbitrary angle is now known to be strictly impossible rather than merely undiscovered? My philosophy discussion group insists that I am moving too quickly here, but nobody has yet put a finger on exactly which step is the weak one, and I would very much like to understand this for myself.
