I'm taking a graduate seminar this semester, "Value Alignment and Rational Agency," which is cross-listed between the philosophy and computer science departments, and this week's unit concerns inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) as a method for inferring human preferences from observed behavior. In our discussion section, we worked through the classic Ng and Russell formulation, in which an agent's reward function is recovered from a policy assumed to be optimal (or near-optimal) with respect to it, and our teaching assistant pointed out the well-known degeneracy whereby the all-zero reward function trivially "explains" any policy whatsoever. What has stayed with me, though, is a further point the professor raised only in passing: that this ambiguity is not merely a matter of insufficient data or overly permissive algorithms that better engineering could someday fix. Even granting unlimited behavioral data and a simplicity prior over both the space of possible reward functions and the space of possible decision-making procedures that could have produced a given policy, there is apparently no principled way to uniquely separate an agent's "true" preferences from its biases, since any observed behavior can be explained equally well by a rational agent with unusual preferences or by an agent with ordinary preferences whose reasoning is systematically distorted.

This bothered me because it seems to undercut something I had taken for granted since an introductory economics course, namely that "revealed preference" analysis lets us bypass thorny questions about what people really want and simply read preferences off their choices. If even a fully specified IRL setup runs into this wall, the thorny questions seem to resurface with a vengeance, especially for cases like addiction or akrasia, where we are strongly inclined to say that someone's behavior does not track what they actually prefer.

Could you lay out, for someone who only has the classroom-level picture sketched above, the most important implications of this identifiability problem for the broader project of building AI systems (whether through IRL proper, reward modeling, or reinforcement learning from human feedback) that are meant to learn and act on human values by observing what humans do? I would especially value your view on whether this is ultimately a practical difficulty that clever priors or auxiliary assumptions about human cognitive biases could tame, or whether it instead points to something deeper and more troubling about the very concept of a "preference" that no amount of algorithmic ingenuity could fully resolve.
