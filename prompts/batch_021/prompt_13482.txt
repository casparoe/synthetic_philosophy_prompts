I'm currently taking a graduate seminar in epistemic game theory, and this past week's material was Aumann's 1976 agreement theorem together with the common prior assumption (CPA) that makes it go through: the claim that any divergence between two Bayesian agents' probability assignments must come entirely from differences in their private information, never from differences in the priors they started with, so that two agents who share a prior and whose posteriors about some event are common knowledge cannot coherently "agree to disagree." Our professor introduced the CPA mainly as an innocuous piece of bookkeeping - a way of keeping disagreement from being built into the model by fiat, rather than as a genuine substantive constraint on rational belief - and Harsanyi's original framework for games with incomplete information tends to get taught in the same spirit.

This sat uneasily with something from a different seminar I'm taking, on formal epistemology, where we spent a while on the dispute (closely associated with Roger White's paper "Epistemic Permissiveness") between the Uniqueness Thesis - the view that a body of evidence rationally permits at most one credal state - and Permissivism, according to which the very same evidence can rationally support divergent credences. It occurred to me that the CPA, pushed back to the point before any agent has received any private information at all, is really just a Uniqueness claim for the limiting case of no evidence: it insists that, absent any evidential asymmetry between agents, rational priors cannot legitimately diverge either. If that is right, then treating the CPA as a harmless technical convenience looks like it is quietly importing the more contentious side of a live dispute in general epistemology. There is a complication I haven't fully worked through, though: Gul's objection that the CPA is essentially unfalsifiable, since for any observed pattern of beliefs one can always posit a sufficiently rich, unobservable type space and a suitably remote common prior from which those beliefs could have emerged through ordinary conditioning - which might seem to suggest the assumption is too toothless to be smuggling in anything as strong as Uniqueness.

For a term paper, I want to defend the more provocative reading anyway. Please argue that the common prior assumption, properly unpacked, does commit anyone who accepts it to something like a Uniqueness Thesis about rational credence at the state of no information, that this remains true even if Gul is correct that the assumption is unfalsifiable in any given application, and that a philosopher already drawn to Permissivism therefore has principled grounds for resisting the CPA as a general requirement of rationality in game-theoretic and economic modeling, rather than treating it as merely a convenient idealization on a par with assuming common knowledge of rationality. I'd like the argument to take the Aumann-Harsanyi motivation for the CPA seriously, rather than simply dismissing shared priors as "unrealistic."
