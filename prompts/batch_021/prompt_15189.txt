(a) Reconstruct one empirical study used to motivate this critique. (b) Explain how John Doris's proposed alternative to standard character-trait ascriptions differs from the conclusion Gilbert Harman draws about whether character traits exist at all. (c) State one influential response that has been offered on behalf of virtue ethics."

"(a) In Darley and Batson's 'Good Samaritan' study, seminary students were told they had to walk across campus to deliver a talk—for some, on the parable of the Good Samaritan itself—and were informed they were either early, on time, or running late. En route, they passed a confederate slumped in a doorway, apparently in distress. Whether a student stopped to help was predicted overwhelmingly by how much of a hurry they were in, not by their stated religious values or by whether they had just been primed to think about helping a stranger. This is taken as evidence that trivial situational variables, rather than robust character traits, are actually driving helping behavior.

(b) Harman's response here is the more moderate one: he suggests people may still have 'local' traits—narrow dispositions keyed to specific situation-types—even if they lack the broad, integrated traits classical virtue theory posits. Doris draws the harsher conclusion, arguing that our ordinary confidence in character traits is just an instance of the fundamental attribution error, and that trait-talk should be abandoned altogether.

(c) In defense of virtue ethics, Julia Annas argues that genuine virtue is like a skill: since each of us is born with a fixed moral character that habituation can only marginally adjust, the fact that most experimental subjects fail to act well under pressure simply confirms Aristotle's own view that true virtue is rare."
