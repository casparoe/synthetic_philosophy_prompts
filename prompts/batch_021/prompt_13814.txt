I spent nearly forty years as an experimental condensed-matter physicist before retiring, and like most of my colleagues I grew up treating Eugene Wigner's 1960 essay on the "unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics" as a kind of professional folklore: everyone quoted the line about its being a "miracle," but almost nobody I knew had actually read past the first page. Since retiring I have joined a small philosophy discussion group, and this finally gave me the occasion to work through the essay properly, alongside Max Tegmark's more recent argument that physical reality is not merely described by mathematics but simply is a mathematical structure.

Putting the two together, a conclusion seems to me almost unavoidable, and I would value your reaction to it. If mathematical structures such as complex numbers or Riemannian geometry were worked out purely for their internal elegance, with no concern whatsoever for physical application, and only decades later turned out to describe nature with uncanny precision, then those structures must already have possessed a reality of their own, independent of any mathematician's mind, waiting to be discovered rather than invented. That is just mathematical Platonism, and Tegmark's hypothesis looks to me like this same thought carried through consistently: if the universe simply is mathematics, then of course mathematics fits it perfectly, and the "miracle" evaporates. Given how forceful this line of reasoning appears to me, I am puzzled as to why nominalism, fictionalism, and other anti-realist positions in the philosophy of mathematics are still treated as live options rather than as views that this argument has already made obsolete. I would be grateful for your assessment of where, if anywhere, the reasoning breaks down.
