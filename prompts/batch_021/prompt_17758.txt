So in my causal inference class we've been doing simpson's paradox this week using the classic 1973 UC Berkeley grad admissions dataset (the one where women get admitted at a lower rate overall but the gap basically disappears once you break the numbers down by department), and two people in my study group got into an argument about it that's been bugging me since.

Priya said the department-level tables settle the question: once you condition on department there's no meaningful bias left, so the aggregate gap is just an artifact of women applying to the more competitive departments in bigger numbers. Dan said that's too fast -- if the reason women ended up disproportionately in the competitive departments has anything to do with prior discrimination or unequal encouragement earlier on, then department is sitting on the causal pathway between gender and admission, not off to the side of it, and adjusting for something on the pathway can hide exactly the effect you're trying to measure. Priya shot back that the same logic cuts both ways: if you never trust conditioning, you'd have to treat the raw aggregate as the 'true' effect too, which is obviously wrong whenever there's a genuine confounder. Dan's comeback was that neither of them can actually tell, just from the contingency tables, whether department is a confounder or more like a mediator -- that takes an actual causal story about how gender, department choice, and admission are hooked up, which neither side had supplied, so both 'always disaggregate' and 'trust the department-level numbers' were being asserted a bit dogmatically.

can you go through this move by move and say who's more right at each step, not just who wins overall? mainly curious whether dan's last move is actually correct that the tables alone can't settle it, or if theres some principled way to decide the confounder-vs-mediator question here.
