"Harman and Doris both draw on social psychology to challenge the Aristotelian idea that people have robust character traits, like courage or honesty, that lead them to act consistently across very different kinds of situations. The strongest evidence for this comes from experiments showing that small, morally irrelevant features of a situation predict behavior far better than character does. In Isen and Levin's well-known study, people who had just found a dime left in the coin return of a phone booth were dramatically more likely to help a stranger pick up dropped papers than people who hadn't found one, even though finding a dime obviously has nothing to do with being a caring person. Doris also discusses Zimbardo's 'Good Samaritan' study, in which seminary students on their way to give a talk on the parable of the Good Samaritan were far less likely to stop and help a stranger slumped in a doorway if they had been told they were running late, suggesting that whether someone stops to help has more to do with how rushed they feel than with their character. Harman explains why we miss this with the fundamental attribution error: we systematically over-explain people's behavior in terms of stable dispositions and under-explain it in terms of the situation they are actually in. Since there is no good evidence that people have the cross-situationally consistent traits that virtue ethics requires, Doris concludes that character does not exist at all, and that the entire Aristotelian project of cultivating virtues should be dropped in favor of an ethics built around designing better situations.

One influential reply comes from Julia Annas, who argues that the situationist experiments misunderstand what virtue ethics is actually committed to. Aristotelian virtue is not a description of how ordinary people already behave; it is a demanding, practical-wisdom-involving achievement that takes a lifetime of habituation to reach, and the fully virtuous person is meant as an aspirational ideal rather than a statistical average.
