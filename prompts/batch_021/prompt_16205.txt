I'm several years into a historical novel built around an episode from my own family's past, and I've hit a snag on the research side that feels more philosophical than editorial, though I can't quite articulate what's bothering me about it. The book follows my great-grandmother, who, according to a cache of letters and a partial diary handed down through my mother, anonymously wrote a series of muckraking newspaper columns in the 1920s that scandalized our small town and eventually forced a corrupt alderman out of office. Over four years I've cross-referenced dates, handwriting, and turns of phrase between the diary and the surviving columns closely enough that I no longer think of this as a working theory: I'd say I know she wrote them, and the entire second half of the novel, along with a fair amount of my family's pride, rests on that.

Last month, the archivist I hired to fact-check the historical sections sent me a newly digitized ledger from the newspaper's business office, recording regular payments to an entirely different woman for "editorial services" across exactly the months in question. My first reaction was that the ledger has to be an error, or must refer to something unrelated, precisely because I already know, from years of much closer study, that my great-grandmother wrote those columns — so whatever this new document seems to show has to be the misleading piece, not my conclusion. I told the archivist as much and didn't ask her to dig further into it.

When I described all this to a friend with no stake in the book, she said that was a suspiciously convenient way to reason: if being confident that you already know something entitles you to write off any future evidence against it without properly examining it, then no discovery could ever count against anything you currently feel sure of, which seems like a bad way to treat any question, let alone one in a novel I've been pitching to my publisher as meticulously researched. I'm not convinced she's wrong, but it also seems unreasonable to let one ambiguous ledger line outweigh years of corroborated work just because it arrived later. I'd like help sorting out where, if anywhere, my reasoning actually breaks down, and how someone in my position should decide how much a single piece of contrary evidence ought to move them.
