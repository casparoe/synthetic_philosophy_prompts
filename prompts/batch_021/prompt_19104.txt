Now retired after a long career in condensed matter physics, I have finally had time to read philosophy properly, and I've become preoccupied with Max Black's 1952 thought experiment about a universe containing nothing but two qualitatively identical spheres, offered as a counterexample to Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles. It strikes rather close to home, since individuating so-called identical particles was something I spent real time on during my working life. Black's own paper, I understand, was itself framed as a dialogue between two disputants, and I would like a worthy successor to it. Please write a dialogue between two equally sharp philosophers who disagree about whether Black's scenario really refutes the principle, with neither serving as a straw man for the other's view: one holds that the spheres remain indiscernible even relationally, since any relation invoked to distinguish them, such as their mutual distance, already presupposes that there are two of them to be related, so their individuality must rest on what philosophers call a primitive thisness — some fact that outruns every qualitative feature of the world; the other holds that a relation which is symmetric yet irreflexive, like being some positive distance from the other sphere, discerns them "weakly" (as the term of art has it) without any such circularity, so that a suitably modest version of Leibniz's principle survives after all. Have them press each other on whether this weak discernibility is doing genuine metaphysical work or simply relabeling the problem, and have them address the further worry that the whole arrangement could be permuted without any qualitative difference in the world. It is fine if they do not reach agreement, so long as each has had to meet the other's actual argument rather than merely restate their own position.
