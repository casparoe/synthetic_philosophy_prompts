I'm a philosophy PhD student working on formal epistemology and decision theory. This term's seminar on choice under risk (cross-listed with the economics department) just spent two sessions on the Allais paradox, and the instructor's line -- which nobody in the room pushed back on -- is that the case for the independence axiom is effectively settled once you bring in dynamic-choice considerations. The move is to embed the common-consequence pair of choices in a single decision tree with a shared chance node at the root; an agent whose static preferences follow the modal Allais pattern (the sure thing over the risky option in the first pair, the higher-expected-value gamble in the second) can then be shown to prefer paying a fee to avoid ever reaching the later choice node rather than letting the tree play out on its own -- which looks like a textbook money pump. I've been reading around since and think this argument is more contestable than the seminar let on, so I'd like help working out the strongest version of the reply before I raise it in section.

Please argue that an agent with Allais-paradox preferences can navigate the sequential version of the problem without being money-pumped, provided she is a "resolute" chooser -- someone who, having settled on a plan at the root node, carries it out at later nodes rather than re-optimizing each choice in isolation as though arriving at it fresh. Then take on directly what strikes me as the sharpest reply available to the seminar's side: that resolute choice buys this result only by quietly relocating the object of preference from individual acts to entire realized plans, so that independence comes out trivially satisfied once outcomes are individuated that finely, leaving the axiom no longer constraining anything interesting about the agent's original, act-by-act preferences over the four Allais lotteries. I'd like to know whether resolute choice is doing real philosophical work here or just redescribing the problem away -- and I want the argument to earn that conclusion rather than merely assert it.
