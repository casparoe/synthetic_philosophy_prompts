Question: In "On Denoting" (1905), Russell presents his theory of definite descriptions as a solution to three puzzles: (1) the apparent failure of substitutivity of identicals in sentences such as "George IV wished to know whether Scott was the author of Waverley"; (2) the apparent violation of the law of excluded middle by sentences such as "The present King of France is bald"; and (3) the puzzle of how negative existentials such as "The present King of France does not exist" can be meaningful and true. State Russell's quantificational analysis of definite descriptions and explain how it resolves each of the three puzzles. In discussing puzzle (2), explain the distinction between the primary and secondary occurrence of a denoting phrase and how it applies to the negation of "The present King of France is bald."

Student answer: Russell's theory analyzes a sentence of the form "The F is G" not as predicating G of some individual picked out by "the F," but as the conjunction of three claims: at least one thing is F, at most one thing is F, and whatever is F is G — formally, ∃x(Fx ∧ ∀y(Fy → y = x) ∧ Gx). Applied to "The present King of France is bald," this comes out false rather than meaningless, since the first conjunct fails: nobody is presently King of France, and a conjunction with a false conjunct is simply false. This is what lets Russell avoid the Meinongian move of admitting non-existent objects for the description to denote.

This machinery also dissolves the substitutivity puzzle. Even though Scott is in fact the author of Waverley, "George IV wished to know whether Scott was the author of Waverley" does not entail "George IV wished to know whether Scott was Scott." The reason is that "Scott" and "the author of Waverley," while codesignative, differ in sense: "Scott" contributes the man himself to what is wondered about, whereas the description presents him under a particular mode of presentation, so George IV can stand in the wondering-relation to the proposition under one mode without standing in it under the other. Substitution of codesignative terms is guaranteed to preserve truth-value only when both terms contribute the same sense, which is not the case here.

The same apparatus resolves the excluded-middle puzzle. "The present King of France is not bald" is scopally ambiguous between two readings, corresponding to the primary and secondary occurrence of the description. On its primary occurrence, the description falls within the scope of the negation, giving ¬∃x(Kx ∧ unique(x) ∧ Bx) — true, since there is no King of France to be bald or not. On its secondary occurrence, the description takes scope over the negation, giving ∃x(Kx ∧ unique(x) ∧ ¬Bx) — false, since there is no King of France to satisfy it. Because one of the two disambiguations is true, excluded middle is not actually violated; the appearance of paradox trades on not distinguishing the scopes.

Finally, negative existentials are handled directly: "The present King of France does not exist" is simply ¬∃x(Kx ∧ unique(x)), the denial of the existence-and-uniqueness claim itself. No object need be presupposed in order for this denial to be true, which is exactly what makes true negative existentials possible without commitment to Meinongian subsistent entities.
