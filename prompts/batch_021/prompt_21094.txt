For an economics elective at my school, we recently read a short piece on "revealed preference" theory: Paul Samuelson's idea that economists should infer what people actually prefer from the choices they make in the marketplace, rather than from anything they say about their own wants, since only the choices can actually be observed and checked. The discussion spilled over into lunch, where two of my friends, Nadia and Owen, ended up in a genuine argument about whether the theory can make sense of our mutual friend Diego, who constantly complains about being broke and says he's saving up for a car, yet keeps spending his paycheck on cosmetic skins for a video game. I mostly just listened, since I could see the appeal of both sides and honestly couldn't tell who was right.

(1) Nadia argued that, on revealed preference grounds, Diego's choices show that skins give him more utility than saving does, full stop - in this framework "utility" is just a label for whatever wins out in his choices, not some separate quantity of well-being he could be shortchanging himself on, so "wanting to save" is just something he says, not something that shows up anywhere in his behavior. (2) Owen objected that this can't be right, because it would make "self-control problem" a meaningless phrase: if utility is defined as whatever gets chosen, Diego can never be said to choose against his own good, yet we obviously do think people sometimes make themselves worse off under temptation - which is exactly why people pay for commitment devices, the modern equivalent of Ulysses having himself tied to the mast. So there must be at least two rankings in play, a heat-of-the-moment one and a longer-run one, and Diego's spending alone doesn't tell you which one deserves to be called his real utility. (3) Nadia replied that this move just relocates the problem: if the long-run, saving-focused ranking is the "true" utility, the only grounds for that are that it's the ranking Owen finds more sympathetic, which quietly reintroduces exactly the kind of unobservable, unfalsifiable psychological utility that Samuelson's whole project was meant to do without. (4) Owen answered that no self-report is needed: if Diego would actually pay a small fee to have the in-game store removed from his account, or sign up in advance for an app that locks away part of every paycheck before he can touch it, that preference for pre-commitment is still a choice, just one made from outside the moment of temptation rather than inside it - and this is exactly the sort of choice data economists who study self-control actually use. (5) Nadia's last word, before the bell cut them off, was that deciding the "outside the moment" choice gets to define Diego's true utility, rather than the "inside the moment" one, is still a ranking rule imposed on the data, not something the data hands you by itself - and once you allow that outside ranking rule in, you're no longer doing pure revealed-preference theory, you're doing something that depends on a further assumption about which of Diego's selves is right.

Neither of them thought they'd conceded anything. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each point, rather than just declaring an overall winner, and note anything you think both of them are overlooking? I'd also be curious whether you think there's a way to hold onto the ordinary idea of "utility" as something Diego could actually have more or less of, after Nadia's last objection, or whether her challenge really does show that the concept can't do the job we normally expect of it once it's taken seriously.
