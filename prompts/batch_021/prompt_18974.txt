Ok so last night I was out with my friend Dan (he's mid-PhD in logic, I just audit philosophy of language courses for fun) and we spent like two hours arguing about Frege — not the sense/reference stuff for once. My professor mentioned a few weeks back that there's this diary of his from 1924, right near the end of his life, where he's just venting: virulently anti-Jewish, wants Jews stripped of political rights, hates the Weimar constitution and parliamentary squabbling, wants a strongman instead, and has time left over to hate on Catholics, socialists and the French too. Dan mentioned that apparently this really messed with Michael Dummett — the guy who basically singlehandedly rebuilt Frege's reputation for 20th century analytic philosophy, and who was himself deep into anti-racist activism back in Britain — once he found out what was in there.

We couldn't agree on whether that's just "great thinkers can also be awful people, nothing more to see" or whether it actually connects to Frege's philosophy somehow, and I went home annoyed and wrote up my real position so I could bring something better than vibes back to Dan. Here's the gist: I think Frege's late politics isn't some random compartment sitting next to his logic, it rhymes with it. His whole career he's fighting psychologism — insisting, against Mill and against basically the whole German logic textbook tradition of his day, that logical laws aren't generalizations about how humans happen to think, they're objective and true whether or not any given mind grasps them, living, as he puts it in "Der Gedanke," in a "third realm" apart from physical things and private mental "ideas." For Frege, disagreement is never two legitimate views, its always someone failing to grasp whats actually there. Now look at the diary: same shape, just aimed at the nation instead of logic. Parliamentary democracy is basically psychologism applied to politics — the idea that what's right gets settled by counting up opinions and votes, instead of by a competent few perceiving the nation's actual objective good and imposing it. The anti-Semitism fits the same mold: Jews get defined as outside the "real," organically unified people whose objective interest politics is supposed to express, the same way a "confused" thinker doesn't get to have an alternative-but-valid logic, they're just wrong. So maybe his logic didn't cause his politics exactly, but one underlying temperament did both — an intolerance for the idea that truth, logical or political, could be plural or up for negotiation — showing up early as anti-psychologism and late, under the weight of a lost war and a republic he despised, curdling into this.

Problem I see myself: if rigid anti-psychologism about logic bred this kind of politics, why didn't it happen to everyone who held it? Husserl was at least as committed to objective logic against psychologism as Frege was, arguably more so, and he was Jewish and ended up persecuted by the Nazis for it — which makes me worried my "rhyme" is just two things that both sound vaguely hierarchical, dressed up to look like an insight. (Minor, maybe-irrelevant aside: I might be misremembering, but I think Frege's actual German for that "third realm" is "drittes Reich," which is an unsettling coincidence with the later Nazi phrase — though I'm pretty sure that one descends from some totally different, older tradition and has nothing to do with Frege.) So: does the historical picture of Frege I'm painting actually hold up? Is the Husserl counterexample as fatal to my argument as I think it is, or is there a way to rescue the "rhyme" claim from it anyway? And is there either a stronger version of this argument that I'm groping towards but not quite landing, or a stronger objection to the whole thing that I haven't thought of? Happy to get picked apart on any of this — I'd rather show up to round two with Dan with something sturdier than a hunch.
