Since retiring after thirty-five years as a structural biologist, I have been using the time to read philosophy I never had room for during my working life. I recently came across the literature on intellectual property and the ownership of ideas -- Locke's labor theory, the utilitarian incentive arguments for patents, and the worry that ideas are not simply like land, since one person's use of an idea does not use it up. It collided almost immediately with a real dispute I witnessed at a reunion dinner for my old lab, between two former PhD students of mine, Claire and Owen. While still in my lab, Claire had written, and published openly, an algorithm combining molecular dynamics simulations with a scoring function of her own design to search for stabilizing mutations in a particular enzyme; years later, now running her own diagnostics company, she personally patented the single most promising variant it had turned up, and has drawn substantial royalties from it ever since.

Owen's opening charge (1) was that this amounted to appropriation dressed up as invention: the algorithm rested on public funding and decades of the field's shared knowledge, and unlike a plot of land, the mutation's usefulness was not diminished by anyone else using it before Claire fenced it off, so the patent did not protect anything scarce -- it manufactured an exclusion that needs its own justification, and 'I found it first' is not one. Claire answered (2) that every invention rests on collective knowledge in just this way, and that turning a computational hit into a validated diagnostic took real money nobody would spend without exclusivity to recoup it, so if resting on public knowledge, or needing a justification for exclusion, were disqualifying, it would void nearly every process patent there is, not only hers. Owen granted (3) that the costly work of validation might deserve some reward, but said that is an argument for rewarding that later work, not for letting Claire personally own the molecular fact itself merely for being first past an automated search -- and he pointed out that, on her own account, it was the algorithm, which she had given away for free, that found the variant, so what she is actually being paid for is filing paperwork on a result that anyone with her own public tool could reproduce. Claire's reply (4) was that almost any invention can be redescribed this way, as merely surfacing a pre-existing possibility -- a new drug, too, is just a new arrangement of already-existing atoms -- so if 'the same public methods would eventually have found it anyway' defeats a claim to ownership, that standard would void almost every patent there is; and she insisted that deciding which of many computational hits was even worth pursuing, validating, and staking a claim on was itself a genuine judgment call, not a clerical afterthought.

I could see real force on both sides and could not settle it myself.
