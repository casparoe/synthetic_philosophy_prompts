I'm a novelist currently drafting a book whose central character is a charismatic wellness entrepreneur -- the sort of person who turns an entirely personal morning ritual into a twelve-week program sold to strangers at considerable expense, and who, at one low point in the manuscript, actually quotes Nietzsche approvingly to a skeptical business partner. Hunting for a plausible source for that quotation sent me back to the autobiographical chapters of Ecce Homo, where Nietzsche devotes page after page to nutrition, climate, and the daily rhythm of work and rest, treating them as more consequential than any question that has occupied philosophers before him. What caught my attention is that he explicitly diagnoses this same failing in someone else: he singles out the Renaissance Venetian Luigi Cornaro, whose famous regimen for a long life was, in Nietzsche's telling, nothing more than a faithful record of what happened to suit Cornaro's own unusually sluggish metabolism -- a personal idiosyncrasy that Cornaro, and generations of grateful readers after him, mistook for a rule of nature. Nietzsche treats this as a genuinely dangerous error, since on his view a given diet, climate, or pace of life is never good or bad in itself, only relative to the constitution of the person living it.

The trouble, for my manuscript, is that Nietzsche then spends the rest of the book doing something that looks uncomfortably similar: recommending dry climates over damp ones, small and carefully timed meals, abstinence from spirits and beer, and solitary walking over reading, in a register that keeps sliding from "here is what I have found works" into "here is what any sufficiently strong nature requires." He does have a ready defense against the charge of repeating Cornaro's mistake -- that this advice is addressed only to a "type," to natures relevantly like his own, so that what sounds like a universal rule is really perspectival advice correctly indexed to a kind of person, exactly the indexing Cornaro failed to supply. My writing group thought I was being unfair to him when I raised this over drinks, so before I let my character get away with the quotation, I would like a philosopher's verdict rather than a novelist's hunch. Please argue, as rigorously as you can, that Nietzsche's own dietary and climatic prescriptions commit the Cornaro error in substance even while formally disavowing it, and that dressing the prescription up in the language of "type" and "instinct" does not change its underlying logical structure. Take the "type" defense in its strongest form rather than in caricature -- if there is a genuine escape route there for Nietzsche, I would rather find out now than build a scene around a mistake.
