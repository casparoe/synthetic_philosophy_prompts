As a software engineer who has played piano since childhood and now performs with a community orchestra on weekends, I don't have any formal training in philosophy. But a violinist in the group who is finishing a philosophy MA recently got into a long argument with me about why instrumental music seems to move people more directly than a painting or a novel does, and she pointed me to the section on music in Book 3 of Schopenhauer's "The World as Will and Representation." I've been working through it in the old Haldane and Kemp translation, since that's the version that's freely available, and while I can parse the individual sentences well enough, I suspect I'm missing the actual argument, probably because I haven't read the rest of the book and don't really know his broader system.

Here is the part that's giving me trouble. Schopenhauer writes that "Music is by no means like the other arts, the copy of the Ideas, but the copy of the will itself, the objectivity of which are the Ideas. This is why the effect of music is so very much more powerful and penetrating than that of the other arts, for these others speak only of the shadow, but music of the essence." A little later in the same section he adds that we "might, therefore, just as well call the world embodied music as embodied will." Between these two remarks, he works out an elaborate correspondence between the different voices of a harmonized piece and what he calls the "grades" of nature: the bass line corresponds to unorganized, inorganic matter, the inner voices to the plant and animal kingdoms, and the melody proper to conscious human striving.

What I can't work out is whether this picture is even coherent by Schopenhauer's own lights. From what my friend has explained, he holds that the will, as the thing-in-itself, is a single, groundless striving that exists entirely outside space, time, and what he calls the principle of sufficient reason — that is, outside multiplicity and change altogether. Yet music seems like about the most temporal, structured, rule-governed thing there is: it unfolds tone by tone and voice by voice according to the constraints of harmony and counterpoint. So how can it be a "direct copy" of something that, on his own account, has no sequence, structure, or parts to be copied in the first place? And is the bass/inner-voice/melody analogy meant as a literal claim that music's formal structure mirrors the structure of nature, or is it more of a rhetorical illustration that isn't supposed to carry real philosophical weight? I'd like to actually understand the argument here rather than fall back on the "music bypasses representation" one-liner I keep seeing repeated on music-theory forums.
