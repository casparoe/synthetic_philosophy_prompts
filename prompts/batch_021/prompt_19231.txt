So a friend sent me this Simone Weil essay on political parties after I was complaining about how broken party politics feels lately, and now I can't stop turning it over in my head. Her core claim, as I understood it, is that every party, not just the scary extremist ones, is basically "totalitarian in embryo": a party's own growth becomes its real, unstated goal, and once that happens members end up subordinating their own judgment about what's actually true or just to whatever keeps the party expanding and unified. Her conclusion, famously, is that we should get rid of political parties altogether, not reform them.

Here's the part that doesn't click for me. If the actual problem is a bunch of unchecked "total loyalty" machines fighting each other and dragging everyone's judgment down in the process, why does she land on "delete all of them" instead of "merge them into one thing you can actually hold accountable"? In distributed systems, when you've got competing subsystems each optimizing for their own growth at the expense of the whole, the fix usually isn't to rip out all coordination, its to consolidate into something governable. A single dominant party would still have the growth-for-its-own-sake problem, sure, but at least you'd have one thing to point at instead of five factions tearing the place apart with nobody accountable for the interactions between them. My friend insists this gets her completely backwards, that a one-party state is closer to her nightmare scenario then her actual proposal, but all he'll say is "you're missing the mechanism, not the math." Can you actually spell out where my one-party "fix" breaks down on Weil's own terms, and what a political system with zero parties is even supposed to look like according to her?
