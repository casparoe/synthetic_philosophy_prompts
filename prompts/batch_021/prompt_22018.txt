So I'm teaching a graduate seminar on interwar French political thought this term, and last week we did Simone Weil's essay on abolishing political parties (the one usually translated as "On the Abolition of All Political Parties," written not long before she died but only published years later). She frames the whole thing in pretty Rousseauian terms -- parties as "partial societies" that fracture the general will the same way factions worried Rousseau in the Social Contract -- and makes a point of noting that democratic politics got along fine without anything like a modern party for a long stretch of history before parties existed. One of my students wrote her response paper arguing that if you actually follow this logic to its conclusion, what Weil is really recommending is consolidating politics into one unified party (or "movement," or whatever label you like) that speaks for the general will directly, rather then letting it get chopped up by competing factions. Her reasoning was basically: if fracturing into competing parts is the disease, unification under a single banner must be the cure.

I found myself nodding along at first, since that does seem like where the Rousseau parallel leads, but somthing about it feels off to me too -- it doesn't sit right with what I remember of Weil's politics more broadly, and I can't quite articulate the problem well enough to write good comments on the paper. Is my student's reading defensible, or is she missing something essential about what Weil is actually arguing for (and against)?
