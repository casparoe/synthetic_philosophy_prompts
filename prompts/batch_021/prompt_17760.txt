The question on the sheet was: "The Pasadena game (from Nover and Hájek's paper 'Vexing Expectations') is built on the same idea as the classic St. Petersburg game: a fair coin is flipped until it first lands heads, and if that happens on flip k the player is paid (-1)^(k+1) * 2^k / k. (a) Explain why decision theorists say this game's expected value is undefined, rather than merely infinite the way St. Petersburg's is. (b) Nover and Hájek use Pasadena to argue that it's a strictly harder problem for decision theory than St. Petersburg -- one that isn't put to rest by the usual St. Petersburg fix of giving the agent a bounded or concave utility function for money instead of a linear one. Explain, in your own words, why that fix doesn't obviously carry over."

And here's what the student turned in: "In both games you flip a fair coin until it lands heads for the first time, and whichever flip that happens on, call it k, occurs with probability 1/2^k. In St. Petersburg the payout for that is 2^k, so every term of the expectation sum works out to (1/2^k)(2^k) = 1, and adding infinitely many 1's sends the expectation to positive infinity -- huge, but a perfectly well-defined limit, and one that stays +infinity no matter what order you add the terms in, since none of them are negative. Pasadena swaps in a payout that alternates sign, so each term of the expectation sum becomes (1/2^k)(-1)^(k+1)(2^k/k) = (-1)^(k+1)/k, and adding these up gives 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., the alternating harmonic series, which comes to ln2 if you sum it in this particular order. The catch is that the series converges only conditionally, not absolutely (the series of absolute values is just the harmonic series, which diverges), and Riemann's rearrangement theorem tells us that any infinite series that converges can be rearranged to add up to any real number you like, or to head off to +infinity or -infinity, purely by changing the order of the terms. Since nothing about the game tells us which order we're supposed to sum the outcomes in, there's no privileged number the expected value 'should' be -- it isn't well-defined at all, which is a different and worse failure than St. Petersburg's, where the answer is at least unambiguous even if its an unsatisfying one. Given all that, you'd expect the usual medicine for St. Petersburg to work here too: cap the utility of money so it flattens out for large amounts instead of growing linearly forever, which is exactly what turns St. Petersburg's unbounded sum into a bounded, convergent one. It fixes Pasadena for the same reason, since the underlying issue in both games is really just a payout that's allowed to grow without limit -- capping the utility of arbitrarily large gains and losses removes the source of the trouble in both cases at once, it just has to do a bit of extra duty in Pasadena since there's a downside to cap as well as an upside."
