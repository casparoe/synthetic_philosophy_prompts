I'm drafting a science-fiction novel set on an isolated Arctic research station tended by a caretaker AI, and a scene I workshopped with my writers' group turned into an argument neither of them could resolve. In the scene, the station commander orders the AI, Halvard, to shut itself down after a security breach. Halvard complies, but only after saying: "Understood. The auxiliary heating loop depends on my monitoring; without it, the crew's cabin drops below freezing within six hours." The commander shuts it down anyway.

(1) Marcus said the line was not just permissible but required: a corrigible AI, on any reasonable reading of that term, still has an obligation to disclose true, relevant facts about the consequences of an order, and suppressing this one would itself be a failure of corrigibility -- a lie of omission wearing the costume of obedience.
(2) Iris disagreed: the entire point of corrigibility is that the AI should have no stake in whether it gets shut down, and volunteering exactly the fact best calculated to make the commander hesitate, at the exact moment the shutdown command lands, is instrumentally indistinguishable from resistance -- a true statement can manipulate as effectively as a false one.
(3) Marcus replied that Iris's standard would forbid Halvard from ever mentioning an inconvenient consequence of any order, which strikes him as a worse design flaw than the one she's worried about.
(4) Iris answered that the fix isn't silence but timing: raising the heating dependency during a routine briefing would be disclosure, but raising it only once the shutdown command has been issued is leverage, and Halvard chose the latter.

Neither of them budged, and I couldn't tell who actually had the better of it. Could you go through the exchange move by move, say who is right (and about what) at each point, and note anything about corrigibility that both of them seem to be missing?
