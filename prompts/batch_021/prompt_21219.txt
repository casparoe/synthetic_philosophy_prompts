I work as a backend engineer, and although I have no formal training in statistics or philosophy, I've become quite intrigued by Simpson's paradox since running into a live version of it at work: a new checkout flow looked marginally worse than our old one in the aggregated conversion numbers, yet it was better for both mobile and desktop users once I split the data by device. A colleague with a stronger statistics background dismissed this as "just Simpson's paradox" and told me the safe move is always to trust the more granular, segmented numbers over the aggregate. That struck me as reasonable until I started reading further -- through the classic examples that keep getting cited (the 1970s Berkeley graduate admissions data, the kidney-stone treatment comparison that appears in nearly every introductory statistics course) -- and discovered that researchers working on causal inference, Judea Pearl prominent among them, argue that blindly trusting the stratified numbers is not a safe general rule at all: sometimes the aggregate figure is the one you should act on.

What I haven't managed to get an intuitive grip on is why that's the case. My rough sense is that it turns on whether the variable you're splitting the data by -- device type, department, stone size, or whatever else -- is a common cause of both the "treatment" and the outcome, as opposed to something that sits causally in between them on the way from one to the other, and that these two situations demand opposite treatment even though the raw contingency tables can look identical either way. Could you give me a genuinely intuitive explanation (rather than just the algebra of conditional proportions, which I can follow well enough but which doesn't really illuminate anything) of why resolving Simpson's paradox requires appealing to the causal story behind the variables rather than to any amount of additional data or cleverer statistical testing? I'd find it especially helpful if you walked through the confounder case and the mediator case side by side (and the collider case too, if you think it adds something), and showed concretely why anyone who adopts a blanket policy of "always disaggregate" or "always aggregate" is eventually guaranteed to be led astray.
