I don't have any formal training in philosophy, but I've spent a fair amount of my spare time this year reading around Henry Sidgwick after a podcast mentioned him mainly as a utilitarian (the "Methods of Ethics" person). What actually hooked me was a throwaway line noting that this same careful, methodical Cambridge don spent decades of his life co-founding and twice serving as president of the Society for Psychical Research (1882–85 and again 1888–93), personally investigating mediums and even running his own experiments with ordinary volunteers to test claims of telepathy. That struck me as such an odd pairing with his reputation for rigor that I tracked down the address he gave at the Society's very first general meeting, on 17 July 1882, and I've been puzzling over one part of it ever since, without quite being able to pin down what he's actually claiming. Here is the passage, exactly as printed in the Society's Proceedings:

"[T]he present state of things is a scandal to the enlightened age in which we live. That the dispute as to the reality of these marvellous phenomena — of which it is quite impossible to exaggerate the scientific importance, if only a tenth part of what has been alleged by generally credible witnesses could be shewn to be true — I say it is a scandal that the dispute as to the reality of these phenomena should still be going on, that so many competent witnesses should have declared their belief in them, that so many others should be profoundly interested in having the question determined, and yet that the educated world, as a body, should still be simply in the attitude of incredulity.

Now the primary aim of our Society, the thing which we all unite to promote, whether as believers or non-believers, is to make a sustained and systematic attempt to remove this scandal in one way or another. Some of those whom I address feel, no doubt, that this attempt can only lead to the proof of most of the alleged phenomena; some, again, think it probable that most, if not all, will be disproved; but regarded as a Society, we are quite unpledged, and as individuals, we are all agreed that any particular investigation that we may make should be carried on with a single-minded desire to ascertain the facts, and without any foregone conclusion as to their nature."

What I can't work out is whether Sidgwick is contradicting himself here or doing something more deliberate. Calling the situation a "scandal" sounds, to my ear, like he already thinks the sceptics are the ones being unreasonable — you don't usually reach for that word unless you think the doubt itself is the failure. But then he turns around and insists the Society is "quite unpledged" and that every investigation has to proceed "without any foregone conclusion." So is the scandal that educated people won't believe in things he privately suspects are real, or is it a more procedural scandal — that nobody, believer or sceptic, has actually bothered to settle the question either way? And if it's the latter, why phrase it in language ("scandal," "attitude of incredulity") that seems to fall specifically on the sceptics, rather than equally on the credulous believers who have also failed to settle anything? I don't know if this is a genuine interpretive crux that people who study Sidgwick have noted, or if I'm just overreading a paragraph of nineteenth-century throat-clearing, but I'd like help working through how the two halves of the passage are meant to hang together, and whether this move — naming unbelief itself as the scandal, rather than any particular phenomenon — reads as a considered rhetorical strategy for someone in his position.
