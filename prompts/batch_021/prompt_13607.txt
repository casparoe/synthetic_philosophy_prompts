I spent most of my career doing statistical mechanics, and since retiring I have finally had the time to read the philosophy I never got to during my working life, mostly in decision theory and the foundations of probability. Recently I returned to the St. Petersburg paradox, which I had only ever met as a homework curiosity: a fair coin is tossed until it first lands tails, and if that happens on the nth toss you are paid 2^n ducats, so that the expected payout of the game is infinite even though almost no one would pay more than a modest sum to play it. I already knew Daniel Bernoulli's classic fix -- replace the expectation of money with the expectation of a concave utility of money, logarithmic being the standard choice, which is finite -- but a footnote in what I was reading sent me down two further paths I was never shown as a student and have not been able to settle for myself.

The first is Karl Menger's old objection that Bernoulli's trick does not really generalize: for any utility function that is unbounded above, however slowly it grows, one can construct a "Super St. Petersburg" variant of the game with payoffs growing just fast enough to make the expected utility infinite once again, so that only a utility function bounded above escapes the problem entirely -- which strikes me as buying the solution at a fairly steep price. The second, and more interesting to me given my own background, is the "ergodicity economics" line associated with the physicist Ole Peters, if I am remembering the attribution correctly: the claim is that the paradox trades on quietly substituting an ensemble average for a time average in what is really a non-ergodic, multiplicative process, and that once you ask what growth rate a single player really experiences under repeated play, a logarithm falls out as the objectively correct quantity to track rather than as a report of anyone's psychological attitude toward money. That struck me, at first, as exactly the ensemble/time-average distinction I spent forty years applying to gases and other many-particle systems, but I have since seen it dismissed by some economists as nothing more than the old Kelly-criterion growth-optimal betting rule dressed up in the language of physics -- which, if right, would mean it has relocated the question of why one statistic deserves priority over another rather than settled it.

What I would like is an essay, written for an intelligent general reader, that sets out these three positions -- Bernoulli's fix, Menger's complication of it, and Peters's dynamical reframing together with the Kelly-criterion objection to it -- clearly enough to follow without prior acquaintance with the literature, weighs them against one another honestly, and then actually commits to an answer instead of surveying politely and stopping. I am not asking for false comfort: a conditional verdict along the lines of "resolved for any realistic agent, but not in principle" would count as a real answer, whereas "reasonable people disagree" would not. What I want to know, in the end, is whether any of this machinery genuinely dissolves the St. Petersburg game, or whether the game is better read as demonstrating a real limit on the use of expectations -- of money, of utility, of growth rate, whatever we choose to average -- once a gamble's tail is heavy enough.
