I don't have any background in philosophy or law — I work as an electrician — but a few years ago I spent a long stretch reading about the trials of former East German border guards, held after German reunification, for shooting people who had tried to cross the Berlin Wall. That led me backward to the earlier, more famous "grudge informer" case that keeps turning up in books about Nazi-era law: a woman who denounced her husband to the authorities for something he said against the regime, in order to get him out of the way, and who was herself prosecuted after the war on the theory that the wartime statute she had relied on was never valid law to begin with. Chasing that case took me to H.L.A. Hart's 1958 essay "Positivism and the Separation of Law and Morals" and then to Lon Fuller's reply to it, and eventually back to Thomas Aquinas, whom both of them treat as the ancestor of the natural-law side even though neither really engages him directly. I've read the relevant parts of the Summa on law closely enough to have my own hunches, but I keep going back and forth, and I would like to see the question worked through properly by someone other than me.

So here is what I would like: a careful reconstruction of how Aquinas himself, dropped into that exact 1958 exchange, would have ruled on it, using his own categories rather than a generic "natural law says unjust laws aren't law" gloss. First, would Aquinas side with Fuller in holding that the Nazi statute simply failed to be law, or would he side with Hart's claim that it was valid law which was simply too wicked to deserve obedience — bearing in mind that Aquinas himself allows that some unjust enactments still call for outward compliance so as to avoid civil disorder? Second, Fuller's "inner morality of law" is explicitly procedural — generality, publicity, clarity, non-retroactivity, and so on — rather than a claim about what the law substantively commands. Would Aquinas recognize that as basically his own idea of natural law in modern dress, or would he judge it too thin, given that his own account of a just law also requires that it be genuinely ordered to the common good? Please commit to definite answers on both points rather than simply cataloguing considerations on each side.
