After forty years as a field ecologist, I retired determined to finally read some philosophy, and I began with Eugene Wigner's essay on why mathematics fits physics so precisely -- a question that had always nagged me, since population and ecosystem models never behaved with anything like that precision. My local discussion group devoted an evening to it, and two members, Renate and Simon, ended up in an argument that the rest of us mostly just listened to.

(1) Renate argued that the puzzle is real. Riemann worked out the general theory of curved, higher-dimensional geometry in the 1850s purely as a generalization of geometry, and roughly sixty years later it became exactly the apparatus Einstein needed for general relativity. Dirac's 1928 equation for the electron had negative-energy solutions that were read, before any experiment, as predicting a new particle, and the positron turned up a few years later. For her, this counts as evidence that mathematical structures are discovered rather than invented, and that nature simply has whatever structure the mathematics has.

(2) Simon called this survivorship bias: mathematicians produce vastly more structures than physics ever uses, and we only hear about the rare hits, never the failures. He added that our most basic mathematics -- arithmetic, Euclidean geometry, calculus -- was abstracted directly from counting, surveying, and motion in the first place, so some rough fit with nature was built in from the very start.

(3) Renate replied that this might explain elementary mathematics, but not Riemann or Dirac: in both cases the mathematics was worked out in full well before the relevant physics existed to test it, so there was no fitting after the fact -- no searching through old mathematics for something that happened to match new data.

(4) Simon answered that this still doesn't require anything as strong as mathematical Platonism: the mathematics that got pursued and developed further was disproportionately mathematics pursued by people already thinking about physical problems -- Riemann's own lecture was itself concerned with the physical nature of space and force -- so a selection effect can operate on which programs get pursued at all, long before any "hit" occurs.
