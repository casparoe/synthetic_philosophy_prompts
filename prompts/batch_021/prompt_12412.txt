hey, I'm a cog sci PhD student (my actual research is on visual motion perception) but I'm sitting in on a phil of mind seminar this term and got stuck on a Reid passage that's paired with the disjunctivism readings. Here's the bit, from Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man (1785), the chapter "Of Perception" (typing this up from my notes so sorry if a comma or two is off):

"If we attend to that act of our minds which we call the perception of an external object, we shall find in it these three things. First, some conception or notion of the object perceived. Secondly, a strong and irresistible conviction and belief of its present existence. And, thirdly, that this conviction and belief are immediate, and not the effect of reasoning."

What I can't get past is how this is supposed to be compatible with illusion at all. If perceiving something always comes bundled with this "irresistible conviction and belief" in its present existance (presumably as perceived, i.e. with whatever qualities you're perceiving it as having), then what happens on Reid's view when you misperceive something, like the classic bent oar in water, or outright hallucination? Is he just committed to perception being infallible by stipulation, which feels like it dodges the argument from illusion rather than actually answers it? He's clearly not an infallibilist about the senses in general (he's got a whole bit elsewhere about sensory "fallacies"), so is there some distinction he's relying on that lets this definition survive contact with ordinary illusions? Also curious how much weight this passage can actually bear as an ancestor of contemporary disjunctivism, vs. people just reading their preferred view back into him.
