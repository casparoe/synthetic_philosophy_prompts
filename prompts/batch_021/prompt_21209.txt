I am at work on a novel whose protagonist — called simply M. in my drafts — underwent a corpus callosotomy in her twenties to control severe epilepsy. Late in the book, a philosopher who has befriended her delivers a dinner-table monologue defending Roland Puccetti's old claim that M. was never a single subject of experience housed in one skull, but two, and that the surgery merely made visible a duality the intact corpus callosum had always kept seamlessly hidden. I want the monologue to read as genuine, rigorous philosophy rather than a strawman, so it has to withstand two objections. The first is Thomas Nagel's point that the evidence from split-brain patients supports "two minds" no better than it supports "one mind" or "an indeterminate case," since enough integrated, whole-body behavior survives the surgery to embarrass any clean two-agents picture. The second is a body of more recent experimental work, associated with Yair Pinto and colleagues, reporting that some split-brain patients can act correctly on information from both visual fields using a single hand — something the classical Sperry/Gazzaniga model of two self-contained hemispheric agents says should be impossible, and which its authors read as evidence for one bottlenecked conscious agent rather than two. Please write the strongest version of the two-persons argument capable of surviving contact with both objections, stating plainly which of its premises must be weakened or abandoned in order to do so, and tell me honestly if you don't think the position can be saved at all.
