I spent thirty-eight years as an experimental physicist, the last twenty of them running the mass standards section of a national metrology institute, and it's only since retiring that I've started reading philosophy in earnest — mostly epistemology, because it keeps circling back to problems I spent my career quietly working around rather than solving. I've now gone through the standard presentations of the regress problem (I believe it's sometimes called Agrippa's trilemma) and the three canonical responses to it — foundationalism, coherentism, and Peter Klein's infinitism — and I can't stop thinking about how each of them would actually cash out along a real calibration chain: the sequence by which an ordinary lab balance is certified against a working standard, which is certified against a national standard, which used to be certified, ultimately, against a single object.

That object was the International Prototype of the Kilogram, a platinum-iridium cylinder kept under multiple locks near Paris, against which every other kilogram on Earth was, in the end, compared — a chain that stopped, by fiat, at one physical artifact rather than at any further reason. In May 2019 the metrology community replaced it with a definition anchored in a fixed value of Planck's constant, so that the kilogram now derives from a small web of defining constants and the experiments that realize them, rather than from a single privileged object in a vault. It struck me, reading about this shortly after finishing a chapter on infinitism, that the old and new systems look almost like rival answers to the regress problem, worked out in hardware rather than argued in a seminar, and I would like help thinking about that properly rather than just gesturing at the resemblance.

Could you build a detailed, technically careful example — drawn from this history, or from calibration practice more generally if that serves the philosophy better — in which foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism don't just redescribe the same traceability chain in different vocabularies, but actually deliver different verdicts about whether one specific, everyday calibration is justified? I'd like it precise enough that someone who has never handled a weight set could see exactly where the three theories part ways, and, if you're willing, your own sense of which position the post-2019 SI comes closest to vindicating — or whether it just relocates the arbitrariness rather than removing it.
