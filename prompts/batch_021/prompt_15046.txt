heres the student's answer:

"Zahavi's handicap principle addresses a basic puzzle in animal communication: if signaling quality were cheap, low-quality individuals could just fake it, so over time signals would stop being informative. Zahavi's answer (1975) was that a signal stays honest only if it's costly enough that faking it isn't worth it for a low-quality individual, so mainly high-quality individuals can afford to produce it. The classic case is the peacock's tail: a huge, brightly colored train is expensive to grow and makes the male slower and more visible to predators, so it's a genuine handicap. Only a male in excellent condition can grow a train like that and still survive, so peahens that prefer big trains are effectively choosing mates that have proven their quality by surviving in spite of a real handicap. Zahavi's idea was pretty controversial at first and mostly dismissed as an unproven, purely verbal argument, but it was eventually given solid mathematical backing by Fisher's runaway selection model, which showed that once a female preference for a trait and the trait itself become genetically linked, they amplify each other generation after generation until they settle at an equilibrium set by the balance of mating advantage against survival cost - proving that a costly ornament like the peacock's tail really can be a stable, reliable indicator of quality."
