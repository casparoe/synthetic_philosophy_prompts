Gregory Kavka's toxin puzzle asks us to imagine an eccentric billionaire who will pay you a large sum of money the moment you form a sincere intention, tonight, to drink a purely unpleasant but non-lethal toxin at noon tomorrow; a reliable test can detect whether you have genuinely formed that intention, so payment never depends on your actually drinking the toxin later. Kavka argues that a rational, self-interested agent cannot form this intention, since once the money is banked there is no longer any self-interested reason to follow through, and you can foresee this tonight.

I would like your best reconstruction of how Thomas Hobbes, reasoning from his account of covenants in Leviathan and especially his reply to the Foole in chapter XV, would have diagnosed this puzzle. Would Hobbes think a purely self-interested person could rationally form and hold such an intention? And does his usual defense of covenant-keeping, which rests on the covenant-breaker's fear of a ruined reputation and exclusion from future cooperative ventures, have any purchase in a case like this one, where there is no counterparty to betray and nothing resembling a reputation at stake? Please reason from Hobbes's own commitments rather than simply importing the toxin puzzle's standard treatment in the contemporary decision-theory literature.
