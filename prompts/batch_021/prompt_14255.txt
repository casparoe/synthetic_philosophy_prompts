Since retiring four years ago after a long career as an evolutionary biologist, I have finally had time to read moral philosophy properly rather than in the margins of grant applications, and I have become quite absorbed by David Benatar's case for antinatalism. A small reading group of fellow retirees and I recently worked through the central "asymmetry" argument in *Better Never to Have Been*, and we found it genuinely elegant as a piece of reasoning: the presence of pain is bad, the presence of pleasure is good, the absence of pain is good even when there is no one around to enjoy that good, and the absence of pleasure is bad only when there is someone who is thereby deprived of it. From these four claims Benatar concludes that non-existence is always preferable to existence, no matter how good a life turns out to be, since existence guarantees at least some harm while non-existence guarantees none.

What none of us could resolve is why this is supposed to stop mattering once a being already exists. If non-existence is strictly better than existence for every sentient being without exception, it seems to follow directly that ending an existence already underway should likewise count as removing a guaranteed harm rather than inflicting one, so that something like a permissive or even favourable attitude toward suicide should drop straight out of the asymmetry. I gather Benatar anticipates this and replies that the interests an already-existing being has in its own continuation are not comparable to the (non-existent) interests of a being that is merely never created, so that the two cases are governed by different considerations entirely. But this reply strikes me as uncomfortably close to simply asserting that existing interests count for more than possible ones, which sounds like exactly the kind of unprincipled status-quo bias one would ordinarily be suspicious of in an argument this austere. Could you help me see precisely where my inference from the asymmetry to a suicide-favouring conclusion goes wrong, if indeed it does, and whether Benatar's way of blocking it is a principled feature of his framework or more of an ad hoc patch on it?
