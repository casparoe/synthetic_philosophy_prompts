My grandmother spent three decades as a serious abstract painter -- two of her large canvases still hang in a regional museum's permanent collection -- before she read Tolstoy's What Is Art? in her sixties and concluded, by his lights, that the paintings had never once infected an ordinary viewer with real feeling, and belonged, in his own phrase, among the "counterfeit" art made for people trained to like clever, difficult things rather than art any neighbor could feel in the chest. She spent her remaining twenty years making small, unsigned woodcuts of Bible stories and neighbors' gardens instead, gave them away rather than sold them, and left explicit instructions that any future exhibition of the early paintings describe them only as "a well-made mistake," never as art in the sense that actually matters. She died last spring, and that museum is now opening a joint retrospective -- the abstractions and the woodcuts in the same three rooms -- for an audience split between curators and donors who regard the abstractions as her real achievement, and the townspeople who own her woodcuts and have never once used the word "oeuvre." As her grandchild, and the one in the family who writes about ideas for a living, I've been asked to give the ten-minute opening remarks. Write the speech: it has to honor her instructions, make sense of the room for both halves of the audience, and not quietly resolve the tension by implying that she, and Tolstoy, simply got it wrong.
