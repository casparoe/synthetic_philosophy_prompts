I don't have any formal background in philosophy or physics, but a few months ago I fell into a long stretch of pop-science videos about determinism and the nature of time, and I got stuck on a question that none of them really answered: when we say something is a "law of nature," is that law something that makes events go a certain way, or is it just a convenient label we attach afterward to a pattern the events happen to form anyway? Digging into this on my own, I found out it's an actual, unresolved dispute in philosophy, between so-called "Humeans," who hold that a law is nothing more than whichever regularity comes out on top when the entire pattern of everything that ever happens is weighed for the best balance of simplicity and strength (strength meaning roughly how much the regularity tells you) -- I gather this is essentially David Lewis's "best system" account -- and "anti-Humeans," who deny this can be the whole story, since then laws couldn't really explain the very patterns they're meant to explain, and who instead hold that a law consists in a genuine relation of necessitation between properties themselves, so the regularity gets produced rather than merely reported after the fact (closer, as far as I can tell, to what David Armstrong argued).

Could you write me a short dialogue between two characters holding these opposing views, set somewhere concrete and unglamorous -- say, the two of them stuck watching one of those Newton's-cradle desk toys work through a demonstration of momentum conservation -- in which each tries to talk the other out of their position? Please don't let either character simply restate their own view and declare victory: press the Humean on whether her "law" amounts to more than a flattering name for the pattern itself, and on whether the simplicity/strength trade-off that is supposed to pick out the laws isn't quietly relative to how we happen to carve the world into properties in the first place; press the anti-Humean, in turn, on the standard complaint that merely christening a further relation "necessitation" doesn't explain why the regularity holds unless he's smuggled in the needed explanatory work rather than actually earning it. I'd like at least a couple of rounds in which each responds to the specific objection just raised rather than to a caricature of it, and I'd like the dialogue to end without a clear winner -- ideally with the two of them trying to pin down exactly what evidence or argument would have to be produced to change either mind, or, failing that, owning up to some doubt about whether their disagreement is about anything beyond a form of words.
