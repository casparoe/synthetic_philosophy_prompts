A friend of mine, who is finishing a PhD in analytic theology, and I spent most of a long drive home from a Good Friday service arguing about penal substitution, and we never really reached an agreement. I had brought up a short paper by David Lewis, "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?", in which he points out an asymmetry in our ordinary intuitions about criminal punishment: most people are willing to let a third party pay a wrongdoer's fine, even a substantial one, but almost no one thinks an innocent volunteer should be allowed to serve someone else's prison term or be executed in their place. Lewis's diagnosis is that substitution only makes sense where punishment functions mainly as a kind of compensation owed to the victim, so that it does not matter who provides it, much as with an ordinary debt; imprisonment and execution, by contrast, do not seem to be discharged just because something equally unpleasant happened to someone else. My friend wanted to resist applying this to the cross by appealing to the idea that Christ, though innocent, identified himself with humanity closely enough to make the substitution intelligible in a way that a stranger paying your fine is not.

I pushed back with a move I recalled from Mark Murphy's paper "Not Penal Substitution but Vicarious Punishment," which sharpens Lewis's worry rather than resolving it: punishment, at least of the imprisonment-or-execution variety, is essentially an expressive act condemning whoever it falls on, and expressive acts cannot be redirected onto a willing volunteer without losing the very meaning that made them punishment in the first place, however closely that volunteer is identified with the guilty party. Murphy takes this to show that strict penal substitution is a conceptual non-starter, and proposes instead that the guilty party's punishment can consist in the suffering of someone standing in a special relationship to them, which he insists is genuinely different from substitution rather than a relabeling of it. My friend was not convinced there is a real difference, and by the end of the drive neither was I.

Write an essay that settles this. Reconstruct Lewis's fine/imprisonment asymmetry and his compensatory diagnosis as precisely as you can, then work through whether "identification" can do the job my friend wanted it to do against the expressive-act objection, and whether Murphy's vicarious-punishment proposal is genuinely distinct from penal substitution or just the same idea wearing a different label. Do not settle for cataloguing considerations on both sides: tell me whether penal substitution, or some suitably repaired descendant of it, survives this challenge, and defend that verdict against the strongest form of the view you reject.
