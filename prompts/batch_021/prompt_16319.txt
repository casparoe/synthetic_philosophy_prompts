I'm a junior working on my IB Extended Essay in philosophy. I've moved away from the standard "was Bentham right about homosexuality" framing and toward a question I find more interesting: can Bentham's own principles explain why he refused to publish his most radical argument on the subject? Below is the draft of my introduction and central argument — I have about 3,500 words left before the final word limit, so this is just the opening section.

Jeremy Bentham reduced morality to a calculus of pleasure and pain, yet the essay in which he applied that calculus most rigorously is one he chose to bury. Sometime in the 1780s he drafted a long manuscript, now usually catalogued as "Offences Against One's Self," arguing that England's laws against consensual sex between men could not survive his own felicific calculus. The acts produced pleasure for the parties involved and no demonstrable harm to anyone else, so the "pain" the law claimed to prevent was really nothing but the disgust of onlookers. Bentham elsewhere names this substitute for real calculation the principle of sympathy and antipathy: legislating according to one's own approval or revulsion and mistaking the feeling for a reason. Weighed against the intensity, duration, and certainty of the suffering caused by exposure, blackmail, the pillory, and the gallows, he concluded the law inflicted vastly more pain than it ever averted.

He never published it. The manuscript sat among his papers until the historian Louis Crompton drew scholarly attention to it in the twentieth century, and it did not reach a full modern edition until Philip Schofield's volume of Bentham's writings on sexual morality appeared decades later still. Bentham said plainly, in notes never meant for print, that he expected publication to cost him his reputation and his influence over the law reforms he cared about more, while doing little to change a single line of statute in his own lifetime.

Most discussions of this stop at calling it a failure of nerve: the calculus said one thing, and Bentham's courage said another. I want to argue that this is too quick, because the silence itself admits a utilitarian justification of exactly the kind Henry Sidgwick would later formalize as "esoteric morality" and Bernard Williams would mock as "Government House utilitarianism": if publishing a true but explosive conclusion would, given a bigoted audience, produce worse consequences than sitting on it, a consistent utilitarian is obligated to sit on it. On this reading, Bentham was not abandoning his principles when he stayed silent; he was applying them one level up, to the act of speaking itself. But the move seems to prove too much. If the calculus can always be turned against publishing its own unwelcome results, I don't know what would count, for a sufficiently cautious utilitarian, as the arrival of the right moment to speak. Bentham seems to have died still waiting for it, and I am not sure his own theory gives him any principled way of recognizing that moment even in hindsight.

I'd appreciate comments on three things in particular: whether I've represented Bentham's actual arguments and the relevant publication history accurately, whether the Sidgwick parallel is a genuine philosophical point or one I'm forcing onto the material, and which of the three paragraphs above is weakest and most needs another layer of support.
