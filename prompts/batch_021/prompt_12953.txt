At a rationalist-adjacent reading group, two regulars got into a running disagreement about a case one of them raised: a sequence of angel investors who each observe only prior investors' yes/no decisions, not their reasons, before deciding whether to fund the same startup. The first two passed; every investor after that passed too, even though several later admitted their private read of the founder had actually been mildly positive.

(1) Elena called this a textbook information cascade: once two 'no' decisions had occurred in a row, each subsequent investor's belief that the deal was bad already sat past the point where a single ordinary-precision private signal could flip it, so discarding their own impression and passing was individually rational and Bayes-optimal -- the tragedy being that a cascade can lock onto the wrong outcome even when most private signals were favorable.

(2) Marcus objected that this was groupthink with extra math: if someone's private read was genuinely positive, discarding it wasn't 'rational,' it was a failure of nerve, and calling it Bayes-optimal launders a bias as a virtue.

(3) Elena replied that rationality here is about the update, not the outcome: with bounded, roughly symmetric signal precision, a lone contrary impression shouldn't outweigh two independent public data points -- which is exactly why such cascades are simultaneously rational and fragile, resting on very little total evidence and reversible by one strong new signal, unlike a real consensus built by pooling everyone's information.

(4) Marcus countered that real investors have no reliable way of knowing their signal precision relative to everyone else's, so invoking 'bounded, symmetric precision' to certify the cascade as rational just imports the model's most convenient assumption into a case where it probably fails -- and mostly serves to make ordinary herding sound principled.
