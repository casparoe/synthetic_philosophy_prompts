As a novelist plotting a story built around a working time machine, I'd rather ground its internal rules in the actual philosophy of time travel than in whatever convention science fiction happens to have normalized. Compare David Lewis's resolution of the grandfather paradox in "The Paradoxes of Time Travel" — which preserves a single, unbranching timeline by distinguishing two senses of 'can' relative to different sets of facts held fixed, so that the traveler is free to attempt to kill his grandfather yet is always, as a brute matter of fact, unsuccessful — with the more familiar branching-timelines resolution, on which the traveler's lethal visit simply diverges into a distinct history, leaving the timeline that produced him untouched. Lewis's account has been criticized for buying its consistency at the cost of an implausible pile-up of coincidences every time the would-be killer is thwarted; the branching account avoids that cost, but apparently only by denying that the traveler who arrives in the past and the person later born belong to one and the same history. Weigh these two costs against each other and defend a definite verdict on which account actually succeeds in solving the grandfather paradox rather than merely relocating it — and say what that verdict implies for whether a time-travel plot can have genuine stakes, or whether branching inevitably hands the protagonist an escape hatch that a single, fixed timeline denies him.
