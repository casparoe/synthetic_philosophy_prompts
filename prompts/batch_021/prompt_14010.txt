I'm on my hospital's ethics committee, and this week's case hits closer to home than I'd like, since my own mom was just diagnosed with early Alzheimers this spring. Our patient wrote a directive eight years ago, while fully competent, refusing a feeding tube or antibiotics once she no longer recognized her own kids -- comfort care only. She's there now: doesn't know her son, can't hold a conversation, but she still hums along to her old records and eats happily off a spoon most days, and she's just come down with aspiration pneumonia. Half the family wants the directive honored as written; the other half says the woman who signed it and the woman in the bed now are different enough, psychologically, that it shouldn't just automatically bind her -- that this person, who actually exists and seems content, would choose differently. Our policy says a directive controls "absent clear and convincing evidence the patient would decide otherwise now," language clearly written for patients who can still talk, not this. Could you draft the short opinion I'll circulate before we meet Thursday: a concrete recommendation on the pneumonia and the bigger feeding-tube decision behind it, with a timeframe, that actually engages the "she's not that person anymore" argument instead of just citing the directive's legal weight?
