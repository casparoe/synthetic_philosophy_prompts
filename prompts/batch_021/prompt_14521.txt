For an extension project in my ethics elective, I've been reading Jeremy Bentham's unpublished essay usually referred to as "Offences Against One's Self," where he argues that England's laws against sodomy should be scrapped because private, consensual acts harm no one, while their punishment — historically execution, plus the constant danger of blackmail once a mere accusation could ruin a man — inflicts enormous, needless pain. He ties this to his attack on what he calls the "principle of asceticism": treating pleasure as suspect simply because tradition finds it distasteful, rather than asking what it actually does to anyone's happiness. I'm curious what he'd say about something he obviously never imagined: AI companion apps built and marketed specifically to give people romantic or sexual intimacy. Running his own felicific calculus — intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, and extent — would he file this under "none of the law's business," the same way he treats sodomy, since only the consenting user is affected? Or do fecundity and purity give him a genuinely utilitarian reason to worry, if this kind of intimacy crowds out the relationships that produce more durable happiness, as opposed to a merely ascetic discomfort with it? I'd like you to actually work through the calculus rather than just asserting an answer.
