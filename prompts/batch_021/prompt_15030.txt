I'm a PhD student in political theory working on nineteenth-century American constitutional thought, and I'm currently drafting a dissertation chapter on the quarrel that split the abolitionist movement at mid-century. I understand each side's conclusions well enough, but I'm having trouble seeing how the disagreement actually functioned as a live argument between two sophisticated interlocutors, rather than as two slogans recited past one another. Please write a dialogue set shortly after Frederick Douglass's public break with William Lloyd Garrison in 1851, in which Douglass defends his newly adopted view that the Constitution, read on its own terms, affords no sanction to slavery, while Garrison presses his older claim that the document is, in his own well-known phrase, "a covenant with death, and an agreement with hell." I'd like them to genuinely engage each other's strongest moves — including what to make of the document's conspicuous avoidance of the words "slave" and "slavery," and whether a text's meaning is fixed by its wording or by the known expectations of those who ratified it — rather than simply restating their own conclusions.
