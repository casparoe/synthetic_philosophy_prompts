I'm a philosophy PhD student on the three-member graduate-student committee that awards our department's annual prize for the best student-authored paper. Under the committee's bylaws, a paper wins only if it clears three independently necessary criteria: originality, argumentative rigor, and significance of contribution. On this year's leading submission, the three of us agree on the criteria but disagree about which ones the paper actually clears: I judge that it meets all three; a second panelist judges that it meets rigor and significance but not originality; the third judges that it meets originality and significance but not rigor. Tallied criterion by criterion, the paper wins each one by majority (2–1 on originality, 2–1 on rigor, 3–0 on significance) and so should receive the prize; tallied by simply polling the three of us on "award" or "no award," it loses 2–1, since I'm the only one willing to support the award outright. Our bylaws say nothing about which tally governs, and I recognize this as a discursive dilemma rather than ordinary disagreement. Could you draft the short memo I'll circulate to my co-panelists recommending which aggregation procedure the committee should formally adopt for future cases -- criterion-by-criterion, direct vote, or some principled alternative -- with reasoning strong enough to persuade a colleague who currently favors whichever procedure you end up rejecting?
