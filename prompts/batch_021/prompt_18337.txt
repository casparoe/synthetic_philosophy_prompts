I'm a senior philosophy major, and the seminar I'm finishing this term, Social Ontology, spent its last three weeks on David Lewis's *Convention* together with some of the evolutionary game theory that grew out of it (Skyrms, mainly), which tries to explain how a norm can take hold across a population purely through repeated interaction, with nobody ever designing it or signing up to it. My best friend and roommate was in the same seminar and wrote her term paper defending Lewis against the standard objection that his account explains why people do conform to a convention but never quite why they ought to; she is getting married in three weeks and has asked me to give the toast, on the single condition that it be "properly Lewisian." Roughly half the room will be people from our cohort who will notice immediately if I fumble the arbitrariness condition or reach for a cheap "love is a Nash equilibrium" one-liner instead of the real apparatus; the other half is her family, who have never heard "common knowledge" used as a technical term and just want to be moved.

Could you write that toast for me? It should run three to four minutes read aloud, stay warm rather than lecture-y, and actually use Lewis's own machinery -- common knowledge, precedent, salience, and especially the arbitrariness condition that separates a genuine convention from a law of nature or the uniquely rational thing to do -- to say something specific and true about this particular couple, rather than hanging generic wedding sentiments off borrowed jargon. Please also work in, briefly, the evolutionary side of things: the idea that the small private rules a couple accumulates -- who does the dishes, which side of the bed, the nickname nobody else is allowed to use -- are less like a contract they negotiated once than like something selected for through hundreds of small repeated interactions. I'd like one real joke about coordination problems or Schelling points that the philosophers in the room will actually laugh at without losing everyone else, and I need the word "arbitrary" kept from curdling into something that sounds like it's diminishing the marriage -- the whole point is that Lewis's picture, taken seriously, turns out not to be cynical about this at all.
