A friend and I had a long argument over dinner last week about Keynes's 1930 essay "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren," and I'd like a neutral read on which of us has the better case. We agree on the basics: Keynes predicted that within a century, compound growth would solve the "economic problem" and leave per-capita output some four to eight times higher, a call that has more or less held up. We also agree that his companion prediction, a fifteen-hour working week made possible by that abundance, plainly never arrived. Where we disagree is on why, and each of us thinks our own explanation is the one his essay actually supports.

My friend points to Keynes's distinction between "absolute" needs, which are satiable, and "relative" needs, which track one's standing against everyone else and can never be finally satisfied. Her view: modern consumption became so thoroughly positional that the second category swallowed the first, so the problem was never really solved for anyone, however rich society became.

I pointed instead to a different worry in the same essay: that people freed from necessity, lacking any "associations or duties or ties," struggle to find purpose in unstructured freedom. Keynes illustrated this with the idle rich of his own day, whom he judged to have mostly failed at exactly that task. The clearest living version, I'd say, is the FIRE (financial independence, retire early) movement, where people who have unambiguously satisfied both their absolute and relative needs still cannot stop, hence "one more year syndrome" and early retirees quietly drifting back into consulting. My friend's comeback was that hustle culture is intensifying, not fading, which fits an escalating status race better than a one-off adjustment problem; I said each generation has to rediscover the purpose problem from scratch, and social media has just made the resulting anxiety more visible, not necessarily worse.

We never settled it. Judging from what Keynes actually argued, rather than just the famous fifteen-hour-week line, whose diagnosis fits his own logic better, or would he have explained the gap some third way? I'd also like to know what you think he would actually say if someone described "one more year syndrome" to him.
