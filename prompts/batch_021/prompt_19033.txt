So we covered van Fraassen's Reflection Principle in my formal epistemology seminar this week (roughly: if you're already sure your credence in some proposition A will be r at a later time, your current credence in A, conditional on that future credence, should also be r), and it kicked off this whole argument after class between two people in my cohort, Nora and Ben, that nobody could settle.

Nora's counterexample: say you know that tommorow you're getting a drug that'll make you 90% confident a coin you already flipped (but never looked at) landed heads, completely independent of how it actually landed. Reflection says your credence in heads right now, conditional on 'tomorrow I'll be 90% confident of heads,' should be 0.9. She said that's obviously wrong, since you already know that future 90% isn't tracking anything about the coin.

Ben said that's not a real counterexample, just a misapplication -- the way our prof explained it in section, Reflection is only meant to tie you to future credences you'd expect from updating rationally on evidence, so a drugged credence is outside its scope from the start.

Nora's comeback: fine, but then the principle isn't doing the work it's advertised as doing. It's sold as a general rationality constraint, backed by a Dutch-book argument, telling you which credence trajectories are okay. If it only applies once you've already certified the future shift as rational, it can never flag an irrational trajectory in advance -- and for ordinary cases like slowly forgetting your evidence, or getting worn down by peer pressure, there's no clean line in advance between 'legitimate update' and 'illegitimate drift' like there is with an obvious drug.

Ben's reply: that's true of conditionalization too -- it only tells you what to do given that e is genuine evidence, and nobody thinks that empties conditionalization of content, so why is the same kind of restriction on Reflection any different?

Nora said there's a real disanalogy: conditionalization builds the evidence-clause into the rule itself, so its upfront about needing e to be genuine. Reflection, as originally stated, was an unconditional constraint relating any two of your own credences across time, defended by an unconditional Dutch-book argument. Quietly adding 'unless the shift is illegitimate' afterward is a retreat from that, not just a clarification of it.
