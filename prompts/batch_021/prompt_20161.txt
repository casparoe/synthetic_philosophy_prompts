I'm editor-in-chief of my high school's student newspaper, and I need a written recommendation to bring to our four-person editorial board — me, two section editors, and our faculty advisor, who has final sign-off before anything runs — before we meet this Wednesday, two days ahead of the school board's vote on renewing funding for the district's Restorative Discipline Initiative. One of our reporters got hold of an internal email thread showing that the drop in disciplinary referrals the administration has cited to parents and the board all year is mostly the product of quietly reclassifying incidents rather than any real change in student behavior, and that two administrators discussed, in writing, how to keep presenting the numbers as "a win" before the vote.

Last spring, in a political philosophy elective, we read parts of Hannah Arendt's essays "Truth and Politics" and "Lying in Politics," and I haven't been able to shake her point that a lie about facts, unlike a lie about arithmetic, never runs into a contradiction, since things really could have happened the way the liar says — which is supposedly what makes this kind of "defactualization" so easy to get away with once it's loose in a community. Our board is split down the middle over what that means for us. Two of us think sitting on this until after the vote is exactly the failure Arendt is describing, since, as she puts it, "freedom of opinion is a farce" if the facts people are voting on aren't secure. Our advisor agrees the numbers were spun, but thinks that running the story days before the vote turns the paper from a record-keeper into a player trying to decide the outcome — which is close to what Arendt means when she says a truthteller forfeits their credibility the moment they step into the political fight instead of just reporting and stepping back.

Could you draft the roughly one-page memo I'd actually circulate before Wednesday? I need it to land on one concrete recommendation — run the story now, hold it until after the vote, or run a narrower version now with a fuller follow-up later — with a specific timeline, rather than a list of considerations on both sides. I'd like it to use Arendt's actual arguments instead of generic talking points about journalism holding power accountable, and I don't want it to brush past our advisor's worry about the paper turning into a political actor — she's the one person on the board who can actually kill the story, so whatever the memo recommends, it needs to give her reasoning a serious answer, not just a majority vote against it.
