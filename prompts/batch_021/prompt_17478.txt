A friend and I spent most of Sunday evening arguing about Parfit's hitchhiker, and the argument only got started because we'd been reading about how researchers working on AI alignment invoke the case to argue for a third theory of rational choice -- "functional decision theory" -- as superior to both the causal and evidential varieties. You know the setup: dying of thirst in the desert, I'm picked up by a driver who will only take me to town if her infallible lie detector confirms that I sincerely intend to pay her $1,000 on arrival; I form the intention, we drive, and I'm dropped off safe and well with the money still in my pocket. My friend, a committed causal decision theorist, insists that once I'm standing in town the ride is already a fixed fact about the past, so paying cannot cause it to have happened, and keeping the money strictly dominates handing it over. When I tried to enlist evidential decision theory on my side, she pointed out, correctly I think, that EDT agrees with her here too, since by then there's no remaining uncertainty about whether I got the ride for conditionalizing to exploit. I kept reaching for Parfit's own term "rational irrationality," for Gauthier's point about the transparency a constrained maximizer needs in order not to be exploited by a straightforward maximizer, and for McClennen's resolute choice, but every time I deployed one of these as though it settled the matter, she had a sharper reply waiting.

Write the dialogue we should have had instead, between the hitchhiker -- freshly arrived in town, money still on her -- and her friend the causal decision theorist, who is urging her not to pay. Both should argue at full strength: the causal decision theorist needs to offer more than "the past is fixed," and the hitchhiker needs to do more than invoke Parfit's phrase as though naming the puzzle were the same as solving it. I'd like several genuine exchanges in which each responds to the specific objection the other has just raised, rather than two monologues running in parallel, and I don't want either of them to concede by the end, because neither of us did.
