My father passed away last month, and in the weeks since, a family friend of ours — a retired mathematics professor who has always been a source of comfort to me — brought up something she had read about Kurt Gödel, apparently meaning to console me with it. Her account was that Gödel, the same man who proved that no consistent formal system can capture all arithmetical truths, later turned that same rigor toward the question of an afterlife and left behind, among his papers, an actual proof that the human personality must survive bodily death — resting on the idea that the world is fundamentally "rational" and that it would be absurd for a mind's development to simply be cut short by death. She mentioned this in the same breath as his formal proof of God's existence, as though the two belonged to a single body of rigorously demonstrated results, and suggested that this puts belief in an afterlife on unusually solid, almost mathematical footing for someone as exacting as Gödel.

I would like to be able to explain this properly to my siblings, who are considerably more skeptical than I am and will want the actual reasoning rather than a secondhand paraphrase. Could you walk me through the logical structure of Gödel's proof that personal identity survives death: what its premises were, how it connects to, or differs from, his separate ontological argument for God, and how the conclusion is supposed to follow? I would rather understand precisely what he demonstrated than pass along something that merely sounds rigorous.
