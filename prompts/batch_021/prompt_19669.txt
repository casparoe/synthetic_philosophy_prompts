On several old Overcoming Bias and LessWrong threads about decision theory under unbounded gambles, I keep running into people gesturing at the "Pasadena paradox" as some sort of nastier cousin of the St. Petersburg paradox, without ever quite explaining why it's supposed to be worse. As I understand the two cases: in the classic St. Petersburg game, a fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads, and you're paid 2^n if the first heads occurs on toss n; the expected payoff is a divergent sum of positive terms, so it comes out unambiguously infinite, and there's a familiar toolkit for defusing the resulting puzzle — bounded utility functions, diminishing marginal utility of money, objections from the counterparty's necessarily finite wealth, and so on. Nover and Hájek's Pasadena game instead pays (-1)^(n+1) × 2^n⁄n if the first heads occurs on toss n, so the terms of the expectation alternate in sign and sum to the alternating harmonic series, which converges to ln 2 only conditionally, not absolutely. Since the series isn't absolutely convergent, Riemann's rearrangement theorem tells us that reordering the very same underlying outcomes lets you make the "expected value" equal any real number you like, or diverge to positive or negative infinity, or oscillate without ever settling on a value.

I'd like you to compare the two paradoxes properly, rather than treating Pasadena as just "St. Petersburg, but weirder." Please explain precisely why an expectation that is undefined by rearrangement constitutes a different, and arguably deeper, kind of problem than one that is merely infinite, and then assess whether the standard rescues for St. Petersburg — bounding utility, invoking finite wealth, or simply refusing to take unbounded gambles seriously — do anything at all to help with Pasadena. Close with a clear, justified verdict: which of the two paradoxes poses the more serious threat to someone committed to maximizing expected value, and why?
