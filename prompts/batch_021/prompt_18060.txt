I've spent a fair amount of the last few years reading through the predictive-processing and free-energy-principle literature—partly triggered by Scott Alexander's old Slate Star Codex review of Andy Clark's Surfing Uncertainty, and partly by chasing citations out of various LessWrong threads on active inference and the Bayesian brain. Last week a friend from my local rationalist meetup and I got into an argument—one that has since escalated into a multi-day Discord thread—about whether Karl Friston's appeal to Markov blankets actually earns him the metaphysical conclusion that he and several of his philosophical collaborators seem to want: that the free energy principle supplies a principled, formal criterion for where a self, organism, or cognitive system stops and the rest of the world begins. I wrote up my side of the argument as a short piece I'm considering posting to the thread, or cleaning up for my own blog, and I'd like an outside read before I do either. I'm not an academic in this area, just someone who has read a lot of the primary and secondary literature over the years, so I'm especially interested in whether I'm being fair to Friston's actual position and to the strongest available reply, rather than to a strawman of it.

Here's roughly what I have so far:

The free energy principle individuates a "thing"—a cell, an organism, a self—by partitioning the variables of a system into internal states, external states, and a Markov blanket of sensory and active states that separates them, such that internal and external states are conditionally independent given the blanket. Because the internal states can then be read as encoding a probabilistic model of whatever external states generated the blanket's sensory part, Friston gets to say that a system "infers" its environment merely by persisting—a move sometimes glossed as self-evidencing. Several of his collaborators have leaned on this to argue that the framework dissolves the old boundary-of-life-and-mind problem: you no longer need to stipulate by fiat where the organism stops and the environment starts, because the statistics of the joint distribution hand you the partition for free.

The trouble, as I understand the objection some critics have pressed, is that having a Markov blanket is cheap. Given more or less any joint distribution over a set of variables, you can find some partition into internal, blanket, and external sets that satisfies the conditional-independence condition, including partitions drawn around thoroughly non-agential systems, or around gerrymandered subsets of a system that nobody would want to call a self. So the mere existence of a blanket can't be what picks out the biologically or cognitively interesting boundary; you need some further, non-formal fact about which blankets are the real ones, as opposed to statistical artifacts of how you sliced up the variables. I know the standard reply is supposed to involve some notion of a "particular" blanket that is dynamically self-sustaining—one that persists because the internal states actively maintain it through action, possibly cashed out via a renormalization-style argument across nested scales, like cells inside organs inside organisms—as opposed to a merely "generic" blanket that is a static artifact of an arbitrary factorization.

But it isn't obvious to me that this rescues the original ambition. Figuring out which partitions count as self-sustaining in the relevant sense seems to require exactly the kind of independent biological or dynamical facts—metabolism, homeostasis, adaptive regulation—that the framework was supposed to let us dispense with. If that's right, the free energy principle inherits the boundary problem rather than solving it; it just relocates the hard question one level down, into which Markov blankets deserve to be called particular.

What I'd like from you: first, tell me whether I've represented Friston's position and its best defense accurately, or whether I'm missing a real rebuttal to the "cheap blanket" worry that would close the gap I think is still open. Second, I'm curious whether you think this kind of objection—that a formalism is being credited with individuative work that's actually smuggled in from outside it—also threatens other projects with similar ambitions, such as integrated information theory's account of the boundaries of a conscious "complex," or the biological-individuality debates in philosophy of biology; I have a hunch these are structurally similar failures, but I haven't worked out whether the analogy really holds. Finally, feel free to flag anywhere the argument is unclear, overconfident, or resting on a term I haven't pinned down carefully enough.
