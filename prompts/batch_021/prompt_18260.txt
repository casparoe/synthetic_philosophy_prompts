Over the past year, my younger brother and I have been at odds over how to divide the responsibilities of caring for our aging mother, and the conflict has brought to the surface a pattern in our family that I keep turning over without resolving. My brother has never been the one who follows through: he distanced himself when our father was seriously ill a decade ago, and he is inconsistent now, frequently cancelling visits at the last minute. When I confront him about it, he tells me, essentially, that this is simply who he is and has always been, and our family's therapist has more or less confirmed as much, tracing the pattern back to the way our parents singled him out as the difficult child from an early age, in constant unfavorable comparison to me and our older sister. My instinct is to insist that he could still choose, in any given moment, to show up anyway. But a few years ago he did try to change, going to therapy on his own and promising to do better, and whether he actually followed through on any of it seemed to depend on some further capacity for persistence that was either present or absent in him on a given day rather than something he had simply decided to have. Whenever I try to locate the exact point at which he becomes the source of his own conduct, rather than merely the outcome of a history he never chose, I fail to find it; and what unsettles me further is that the same seems true of my own more admirable habit of always showing up, which I can trace just as easily to parents who rewarded me for competence from childhood onward.

I mentioned this to a friend recently, expecting her to simply take my side, and instead she asked a question I still can't answer: at what point, exactly, does either of us stop being merely the product of what happened to us and start being responsible, in some fuller sense, for what we do? I still feel angry at my brother, and I still believe, in some ordinary sense, that I am right to be the more dependable one, but I no longer know what would justify treating either of those reactions as anything more than a description of how we each happen to have turned out. Is there a flaw in this line of thinking that I'm not seeing, or does it point to something real and troubling about when, if ever, a person can be said to deserve blame or credit for how they act?
