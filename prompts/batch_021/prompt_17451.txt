I cover AI and the nerdier end of theoretical computer science for a living, and I've been chasing a piece about how casually machine learning people toss around "Occam's razor" as though Solomonoff and Kolmogorov basically settled it decades ago. That's how I ended up at the bar after an algorithmic-information-theory workshop last month, half-listening to two people go at it: Ana, who works on compression and information theory, and Ben, a philosophy-of-science postdoc who'd given the afternoon's talk on confirmation and simplicity. I got most of it down on my phone afterward but I honestly can't referee it myself, so I'm hoping you can.

Ana's opening move was that Kolmogorov complexity finally makes "prefer the simpler hypothesis" rigorous instead of just aesthetic, because of the invariance theorem: swap universal machines and your complexity numbers only shift by some fixed constant, so which language you pick stops mattering once strings get long enough. Ben said the word doing all the work there is "asymptotically" — the constant is fixed, sure, but it can be enormous, way bigger than any two real hypotheses a scientist would actually be choosing between, so your pet universal machine can still make either one look simpler for every string anybody will ever care about. Ana's comeback was that this isn't a special problem for her side: AIC, BIC, any model-selection rule you like has some equally unprincipled choice baked into it — a parametrization, a prior, a basis — and at least Kolmogorov complexity comes with Solomonoff's convergence result attached, which ordinary statistics doesn't hand you for free. Ben's reply was that the convergence theorem is a fact about an uncomputable idealization — nobody can compute K(x) or actually run Solomonoff induction on anything — so it's existence doesn't tell you whether the shortcuts real scientists use are truth-tracking, any more than a theorem about a frictionless plane tells you your actual ramp is well built. Ana said fine, but plenty of good science leans on ideals nobody's ever run — nobody's built a real Carnot engine either — and MDL and its relatives inherit their justification from the ideal the same way real engines inherit something from thermodynamics. Ben's last move was that the analogy fails: you can always gerrymander a description language so that any predicate at all comes out short, Goodman's grue problem all over again, and there's no independent fact about which universal machine is the "right" one, the way there's an independent fact about how heat behaves — so the whole apparatus just re-poses the old problem of justifying a choice of predicates in scarier notation, instead of answering it.

Neither of them budged, and honestly both seemed to know their stuff. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each step, rather than just handing down an overall winner? And if there's some consideration that would've actually settled part of this and neither of them thought to raise it, I'd like to know what I missed.
