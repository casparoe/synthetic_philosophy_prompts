I've been falling down a rabbit hole for a feature I'm writing on free will and the neuroscience-meets-sentencing debate (you know, the arguments about whether brain scans should count as mitigating evidence in court), and I keep getting stuck on this Galen Strawson paper from the 90s, I think its called "The Impossibility of Moral Responsibility" or something close to that. His "Basic Argument" isn't the usual determinism-kills-free-will story — the claim is that you can't be truly, ultimately responsible for anything you do even if the universe turns out to be partly random. The logic goes: you act the way you do because of the way you are (your character, values, motives, whatever); to be responsible for the act you'd have to be responsible for being that way; but trace that back and you'd have to have made yourself that way through some earlier deliberate act, which itself came out of a still-earlier version of you that you also didn't choose, and so on backwards, with no point where you actually get to author yourself from nothing. Random, uncaused blips in your personality don't rescue you either, since that's just luck, not "you." It's a regress argument more than a determinism argument, which is apparently why some philosophers treat it as harder to dodge than the classic free-will-vs-determinism fight, and honestly it's definately been messing with my head a bit.

Here's the detail that hooked me for the piece: turns out Strawson's own dad, P.F. Strawson, is one of the philosophers most famous for defending ordinary moral responsibility (his "Freedom and Resentment" essay, the idea that praise, blame, resentment and gratitude don't need deep metaphysical backing to be legitimate, they're just built into being human). So there's this odd family irony sitting at the center of the debate. Every compatibilist response I dig up online feels like it dodges the actual regress rather than meeting it head-on — "we still need responsibility-talk to function as a society" isn't really an answer to the regress, it's a shrug. Could you write me a dialogue, maybe 10-14 exchanges, between someone defending Strawson's Basic Argument and a genuinely sharp compatibilist, where the compatibilist actually engages with the self-authorship regress step by step instead of changing the subject to practical necessity? It doesn't need to resolve neatly — I'd honestly rather it end with both sides pinpointing exactly where they stop agreeing, since that seems more interesting than a fake knockout punch either way.
