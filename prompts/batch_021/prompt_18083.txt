I'm a PhD student working on kalām theories of action, and I keep running into a tension in al-Ghazālī's occasionalism that the secondary literature tends to pass over quickly. His argument from the fire-and-cotton case is meant to show that created things have no genuine efficient causal power over their effects; he extends the same reasoning to human beings, so that even the link between my willing to raise my hand and my hand's rising is just another God-instituted habit, not something I actually bring about. The Ash'arite doctrine of kasb ("acquisition") is supposed to preserve my responsibility for the act anyway, by saying that I "acquire" what God creates in me rather than cause it. What I can't work out is what is supposed to distinguish an "acquired" act from a merely occasioned bodily event - a shiver, a sneeze, the tremor of a palsied hand - given that neither is efficiently caused by the agent in whom it occurs.

Construct two structurally parallel scenarios - one an obvious case of kasb that grounds praise or blame, the other an obvious non-action of that reflexive sort - chosen so that, using only resources internal to Ghazālī's own occasionalism, it is genuinely hard to say why the first counts as an acquired act and the second does not. Then tell me whether kasb has the resources to draw a principled line between your two cases, or whether the example shows "acquisition" collapsing into a distinction without a difference.
