Here's the question they were given:

"Reconstruct the reasoning behind Bostrom's simulation trilemma, including the role of the substrate-independence assumption. Then explain why the argument itself doesn't tell us which of the three disjuncts is true, and briefly assess whether this is a weakness."

And here's the student's answer:

"Bostrom's argument rests on a premise he calls substrate independence: the idea that what matters for conscious experience is the right kind of computational organization, not the specific physical material running it, so a simulated brain running on a powerful enough computer could in principle have exactly the same experiences as a biological one. Given that, he asks us to imagine a 'posthuman' civilization with enough computing power to run huge numbers of 'ancestor simulations' — detailed, populated recreations of the kind of world their ancestors lived in, indistinguishable from the inside. Since even one civilization running a modest number of these would create far more simulated people than there ever were real ones, Bostrom concludes that at least one of three things has to be true: almost no civilizations survive to reach that posthuman stage, or almost none of the ones that do ever choose to run ancestor simulations, or almost everyone with experiences like ours is in one right now.

The argument itself can't tell us which disjunct holds, because it's a conditional structure — it shows the three possibilities exhaust the space given the setup, but it has no way of feeding in the actual, contingent facts about how many civilizations self-destruct or what posthumans would want to do with their computing power. So the trilemma is genuinely silent on the question that matters most. I don't think that's a weakness, though: since we have no special reason to think option one or option two is true, the argument's real force is that it pushes us toward taking the third option seriously and concluding we are probably being simulated right now. Bostrom makes a similar point himself when he notes that this isn't really a skeptical scenario like Descartes' evil demon, since even a simulated world is a real world with real physical laws running underneath it, just implemented differently — so almost everything we ordinarily believe about our surroundings would still be true even if we turned out to be in one."
