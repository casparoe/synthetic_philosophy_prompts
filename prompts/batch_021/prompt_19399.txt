I am outlining a novel that pairs a decommissioned symbolic-AI system from the 1980s with a present-day language model. While researching the premise, I ended up refereeing an argument between two consultants I had brought in -- a philosopher of mind and a machine learning engineer -- over whether large language models have actually solved the frame problem or merely made it invisible.

The engineer opened by arguing that the question is moot for this kind of architecture: an LLM never reasons via explicit axioms about what changes and what persists after an action, so the classical bookkeeping problem McCarthy and Hayes posed in 1969 -- how to avoid stating every "non-effect" of every action -- simply doesn't arise. Relevance falls out implicitly from attention over context, the way a cyclist stays upright without solving differential equations.

The philosopher agreed that this narrow, logical version of the problem was essentially dealt with decades ago, by formal patches like circumscription and successor-state axioms, and isn't what's interesting anymore. But she said the deeper worry -- the one Dennett and Fodor actually cared about -- was how any finite system bounds the space of things worth even considering, out of an unbounded world of facts. Doing this statistically can make the problem invisible without solving it, she said, and that is exactly what's on display when a model gives a fluent, confident answer that quietly ignores something any competent adult would flag: it hasn't ruled out the irrelevant possibilities, it never represented them as candidates worth ruling out in the first place. Call it Dennett's paralyzed robot with better PR.

The engineer replied that humans have no principled solution to that problem either -- we fixate on salient irrelevancies, miss the obvious, and confabulate reasons afterward -- so if an unprincipled, statistical approach to relevance disqualifies a system from having solved the frame problem, it disqualifies us too, and yet no one says human cognition merely hides the problem.

She answered that the disanalogy is grounding: a person can act, observe the result, and revise, so human relevance-judgments answer to the world and not only to a training distribution, which is why humans typically degrade more gracefully in genuinely unfamiliar situations.

He said that story proves too much: embodied, sensor-equipped robots running reinforcement learning in real environments don't obviously handle novel situations better, and plenty of human relevance failures -- superstition, motivated reasoning, prejudice -- persist despite full embodiment. Until she can specify what grounding actually buys beyond "whatever humans have," he said, it's a label for the gap, not an explanation of it.

Neither of them budged, and I have kept turning the exchange over since. Could you go through it move by move and say who has the better of the argument at each step -- not just who wins overall -- and flag anything important that both of them are overlooking? End with a one-sentence verdict on the underlying question: have large language models solved the frame problem, dissolved it, or just hidden it?
