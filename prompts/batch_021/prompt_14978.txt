For the past year or so I've been following the value-learning discussions on the Alignment Forum and LessWrong, and something has started to bother me about how inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) is usually motivated. The standard pitch — infer an agent's reward function purely from observing what it does, without ever asking it what it wants or positing unobservable mental states — sounds to me like a technically souped-up version of something economists have been doing since Samuelson: revealed preference theory, which tries to derive a consumer's preference ordering entirely from their choices under a budget constraint. I mentioned this over dinner to a friend with an economics background, and she pointed out that her field has spent decades worrying about exactly the failure mode I would naively have expected IRL to run into: the weak, strong, and generalized axioms of revealed preference (WARP/SARP/GARP) have to be imposed on choice data before it can be said to "reveal" any preference at all, and even then, Sen and others have argued that this move quietly collapses "preference" into "choice," severing any principled link to well-being or considered judgment. On the machine-learning side, I recall a similar-spirited impossibility result — I believe due to Armstrong and Mindermann — showing that no simplicity prior over reward functions can, by itself, distinguish a rational agent pursuing reward R from an anti-rational agent pursuing its negation, since behavior alone is compatible with either.

Given this, I'd like you to compare inverse reinforcement learning with the more general project of revealed-preference reasoning, of which it seems to be one instance. Concretely: where does IRL's added apparatus — explicit probabilistic models of noise and sub-optimality, priors over reward functions, sequential rather than one-shot choice — actually buy epistemic progress on the problem of inferring an agent's "true" preferences from its behavior, and where is it simply the same old identification problem dressed up in new mathematical clothing? Please don't just lay the two frameworks out side by side: tell me whether, in your considered view, IRL escapes the objections that have dogged revealed preference theory since Sen, or whether it remains, at bottom, in exactly the same boat — and defend whichever verdict you reach.
