Last month I was promoted from analyst to team lead, and this week I must formally reprimand a colleague for inflating his billable hours — conduct I myself engaged in routinely for two years before the promotion, was never caught doing, and have never disclosed to anyone. In reading around the philosophical literature on hypocrisy and the standing to blame since then, I have noticed that most discussions treat public confession, restitution, or punishment as necessary for a reformed wrongdoer to regain standing to condemn the same conduct in others; private reform alone is not treated as sufficient. I would like you to argue for the opposite position: that a sincere and sustained private change of heart is, by itself, enough to restore full standing to blame, even absent any disclosure of the earlier wrongdoing. Please engage seriously with the strongest objection to this view — that condemning in others an offense one has concealed in oneself is not genuine reform but a continuation of the original deception, and so cannot ground standing — rather than dismissing it too quickly. To be clear, I am not asking whether the reprimand will strike my colleague as credible; I want to know whether I would, as a matter of fact, possess the standing to issue it.
