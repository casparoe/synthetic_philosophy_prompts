Now retired after a career in condensed-matter physics, I absorbed P.W. Anderson's essay "More Is Different" early on as something like the field's founding manifesto: each level of organizational complexity throws up genuinely new regularities that cannot in practice be derived from the level below, even granting that everything is ultimately built out of the same fundamental constituents. Since retiring I have been reading in analytic metaphysics and have come across the grounding and fundamentality literature associated with Schaffer, Fine, and Bennett, including the further dispute over whether grounding chains must terminate in something fundamental at all. Schaffer himself argues they must, though his priority monism locates that terminus at the top, in the cosmos as a whole, rather than at the bottom, in particles; ranged against this are those who take infinite descent, with no fundamental level whatsoever, to be a coherent and perhaps even preferable possibility. Please describe the most important implications of this literature for the Andersonian picture, and in particular for whether a physicist ought to be troubled by the fact that fundamental physics has never actually located, and may never locate, a genuinely fundamental level.
