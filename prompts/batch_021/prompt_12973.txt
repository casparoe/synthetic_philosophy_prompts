I'm putting together a handout for my formal epistemology seminar and want to run something by you first. I.J. Good has this old theorem showing that a Bayesian who's about to make a decision should never turn down a free peek at more evidence - expected utility with the extra info is always at least as high as without it, so long as she updates on it and then acts optimally. Seems pretty airtight. But Hirshleifer's classic result on information and insurance markets shows almost the opposite: if a public signal about who's going to get sick, or whose house will burn down, arrives before people get the chance to write there insurance contracts, everybody can end up worse off, because the risk-pooling that would've benefited both sides is no longer possible once the uncertainty is gone for one side. So which is it, is more information always weakly good for a rational agent, or can it actually make people worse off? Don't just say 'it depends' - pin down exactly which assumption in Good's proof Hirshleifer's scenario violates, and explain why that assumption was doing all the work.
