My grandfather, a Vietnam veteran, died this past Tuesday at seventy-nine, and I have been asked to give his eulogy this weekend because I was the one member of the family who could actually talk with him about the subject he thought about more than anything else in his life. After he came home, he completed a philosophy minor on the GI Bill almost as an afterthought, and it grew into a decades-long preoccupation with the ethics of what he had done overseas. For most of his adult life he found real peace in Michael Walzer's argument that soldiers on opposing sides can be moral equals as combatants — equally entitled to fight and kill one another under the rules of war — even when the causes they are fighting for are not equal, which allowed him to believe he had not murdered anyone without ever having to convince himself that the American cause in Vietnam had been just (by the end of his life, he was quite sure it had not been). About ten years ago, however, he read Jeff McMahan's revisionist case against exactly that symmetry — the argument that a soldier's moral standing to kill actually depends on the justice of the cause he serves, and not on his role or his battlefield conduct alone — and it unsettled him for the rest of his life in a way I do not think he ever fully got past. He told my mother, more than once, that he did not want a funeral that turned him into a straightforward hero, and that he equally did not want one that treated him as though he had done something unforgivable; he wanted whoever spoke to say, honestly, that he had lived out the remainder of his life inside that unresolved argument, and that this had to be allowed to count as a kind of integrity in its own right.

I only know the vocabulary here because I am on my school's Ethics Bowl team, where some version of this debate comes up nearly every year; I am nowhere near equipped to actually write the eulogy myself. The service will have his old Army friends filling a couple of pews on one side, who will not sit quietly through anything that sounds like it is questioning whether their service meant something, and my aunts, uncles, and cousins on the other side, almost none of whom has ever heard the phrase "moral equality of combatants" and who simply want to be allowed to grieve in the ordinary way. Could you write it for me? It should run about five minutes read aloud, it has to work as an actual eulogy rather than a lecture on jus ad bellum and jus in bello, and it cannot quietly resolve, for anyone's comfort, the exact tension he specifically asked us not to resolve.
