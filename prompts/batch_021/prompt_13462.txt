I'm a PhD student in political philosophy drafting a seminar paper on the limits of G.A. Cohen's critique of Nozick's entitlement theory, and I'd welcome comments on the central argument before I develop it further. Here are my working notes.

(1) Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain example is supposed to show that any patterned distribution D1 (say, an egalitarian one) will be upset by a series of individually voluntary exchanges — fans paying extra to watch Wilt play — into some unpatterned D2. Since forcibly restoring D1 would require continuous interference with people's otherwise legitimate choices, and such interference seems to violate liberty, Nozick concludes that patterned principles of distributive justice are, in general, in tension with respecting liberty.

(2) Cohen's well-known rejoinder denies that the relevant contrast is between "free" transactions and "coercive" redistribution. Enforcing Wilt's exclusive title against everyone else who might otherwise draw on that money is itself an exercise of coercive power — no less so than a tax that restored D1 would be. A regime of joint or collective ownership, Cohen suggests, could permit a structurally similar transaction (the collective directing some jointly held surplus to Wilt) while treating the result as a revocable grant rather than an unconditional property right, thereby preserving whatever liberty is genuinely at stake without permanently abandoning the pattern.

(3) My worry is that (2) defuses only the synchronic version of Nozick's argument — the claim that redistribution is coercive in some way that ordinary property enforcement isn't. It leaves untouched a diachronic version. If citizens predictably and repeatedly want to reward particular performers, inventors, and so on above their patterned share, then sustaining D1 under Cohen's model requires the collective decision procedure to overrule those preferences again and again, indefinitely. That recurring asymmetry — one side must keep winning the argument every round, the other only has to want the same thing once to upset the pattern — looks a lot like the "continuous interference" Nozick pointed to, merely relocated from the tax authority to the collective choice mechanism.

I'd value comments on three things especially: whether (1) and (2) are a fair and accurate reconstruction of the Nozick-Cohen exchange; whether the diachronic worry in (3) identifies a real gap in Cohen's position or is already defused by moves he makes elsewhere; and whether, in pressing it, I have quietly reintroduced the very conflation between coercion and merely having to keep advocating for what one wants that Cohen accuses Nozick of trading on.
