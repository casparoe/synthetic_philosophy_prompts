I've spent the last couple weeks reporting on how outfits like GiveWell and Open Philanthropy actually decide which charities are "most effective" per dollar, and I definately fell down a rabbit hole into an argument among economists that apparently nobodys ever actually settled. The practical version: is it even meaningful to say that curing ten people's blindness does more good than saving one life, or that a dollar handed to a subsistence farmer does more good than the same dollar handed to a burnt-out tech worker? One whole tradition in economics (goes back to Robbins in the 30s, if I'm remembering right) says no — you have no access to anybody's inner experience but your own, so "comparing" how much two different people gain is a value judgement wearing a lab coat, not a measurement. The other tradition (more the Harsanyi/utilitarian line) says that without some willingness to make interpersonal comparisons you can't actually do welfare economics or policy at all — you just end up wherever Arrow's impossibility theorem dumps you, or you quietly let the status quo win by default, which is its own enormous value judgement dressed up as neutrality.

Can you write me a dialogue between two economists holding these two positions? Set it somewhere concrete, like the two of them stuck on a foundation's grant committee, trying to agree on how to weigh "lives saved" against "cases of blindness cured" against "incomes doubled" before a funding decision goes out the door. I don't want the skeptic to be a pushover who just throws up their hands (real committees don't get to do that, they still have to decide something), and I don't want the defender to just wave away the "how would you even verify that" problem — they should each have to answer the other's actual best objection, get into specifics like weightings and edge cases and maybe cardinal vs ordinal utility, rather than each just restating their opening position a bit louder.
