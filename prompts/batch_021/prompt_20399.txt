I'm about two-thirds through a novel set in Cambridge, Massachusetts in the 1870s, built around two young philosophers who are close friends but cant stop needling each other about what "truth" even means -- they're loosely (very loosely, don't hold me to the history) modeled on Peirce and James back when they were both still turning up to that little Metaphysical Club. My writing group keeps telling me the philosophy talk slows the plot down, but I'd rather get it right than cut it, so I've actually been reading the primary texts instead of just working off summaries, and I got stuck on a passage I want my Peirce-ish character to basically recite in one scene.

Here's the bit, from "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" (typed up from my own copy, so apologies if I fat-fingered a comma somewhere): "...different minds may set out with the most antagonistic views, but the progress of investigation carries them by a force outside of themselves to one and the same conclusion. This activity of thought by which we are carried, not where we wish, but to a foreordained goal, is like the operation of destiny. No modification of the point of view taken, no selection of other facts for study, no natural bent of mind even, can enable a man to escape the predestinate opinion... The opinion which is fated to be ultimately agreed to by all who investigate, is what we mean by the truth, and the object represented in this opinion is the real... reality is independent, not necessarily of thought in general, but only of what you or I or any finite number of men may think about it; and though the object of the final opinion depends on what that opinion is, yet what that opinion is does not depend on what you or I or any man thinks."

I've read this about six times now and I honestly can't tell if Peirce is just being colorful with words like "destiny," "fated," and "predestinate" -- a fancy way of saying inquiry converges no matter where it starts -- or if he's smuggling in some heavier claim about truth being metaphysically fixed in advance, which seems like a weird thing for a guy so committed to fallibilism to say. The last part also reads like a flat contradiction on first pass: reality is independent of thought, except it isn't independent of thought, just of any one thinker's thought. Can you actually untangle that for me instead of just waving it off as "he means the ideal long run"? And since it matters for how hard my two characters should clash on the page -- how far does this convergence picture of truth really get you from something like James's later "cash-value" idea, where truth is basically about what pays off for a believer here and now? I don't want their big argument to boil down to a misunderstanding, I want it to be a real, substantive split.
