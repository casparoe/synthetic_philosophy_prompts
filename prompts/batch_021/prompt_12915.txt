Neither of them budges easily, so it went several rounds, roughly reconstructed here:

(1) Nadia: depression is a malfunction, full stop. The mood system's job is to respond to loss and setbacks and then let go, and in a real depressive episode it just stops doing that job. That's the whole reason we call it a disorder instead of a personality or a rough patch — something is failing to do what it's supposed to do.

(2) Marcus: "supposed to do" is where you're smuggling in the entire disagreement. The standard philosophical line is that a trait's function is whatever it was selected for — its etiological function. But there are real evolutionary hypotheses, the social risk hypothesis, the analytical rumination hypothesis, on which low mood, withdrawal, even rumination were themselves selected for: to conserve energy after a defeat, signal submission and head off further attacks, or force slow, careful analysis of a failing life plan. If either is even roughly right, a depressive episode might be the system doing exactly the job it was built for. It's not obviously malfunctioning by the very standard she just invoked.

(3) Nadia: fine, maybe some version of that story covers a bad week after getting fired. It doesn't cover six months of it with no real trigger and someone wanting to die. Selection didn't build a system whose job is to make you kill yourself. So even if the mechanism sometimes fires appropriately, here either the trigger or the dial is broken — it's firing on the wrong inputs, or blowing way past any setting that ever paid off. Thats still a malfunction.

(4) Marcus: notice what you just did, though — you stopped asking what the system was selected for and started asking whether this response is well-calibrated to this input right now. That's a causal-role notion of function, not an etiological one, and it's a perfectly respectable theory of function! But you don't get to borrow the authority of "this is literally what natural selection designed it to do" and then quietly swap to "this is a badly-calibrated reaction given current circumstances" the second the evolutionary story stops cooperating.

(5) Nadia: then I'll bite that bullet. I don't need "malfunction" to be an evolutionary fact. Clinically all I need is that the state causes serious harm and wrecks someone's ability to function. I was never doing amateur evolutionary biology at 2am with a suicidal patient.

(6) Marcus: that's exactly the move Wakefield's account exists to block, though — "harmful dysfunction" has two parts for a reason. Pure harm can't tell a disorder from things that are harmful but not disorders: a parent's grief after losing a kid wrecks their functioning for a long time and isn't their attachment system doing anything pleasant, but we don't call that a disorder. Drop the historical half and you can't explain why not, which is exactly the overreach people accused the DSM of when it dropped the bereavement exclusion.

(7) Nadia: grief tracks its object and fades as the loss gets absorbed — that's checkable. A mood that stays low long after it's stopped tracking anything, or that generalizes to everything, is doing something different in kind, not just running too long. I don't need a story about the Pleistocene to say that.
