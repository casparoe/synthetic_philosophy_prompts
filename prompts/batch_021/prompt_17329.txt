I'm a novelist working on a book set in Edinburgh in the 1760s, built around a fictional former student of David Hume who spends much of the novel trying, and mostly failing, to out-argue his old teacher in front of their mutual friends. In researching the period I've been re-reading Hume's Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, hoping to borrow some of his actual argumentative moves for the dialogue, and I got stuck on a wrinkle in his treatment of causation that I would like to understand properly before I put it in anyone's mouth. Here is the passage, from Part II of Section VII, where Hume gives his well-known definition of "cause":

"[W]e may define a cause to be an object followed by another, and where all the objects similar to the first are followed by objects similar to the second. Or, in other words, where, if the first object had not been, the second never had existed."

The phrase "or, in other words" is what gives me trouble. Hume presents the two clauses as different ways of saying one thing, but they do not strike me as equivalent. The first clause just describes a pattern that happens to hold among actual events: things of the first kind are invariably trailed by things of the second kind, the way one collision on a billiard table is always trailed by another ball's motion. The second clause is a counterfactual, concerning a scenario that never actually occurred: if that first ball had sat still, the second would never have moved. One claim seems to be about how the world in fact runs; the other is about how some non-actual version of it would have run. I would like my character to be able to press Hume on exactly this seam—to ask him what entitles him to treat a claim about what regularly happens as interchangeable with a claim about what would have happened under different circumstances—but only if that is a real seam and not a criticism I have invented for dramatic convenience. Is there a genuine gap between these two clauses, and if so, could you help me understand what is actually at stake in it, ideally with some sense of how later philosophers who prised Hume's two clauses apart have made use of the distinction?
