I sit on my university's honorary degrees committee, which votes next month on a nomination for a former president. He is credited with ending a long, grinding war three decades ago by ordering a single overnight strike that destroyed the enemy capital's power grid and central food depots, killing an estimated 60,000 civilians. The nomination file describes the target as "war-sustaining infrastructure." Internal planning documents shared with the committee, however, indicate it was chosen because destroying it — and the civilians who depended on it — was expected to break the enemy government's will to keep fighting, which it did within days, forestalling an invasion that planners on both sides had projected would cost far more lives. A majority on the committee wants to honor him for shortening the war and for his statesmanship since; a minority calls the strike murder regardless of the tally of lives saved, and isn't moved by the fact that he signed an authorization rather than piloting anything himself. Drawing on G.E.M. Anscombe's arguments in "Mr. Truman's Degree" — particularly on intention, the doctrine of double effect, and why she thought a good outcome cannot redeem the deliberate killing of innocents — please draft the short memo I'll circulate to the committee before the vote. It should recommend a specific outcome, and it should take the strongest argument against that recommendation seriously rather than dismiss it.
