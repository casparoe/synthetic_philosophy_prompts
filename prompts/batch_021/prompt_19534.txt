Last month I accepted a certain, guaranteed settlement in a contract dispute rather than proceed to arbitration, even though my own lawyer's expected-value calculation made the arbitration route worth noticeably more on average. When I described the decision to a friend with an economics background, she called it a textbook case of irrational risk-aversion — unless, she said, I could point to some diminishing marginal utility of money to justify it, which given the sums involved I honestly could not. Reading around the topic since, I've found that her confidence was not obviously warranted: Lara Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility theory, developed at length in *Risk and Rationality*, treats risk-aversion as a rational attitude in its own right, independent of the shape of one's utility function over outcomes, at the price of rejecting the independence axiom underwriting both von Neumann-Morgenstern and Savage-style expected utility theory. But I've also come across the standard rejoinder: giving up independence this way seems to expose an agent to being money-pumped, or otherwise turned into a sure loser, across a sequence of suitably related choices, unless something like a resolute (rather than sophisticated) choice policy is adopted to block the exploitation — and critics don't think that policy's costs are worth paying. Write an essay that weighs these considerations and reaches a definite verdict: can risk-aversion like mine be genuinely rational over and above whatever diminishing marginal utility can license, or does its vulnerability to exploitation in sequential choice show that theories like Buchak's dress up a mistake in respectable formalism? Take a side and defend it, engaging directly with the dynamic-choice objection rather than setting it aside.
