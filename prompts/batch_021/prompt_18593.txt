I'm a backend/ML engineer, and lately I've been working on multimodal models — the kind of thing where you pretrain on one sensory modality and then poke at whether the learned representations transfer zero-shot to a different one. A few weeks back I was reading a paper on cross-modal transfer that dropped a footnote comparing the setup to something called "Molyneux's problem," credited to a 17th century letter to John Locke. I went down a rabbit hole and dug up the actual passage in Locke's *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (Book II, Chapter IX, the section on perception — its numbered §8 in the copy I found online). The old-timey prose is rough going but here's the bit with the actual problem, which i copied out as faithfully as I could:

"I shall here insert a problem of that very ingenious and studious promoter of real knowledge, the learned and worthy Mr. Molineux, which he was pleased to send me in a letter some months since; and it is this: 'Suppose a man born blind, and now adult, and taught by his touch to distinguish between a cube and a sphere of the same metal, and nighly of the same bigness, so as to tell, when he felt one and the other, which is the cube, which the sphere. Suppose then the cube and sphere placed on a table, and the blind man be made to see: quaere, whether by his sight, before he touched them, he could now distinguish and tell which is the globe, which the cube?' To which the acute and judicious proposer answers: 'Not. For, though he has obtained the experience of how a globe, how a cube affects his touch, yet he has not yet attained the experience, that what affects his touch so or so, must affect his sight so or so; or that a protuberant angle in the cube, that pressed his hand unequally, shall appear to his eye as it does in the cube.' I agree with this thinking gentleman, whom I am proud to call my friend, in his answer to this problem; and am of opinion that the blind man, at first sight, would not be able with certainty to say which was the globe, which the cube, whilst he only saw them, though he could unerringly name them by his touch."

Heres what's bugging me. Just before this bit, in that same section, Locke's already made a whole separate argument that even an experienced sighted adult doesn't directly "see" a globe as round — he says what actually hits the eye/mind is basically a flat circle with shading on it, and it's only years of habit that lets our "judgment" silently convert that into the perception of a solid convex thing, so smoothly that we never notice the conversion happening. But then when he gets to Molyneux's letter, both he and Molyneux seem to be assuming the newly-sighted guy would get some determinate, distinct visual "look" for the cube and a different one for the sphere the instant he opens his eyes, and the only thing missing is knowing which look goes with which felt shape. Those two bits feel like they're in tension to me: if seeing shape at all is already this judgment-built-up-through-habit thing, per the globe passage, how can Locke and Molyneux so confidently assume the newly-sighted man has any stable shape-look to correlate with touch in the first place, rather than just an undifferentiated blur? Am I misreading one of these two bits, or is this an actual soft spot in how Locke sets up the problem?
