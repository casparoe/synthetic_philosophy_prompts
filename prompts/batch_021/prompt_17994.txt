Here's the question as it was given: "The fine-tuning argument infers a cosmic designer from the fact that the physical constants of our universe fall within the narrow range needed for life. Ian Hacking has objected that treating this fine-tuning as evidence commits an 'inverse gambler's fallacy.' (a) Reconstruct the fine-tuning argument and Hacking's objection, including the gambler's-fallacy analogy it trades on. (b) Explain how John Leslie's 'firing squad' thought experiment is meant to answer Hacking. (c) In a paragraph, say whether you think Leslie succeeds."

And here's the answer that got circulated (retyped exactly, so any clunky bits belong to the student, not me):

"(a) The fine-tuning argument points out that the fundamental constants of physics - the strength of gravity, the cosmological constant, the mass difference between a proton and a neutron, and so on - all have to sit inside an extremely narrow range for stars, chemistry and life to be possible at all. Defenders of the argument say this is much more probable if the universe was designed with life in mind than if the values were simply due to chance, so by ordinary confirmation-theoretic reasoning (a higher likelihood under one hypothesis than another counts as evidence for it) the fine-tuning favours design over chance.

Hacking's inverse gambler's fallacy objection says this reasoning doesn't actually work, because it has the same structure as a known probabilistic mistake. The ordinary gambler's fallacy is thinking a string of losses makes a win 'due.' The inverse version is: you walk into a casino, see a stranger roll double sixes on their very first roll, and conclude they must have been playing for hours already - which is a mistake, since the odds of any one roll coming up double six don't depend on how many other rolls came before it. Hacking's charge is that inferring design (or, for that matter, a multiverse) from the fine-tuned values we observe commits exactly this error: the fine-tuning of our universe doesn't tell us anything about whether some further hypothesis is true, for the same reason the double six doesn't tell the onlooker anything about the number of previous rolls.

(b) Leslie tries to answer this with the firing squad case: fifty expert marksmen fire at you at point-blank range, and, remarkably, you survive. Leslie's diagnosis is that since you'd only ever be around afterward to be puzzled about it if you happened to survive, your survival is guaranteed from your own perspective and isn't actually evidence of anything - you can't treat it as a mystery calling for an explanation like bribery, because the alternative was never something you could have observed in the first place. Carried back to cosmology: once you factor in that we could only ever find ourselves inhabiting a life-permitting universe, our observing fine-tuning stops being the kind of surprising coincidence the design argument needs it to be - it's just a precondition of our being around to ask the question.

(c) I think Leslie is right, and that this is actually a more fundamental reply to the design argument than the usual appeal to a multiverse: even without assuming other universes exist, the mere fact that any observer is guaranteed to find themselves in observer-permitting conditions already drains the fine-tuning of its evidential force, so there's nothing left over for a designer to explain. Hacking's fallacy and Leslie's selection effect are really making the same underlying point - that inferring anything at all from a fact we were guaranteed to observe anyway is illegitimate - so between the two of them the fine-tuning argument never gets off the ground."
