Here's the "student's" answer:

According to the traditional trichotomy thesis, whenever two things are evaluatively compared, exactly one of three relations must hold between them: one is better, the other is better, or they're equally good. Ruth Chang thinks this leaves something out. In a genuine hard choice -- say, deciding between becoming a corporate lawyer or a wildlife biologist -- neither career is better than the other, but they also aren't equally good, because if you slightly sweetened the lawyer job (a bit more salary, say), that still wouldn't be enough to make it better than the biologist job. Since two options that are truly equally good should get tipped by even a small improvement to one of them, this shows lawyer and biologist are related by a fourth relation, which Chang calls being "on a par." And because the two careers are incommensurable -- there's no common currency, like money, that lets us rank them on a single scale -- there's ultimately no fact about which is better, and it's this underlying incomparability that makes the choice so hard and, in a sense, up to the chooser. Joseph Raz makes essentially the same argument in The Morality of Freedom: when values are incommensurable, reason runs out and it takes an act of will to decide, since there's no further fact to be discovered. Chang's notion of parity basically just puts a label on the gap in reasons Raz had already described.
