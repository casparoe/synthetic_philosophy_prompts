I'm writing a piece on how nineteenth-century constitutional arguments get borrowed, often selectively, by today's legal combatants, and Frederick Douglass is my central case study. Douglass began his public career agreeing with William Lloyd Garrison that the Constitution was, in Garrison's words, a "covenant with death, and an agreement with hell," so irredeemably proslavery that no true abolitionist could vote or hold office under it. By the early 1850s, however, he had broken publicly with Garrison, persuaded by Gerrit Smith and Lysander Spooner that the document's actual text — its refusal to use the words "slave" or "slavery," its use of the neutral term "person," its Preamble's promise of liberty to "We the People" — made it, read on its own terms, an antislavery charter, whatever some framers had privately intended or the nation had subsequently practiced. He turned this same textual argument on Chief Justice Taney a few years later, rejecting Taney's claim in the Dred Scott decision that Black Americans' exclusion from citizenship followed from the framers' original understanding.

For the piece, I need your best reconstruction of how that specific interpretive method, rather than Douglass's general reputation as an abolitionist, would bear on a dispute he never lived to see: the argument, made by some officials and legal advocates today, that children born on American soil to undocumented or temporarily present parents are not "subject to the jurisdiction" of the United States within the meaning of the Fourteenth Amendment, and so are not automatically citizens. Would Douglass's own anti-Garrisonian, anti-Taney textualism push him toward that restrictive reading or against it? And can the specific method he forged to expand who counted as a full "person" under the Constitution be invoked with a straight face on either side of today's fight over who counts as a citizen?
