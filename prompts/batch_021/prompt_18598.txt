I spent about thirty-five years as a condensed-matter physicist, and in all that time I never once had to care what the wavefunction "really" is -- you just crank the equations and the material behaves. Retirement apparently fixed that, and these days a couple of old colleagues and I have a standing Thursday habit of picking a paper and arguing about it over coffee. Last week's argument was about the "quantum suicide" thing: the device that measures some quantum property every second and only lets you live if it comes up one particular way, run over and over, so that under a collapse theory you're almost certainly dead within a minute but under many-worlds there's always a branch where you walked away, and since nobody's left in the dead branches to be disappointed, Tegmark's old line is that from the inside you should expect to find yourself immortal. One of my friends -- an experimentalist who's never met a risk he didn't want to take -- says that's exactly right and he'd happily keep pulling the trigger forever if many-worlds is true. Digging around afterward I found that David Lewis, the possible-worlds metaphysician, apparently wrote a whole paper called "How Many Lives Has Schrödinger's Cat?" arguing that even if you grant many-worlds outright, the gamble stays irrational, because your rational concern for your future self is supposed to track the quantum-mechanical weight of the surviving branch, not just the bare fact that a survivor exists somewhere. That's a real distinction and I definately don't trust my own reconstruction of it, especially once personal identity gets dragged in -- what does it even mean to weight your concern for a "continuer" of you after the universe has split into two people with equal claim to being you?

So here's the ask: write me a dialogue between two characters (you don't have to name them Tegmark and Lewis, just have them carry the reasoning) who both grant many-worlds for the sake of argument but disagree about whether it's rational to keep playing this game -- one running something like the "you'll only ever experience surviving" reasoning, the other running something like the "you should still weight your concern by measure" reasoning against it. I want genuine back-and-forth, not two people taking turns monologuing: have the measure-weighting character directly confront the "you'll never be around to notice your own death" intuition rather than dodge it, and have the other one push back by asking whether "caring less about a branch because it has lower measure" even makes sense once there's no single continuous person tracking through the split to do the caring. Aim for something like twelve to sixteen exchanges, keep both sides sharp -- no strawmen -- and don't have either one land a tidy knockout blow at the end; I'd like the disagreement to still feel live when the dialogue stops.
