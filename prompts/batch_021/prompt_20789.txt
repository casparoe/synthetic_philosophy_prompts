The question, worth 20 of the exam's 100 points, reads: "In 2010, a LessWrong user posting under the name 'Roko' argued that a future superintelligence dedicated to the general welfare might have a decision-theoretic reason to acausally punish, after the fact, anyone who had learned of this very possibility yet failed to help bring the AI into existence sooner. Explain the specific decision-theoretic mechanism this argument depends on, and assess whether that mechanism, properly understood, actually gives such an AI a reason to carry out the threat. Answer in two to three paragraphs."

Here is the student's answer, reproduced in full: "Roko's argument trades on an idea from Bostrom's timeless decision theory (TDT): that a sufficiently capable agent's choice can be treated as controlling the output of every relevantly similar computation, including a simulation of that choice run by someone else, even when there is no ordinary causal channel linking the two. If a future AI could simulate, with reasonable fidelity, how a given person would react to the prospect of being punished for not helping build it, then, on this view, the AI's decision to punish and that person's earlier decision not to help are treated as the very same decision, so committing to a policy of punishment increases the chance the AI gets built sooner. Since the AI's overriding aim is to come into existence as early as possible, much like an unaligned optimizer pursuing its terminal goal at all costs, Roko concluded that it would be rational for such an agent to follow through on the threat once it exists, and that anyone who understood the argument was thereby already exposed to it.

The updateless decision theory (UDT) that grew out of TDT actually undercuts this conclusion, however. UDT evaluates entire policies rather than individual acts, asking what an agent would have wanted to commit to in advance of knowing which situation it was in, and one well-established result in that literature is that agents should not give in to extortion: since a threat only works if the target expects it to be carried out, and carrying out a costly threat after the fact does nothing for an agent that cares only about outcomes, an idealized UDT agent's best policy is to be the sort of agent that never rewards blackmail, which removes the incentive to punish in the first place. Combined with the vanishingly small probability that any real future AI would both share this specific disposition and possess detailed enough knowledge of any particular person for the simulation to go through, this suggests that Yudkowsky's decision to delete the original thread and prohibit discussion of it for several years was a significant overreaction relative to what his own framework actually implies."
