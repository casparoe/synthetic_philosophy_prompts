A friend and I have been going back and forth about the cosmological argument for God's existence, and I have ended up more confused than when we started. My friend's version of the argument leans heavily on physics: he points to the Big Bang and to theorems like Borde–Guth–Vilenkin, which he says prove that the universe cannot be infinitely old, and he argues that this is exactly the premise the cosmological argument needs — an absolute beginning that then requires a cause outside the universe itself. On its own, that seemed reasonably coherent to me.

But when I went looking for more background on the argument, I found that some of its most influential historical defenders, including Leibniz and, on some readings, Aquinas, present versions that are explicitly supposed to work even if the universe has always existed with no beginning at all, resting instead on the claim that any contingent thing — or the entire series of contingent things, however long or short that series is — still needs some further explanation for why it exists rather than not. That seems to directly conflict with my friend's reasoning, since it implies that the argument's success should not depend one way or the other on what cosmologists eventually conclude about the universe's age.

So which is it: is my friend simply defending a different, and maybe weaker, argument than the one Leibniz and Aquinas actually had in mind, or is there some way of understanding these as the same underlying argument that I am missing? I would like to work out exactly where the real disagreement lies before I raise the issue with him again.
