I don't have any formal background in philosophy, but I've spent a good deal of time working through David Lewis's counterpart theory on my own, and there's a standoff in the literature I haven't been able to resolve. Kripke's famous complaint is that when we say something like "Humphrey could have won the election," we mean that the very same Humphrey could have won, not that someone else who merely resembles him wins in another world -- and that Humphrey himself would hardly take comfort in a stranger's victory, however close the resemblance. That looks like a real problem for counterpart theory. But the obvious alternative -- insisting that Humphrey is literally, numerically the same individual across worlds -- runs into what Lewis calls the problem of accidental intrinsics: if one and the same person exists in two different worlds with, say, a different build in each, then either that person has flatly incompatible intrinsic properties, or those properties have to be relativized to worlds in a way that seems to turn them into disguised relations rather than genuine intrinsic features. Every source I've found deals with one objection or the other, never both at once. Could you write a dialogue between a committed counterpart theorist and an equally committed defender of transworld identity, in which each opens with their strongest objection to the other's view and is then forced to actually grapple with the comeback, rather than the two of them just talking past each other?
