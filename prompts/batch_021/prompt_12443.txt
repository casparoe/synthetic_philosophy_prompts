I spent the better part of four decades as a theoretical cosmologist working mostly on inflationary models, and it is only in retirement that I have finally had time to read philosophy properly, rather than in the stolen hours between grant deadlines. Working through David Lewis's *On the Plurality of Worlds* recently—his case for modal realism, the thesis that every possible world is exactly as concrete and "actual" as our own, merely isolated from it causally and spatiotemporally, with actuality itself reduced to an indexical, rather like "here" or "now"—I could not shake the feeling that I had met this idea before, wearing different clothes. It seemed to echo, to varying degrees, the multiverse proposals I spent a career taking half-seriously: the endlessly nucleating bubble universes of eternal inflation, Everett's branching quantum worlds, and, closest of all, Max Tegmark's hypothesis that every self-consistent mathematical structure is physically instantiated somewhere, which I seem to recall Tegmark himself describing as a form of modal realism restricted to the mathematical. When I raised the parallel with a former colleague who is still active in the field, he dismissed it as no more than a coincidence of rhetoric, insisting that philosophers and physicists reach for extra worlds for entirely different reasons. I was not convinced he was right, but I could not have told you exactly why.

I would like your help thinking this through properly. Could you compare Lewis's modal realism with the multiverse hypotheses that come out of physics—concentrating on whichever one or two make the comparison sharpest, though Tegmark's seems the natural first candidate—and address, in particular: (1) whether the standard objections to modal realism, especially Lewis's own "incredulous stare" and the epistemological worry that we could have no evidence about worlds with which we have no causal contact, apply with equal force to the physical multiverses, or whether the latter occupy a meaningfully better epistemic position; and (2) whether positing the extra worlds is really doing the same theoretical work in both cases, given that Lewis wants a reductive analysis of modal and counterfactual talk, whereas physicists seem to inherit their multiverses as an unwelcome consequence of otherwise well-supported theories rather than as something sought for its own sake. Where, if anywhere, does the analogy genuinely hold, and where does it quietly break down?
