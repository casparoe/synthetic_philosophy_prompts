So for the last couple months I've been kind of stuck on this thing from Roger Penrose (his book, Emperor's New Mind, or maybe it was the second one, Shadows of the Mind, I honestly mix the two up half the time) about why he thinks a computer can never really do math the way a person does. The rough idea as I understood it: give me any computer program that only ever proves true statements about numbers and never a false one, and Godel's theorem lets you build a specific sentence that program literally cannot prove -- but a person looking at the program from outside can just see that the sentence has to be true. Since that trick works on any program whatsoever, Penrose's move is that our own mathematical understanding can't just be one of these programs running in our head, otherwise the same trick would work on us too and we'd be stuck just like it is.

I ran this by a friend of mine who's good at math and she kind of laughed it off, said it's called the "Lucas-Penrose" argument and pretty much no logician takes it serious anymore. When I asked why not, her answer was basically: the whole thing needs you to already be sure the program (or you, since you're supposedly the one doing the beating) isn't secretly inconsistent somewhere deep down, and nobody gets to be sure of that about their own head, so the argument never even gets off the ground. That sounded fair to me -- except then I went down a rabbit hole online and found out Penrose actually has a whole reply to exactly that objection, where he says the real requirement isn't plain "consistency" but something he calls "soundness," which is apparently related but not the same thing. I honestly can't tell from what I read whether soundness is asking for more than consistency or is somehow a way around needing it, and I dont really have anyone else to ask about this.

Can you actually build out the strongest version of Penrose's comeback here, the one that engages with "how could you ever know your own reasoning is consistent" head-on instead of dodging it? Im not looking for a "some people think X, some think Y" survey, I want the actual best case for why Penrose might survive that objection, argued as hard as it can be argued -- and then, honestly, whether you think it actually holds up once it's laid out that clearly.
