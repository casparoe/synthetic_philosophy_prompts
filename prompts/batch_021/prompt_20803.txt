For thirty-odd years I worked as a field geologist, logging thousands of mineral specimens on the working assumption that whatever regularities I found in the samples already examined would hold for those not yet unearthed. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy in earnest, and I have come to see that Hume's old problem of whether induction can be justified at all is not the only difficulty: Nelson Goodman's grue paradox raises the further, and to me more unsettling, question of which regularities among the endlessly many that fit our data we are even entitled to project. Recall the setup: call an emerald "grue" just in case it is green and examined before some future time t, or blue and not examined before t; every observation confirming "all emeralds are green" confirms "all emeralds are grue" equally well, yet the two hypotheses disagree completely about emeralds first examined after t. Goodman's own reply appeals to the greater historical "entrenchment" of the predicate green in our inductive practice, but this looks to me like a report of habit rather than a justification -- especially once one notices that, redescribed in a language whose primitives are grue and bleen, it is green and blue that come out looking disjunctive and gerrymandered. I would like an essay that pushes past mere exposition of the puzzle to an actual verdict: weigh entrenchment against rival proposals -- Quine's suggestion that our sense of projectible similarity reflects an evolved disposition to track real natural kinds, and Bayesian appeals to the prior probability of hypotheses -- and determine whether any of them supplies a genuinely non-circular reason to prefer green over grue. Defend a definite verdict rather than leaving the new riddle of induction open.
