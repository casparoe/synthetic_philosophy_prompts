I was at a small workshop on formal epistemology a few weeks back, gathering background for a piece on how philosophers argue about probability, and after the last talk -- on van Fraassen's Reflection Principle -- two people in the audience carried the argument into the pub afterward and never really settled it. Quick refresher in case it's useful: the principle says your current credence in some claim, conditional on the supposition that your future credence in it will be some specific value x, should itself just be x -- basically you're meant to treat your own future opinion as an expert. Van Fraassen's argument for this is a "diachronic Dutch book": if you don't do it, and you're already certain what your future credence will be, a bookie can sell you a set of bets now and later that guarantees you a loss whatever happens.

The two arguing were Nadia, a postdoc, and Colin, a decision-theory PhD student. Colin opened with the classic worry: say you know that tomorrow you'll be given something that wipes your memory of the movie you're watching tonight, so tomorrow your credence that "the twist happened" will just be a flat 50/50 guess. Reflection, he said, commits you to already holding 50/50 credence right now, conditional on that prediction -- and that's nuts, since you just watched the movie and anticipated amnesia isn't evidence against something you currently know perfectly well. Nadia answered that he was attacking a version nobody actually holds: the principle only binds you to a future credence you expect to be the rational upshot of whatever total evidence you'll have then, and loosing evidence to amnesia obviously isn't that, so it's simply silent on his case rather than refuted by it. Colin's comeback was that this "fix" quietly empties the principle out: once a future credence only counts when it's already the rational response to future evidence, the whole thing collapses into "update on your evidence," which nobody was disputing, and that's a much thinner payoff than what a Dutch book argument was supposed to earn you. Nadia pushed back that it isn't empty at all -- it still rules out something people do all the time, like already expecting to end up more confident of something for no evidential reason (planning to only read sources that'll flatter a belief you'd like to keep), and that's a real, checkable-in-advance commitment. Colin's last word was that Nadia's distinction can't actually be used by the believer in the moment: from the inside, "I'll be more confident tomorrow because of evidence I'll get" and "I'll be more confident tomorrow because I'm about to indulge a bias" can look identical -- and even formally amount to the same conditional credence -- so a principle that's supposed to tell you, right now, whether to trust your future self isn't doing the job it was billed for.

Neither of them gave any ground and I honestly can't referee this myself. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who's got the better of it at each point, rather than just handing one of them the overall win -- and mention anything you think they're both quietly assuming about what the Reflection Principle is even supposed to do?
