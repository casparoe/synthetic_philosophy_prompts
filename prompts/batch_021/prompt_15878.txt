Question (20 points). In the Theodicy and the Monadology, Leibniz argues that God, in creating, brought about the best of all possible worlds. Reconstruct this argument. Your answer should address: (a) how the Principle of Sufficient Reason motivates the demand for an explanation of why this world exists rather than some other; (b) what Leibniz means in calling this world "the best"; (c) the role of compossibility in explaining how a world containing evil could nonetheless be the best possible one; and (d) in what sense, if any, the existence of this world is necessary.

Rubric: five points each for (a) PSR and the demand for explanation, (b) the meaning of "best," (c) compossibility and the problem of evil, and (d) necessity versus contingency.

Student's answer:

"Leibniz's argument starts from the Principle of Sufficient Reason: nothing is true or exists without a reason why it is so and not otherwise. Truths about the actual world are contingent, and Leibniz holds that contingent truths differ from necessary ones in that they cannot be resolved into an identity through a finite chain of analysis; only an infinite analysis would reveal why they hold. Since no contingent fact can supply its own reason, the ultimate explanation of why this world exists rather than another must lie outside the series of contingent things altogether, in a necessary being whose own existence requires no further cause: God.

Because God is supremely wise and good, he does not create arbitrarily. Among the infinity of possible worlds represented in the divine understanding, he actualizes whichever is "best." Leibniz's standard of bestness is not narrowly moral but metaphysical: the best world is the one combining the greatest variety of phenomena with the simplest possible laws. Since a wise and benevolent God could not knowingly settle for more net suffering than necessary, this standard in practice comes down to whichever candidate world has the greatest surplus of good over evil once all creatures are taken into account.

This is where compossibility does its work. Not all possible substances can coexist in a single world; a possible world is a maximal set of compossible individuals, complete with their entire histories. God cannot therefore stitch together the single best feature of every possible world, because many goods are not compossible with one another. Some metaphysical, physical, or moral evil is consequently an unavoidable feature of whichever compossible set is, overall, most perfect; God permits it rather than willing it directly, since he wills each good antecedently but wills only the best total series in his final, consequent decree.

Lastly, because God's nature cannot change, he could not have actualized a world other than the best one: choosing a lesser world would have been as contradictory as willing a four-sided triangle. This world's existence, though it flows from God's free choice, is therefore necessary in the strict sense, and the non-existence of any alternative world is not really possible, only conceivable in the loose way a false theorem seems conceivable before its refutation is found."
