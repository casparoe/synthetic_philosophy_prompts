Ok so in econ class our teacher did that classic dollar auction demo as an example of escalation of commitment - she auctioned off a $20 bill, the top bidder wins the $20, but the second-highest bidder still has to pay whatever they bid and gets nothing. Bidding blew right past $20 like it apparently always does, and the top two bids ended up at $34 and $31.

After class, two people in my class, Marcus and Elena, got into an argument about whether the people still bidding past $20 were actually being irrational, and neither would let it go.

Marcus said once you're bidding past $20 that's just the sunk cost fallacy, throwing good money after bad since you can't get your earlier bids back either way.

Elena said not quite - your last bid is already gone no matter what you do next, so in the moment the real choice is "pay a bit more for a shot at winning something back" vs "definitely get nothing for what you already put in," and that's not obviously irrational, just a genuinely bad spot to be stuck in.

Marcus said fine, but that logic never stops, it would justify bidding $200 for a $20 bill, so continuing has to become a mistake at some point, or the whole idea of "escalation of commitment" doesn't mean anything.

Elena said that's exactly what makes it a trap and not just a fallacy - every single bid can look locally reasonable, and the two bidders still end up way worse off together, so maybe the real mistake isn't any one bid, its agreeing to play something shaped like this without setting a hard limit for yourself first.

Marcus said that feels like a cop-out though, because if no bid is ever individually irrational you can't really blame either of them for a dumb outcome, and that seems wrong too.

They made me be the tiebreaker and I honestly couldn't decide. Can you go through their exchange move by move, say who's got the stronger point at each step (not just who wins overall), and point out anything you think both of them are missing?
