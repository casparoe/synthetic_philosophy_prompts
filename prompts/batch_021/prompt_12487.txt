I'm a backend engineer, and a launch review at work sent me down a rabbit hole I haven't been able to climb out of on my own. We ran an A/B test on a new checkout flow, and the new version (B) beat the old one (A) on conversion rate in literally every segment we sliced the data by — new users, returning users, mobile, desktop, each country separately — yet A came out ahead once we pooled every user together. Someone on the call shrugged it off with "that's just Simpson's paradox" and linked me the Wikipedia page with the classic UC Berkeley admissions example. I followed the arithmetic well enough; it's a weighted-average artifact that shows up once the segment sizes shift between the two arms. What nobody on the call could actually answer was the question that mattered for shipping the feature: which number were we supposed to act on, the pooled one or the segmented ones?

Reading around since then, I've come to suspect that "it depends on the data" is a cop-out, and that the real answer lives outside the dataset itself, in some causal story about how the segment variable relates to the treatment and the outcome. I keep running into Judea Pearl's name in this context, along with a distinction between the segment being a "confounder" versus a "collider" or a "mediator," where apparently only one of those configurations licenses trusting the pooled comparison — but I don't have the statistics or philosophy background to reconstruct why that's true. I also came across an old claim, possibly garbled by now in my memory, that the paradox is entangled with Savage's "sure-thing principle" from decision theory (roughly: if you'd choose A over B whether or not some condition holds, you should just choose A over B outright), and that Simpson-style reversals look like a real counterexample to that principle unless something subtle is going on.

Could you actually work through this rather than just restating the paradox itself? Specifically, I'd like to understand why there is no purely statistical rule — something that looks only at the joint distribution of the variables, with no causal assumptions built in — that can tell you whether to trust the pooled comparison or the segmented one; what, concretely, distinguishes the cases where pooling is the right call from the cases where segmenting is, in terms of the underlying causal structure; and whether the sure-thing-principle angle amounts to a genuine crack in rational-choice theory or a confusion that dissolves once you think about it causally.
