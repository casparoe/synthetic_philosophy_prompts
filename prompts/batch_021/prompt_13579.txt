**Question (20 points).** In the Nyāya theory of anumāna (inference), explain how the classical inference "the hill has fire, because it has smoke" is supposed to work.
(a) Name Nyāya's four pramāṇas and locate inference among them. [3 pts]
(b) Set the inference out in full five-membered (pañcāvayava) form. [5 pts]
(c) Explain what vyāpti is, why Nyāya insists it must be stated running from smoke to fire and not the reverse, and how the notion of an upādhi is used to rule out the reverse generalization. [8 pts]
(d) Say what kind of hetvābhāsa (fallacy) would be committed by someone who, seeing mist rising off a distant hill, mistook it for smoke and inferred that the hill was on fire. [4 pts]

**Submission.**

Nyāya recognizes four pramāṇas, or means of knowledge: perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), comparison (upamāna), and testimony (śabda). Inference is the second of these; unlike the Cārvākas, who accepted only perception, Nyāya treats it as a fully independent and reliable source of knowledge in its own right.

The stock example is set out in five members:
1. Pratijñā (thesis): the hill has fire.
2. Hetu (reason): because it has smoke.
3. Udāharaṇa (example): wherever there is fire, there is smoke, as in a kitchen.
4. Upanaya (application): and the hill has smoke of that kind.
5. Nigamana (conclusion): therefore, the hill has fire.

This depends on there being a genuine vyāpti, or invariable concomitance, between smoke and fire; otherwise the hetu would be a merely accidental accompaniment rather than a reliable sign. Nyāya is careful here because a naive generalization can be wrecked by an upādhi — a condition riding along with the reason without being essential to it. The standard illustration is wet fuel: someone who generalized only from smoky fires might think heat and smoke always travel together, when really it is the wetness of the fuel doing the work of producing visible smoke, not the fire as such. Nyāya's method is to strip out such upādhis through tarka (suppositional reasoning) until what remains is a bare concomitance that holds unconditionally in both directions — which is exactly why the udāharaṇa above can be stated the way it is.

If someone mistook rising mist for smoke and inferred fire, this would be a case of the savyabhicāra (or anaikāntika) hetvābhāsa, the "inconclusive" or "erratic" reason: just as "knowable" fails to guarantee non-eternality, since it is found among both eternal and non-eternal things, "apparent smoke" here fails to guarantee fire, since it is found in cases, like mist, where there is no fire at all.
