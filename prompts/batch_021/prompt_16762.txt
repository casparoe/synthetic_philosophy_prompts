Last year, while traveling alone abroad, I had my bag stolen the night before an early flight home for a family emergency. Without cash or working cards, I had no way to cover a same-day replacement ticket, which the airline would only issue against immediate payment. A stranger in line behind me overheard what had happened and simply paid for it herself, several hundred dollars, telling me she would only do it because she was confident, from a two-minute conversation, that I would send the money back. She wrote down an account number and disappeared into the terminal before I even asked her name: no contract, no way to trace me, and effectively no chance we would ever cross paths again. I wired the money the moment I landed, without a second thought.

My brother-in-law, who thinks about these things in fairly economic terms, later pointed out that once the ticket was in my hand, paying her back could not get me anything further: the flight was already secured, she had no recourse, and the whole episode was permanently behind us. By his account, repaying her was pure loss, and the only genuine puzzle is why she trusted a stranger enough to front the money at all. I spend more time than is probably healthy on forums arguing about thought experiments like trolley problems and the veil of ignorance, and I have a nagging sense that this situation is a real-world instance of some standard puzzle, but I cannot place it or work out for myself where, if anywhere, his reasoning goes wrong.

I would also like to understand what to make of the fact that reneging never registered as a live option for me in the moment: was that simply lucky character, or was it doing some rational work that a cleverer, more narrowly self-interested version of me would have missed? Was my brother-in-law right, and if not, where exactly does his reasoning break down?
