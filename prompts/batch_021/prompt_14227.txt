Here's the student's answer, more or less verbatim: "Behind the veil of ignorance, the parties don't know their class position, natural talents, or particular conception of the good, so they can't design principles to favor themselves. Rawls argues that in this situation the parties would reason via maximin — ranking principles by their worst possible outcome and choosing whichever principle's worst outcome is best — rather than by calculating expected utility. He gives three considerations for this: the parties have no reliable basis for assigning probabilities to the different social positions they might end up in, the minimum guaranteed under maximin is high enough that there's little to gain by risking it, and the worst outcomes under the rejected alternatives are ones a person could hardly accept. What these three considerations really amount to, though, is that the parties are being modeled as strongly risk-averse, and it's this risk-aversion that ultimately does the work of ruling out expected-utility maximization. Harsanyi's version of the veil of ignorance is structurally similar but licenses a different inference: his parties are willing to assign an equal probability to being any given person in society, and then maximize expected utility given that assumption, which, combined with standard assumptions about diminishing marginal utility of income, yields average utilitarianism rather than the difference principle. Within Rawls's complete theory, equal basic liberty is lexically prior to the second principle as a whole, so liberty can never be traded away for economic gains, and within the second principle itself, the difference principle takes priority over fair equality of opportunity, since unequal income and wealth are the inequalities that most directly affect people's life prospects, whereas fair equality of opportunity is more of a procedural safeguard that only kicks in once the bigger distributive question has been settled."
