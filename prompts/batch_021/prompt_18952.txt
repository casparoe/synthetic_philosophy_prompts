Ok, this is going to sound like a weird ask but it's not fiction — it's an actual mess in my writers' co-op and I could use an outside brain on it. I'm a novelist myself (working on book number three right now) and I've been part of this co-op — about twenty of us, novelists and story writers — for something like eight years; we split studio rent, run a tiny emergency grant fund, that kind of thing. I'm on the three-person grievance panel, and one member, Deb, has filed a complaint against another member, Marcus, over the novel of his that sold pretty big last year. She says that back in 2019, at an after-workshop drinks night, she pitched him — in real detail, over something like forty minutes — the entire conceit his book ended up being built around (I won't describe the actual premise, but picture something like "a generation-ship crew that no longer knows it's on a ship at all"). She says he wrote basically nothing that night and everything two years later, alone, and never credited or paid her a cent. Marcus doesn't deny the conversation happened. His defense is that he'd already been circling something similar before that night, that a premise floating around at a bar isn't "developed" in any sense that should count, and that our co-ops charter's only relevant line — "collaboratively developed material remains shared property" — doesn't apply since he alone wrote every sentence of the actual book. Deb's comeback is that the premise was doing most of the work that made the thing sellable in the first place, and that if the panel lets "I'm the one who typed it" be the whole test for ownership, we're basically ruling that ideas are worth nothing the moment someone else claims to have already been thinking along similar lines anyway — which conveniently is a defense nobody can ever disprove.

We vote next Thursday and I have to circulate a written recommendation before then, so could you draft that memo for me? I want an actual verdict in it — dismiss the grievance, uphold it with some form of credit or compensation, or something conditional in between — not a tour of copyright law that ducks the decision. And please don't just lean on the standard "ideas aren't protected, only expression is" line and call it done, since that's basically the move Marcus is already making, and it feels like it dodges Deb's point rather than answers it. Whichever way you land, take the loosing side's best argument seriously and explain why it still doesn't (or does) carry the day. This one matters to me more than it probably should, partly because my own current book started as a throwaway line a friend made at dinner, and I've been low-key rehearsing my own defense in my head this whole time.
