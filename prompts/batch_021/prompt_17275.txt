I've been reading about the discursive dilemma in judgment aggregation, where a panel of judges can each hold perfectly consistent views, say on whether a contract was valid, whether it was breached, and therefore whether the defendant is liable, yet a plain majority vote taken separately on each of those three propositions can produce a group verdict that is logically inconsistent, even though nobody on the panel reasoned badly. This strikes me as a fairly ordinary algorithmic problem rather than a deep philosophical one: propositionwise majority voting is just a naive aggregation rule that ignores the logical dependencies between the propositions, not unlike a distributed system ending up in a corrupted state because writes were committed out of dependency order. Switching to a smarter rule, such as a weighted quota on the conclusion, a supermajority threshold, or a constraint solver that outputs whichever fully consistent verdict is closest to what each judge actually voted, seems like it should make the inconsistency disappear entirely. So why do List and Pettit's generalized impossibility results get treated as being as fundamental to social choice theory as Arrow's theorem, rather than as describing a specific, already-patched bug in naive majority rule?
