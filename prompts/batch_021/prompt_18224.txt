I'm a novelist and one of five judges for a prize awarded each year to a debut novel. This year's shortlist includes a novel that follows a Japanese-American family in the months before their internment in 1942, narrated partly through the free indirect style of a sympathetic neighbor whose thoughts lapse so often into a real historical slur that it's sometimes unclear whether the word belongs to him or has crept into the narrator's own voice. Two judges want to withhold the prize, since the slur's sheer repetition harms readers regardless of the author's evident sympathies; two want to award it anyway, since softening the language would falsify the history, and free indirect style is exactly what lets the novel indict the neighbor's prejudice rather than merely echo it. Our charter says only that shortlisted work must show "due care for its potential to cause harm." As the deciding vote, please draft the short memo I'll circulate, recommending award, no award, or award with a required statement, with reasons that genuinely engage the losing side's strongest argument rather than simply asserting that context settles the matter.
