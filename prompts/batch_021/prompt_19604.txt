I've been team-teaching a graduate seminar on modal metaphysics this term, and last week's session was devoted to the standoff between David Lewis's counterpart theory and the more orthodox Kripke–Plantinga picture of transworld identity. Somewhere in the discussion, one of my students raised something that has stuck with me since: Leibniz, quite independently of any twentieth-century apparatus, seems to have already committed himself to something very like Lewis's thesis that no individual exists in more than one possible world. In his correspondence with Arnauld over the "complete concept" of Adam, Leibniz concedes that an Adam who did not sin would not be our Adam at all but a distinct possible individual, since sinning is built into the very notion that makes Adam Adam; and in the closing narrative of the Theodicy, where Pallas Athena leads Theodorus through the Palace of the Fates, we are shown room after room containing a different possible Sextus Tarquinius, each a slightly different person living out a different fate, rather than one and the same Sextus undergoing different adventures. Benson Mates and other commentators have accordingly read Leibniz as a kind of proto-counterpart theorist avant la lettre.

For a short piece I'm drafting for my students, I'd like your help pushing this resemblance further than a mere anecdote. Suppose Leibniz were confronted with the finished twentieth-century debate: Lewis's counterpart relation, cashed out as qualitative similarity across genuinely distinct, causally isolated worlds sharing no parts; and against it, Kripke's insistence that possible worlds are stipulated rather than discovered by telescope, so that we can simply designate one and the same individual as inhabiting various worlds without any resemblance-based surrogate, together with Plantinga's related view that individuals carry world-transcending essences securing their literal identity across them. Which side do you think Leibniz would ultimately take, and which of his own commitments — the identity of indiscernibles, the doctrine of complete concepts, his rationalist principle that every truth about a substance must be grounded in that substance's own nature, or his express worry, pressed on him by Arnauld, that none of this be allowed to collapse into fatalism — would do the most work in pushing him there? I would also welcome your view on whether Leibniz's own framework instead gives him the resources to resist the dichotomy altogether, rather than simply landing on one side of it.
