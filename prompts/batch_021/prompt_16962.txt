I'm taking an upper-level seminar on metaphysics this semester that has spent the last few weeks on the identity of indiscernibles, and I could use some help untangling a passage we read just before moving on to Max Black's well-known 1952 dialogue about two qualitatively identical spheres suspended in an otherwise empty, symmetric universe. In lecture, our professor argued that Black's thought experiment isn't actually a decisive counterexample to Leibniz's own commitments, on the grounds that Leibniz's version of the principle was never intended as a logical or necessary truth in the first place. A couple of us pushed back on this in office hours, but we couldn't settle the question, so I would like a more careful outside opinion on the text itself.

The passage in question comes from sections 8 and 9 of the Monadology: "Yet Monads must have some qualities, otherwise they would not even be existing things. And if simple substances did not differ at all in their qualities, there would be no means of perceiving any change in things... Also, every Monad must be different from every other. For there are never in nature two beings which are exactly alike, and in which it is not possible to find some internal difference, or one founded on an intrinsic denomination."

What exactly is the modal status of the claim in the second half of that passage? On one reading, it is close to an analytic truth about individuation: qualitative sameness without any internal, non-relational difference just amounts to numerical sameness, so that two supposedly indiscernible substances would really be a single substance under two names (Leibniz uses almost that exact phrase elsewhere, in his correspondence with Clarke). On another reading, the claim is downstream of the principle of sufficient reason together with God's choosing to create the richest and most varied of all possible worlds, in which case section 9 would tell us only that no two things happen to be exactly alike in the actual world, leaving it open whether some other possible world -- including, perhaps, Black's imagined one -- might contain genuine qualitative duplicates. I would like help working out which of these readings the Monadology passage itself actually supports, or whether it is genuinely ambiguous between them. I would also like help thinking about whether, given how much weight the passage places on "intrinsic denomination," Black's spheres are even a fair test of the principle stated here, since the only apparent difference between the two spheres is the purely relational fact of their distance from one another, and by the scenario's own symmetry that fact is identical for both. Does Leibniz's apparatus in this passage give him a principled way of dismissing Black's scenario as ill-posed on its own terms, in something like the way he dismissed a uniform shift of the entire material universe through space as no real alternative at all, or does the passage leave him with no such resource against Black?
