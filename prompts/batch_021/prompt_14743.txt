I've noticed the Nash bargaining solution being invoked constantly in rationalist-adjacent writing on splitting gains from cooperation and on aggregating conflicting values under moral uncertainty, almost always with a passing nod to Kalai and Smorodinsky's 1975 alternative as "the other axiomatization" before the discussion moves on. That has always felt like a missed opportunity, since the two solutions share Nash's Pareto-optimality, symmetry, and scale-invariance axioms but part ways on a single one: Nash keeps independence of irrelevant alternatives, while Kalai-Smorodinsky trades it for a monotonicity condition pegged to the "ideal point." No solution can satisfy both axioms at once alongside the shared three, so choosing between them really means choosing which failure mode you can live with — a miniature version of the fork Arrow's theorem forces on social choice, except here both branches have serious, committed defenders rather than one obviously losing candidate.

I'd like to see that fork dramatized the way I've seen causal-vs-evidential decision theorists dramatized standing in front of Newcomb's boxes. Please write a dialogue between two game theorists jointly appointed as binding arbitrators in the dissolution of a two-person business partnership, tasked with handing down an award dividing the remaining assets. Working independently, the two have arrived at almost identical splits — until their paralegal interrupts with news that reshapes the bargaining problem: a client wants to renew a lucrative multi-year contract, but a licensing technicality means only one of the two ex-partners could ever legally hold it going forward, so that partner's best conceivable outcome has just risen substantially while the other partner's own fallback and ceiling are untouched. With the hearing at which they must announce their award an hour away, the two arbitrators discover they no longer agree on whether, or how much, this development should move the split. I'd like the two of them to really engage on this point: the Nash partisan can draw on independence of irrelevant alternatives and its non-cooperative pedigree (the way something like Rubinstein-style alternating-offer bargaining converges to the Nash solution), and the Kalai-Smorodinsky partisan can press the fairness intuition built into monotonicity, but each should also have to sit with the other's strongest objection, including whatever price their own axiom quietly extracts elsewhere. I'd like them equally sharp and genuinely responsive to one another rather than talking past each other, and I'd rather the dialogue land on a real, clearly stated crux — each conceding what is right in the other's position — than resolve into a tidy verdict.
