For the past year I've contributed to a small interview segment my theatre company runs after closing night, in which we sit down with an academic or a fellow practitioner to talk through the ideas underneath the production, and I've just been asked to host the next one myself. The timing feels strange in a way that's making the prep harder than usual: I spent this past spring in a five-month run playing a controlling, manipulative husband in a fairly dark domestic drama, and there were evenings, walking home, when I genuinely could not separate the residue of anger I was carrying from anything that was actually mine. I mentioned this, half as a joke, to a friend finishing her doctorate in eighteenth-century French theatre, and she pointed me toward Rousseau's Lettre à d'Alembert sur les spectacles -- his broadside against d'Alembert's Encyclopédie proposal that Geneva acquire a public theatre -- where a good part of the case against the acting profession turns out to rest on exactly the kind of thing I had just lived through.

Our guest has written extensively on that quarrel and is known for a reading that cuts against the usual one: she argues that Rousseau's real target was never theatrical representation as such but the political economy of the professional, ticketed stage in a small republic, and she uses that distinction to deflect the obvious charge that his own career as the composer of Le Devin du village and the author of Narcisse made him a hypocrite. I'd like eight to ten interview questions that don't simply give her room to restate that defense. I want at least one question that goes after the self-loss argument about acting on its own terms -- what exactly is supposed to be corrupted when someone practices feigning passion for a living -- and that tests whether Starobinski's transparency-and-obstacle framing actually explains that or just redescribes it; one on why Geneva's republican character was carrying so much of the argumentative weight against d'Alembert, and whether the objection generalizes past a small city-state at all; one on the considerably less comfortable material about the male cercles, actresses, and female spectators, which her published work mostly steps around; and one that forces the hypocrisy question back onto the operas and the comedy specifically, rather than letting the political-economy reading settle it in the abstract. For each, please add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, so the conversation doesn't stall on the rehearsed version of it.
