I work the night shift as a security guard at a large distribution warehouse, and a genuine puzzle from the job has been nagging at me for weeks, to the point where I have taken to sketching numbers on napkins during my breaks. Once a shift, my round takes me down one long, poorly lit corridor lined with storage doors. Two of these doors, spaced about ten paces apart, are physically identical to one another and to nothing else on the route, a leftover from a renovation years ago, and the overhead lights on that stretch of corridor have also been broken for as long as I have worked there. Between the darkness, the identical doors, and the sheer monotony of passing dozens of other, ordinary doors every night, I find that by the time I reach either one of this particular pair, I genuinely cannot tell whether it is the first of the two or the second, and I have no reliable memory of having already passed the other one earlier in the same walk. I have considered marking one of them somehow, but staff on other shifts use the same corridor and would either remove any mark or be misled by it, so that option is closed to me.

Here is why the distinction matters. Standard procedure is simply to walk past every door to the end of the corridor and badge out. I have also been asked, as an unofficial extra task, to go through the second of these two particular doors on my way past, since it leads to a store-room where a gauge needs checking, which earns me a $200 bonus at the end of the month. If I go through the first one instead, I end up on an unfinished ledge that trips a safety sensor, earning me a $50 fine and a note on my record. If I simply walk past both without going through either, which I am always allowed to do, nothing good or bad happens at all. Crucially, going through either door removes me from the corridor for the rest of the night, so there is never a second attempt: I encounter the first door, and only if I do not go through it do I go on to encounter the second one; if I do not go through that one either, I simply finish the round having done nothing special. Because I cannot tell the two doors apart, my only honest option is to decide, in advance, to go through an unidentified door of this kind with some fixed probability, the same probability both times, and let matters fall where they fall.

This is where something has started to genuinely confuse me. On a day off, working purely from the figures, I can sit down and calculate which fixed probability would earn me the most money on average across many hypothetical nights, and I arrive at one particular answer. But when I instead picture myself actually standing in front of one of these doors on an ordinary night, with no idea whether it is the first or the second, and reason it through from inside that moment instead ("I truly cannot distinguish these two, so as far as I am concerned it is a fair fifty-fifty chance which one this is, and here is what that implies I should do"), I arrive at a noticeably different answer. I mentioned this to a friend who enjoys probability puzzles as a hobby, and he told me this kind of mismatch is apparently a recognised source of disagreement among people who think about these things carefully, though he could not explain to me why it happens. I have no formal training in mathematics or philosophy, only a long-standing amateur habit of working through puzzles like this on my own, so I would genuinely appreciate a thorough explanation: why do these two ways of arriving at "the right odds" fail to agree, when in both cases I seem to be reasoning about exactly the same night, the exact same two doors, and exactly the same information? Is one of my two calculations simply a mistake I should discard, or are they quietly answering two different questions that only sound identical? And whichever it turns out to be, which number should actually govern my hand at the moment I am standing in front of the door?
