I'm a PhD student in economics currently TAing our department's graduate game theory sequence, and a recurring point of confusion in office hours — for the students, and if I'm honest occasionally for me too — concerns how the various solution concepts built on dominance and best-response reasoning relate to one another once mixed strategies enter the picture. Everyone can recite that iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies gives a "reasonable" prediction, and everyone can recite the definition of rationalizability, but when a sharp student asked me last week to say precisely which of these procedures is more restrictive than which, and where correlated equilibrium fits into that hierarchy, I found myself reconstructing the argument on the fly rather than stating it cleanly. I'd like to check my reconstruction against yours before turning it into a handout.

Concretely, consider an arbitrary finite two-player normal-form game (let's set aside the extra wrinkles that appear with three or more players and correlated versus independent conjectures). Define six sets of strategies: (i) NE, the strategies played with positive probability in at least one Nash equilibrium; (ii) CE, the strategies played with positive probability in at least one correlated equilibrium; (iii) RAT, the rationalizable strategies in the Bernheim–Pearce sense (those surviving iterated deletion of strategies that are never a best response to any belief about the opponent's play); (iv) ESDS-mix, the strategies surviving iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies where a strategy may be eliminated in a round if it is strictly dominated by some mixed strategy; (v) ESDS-pure, defined identically except that only domination by another pure strategy licenses elimination; and (vi) EWDS, the strategies that can survive some iterated procedure of eliminating weakly dominated strategies (mixed strategies allowed as dominators), continued until no weakly dominated strategy remains.

Please rank these six sets from smallest to largest by set inclusion — that is, group them into tiers such that every set in an earlier tier is guaranteed, as a matter of logical necessity holding across every finite two-player game, to be a (weak) subset of every set in a later tier. Tie together any sets that must always coincide, and, separately, flag any set that cannot be given a well-defined place in such a linear ranking at all (for instance because it isn't uniquely pinned down independently of some further choice, or because its relation to the others isn't fixed across all games), with a sentence explaining why. Please use exactly the labels NE, CE, RAT, ESDS-mix, ESDS-pure, and EWDS, and format your answer as a JSON object like this:
