I'm a PhD student in philosophy of religion, and back home I'm also an ordained elder in my denomination, which is how I ended up on the regional committee that has to sign off before a chaplaincy resident can go for board certification -- basically the body that decides whether someone's faith is "examined and mature enough" to represent the church to dying people. We've got a genuinely hard case on the docket and I have to draft the discernment memo the committee votes on in two weeks, so I want a rough first pass before I bring anything to the group.

The candidate, who by every account is one of the best CPE residents our hospital has ever trained, turned in a theological reflection paper that basically reconstructs Schellenberg's argument from divine hiddenness from scratch, out of two years of sitting with dying patients: if a perfectly loving God existed, she argues, that God would make sure everyone capable of a relationship with him had what they needed to believe it, and since she and plenty of the patients she's sat with are about as nonresistant as a person can be and still come up with nothing, she can't honestly affirm belief in a personal God anymore. She still wants to be a chaplain, though -- her framing is that she ministers "from inside the silence, not despite it," and she brings up Mother Teresa's letters, the ones published years after her death showing decades of feeling completely abandoned by God, as basically her own situation with the volume turned up. Half the committee thinks this is disqualifying on its face, since our standard requires "a mature, examined faith, including belief in a personal God," before we'll endorse anyone; the other half thinks turning her down means rejecting the one candidate who's actually thought this through more honestly than anybody else we've ever endorsed, and that whoever wrote the standard never had this case in mind.

Could you draft the memo for me? I need it to land on something concrete -- endorse, deny, or endorse with a specific condition and a date to revisit -- not just lay out both sides, and it needs to engage her arguement as the philosophy it actually is, rather than just quoting the standard back at her or waving at "even saints have doubts" like that settles anything. It should also directly address the hardest version of the worry on the other side: she is going to be alone in a room with someone dying who looks up and asks her point blank whether God is really there, and the committee needs some sense of what she'd say to that, and whether that's a problem.
