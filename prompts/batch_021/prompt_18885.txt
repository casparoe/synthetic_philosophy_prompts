ok this has been living in my head since a coworker of mine (we build reasoning models) brought it up at standup of all places - whenever a new model does well on math olympiad benchmarks someone always goes "doesn't matter, Godel already proved a machine can never see the truth of its own Godel sentence the way we can, so its not real understanding." I went down a rabbit hole and learned this is basically the Lucas/Penrose argument, and that Godel himself actually only ever committed to a more cautious either/or (the mind isn't a machine, OR there are absolutely undecidable math truths), not Lucas's ballsier "the mind definitely isn't a machine." Can you argue that neither version survives, once you push on the problem that's been bugging me: to run the argument at all you (or Lucas, or Penrose, or any of us) need to already know that your own mathematical faculty is consistent/sound, and that's exactly what Godel's second theorem says a sufficiently strong consistent system can never prove about itself. I specifically want that objection pushed all the way through to Godel's own modest disjunction, not just used to dunk on Lucas's overreach - every explanation I can find online does the easy part and lets Godel's original version off the hook.
