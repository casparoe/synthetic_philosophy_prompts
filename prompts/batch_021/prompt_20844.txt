Something happened on my team a few weeks back and I can't stop turning it over in my head even though everyone else has moved on. We had a CI test fail right before we were about to ship a payments feature. My teammate glanced at the failure for about ten seconds and said "nah, that one's flaky, I already traced through that whole function twice and I know its correct," told our lead to just re-run the pipeline, and we shipped anyway. Turned out it was a real bug and we ate a two-hour partial outage that weekend. The postmortem basically concluded "should've investigated the failure instead of assuming," which is true but doesnt quite get at the thing that's actually bugging me.

Here's what I keep getting stuck on: even in a world where he'd turned out to be right and it really was just a flaky test, there's something off about the reasoning itself, separate from the outcome. He was basically saying "I already know this code is correct, therefore any test result that disagrees with that must be wrong, therefore I don't need to actually look at it." But if that move is legit, it seems like you could use it to wave away literally any inconvenient piece of evidence about anything you're currently confident about, forever, which seems like a terrible way to reason in general even if it happens to work out most days. I don't have any background in philosophy so I have no idea if this is some already-named thing that's been picked apart a hundred times, but it feels too clean a pattern to be original to my coworker. Is there an actual defect in reasoning that way, or does treating "I already know I'm right" as license to skip checking new evidence just come with the territory of ever being confident about anything at all?
