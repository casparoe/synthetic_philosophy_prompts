I've been reporting a longform piece on the philosophical prehistory of our arguments about what gives money its "value" — prompted partly by how often people say cryptocurrencies lack the "intrinsic value" that gold or wheat supposedly possess. Chasing that phrase backward through the history of economic thought landed me, to my mild surprise, in Aristotle.

In the Nicomachean Ethics — in Book V's discussion of justice in exchange — Aristotle worries about how a builder and a shoemaker can trade fairly when a house and a pair of shoes seem to have nothing in common; he even works out a small numerical example, so many beds for a house, to show how money can render radically unlike goods commensurable by standing in for need (chreia), while more or less conceding that the resulting equality is a practical convention rather than a fact about the goods themselves. What I hadn't clocked until this week is that Marx revisits this very passage near the start of Capital, Volume One: he credits Aristotle with being the first thinker to see that treating two commodities as equal in exchange presupposes some common "substance" between them, yet argues that Aristotle could never have named that substance — abstract, undifferentiated human labor — because a society resting on slavery could not yet conceive of the equality among persons and kinds of labor that the concept requires. When I mentioned this to an economic historian I'm interviewing for the piece, she waved it off as Marx projecting his own theory backward onto a text that wasn't built to bear it; that offhand dismissal is what's made me want to look at the argument properly.

Could you help me work through the comparison rather than just summarizing each thinker in turn? First, assess Marx's claim as a piece of intellectual history: is he identifying a genuine limit within Aristotle's own conceptual resources, or retrofitting the labor theory of value onto a question Aristotle wasn't trying to answer? Second, and more substantively, weigh the two accounts of commensurability on their merits — Aristotle's, in which unlike goods become exchangeable via need and its conventional proxy, money, against Marx's, in which they become exchangeable via the abstract labor congealed in them. Which comes closer to explaining what actually makes exchange possible, and why? I'd rather you commit to a real verdict and defend it against the strongest version of the view you're setting aside than split the difference.
