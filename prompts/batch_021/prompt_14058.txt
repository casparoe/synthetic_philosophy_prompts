So this happened at a ramen festival a couple weekends ago and it's been rattling around in my head since. There's two stalls right next to each other, both brand new with zero online reviews, basically indistinguishable as far as any of us could tell just looking at them. One had a line wrapped around the block, the other had maybe two people standing at it. Our whole group of eight just kind of drifted into the long line without ever really discussing it, and later that night two friends of mine, Nate and Ola, ended up arguing about whether that was actually a defensible way to pick.

Nate's opening move was that joining the line was basically irrational herd behavior: nobody in it, us included, had any real private info about which stall was better, so the line length was just people copying people copying people, all the way back to whichever couple of randos happened to walk up first. If those first movers had wandered right instead of left, the "good" line would be on the other stall right now, so the line length carries close to zero actual information about the food, and treating it as evidence was a mistake. Ola's comeback was that this is literally the textbook picture of a rational information cascade, not a mistake: once the queue's long enough it becomes individually rational for the next passerby to disregard even a decent private signal (a good sniff, a peek at the bowls) and just copy everyone else, since the inferred signal from N people's choices swamps your one data point. Nate's follow-up to that was one I honestly couldn't evaluate myself: fine, but doesn't the cascade model itself say that once it kicks in, people stop feeding new private information into it, so the "evidence" stops accumulating after the first couple of genuine signals? By the time you're person 30 you're not inferring from 30 independent opinions, you're inferring from maybe 2, laundered through 28 copies -- so calling it "rational" is true in some narrow decision-theory sense but kind of misleading if it's supposed to reassure you the food's actually good. Ola conceded the mechanism but not the point: yes, cascades run on very little real information and are famously fragile for exactly that reason, but that's not a flaw in the theory, it's a predicted feature -- and the fragility is exactly why one new bit of public info (someone up front visibly spitting their noodles out) could flip the whole line in seconds, unlike a reputation built up over years. Nate's last jab was that the formal cascade models assume people act one at a time in sequence, each seeing the full history before choosing, whereas we could all just see both lines "simultaneously" with no fixed order, so maybe the framework doesn't strictly apply to an actual physical queue. Ola's reply was basically a partial concession: the order's still there, just compressed (whoever glances over first still effectively moves first), but she admitted the clean math behind cascades -- the actual thresholds and likelihood-ratio bounds -- assumes a tidier setup than any real line ever gives you.

I've read enough rationalist-blog-adjacent stuff over the years to recognize the general shape of what they're both gesturing at (pretty sure this exact two-stalls setup is the go-to illustration in the original cascades literature, Bikhchandani/Hirshleifer/Welch or maybe the Banerjee paper, I can never keep those two straight), but I couldn't actually tell who was landing real points versus who just sounded sophisticated. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who's more right at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner, and flag anything important that both of them seem to be missing? I'm especially curious whether Nate's last objection (sequential model vs. a real, kind-of-simultaneous queue) actually damages Ola's case or is weaker than either of them treated it as.
