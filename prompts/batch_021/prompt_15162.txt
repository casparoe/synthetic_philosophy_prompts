I have been reading about Henry Sidgwick's decades-long involvement in the Society for Psychical Research, which he helped found in 1882 and served as president of on more than one occasion. What strikes me is how odd the pairing looks in retrospect: the author of The Methods of Ethics, a philosopher renowned for an almost pathological scrupulousness, spending his evenings sitting in on séances and auditing card-guessing experiments with mediums and schoolchildren. This was not idle curiosity. Sidgwick seems to have believed that his own ethical theory had an unfilled gap in it — the "dualism of practical reason," the unresolved conflict between the authority of self-interest and the authority of duty, which he thought could only be closed by some further fact, such as some form of personal survival after death, underwriting morality — and he hoped psychical research might supply that fact empirically, especially once he had resigned his Trinity fellowship rather than go on affirming doctrines he no longer believed. And yet, by every account, he never found anything he regarded as conclusive: he was known among his fellow investigators for his almost compulsive habit of hunting down the flaw in any positive result, including the Society's own, and one of the investigations he was most closely involved in, the "thought-reading" experiments with the Creery sisters, began as one of the Society's most striking successes and ended in the discovery that the girls had been signalling to one another.

With all of that in mind, I would like you to reconstruct, in some depth, how Sidgwick might have reacted to a much later episode: Daryl Bem's 2011 paper in a mainstream psychology journal, reporting a series of experiments that seemed to show ordinary people's choices and recall being statistically nudged by random events that had not yet occurred, and the controversy that followed it — the argument that ordinary significance testing was far too generous to a claim of that kind, so that a more skeptical Bayesian analysis made the effect largely disappear, several well-publicized failures to reproduce the results, and the episode's later status as one of the standard examples cited when explaining why psychology underwent its replication crisis. Would Sidgwick have treated this as belated vindication of the kind of evidence he spent decades failing to find, or would the very habits of mind that made him so hard to satisfy in his own research have made him side with the critics? I am especially interested in whether, on your reconstruction, his judgment about the quality of the science would come apart from his sense of what a genuine result of that sort would have meant for the philosophical stakes — the dualism of practical reason, the question of survival — that drew him to the subject in the first place.
