A friend and I stayed up far too late last week arguing about something that began as an offhand remark and turned into a genuine disagreement: what actually entitles someone to credit for an idea? She's an engineer in industry and I'm mid-PhD, and I suspect that difference is part of why our instincts pointed in opposite directions from the start. Her view was that whoever first frames a problem in a promising way — whoever says, in effect, "what if we tried X to get Y" — deserves the lion's share of credit for whatever follows, because that framing is what set the whole direction that made the result possible. Mine was that framing a problem is cheap next to solving it: until someone works out an actual, operative way to make X produce Y, all you have is a hope, and hopes don't get patented or credited on their own. We traded examples for two hours — hers mostly about mentors and collaborators who "saw it first," mine about the specific, hard-won technical fixes that make an idea actually work — and neither of us budged.

The argument stopped being academic a few days later, because I sit on my university's intellectual property committee, and this week we have to resolve almost exactly this dispute for real. A doctoral student in one of our biomedical engineering labs has filed an invention disclosure, naming herself as sole inventor, for a dissolvable microneedle patch that releases insulin at a controlled rate as it degrades. Her faculty supervisor has objected in writing: applying microneedle arrays to diabetes management was his idea, he secured the funding, and he proposed the initial polymer family at a lab meeting months before she built anything. As a matter of long-standing practice in his lab, moreover, he expects to be named on every patent that comes out of it. The student's response is that microneedle drug-delivery patches were already well known before her project began, so "try this for diabetes" was not itself an inventive act. What actually makes the device work, and what she takes to be the real novel contribution, is the specific polymer blend and fabrication sequence she worked out on her own, after discovering — to everyone's surprise, including her supervisor's — that his suggested materials broke down far too quickly at body temperature to hold their shape. Our committee's written policy says only that inventorship must follow "applicable legal standards," without specifying what that means for a case like this, and we are under real time pressure: she is due to present preliminary results at a conference in three weeks, and any public disclosure made before a filing will foreclose patent rights in most countries outside the US.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate to the committee before we reconvene early next week? It should recommend a specific resolution — who should be named as inventor or inventors, and on what basis — together with a concrete process and timeline for completing a filing before the conference, and it should take the supervisor's objection seriously as a substantive position rather than as mere territoriality, even if it does not ultimately carry the day.
