I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or law, but I've been reading around constitutional theory as a side interest, and a disagreement with a friend has left me stuck on something. The starting point is Jon Elster's use of the Ulysses story as a model of rational precommitment: Ulysses has his crew tie him to the mast and stop their own ears with wax so that his future, Siren-addled self cannot seize the wheel and wreck the ship. Elster, and later Stephen Holmes, extend this to constitutions — entrenched bills of rights, supermajority amendment rules, judicial review — treating them as devices that protect a polity's considered long-term judgment from its own future passions or factions. What bothers me is that a constitutional "people" isn't a single continuous agent the way Ulysses is. The generation that writes an entrenched clause and the generation restrained by it a century later aren't the same self in any obvious sense, so it's not clear the binding is self-binding at all, rather than one group of people shackling a completely different group that never agreed to anything. (I gather this bothered Elster too, since by the time of "Ulysses Unbound" he seems to have walked back a fair amount of his own earlier optimism about the analogy.) It also reminds me of something from my day job: we sometimes make a migration or a config change deliberately irreversible precisely so that nobody, including our future selves, can cave to pressure and roll it back — a toy version of the same move — though I'd like the essay itself to stay on the constitutional case rather than the software one.

I'd like you to write me an essay that takes a real position on this rather than just surveying it: is constitutional precommitment a coherent and legitimate extension of the Ulysses idea, or does the whole framework break down once you scale it up from one person to a changing collective? I think the sharpest form of the question is what I'd call the precommitment dilemma: a genuinely irrevocable clause — something like Article 79(3) of the German Basic Law, which puts human dignity and the basic federal and democratic structure permanently beyond the reach of any amendment procedure, no matter how large the majority — is the closest real-world analogue to Ulysses actually lashed to the mast, but for exactly that reason seems hardest to justify democratically, since it forecloses even a future, fully-informed consensus from ever revisiting it. An ordinary amendable entrenchment (say, a two-thirds requirement) is easier to defend but starts to look less like genuine precommitment and more like a speed bump Ulysses could talk his way past if he pushed hard enough. Please have the essay engage with Holmes's argument that such constraints can be enabling rather than purely restrictive of democracy, and with the disagreement-based objection (in the spirit of Jeremy Waldron) that precommitment reasoning quietly assumes we already know which "self" — the constitution-writers or the constrained later majority — is the sober, farsighted one, when that is usually exactly what people disagree about. And don't let the essay end on "there are good arguments on both sides": I want an actual verdict, even a qualified one — something like "precommitment talk is defensible for rules that protect the conditions of future democratic choice but not for rules that lock in substantive policy outcomes" would count as a real answer, and so would a flat yes or no, as long as it's argued for.
