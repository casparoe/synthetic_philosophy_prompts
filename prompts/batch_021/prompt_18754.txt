I write about science and ideas for a national magazine, and partly on the strength of a piece I once did on Diane Vaughan's sociological account of the Challenger disaster, I've been invited to join the advisory committee for a mid-sized science museum's new permanent gallery on engineering failures. The committee has more or less finished the physical exhibits — the O-ring cross-sections, the photographs of ice on the launch tower, a reconstruction of Roger Boisjoly's overruled warnings the night before launch — but we've stalled for two meetings running on the wall text that has to explain, in a few hundred words, why the disaster actually happened. One faction, led by an organizational sociologist on the committee, wants the text built around Vaughan's argument that the disaster was the predictable output of a rule-bound bureaucracy that had gradually redefined a known anomaly into acceptable risk — a pattern she and others have since traced in the Columbia disaster and in industrial accidents with no connection to NASA at all — and they want visitors to leave recognizing the pattern in their own workplaces. A second faction, whose loudest members are a documentary producer and a retired flight director, thinks this turns a tragedy into a tidy case study and quietly lets the specific people involved off the hook; they want the text to stay close to the actual sequence of phone calls and reversed recommendations on the evening of January 27th, on the theory that this is simply what happened, and that the "pattern" is something imposed afterward by people who already know how the story ends. One committee member keeps saying the fight reminds her of a debate from a philosophy of science elective she took decades ago, something about general laws versus telling a story, but nobody can reconstruct the argument well enough to actually use it.

Fabrication of the panels begins in three weeks, so the museum director has asked me to draft the memo that will settle this before it goes to a vote. Could you write that memo for me? I'd like it to recommend a specific framing for the wall text rather than splitting the difference, to lay out the reasoning in a way the losing side could read without feeling either dismissed or patronized, and to say something substantive about whether explaining a disaster as an instance of a general, recurring pattern and explaining it as an unrepeatable sequence of particular choices are actually in the tension everyone on the committee assumes they are.
