I'm a retired evolutionary biologist (spent about thirty-five years mostly counting dung beetles, if you're curious) who's gotten pretty seriously into philosophy since retiring, largely through a little discussion group some of us run out of the local library. A few months back we worked through Rabinowicz and Ronnow-Rasmussen's paper with the great title 'The Strike of the Demon' — the one about fitting attitudes and value, featuring an evil demon who'll torture you unless you admire a perfectly worthless saucer of mud — and I got completely hooked on the 'wrong kind of reasons' problem that comes out of it. As an old biologist I can't help smirking every time someone talks about 'fitting' attitudes, given how loaded that word already is in my own field, but I gather that's just a coincidence of terminology and not actually relevant to anything.

Since then I've tried to track down as much of the follow-up literature as I can get my hands on through our university's alumni library access: Jonas Olson's proposal that a reason only counts as the right kind if its formulation makes no reference back to the attitude it's supposed to be a reason for, Pamela Hieronymi's paper arguing that the whole right/wrong-kind distinction doesn't even make proper sense once you're talking about actions rather than attitudes, Jonathan Way's argument that so-called wrong-kind reasons are really just reasons to want or bring about an attitude rather than reasons for having it, and Mark Schroeder's broadside against the standard 'object-given vs. state-given' story that most people use to draw the line. Honestly, at this point I'm loosing track of which of these the specialists actually still take seriously and which are considered basically dead on arrival.

So here's my ask: could you rank the major proposed solutions or response-strategies to the wrong kind of reasons problem (for fitting-attitude/buck-passing accounts of value specifically) by how successful you think they genuinely are at solving the problem, as opposed to just relabelling or dodging it? I'd like it as a JSON tier list, something along these lines: {"S": [...], "A": [...], "B": [...], "C": [...], "F": [...]}, with the most successful in S and the ones that basically don't work at all in F. If it's not too much bother, maybe add a half-sentence gloss for each entry on why it lands where it does. Doesn't need to be exhaustive — just the main contenders as you see the field.
