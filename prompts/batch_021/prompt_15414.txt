After forty years running a laboratory in evolutionary genetics, I retired three years ago and have since devoted a good part of my time to philosophy of science, a subject I had always found congenial but never had the leisure to study systematically. My reading recently took me through Karl Popper's remarks on Darwinism, and I confess to being rather unsettled by what I found there. In Unended Quest, his 1976 intellectual autobiography, and in related writings from the same period, Popper argues that the principle of natural selection, once fitness is defined in terms of survival and reproductive success, collapses into something close to a tautology, and he describes the general Darwinian framework not as a testable scientific theory in his own strict falsificationist sense but as a "metaphysical research programme." A few years later, in a lecture on natural selection and the emergence of mind that he delivered in the late 1970s, he announced what he explicitly called a recantation of his earlier position. Having now read both the original charge and the recantation with some care, I remain unsure how much actually changed: Popper still seems intent on separating the untestable, framework-like status of the general theory from the testability of the particular, singular hypotheses it generates, which sounds to my ear rather like a restatement of the original worry in gentler language.

I raised this over dinner with two philosopher friends, who promptly disagreed with each other and with me, so I would like a more disinterested opinion in essay form. Please address, in turn: whether Popper's original tautology and "metaphysical research programme" charge against Darwinism was justified by his own falsifiability criterion; whether his later recantation amounts to a substantive change of mind or is largely a change of emphasis that leaves the core worry intact; and how much weight should be given to the concern, voiced at the time by working biologists, that Popper's remarks - whatever their technical merit - handed unearned ammunition to creationist critics of evolution. Weigh the strongest considerations on both sides, drawing where relevant on the distinctions Popper relies on among singular existential statements, universal laws, and metaphysical research programmes, but please do not close on a note of pure ambivalence. I would like a definite verdict: on balance, is Popper's mature, post-recantation position defensible, or should the general theory of evolution by natural selection simply be regarded as falsifiable science that Popper was wrong ever to have doubted?
