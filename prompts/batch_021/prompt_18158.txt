I sit in on a philosophy-of-biology reading group that recently spent a session on Gould and Lewontin's 1979 paper attacking what they called the "Panglossian" adaptationist programme, and the discussion of spandrels ended up spilling over into a specific case Gould himself later popularized: female orgasm. Two members of the group, Nadia and Felix, kept arguing about it afterward over drinks, and neither managed to convince the other -- so I'm hoping you can sort out who had the better of it, which I couldn't.

Nadia opened by arguing that female orgasm is a textbook spandrel: because the clitoris develops from the same embryonic tissue as the penis, the neural and vascular equipment for orgasm comes along "for free" as a byproduct of shared developmental pathways, an idea originally due to Donald Symons and later endorsed by Gould as an extension of his and Lewontin's argument beyond simpler cases like male nipples. Felix objected that this move is too quick: identifying a developmental origin doesn't rule out that selection has since gone on to shape the trait for a job, and he pointed to the sperm-retention ("upsuck") research associated with Baker and Bellis, which linked orgasm timing to how much ejaculate gets retained, suggesting that whatever its origin, the trait's current form has been fine-tuned by selection. He added that "it's just a spandrel" can be exactly the sort of unfalsifiable, evidence-proof verdict that Gould and Lewontin accused adaptationists of reaching with their just-so stories, only running in the opposite direction. Nadia countered that the upsuck findings rest on small samples and indirect, self-reported measures that haven't held up well under later scrutiny -- she cited Elisabeth Lloyd's review as the most thorough case for this -- and that the byproduct account correctly predicts what adaptationist stories struggle with: how unreliably female orgasm is tied to intercourse itself, compared to the near-universal coupling between male orgasm and ejaculation you'd expect if selection had a specific job riding on it. She added that, unlike a just-so story, her account rests on an independently motivated mechanism (shared embryological origin) that actually predicts this pattern, rather than being invoked only because no adaptive story happens to be available. Felix's closing point was that the asymmetry Nadia was drawing is less clean than she thinks: adaptationist proposals like the one tying orgasm frequency to partner quality also rest on independently documented phenomena, and a trait being unreliable in one context (intercourse) doesn't preclude its having a real function tied to another (say, extended courtship or partner assessment) -- so, he suggested, her confidence that her explanation is principled while his is ad hoc might just be a preference for Gould's side of the larger debate, dressed up as methodology.
