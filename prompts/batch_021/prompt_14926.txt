A small reading group that grew out of some old rationalist meetup friends has been working through Hegel's Elements of the Philosophy of Right, and we've just reached the sections near the end of Part Three where he lays out his account of sovereignty and international law. A few pieces of the argument stood out to us: he insists there is no power standing over sovereign states the way a praetor or magistrate stands over quarreling citizens, so relations between states remain at the level of contingency and, absent agreement, can ultimately only be settled by force; he treats individuals' willingness to risk their own life and property for the state as itself ethically significant, not merely as an unfortunate cost of war, since it demonstrates the priority of the ethical whole over private particularity; and in one of the additions to the text he makes the striking claim that a sufficiently long peace lets nations go stagnant and corrupt, the way a becalmed sea turns foul, with periodic war doing for the ethical health of peoples what storms do for the sea. All of this is aimed pretty explicitly against Kant's proposal for perpetual peace secured through a league or federation of states, which Hegel argues can never fully escape contingency, since it would still rest on the separate and ultimately arbitrary wills of the member states.

I'd like your best reconstruction of how Hegel would react if he could see the last seventy-plus years of great-power peace maintained mainly through nuclear deterrence (something like the "Long Peace" that Steven Pinker and others describe), together with the broader shift away from mass citizen armies toward drone strikes, cyberattacks, and sanctions regimes carried out by small professionalized cadres rather than mobilized populations. Does a nuclear-armed peace look, on his own terms, like exactly the stagnating calm he worried about, or could mutual deterrence itself count as enough of a "negative," will-against-will relation between sovereign states to do the work he needed war to do, without any actual fighting? Given that his account of war seems to presuppose conflicts limited enough to preserve mutual recognition between sovereigns and the possibility of a future peace, would he treat weapons capable of annihilating whole societies as just a more intense version of the same phenomenon, or as breaking the concept of war he's actually describing? And separately, since so much of his argument leans on individual citizens directly risking life and property, does warfare waged mostly by remote pilots, intelligence services, and financial instruments, rather than through mass mobilization, drain war of the ethical significance he assigned to it, or would he see some functional equivalent operating at the level of the state as a whole? I'm less interested in the standard one-line gloss that "Hegel thought war was good for nations" than in an answer that works through the actual moving parts of his argument, and that flags which parts of the extrapolation follow fairly directly from his stated views versus which are more speculative.
