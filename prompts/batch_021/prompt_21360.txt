I've read enough Overcoming Bias and LessWrong material on causation to know the usual framing: Humean regularity theories pitted against causal-powers or necessitarian accounts, with the debate almost always presented as beginning with Hume's skepticism about necessary connection. But essentially the same fight, waged for very different stakes, took place five centuries earlier. In his discussion of causality in The Incoherence of the Philosophers, al-Ghazālī argued that the link between fire touching cotton and the cotton's burning is not a necessary connection grounded in the natures of things, but merely a customary one ('āda) that God could suspend at will — which is exactly what leaves room for miracles without logical contradiction. Ibn Rushd (Averroes), in his point-by-point rebuttal, countered that stripping causation of necessity this way dissolves the fixed natures that make science, definition, and even a word like "fire" coherent.

Write a dialogue between al-Ghazālī and Ibn Rushd — imagined as a direct exchange, since al-Ghazālī died fifteen years before Ibn Rushd was born — that works through the fire-and-cotton case in real depth. Neither should get the better of it too easily: Ibn Rushd should press al-Ghazālī on whether "custom" is doing any genuine explanatory work or simply relocates necessity onto God's will without actually eliminating it, and al-Ghazālī should press Ibn Rushd on how a world depending at every moment on a freely willing God could contain relations of genuine necessity rather than merely very reliable ones. Let the exchange end with the disagreement sharpened rather than resolved.
