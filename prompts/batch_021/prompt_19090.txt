I have been reporting on the philosophical assumptions embedded in Silicon Valley's various immortality projects — cryonics, "whole-brain emulation," the promise that a sufficiently detailed scan of a person's connectome could be run on different hardware and count as that person continuing to exist. While researching the piece, I kept returning to Kurt Gödel, less because anyone in that world cites him than because he seems like an unusually well-documented test case. Gödel defended, in letters to his mother Marianne and in the philosophical conversations later recorded by Hao Wang, a genuinely rationalist argument for an afterlife: roughly, that it would be inexplicable for a world as "rationally organized" as he took ours to be to develop a being with a wide field of intellectual and moral potential only to extinguish it before more than a fraction of that potential could ever be realized. At the same time, his 1951 Gibbs lecture defended the disjunction that either the human mind infinitely surpasses the power of any finite computing machine, or else there exist absolutely undecidable mathematical propositions of a certain kind — and by most secondhand accounts of his private views (again via Wang, and via Rudy Rucker's recollections of their conversations), he favored the first horn, insisting minds are not, in the relevant sense, machines.

That combination seems to pull in opposite directions once you confront it with something Gödel never had to consider directly: the proposal that a person might survive bodily death by being scanned and run as a computational process, or that this is precisely what an afterlife would have to look like if there is one. Would Gödel, on your best reconstruction, have welcomed mind-uploading as a vindication of his instinct that something about a person survives, or would his anti-mechanism have made him regard it as a kind of fraud — a simulation of a person's reasoning that preserves none of whatever it is that actually persists? I would also like your judgment on whether his unpublished ontological proof of God's existence, which he treated as serious philosophical work rather than a formal curiosity, gives him independent resources for rejecting a purely computational picture of survival even if he granted that a machine could reproduce human reasoning step for step. Please write this up as a careful, textually grounded reconstruction rather than a caricature, and flag explicitly where you are extrapolating beyond what Gödel is actually on record as having believed.
