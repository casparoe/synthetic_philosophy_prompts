I've spent a long time reading rationalist blogs and forums, where discussions of Bayesian epistemology like to invoke the claim that a rational agent should never expect cost-free evidence to leave them worse off in expectation, typically crediting this to I.J. Good's short 1967 note "On the Principle of Total Evidence," which proves the claim within L.J. Savage's decision-theoretic framework. The same corners of the internet also talk a great deal about "information hazards," cases where learning something seems to make you actively worse off, which always struck me as being in some tension with Good's result, even though I gather the standard resolution is that the theorem is a claim about expected utility computed ex ante, before any particular piece of news arrives, rather than about how any specific realized piece of information will feel once you have it. What I have never seen properly spelled out, and would like your help with, is how Good's theorem relates to the substantially earlier and seemingly far more general theorem due to David Blackwell on the comparison of statistical experiments, which gets invoked in the same breath in some of the more technical writing in this space, as though it were simply a fancier restatement of the same fact.

As I understand them: Good's theorem says that for a Bayesian agent with a fixed utility function and prior, facing one fixed decision problem, the expected utility of observing a cost-free experiment and then choosing the best action given whatever it reveals is never lower than the expected utility of choosing the best action without observing anything, which drops out of a short Jensen's-inequality argument about the convexity of the "best attainable expected utility, as a function of one's posterior" mapping. Blackwell's theorem, by contrast, defines one experiment as more informative than a second when the second's signal distribution can be reproduced by "garbling" the first, that is, by passing its output through some extra randomization that does not itself depend on the true state of the world, and then establishes that this garbling relation holds exactly when every possible decision-maker, with any action set, utility function, and prior whatsoever, would obtain at least as high an expected utility from the first experiment as from the second, across every decision problem they might face. This yields only a partial order over information structures, since most pairs of experiments cannot be ranked against one another in this all-decision-problems-at-once sense.

My tentative hunch, which I would like you to check, is that Good's theorem amounts to nothing more than the special case of Blackwell's ordering in which the "less informative" side of the comparison is a completely uninformative experiment, one whose output is statistically independent of the true state of the world; since any experiment can trivially be garbled down to such an experiment by simply discarding the signal and outputting noise, Blackwell's biconditional would then automatically deliver Good's conclusion for every decision problem, utility function, and prior at once, which is if anything a stronger statement than the one Good actually proved. If that reduction is correct, it would suggest that Good's short convexity argument only ever needs the logically "easy" half of Blackwell's biconditional, the direction running from garbling to universal preference, while the genuinely deep and hard-won content of Blackwell's theorem lies in the converse: that being universally weakly preferred across every conceivable decision problem is enough to force the existence of a garbling. I would like you to work through whether this reduction actually holds up, state explicitly which half of Blackwell's equivalence Good's argument is implicitly using, and then offer your own considered judgment on which of the two results should be treated as the more fundamental one for understanding why gathering evidence is nearly always instrumentally rational, and correspondingly, whether there remains any sense in which Good's theorem is genuinely special rather than a mere trivial corollary of Blackwell's much more general result.
