In my philosophy elective we covered the conditional analysis of dispositions (something is fragile, say, if and only if it would break if struck) and its two classic problem cases: finks, where the causal basis for the disposition itself switches on or off exactly when the stimulus hits (the textbook example is a live wire a sorcerer disconnects the instant before it's touched), and masks, where the basis stays completely intact but something separate blocks the outcome (a fragile vase wrapped in bubble wrap that never actually breaks). Two people in my class, Lena and Omar, got into an argument about which category a real case falls into. Lena is allergic to shellfish — untreated, it gives her hives — but she takes an antihistamine the moment she realizes she's eaten any, before symptoms start, so she's never actually broken out. Omar says this is a mask: the allergy itself is still fully there, and the antihistamine just blocks it from outside, like the bubble wrap. Lena disagrees: bubble wrap never touches the vase's molecular structure, but the antihistamine binds the very receptors that would otherwise trigger the swelling, so it is intervening inside the mechanism rather than blocking it externally, which sounds like a fink, not a mask. Omar replied that if reaching inside the mechanism is enough to make something a fink, then almost nothing would count as a mask, since any real blocker has to causally interact with the mechanism somewhere. Lena granted that, but said the burden was now on Omar to supply a better test, and neither of them found one. Could you go through their exchange point by point and say who has the better of it at each step? Then give your own verdict: is Lena's allergy a fink, a mask, or a case the fink/mask distinction doesn't cleanly sort — and if it's the latter, what's missing from how Lena and Omar were drawing the line?
