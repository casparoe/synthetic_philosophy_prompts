A friend of mine who's way too into quant trading keeps telling me the Kelly criterion isn't just a handy bet-sizing rule but actually the correct theory of rational choice under uncertainty, full stop -- he calls it "geometric rationality." His pitch (which I think he's borrowing from the ergodicity-economics crowd, and maybe some rationalist-blog sequence too) is that ordinary expected-value/expected-utility theory secretly assumes you can average outcomes across a huge ensemble of parallel copies of yourself, which isn't the situation any actual person is ever in, and that once you switch to averaging over time instead -- the way a real agent living through one long sequence of bets actually does -- maximizing expected growth rate (i.e. Kelly betting, maximizing expected log wealth) falls out as THE rational strategy, not just a strategy that happens to match one particular flavor of risk-aversion. I also vaguely remember there's some old, semi-famous takedown of this whole line of thinking -- Samuelson, I think -- and the joke is that its written entirely in one-syllable words, arguing that treating Kelly as a universal rule just smuggles in a specific utility function while pretending its utility-free.

I don't have the background to referee this myself, so can you write me an essay on it? Lay out the strongest version of the "geometric rationality" case for why Kelly-style reasoning should replace, or at least discipline, ordinary expected-value thinking, then lay out the best objections to that case, and then actually pick a side and defend it instead of landing on "well, both have some merit." If your honest answer is "it depends," that's fine, but only if you say exactly what it depends on -- spell out the conditions under which one framework should win out over the other, the way a decent doctor tells you "surgery only if the tumor is malignant" instead of just shrugging and saying every case is different.
