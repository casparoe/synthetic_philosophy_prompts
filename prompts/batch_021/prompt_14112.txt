I've spent a long time on rationalist blogs and forums, where the going view is that credences, not flat-out beliefs, are the real currency of epistemic life: you're supposed to carry around a number between 0 and 1 for everything, and "belief" is at best a convenient gloss for having a credence above some threshold. That picture ran into some friction for me recently when I found myself comparing Kyburg's lottery paradox with Makinson's preface paradox. In the lottery case, you seem justified in believing, of each ticket in a large fair lottery, that it will lose, and also justified in believing that some ticket will win — so the conjunction of all your "it will lose" beliefs contradicts a further belief you're equally entitled to. The preface case runs what looks like the same trick on an author: she is justified in believing each sentence of her book individually, yet, going by ordinary experience with long books and fallible authors, also justified in believing that the book contains at least one error somewhere. People in the threads I follow tend to treat these two cases as the same puzzle in different clothes, on the assumption that whatever move dissolves one paradox should dissolve the other.

I'm no longer sure that assumption survives scrutiny, and I'd like an actual verdict rather than another side-by-side restatement of the two puzzles. Please write an essay that weighs the case for giving the lottery and preface paradoxes one unified solution against the case for treating them asymmetrically — for instance, because the tickets in a lottery are interchangeable and probabilistically symmetric in a way the sentences of a book are not, or because it seems much harder to deny that the author "really" believes each sentence of her book than to deny that a ticket-holder "really" believes her particular ticket will lose. Weigh the leading strategies — rejecting agglomeration/conjunction closure across the board, rejecting the Lockean thesis that ties rational belief to a probability threshold, distinguishing belief from mere rational acceptance, or some other route — against each other as applied to both puzzles, and close by committing to a specific verdict on whether the two paradoxes really do call for the same medicine, with your reasons for landing there.
