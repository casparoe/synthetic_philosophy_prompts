I was recently called for jury duty, and the case I sat on turned entirely on which of two witnesses' conflicting testimony to believe, since there was no physical evidence either way. During deliberations, one juror argued that we had no business crediting anyone's testimony unless we had independent grounds for thinking that particular witness was reliable, while another insisted we should simply believe witnesses by default and discount them only if something specific gave us reason to doubt them. Curious afterward, I read that this mirrors a real dispute in epistemology between "reductionist" and "anti-reductionist" accounts of testimonial justification, traced back to Hume and Reid, respectively. What I can't work out is how the reductionist side avoids a circularity problem: the inductive track record it wants to cite in support of testimony's general reliability seems to be something I know about only through other people's testimony (records, textbooks, secondhand corroboration), not through any checking I have done myself. Could you walk me through the main ways reductionists have tried to answer this circularity worry, and why anti-reductionists don't consider those answers fully successful?
