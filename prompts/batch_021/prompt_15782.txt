Here is the answer as submitted, verbatim:

"The Duhem–Quine thesis begins from the observation that no scientific hypothesis is ever tested in isolation: deriving an observable prediction from a hypothesis always requires auxiliary assumptions about instruments, initial conditions, and background theory, so when a prediction fails we can always relocate the blame to one of the auxiliaries rather than to the hypothesis itself. Quine generalizes this into a holism about the entire web of belief: because our statements about the world face experience only as a corporate body and not individually, no single statement is ever forced on us by experience alone, since we can always choose instead to revise something else in the system, however central that revision turns out to be. From this holism, philosophers of science have extracted an underdetermination thesis: for any theory T compatible with the evidence, we can in principle construct a rival T*, incompatible with T, that is equally compatible with that evidence, simply by adjusting the auxiliaries attached to T* in whatever way is needed.

Laudan and Leplin's objection is that this result trades only on the bare logical possibility of cooking up an empirically equivalent rival, which is too cheap to be philosophically interesting. They make two points against it. First, empirical equivalence is not a fixed relation between two theories, since it depends on which auxiliary assumptions we happen to conjoin with them, and an alleged equivalence can be broken once some new connection to another part of our theoretical picture is noticed. Second, and more damagingly, even a genuine case of empirical equivalence would not establish that the two theories are equally well confirmed, since evidential support need not track shared observational consequences alone; considerations like unifying power or explanatory economy can favor one member of an empirically equivalent pair over the other. So the mere existence of a logically constructible rival does not show that theory choice is ever actually up for grabs.

Stanford thinks his unconceived alternatives argument survives this objection precisely because it is not a Duhem–Quine argument in disguise: it is an empirical, historical induction rather than an appeal to bare logical possibility. Looking across past episodes in the history of science, Stanford argues, we find a recurring pattern in which scientists confident in their best theory failed even to conceive of an empirically equivalent alternative that later investigators did hit upon, and that turned out to be at least as well confirmed by the evidence already available. Because these alternatives are drawn from the historical record rather than manufactured by algorithm, Stanford's rivals are not the sort of cheap logical trick Laudan and Leplin were objecting to; they were live, serious competitors that simply had not yet occurred to anyone. Stanford develops this most fully through the sequence of Ptolemaic, Copernican, and Keplerian astronomy, arguing that each generation's account of planetary motion was unable to anticipate the successor view, even though that successor was, on the evidence then available, just as well supported as the theory it would eventually replace.

I think this is a genuinely stronger position than the classical argument, since it forces us to take seriously actual, historically attested failures of scientific imagination rather than merely logically possible ones. My hesitation is that Stanford still needs the inductive step from 'past scientists routinely missed serious alternatives' to 'our present theories are probably in the same position,' and it isn't obvious this generalizes cleanly to a scientific community that is now far more self-conscious about, and better resourced for, the deliberate search for rival hypotheses than were Stanford's historical subjects."
