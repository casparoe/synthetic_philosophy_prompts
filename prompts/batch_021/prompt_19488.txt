After a career in conservation biology -- much of it spent trying to get population counts and trend data across to policymakers and the public -- I've used my retirement to read more widely in philosophy of science, and I now co-host a small podcast on the subject with two former colleagues. Next month's guest is a philosopher who works on Otto Neurath's Isotype picture-language project, and I don't want the conversation to settle for 'Neurath invented the infographic.' Please draft eight to ten questions that push into the substance of his position: at least one on the rule that a rise in quantity be shown by repeating an icon rather than enlarging it, and what exactly is supposed to go wrong, perceptually or epistemically, if you scale the icon instead; one on how Isotype's ambition for a wordless, universally legible sign system relates to -- or diverges from -- Wittgenstein's picture theory of the proposition, given how differently the two projects use the word 'picture'; one on whether a system designed for audiences with little training in reading tables, graphs, and numbers still holds up now that pictorial infographics are everywhere; and one on whether a small, fixed stock of standardized signs sits comfortably with Neurath's own anti-foundationalist image of knowledge as a boat that can never be rebuilt from dry dock. For each, note the answer he's most likely to give and add a follow-up that keeps the pressure on.
