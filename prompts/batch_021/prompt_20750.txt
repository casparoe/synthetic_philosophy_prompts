I've never studied philosophy or mathematics formally, but for the past year or so I've been teaching myself the basics of subjective probability, starting from an interest in how professional bettors and bookmakers set odds and working backward from there into some Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy articles and a couple of secondhand decision theory books. A few people I know from an online forum devoted to this stuff have a standing video call every few weeks to talk through what we've each been reading, and at the last one, two of them — I'll call them Nadia and Owen — spent almost the whole session arguing about Dutch book arguments. I wrote up the exchange afterward because I wanted to work through it properly myself, but I don't know the material well enough to judge who was actually right at each stage, so I'm hoping you can help.

Here, as best I can reconstruct it, is how the argument went. (1) Nadia opened with the classic argument: if your credences over some set of mutually exclusive, exhaustive outcomes don't obey the probability axioms, a bookmaker can offer you a set of bets that you would each judge fair or better given your own credences, yet which together are guaranteed to lose you money no matter what happens — which she took to show that incoherent credences are genuinely irrational, not just unlucky. (2) Owen objected that this only shows you're exploitable if a bookmaker who happens to know your odds actually shows up and builds the right combination; absent that, or if you simply decline to bet, nothing bad happens, so at most this establishes a conditional practical danger, not that the credences themselves are irrational. (3) Nadia answered that the money is really beside the point: the construction shows you rate every bet in the set as acceptable by your own lights while the set as a whole is certain to cost you, and that mismatch is already an inconsistency among your evaluations whether or not anyone ever collects on it — much as affirming several claims while rejecting their conjunction is inconsistent whether or not you're ever made to act on it. (4) Owen replied that this move only works if treating a bet as "fair" is already a reliable stand-in for your actual credence, which he didn't think she'd earned the right to assume, and added that the more ambitious use of this kind of argument — to show you're rationally required to update by conditionalizing on new evidence — needs the bookmaker to know your entire future updating policy in advance, a far less innocent assumption than anything the one-off version needs. (5) Nadia countered that there's a converse result that settles the main question regardless: if your credences do satisfy the axioms, no bookmaker, however clever, can build a sure-loss combination against you, and this two-way fit between coherence and safety is exactly the clean boundary showing the axioms are the right constraint on rational credence. (6) Owen answered that the converse result only shows coherence keeps you safe from this one betting game, not that your credences are otherwise reasonable — a wildly miscalibrated but internally coherent credence (near-certainty in something absurd, say) is just as immune to a Dutch book as a sensible one, so even granting Nadia everything, the whole framework would be settling a narrow, formal question rather than the broader one about rational belief.

Neither of them budged, and the call ended with both a little annoyed. Could you go through these six moves one at a time and say, for each, whether it's basically correct, correct as far as it goes but overstated or incomplete, or actually mistaken — with a couple of sentences justifying each verdict? I'd also like to know whether there's some consideration neither of them raised that actually bears on who's right here. A brief closing line on who had the better of the overall exchange would be welcome, but the point-by-point assessment is what I'm really after.
