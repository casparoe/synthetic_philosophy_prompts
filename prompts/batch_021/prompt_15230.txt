My sister is finishing a philosophy PhD on money-pump arguments and what she calls 'diachronic rationality' -- the idea that being rational isn't just about the choice in front of you, but about not being predictably exploitable across a whole sequence of choices over time. She's getting married in three weeks, and I've been asked to give the toast. I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training beyond a decade of her explaining this material at family dinners, but I've absorbed the basics: an agent with cyclical preferences, or one who keeps revising a plan whenever the next trade looks marginally better, can be led in a circle and drained dry one 'rational' step at a time -- the money pump. She also distinguishes three ways an agent can handle its own future choices: naively, not seeing the exploit coming; sophisticatedly, seeing it coming and planning around a future self it doesn't trust; or resolutely, carrying out a plan made in full knowledge that a later self might prefer to abandon it. Around half the guests are people from her department who will recognize all of this immediately; the rest are relatives who just want a nice toast about two people in love. Please write a five-minute speech that uses that three-way distinction to say something honest about the vows they are about to make, without collapsing into 'follow your heart, always' -- which, given what she has spent five years arguing, sounds much more like the naive agent's mistake than a compliment -- and without turning into a seminar that leaves the non-philosophers behind.
