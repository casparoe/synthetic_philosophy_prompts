I've been falling down a bit of a rabbit hole with Pascal's Pensées this week for a piece I'm sketching out on the history of "might makes right" style arguments, and I keep getting stuck on the fragments where he talks about justice and force — the famous "justice without force is powerless, force without justice is tyrannical" line, plus that other bit (lifted almost wholesale from Montaigne) about how the "mystical foundation" of a law's authority can't survive being examined too closely, so the wise just don't look too hard. Read one way this makes Pascal sound like a total cynic about justice, basically a 17th-century Thrasymachus who thinks "justice" just IS whatever force has managed to entrench itself as custom, and all the talk of fairness is theatre for the masses. But then there's this other strand running through the Pensées too — the "raison des effets" material about le peuple, the "demi-habiles" who see through the theatre and get dangerously subversive about it, and the truly wise who circle all the way back round to obeying the exact same customs as the naive peuple, but with a totally different "pensée de derrière" sitting behind it. That seems to gesture at Pascal actually beliving there IS a real justice, we just can't access it with unaided reason after the Fall, so force-backed custom ends up being more of a stopgap for a broken world than the whole truth.

My editor wants a sidebar on "what Pascal actually thought" instead of just the wager stuff everyone's already read a hundred times, so could you write me an essay that takes an actual stand on this? Walk through the case for reading Pascal as a genuine cynic / proto-Hobbesian about justice (it reduces to force, full stop, no residue) versus the case for reading him as more of an Augustinian fideist (real justice exists but is closed off to reason, force is a fallen-world substitute rather than a replacement), leaning on the specific bits of the Pensées that pull in each direction. Don't just leave it at "both readings have merit" — I want you to actually land somewhere and defend it. If it's useful you can gesture at how later readers (Hobbes, Nietzsche, even Derrida's riff on that "mystical foundation" line) each seem to grab onto one half of Pascal and run with it, but that's optional garnish, not the main course.
