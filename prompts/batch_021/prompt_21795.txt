Ok so this is a weird one but I think it's an actual material-constitution problem that landed in my real life, not just a seminar reading. This semester I'm in a metaphysics course that just spent two weeks on the statue and the clay -- Gibbard's Lumpl and Goliath, the whole "the clay can survive being squashed but the statue can't, so by Leibniz's Law they're not identical, but then how are there two objects in the same place made of the same stuff" thing -- and completely separately I'm the student rep on my university's Art Collections Committee, where a case just came in that's basically that thought experiment with a lawyer attached. We have an unfired clay maquette by a mid-century sculptor, given to the university on the condition (per the gift agreement) that it stay "substantially unaltered" and remain on display as "an original work," or else it reverts to the sculptor's estate. Two summers ago a pipe burst in the storage annex over a long weekend and the thing basically slaked down into a shapeless mound of wet clay before anyone found it. Our conservator, using old photographs and a 3D scan that had been taken for the digital archive, reworked the reclaimed clay back into the figure's exact original form, and its been back on the wall ever since like nothing happened.

Now the estate's lawyer has found out and sent a letter: their position is that the original was destroyed the moment it lost its form -- the artist's actual hand-worked surface is gone even though the shape is identical -- so what's on the wall is a reconstruction, not the original, meaning the university breached the gift agreement and the piece should revert to them, or at least be relabeled. Our conservator's position is that it's literally the same clay, reshaped to the same form off the same references, and that this is just normal restoration practice, full stop. Our insurer is seperately asking whether the flood counts as a "total loss" event that should've paid out years ago regardless of how good the reshaping turned out. The Provost wants the committee's recommendation memo before next week's meeting since the lawyer's letter has everyone worried about actual litigation. Could you draft that memo for me? I want a real recommendation -- basically, do we maintain this is still the same maquette that's always been in the collection, or concede it's a new object that just resembles the old one, and what that implies for the reversion clause and the insurance question -- with concrete next steps, not just a summary of both sides. But please take the estate's argument seriously as a genuine metaphysical position rather than waving it away with "restoration happens all the time," because I think that's actually the hard part here.
