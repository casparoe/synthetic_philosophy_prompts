In my upper-level metaphysics seminar we've been working through Max Black's thought experiment of a universe containing nothing but two qualitatively identical iron spheres, offered as a counterexample to Leibniz's Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles. This week's reading grants that the spheres share every intrinsic property, and that no asymmetric relation distinguishes them either (neither is larger than, or to the north of, the other), yet still maintains that the Principle survives because the spheres are "weakly discernible": each bears an irreflexive relation to the other — say, being some nonzero distance from it — that it does not bear to itself, and an irreflexive relation cannot, on pain of contradiction, hold of a single thing, so its holding here guarantees that there are two things rather than one. I can't shake the suspicion that this is a sleight of hand rather than a genuine solution: to describe the relation as holding between the spheres, rather than reflexively within a single sphere, already seems to assume there are two of them, which is exactly the point at issue. Please argue that the appeal to weak discernibility does not actually rescue the Identity of Indiscernibles from Black's spheres, because it presupposes, rather than establishes, the very numerical diversity it is meant to explain.
