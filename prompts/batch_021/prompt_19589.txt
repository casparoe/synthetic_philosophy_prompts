I covered a public lecture on multiverse cosmology last month for a piece on how physicists have quietly borrowed a term that actually originates with Leibniz — "possible worlds" — and during the Q&A someone raised his old claim that ours is the best of them. Afterward, at the reception, two of the invited commentators kept arguing about it, and I wrote down the exchange as faithfully as I could. Elena works on philosophy of religion; Marcus on modal metaphysics.

1. Elena opened with the standard complaint: the thesis is refuted many times over by history — the Lisbon earthquake, the Holocaust, childhood cancer. A being that is omnipotent, omniscient, and perfectly good, and could have made things even slightly better, cannot be described as having produced the best possible world.

2. Marcus replied that this misreads the actual argument: nothing in it requires that individual evils be removable at no cost elsewhere. Because not every logically possible state of affairs is jointly realizable with every other — "compossible," in Leibniz's term — deleting one evil might mean losing an entire web of connected goods; you cannot pull one thread without unraveling the pattern.

3. Elena said that move saves the thesis only by making it untestable: whatever horror you name, Marcus can always say its removal would have cost more elsewhere. A claim immune to every conceivable counterexample isn't an explanation, it's an escape hatch.

4. Marcus answered that "falsifiable" is the wrong standard to apply in the first place: Leibniz isn't offering an empirical hypothesis but a metaphysical conclusion drawn from the principle of sufficient reason plus divine perfection — no more appropriately tested by observation than a geometric proof.

5. Elena granted that, but pressed further: if the principle of sufficient reason guarantees that a perfectly wise, perfectly good being must select the uniquely best option, in what sense were the alternatives ever really possible? "Best of all possible worlds" implies genuine competitors that lost; what Marcus was describing sounded like the only possible world — the very necessitarianism, verging on Spinozism, that Leibniz spent his career denying.

6. Marcus said Leibniz has a standing reply: the rejected worlds remain possible in themselves, since their descriptions involve no contradiction; God's choosing the best is only morally necessary, grounded in his wisdom and goodness, and not necessary in the strict, logical sense that a contradiction would be. Whether that distinction really earns the contingency it's supposed to buy, he admitted, is exactly what's been disputed since Leibniz's own contemporaries first suspected him of covert Spinozism.

Neither of them would budge, and I honestly couldn't tell who came out ahead. Could you go through the six moves and say, for each, whether the point actually lands, is only partly right, or is a dodge, and why — then tell me, on balance, whether Marcus's distinction in move 6 does genuine work against Elena's challenge in move 5, or merely relabels it, and whether there's a consideration neither of them raised that should bear on the question.
