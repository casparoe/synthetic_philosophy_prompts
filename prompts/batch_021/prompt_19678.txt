Kant holds that we can have no direct duties to animals, since duties in the strict sense presuppose a capacity for rational self-legislation that only persons possess; our obligations regarding the treatment of animals are therefore merely indirect, resting on the thought that cruelty toward them erodes a natural sympathy we need in order to fulfill our direct duties toward other human beings. Kant's discussion, in the Lectures on Ethics, of the aging dog that has served its owner faithfully and might be owed something like gratitude is the classic illustration of how far such indirect concern can extend. Christine Korsgaard, in her Kantian treatment of our obligations to other animals, argues that this indirect-duty view rests on a conflation, within Kant's own account of what makes rational beings "ends in themselves," of two distinct grounds for that status — and that once these grounds are properly disentangled, direct duties to sentient animals follow from premises Kant himself accepts. Compare Kant's original position with Korsgaard's revisionary argument. Does Korsgaard succeed in deriving direct duties to animals from genuinely Kantian premises, or does her argument tacitly abandon what is most distinctive about Kant's grounding of moral status in rational agency? Justify your answer.
