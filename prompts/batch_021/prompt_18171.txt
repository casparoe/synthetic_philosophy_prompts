For a graduate seminar on classical Indian epistemology, write a short dialogue between a Naiyāyika philosopher and a Buddhist epistemologist working in the tradition of Dignāga and Dharmakīrti. Stage it as a formal vāda, the truth-directed style of debate that the Nyāya-sūtra itself distinguishes from eristic wrangling (jalpa). The Naiyāyika should defend śabda — knowledge arising from the testimony of a trustworthy speaker (āpta) — as a pramāṇa in its own right, irreducible to anumāna. The Buddhist should argue that accepting testimony is at bottom a species of inference: understanding what a trustworthy speaker's words signify, and trusting that what they signify is true, are both mediated by inferential connections (between word and intended meaning, and between the speaker's known reliability and the claim's truth), so that no separate, fourth pramāṇa is needed. Have the two press each other on whether this "inference" really satisfies the same condition that anumāna satisfies in paradigm cases like inferring fire from smoke — a grasped and examined vyāpti linking sign to signified — and on whether the Naiyāyika can specify what is distinctive about verbal cognition without covertly relying on something inference-like. Neither side should simply restate its position: each should concede exactly what the other's strongest point forces it to concede.
