I spent thirty-five years as an experimental physicist working on precision tests of general relativity, and since retiring I have been sitting in on a philosophy of physics reading group that the local university's philosophy department kindly lets a few of us retirees attend alongside its graduate students. Two sessions ago the group worked through the hole argument: the old worry, revived by Earman and Norton, that if the points of the spacetime manifold are substances with an identity of their own, then a diffeomorphism that acts as the identity everywhere except inside some matter-free "hole" region generates two solutions of Einstein's field equations that agree on every physical fact outside the hole yet disagree, point by point, on what happens inside it -- so that fixing the entire state of the universe up to the hole's boundary still leaves the metric's fate within it radically undetermined. The argument itself was not hard to follow, coming from physics, but the disagreement it produced between two of the regulars afterward, over coffee, is the part I have not been able to adjudicate on my own.

(1) Marcus, who wanted to draw an anti-substantivalist moral, said the argument is close to decisive against treating spacetime points as substances with primitive identity: since general covariance guarantees that both diffeomorphic models are equally good solutions, no specification of matter and metric outside the hole, however complete, determines which point ends up with which metrical properties inside it, and an indeterminism that traces entirely to bare identity facts about points, rather than to anything physical, amounts to a reductio of the view that generates it. (2) Elena replied that this only refutes a naive "manifold substantivalism": a sophisticated substantivalist can accept what is sometimes called Leibniz equivalence and hold that the two diffeomorphic models are not rival possibilities at all but a single possibility described twice, the way two coordinate charts describe one situation twice; substantivalists keep spacetime points as real entities, they simply give up the idea that a point's identity floats free of the metrical role it plays, so the indeterminism never arises. (3) Marcus responded that this move was tailored solely to block the argument, and that it costs the view everything that made it substantivalism in the first place: if a point simply is whatever plays a given metrical role, with no further fact about which point it is, then the claim that spacetime points exist independently of the metric has been quietly emptied of content, leaving relationalism in substantivalist dress. (4) Elena pushed back that the charge of ad-hocness is unfair, since treating diffeomorphic models as physically equivalent is exactly how physicists already treat gauge freedom elsewhere -- coordinate transformations, Yang-Mills symmetries -- so the substantivalist is only extending an independently motivated policy to this case, not inventing a special exemption; she added that relationalists owe their own account of which relational configurations count as "the same" possibility, so denying points a primitive identity does not by itself erase what is distinctive about holding that the metric is a field on a substratum, which relationalism denies outright.
