I'm a third-year philosophy major currently taking a seminar on the philosophy of language whose last few weeks have been devoted to the semantics and pragmatics of slurs, and, half by coincidence, this term I was also elected the student representative on my university's Hearing Board, the joint faculty-student panel that adjudicates conduct complaints referred up from the Dean of Students' office. We've just been handed a case that the office couldn't resolve administratively, and I keep noticing how closely it tracks the debate we've been reading for class, especially Anderson and Lepore's "prohibitionist" idea that slurs are simply taboo words whose offensiveness survives negation and reported speech in a way that's hard to square with the more familiar rival views on which the offense is carried by some special content the word expresses or implicates.

Here is the case. A linguistics professor, lecturing on exactly this debate, wrote out a specific racial slur on the whiteboard rather than substituting a euphemism like "the n-word," in order to walk her class through the fact that a sentence containing the word remains offensive even once it is embedded under negation or reported in indirect speech, one of the central data points any theory in this area has to explain. A student in the room, who belongs to the group the slur targets, filed a complaint arguing that the professor violated the conduct code's ban on "displaying language that a reasonable person would find demeaning toward a protected group," and that it makes no difference that she was mentioning rather than using the word, since on the student's view the injury that seeing or hearing it produces is the same either way. The professor's defense is that a euphemism would have begged the very question under discussion: part of her point was that swapping in "the n-word" changes the linguistic data itself, since the substitute phrase doesn't reliably produce the same intuitions of residual offense on which the debate she was teaching actually turns. Our conduct code exempts "legitimate pedagogical, scholarly, or artistic purposes" from the general ban on demeaning language but says nothing about what makes a purpose legitimate or how that exemption is supposed to interact with the mention/use distinction, and the Board is currently split down the middle.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate to the rest of the Board ahead of our meeting next Tuesday, addressed to the Dean of Students. It should recommend one concrete resolution — dismissing the complaint, upholding it with a specific sanction, or some intermediate outcome such as a required statement without formal discipline — propose a standard the Board could actually apply to future cases of this general shape, and take the complainant's position seriously on its own terms rather than treating it as a simple failure to grasp the mention/use distinction.
