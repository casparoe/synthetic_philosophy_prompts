So I'm poking around for a piece I'm working on about trust and misinformation — basically, when is it actually rational to believe something purely because someone told you, rather than because you checked it yourself — and it's sent me down an epistemology rabbit hole. I reread the bit in Hume where he argues testimony only carries evidential weight because we've built up, through our own experience, a track record of testimony lining up with reality (so its really just parasitic on induction), and then Reid's reply, where he basically says thats backwards: we've got a built-in, non-inferential disposition to trust what people tell us (the "principle of credulity," matched by a "principle of veracity" on the speaker's end), so testimony is its own basic source of knowledge, on par with perception.

Then a philosopher I follow mentioned, kind of offhand, that classical Nyaya epistemology in India had a version of this exact fight over two thousand years earlier. The Naiyayikas count sabda (verbal testimony) as one of only four legitimate pramanas — the recognized sources of knowledge — alongside perception, inference and comparison, while the Buddhist epistemologists in the Dignaga/Dharmakirti lineage rejected that and tried to reduce testimony down to a kind of inference from the speaker's reliability. I don't know this material well enough to place it on the Hume/Reid map myself, especially once you dig into how Nyaya actually defines an "apta" (their term for a speaker whose say-so counts as a genuine source of knowledge) and what has to go right, causally, for a sentence to generate real knowledge instead of just a true guess.

Could you write up an actual comparison of the Nyaya account of testimonial knowledge against the Hume-vs-Reid debate, just for my own understanding (not for publication, so don't worry about making it punchy, just get it right)? Mainly I want to know: does Nyaya's view count as anti-reductionist in Reid's sense, or does it collapse back into something closer to Hume once you notice it builds reliability conditions right into the definition of an apta, or is it doing something else again — more like a reliabilist theory of knowledge generally, judged by whether the causal process worked rather than by any principle about trust as such? Whichever reading you land on, justify it, and also tell me which of the two traditions handles the classic Gettier-ish worry more convincingly: a generally trustworthy person telling you something true completely by accident, for reasons that were themselves mistaken. Does either framework actually block that case from counting as knowledge, or do they both let it slide through?
