I've been chewing on this for weeks and it turns out I basically reinvented a real philosophical position before I even knew it existed. The setup is an actual thought experiment from a guy named Taurek who wrote about it back in the 70s: you've got one dose of a drug, enough to completely cure one person -- call him David -- or, split into fifths, enough to cure five totally unrelated strangers instead, not both. Everyone's gut reaction is "obviously save the five, 5 is more than 1," but Taurek's whole point is that theres no single person anywhere for whom loosing the five is a bigger loss than loosing David -- it's only ever a loss to each of them on their own, so adding up harms across different people and saving whichever side's pile is bigger is kind of a category mistake. He even says you could just flip a coin instead of automatically going with the five. Apparently a bunch of philosophers (Parfit included) piled on afterward to defend numbers counting.

Can you write me a dialogue that happens right at the moment of decision, drug in hand, between someone arguing Taurek's actual position and someone who's convinced the numbers have to matter? Don't let the "numbers" person get away with just repeating 5>1 -- make them really engage with that "no one for whom it's worse" move and the separateness-of-persons idea, and have them push the Taurek guy on whether he'd seriously flip a coin if it were David against a thousand people instead of five. I want real back-and-forth where both sides land actual points on each other, nobody strawmanned, and it doesn't need to end with either side clearly winning.
