Over the past few months I've started volunteering several evenings a week in the intake ward of my local animal shelter, mostly helping the vet techs clean wounds, change dressings, and hold down frightened strays. It's brought me closer to animal distress than I've ever been, and it's left me thinking about something I never had to take a position on before — especially now that I also have a rescue dog of my own at home, who likewise can't tell me in words when something is wrong.

During a recent shift we were cleaning a badly infected wound on a stray cat who howled, thrashed, and kept trying to bite throughout the whole procedure. Afterward I asked the staff veterinarian, someone I respect enormously, whether it ever gets to her how much pain the animals seem to be in. She said, in effect, that I shouldn't be too quick to equate the yelping and thrashing with pain in the sense I mean it. A sufficiently complicated machine, she pointed out, could produce exactly the same withdrawal-and-vocalization pattern in response to tissue damage without there being anything that experiences it; a smoke detector reacts to smoke without being alarmed. For her, real suffering requires more than a mechanism reacting appropriately to damage — it requires a subject for the suffering to belong to — and since the cat has no language with which to tell us anything about its condition, she doesn't think we're entitled to assume there's a "someone in there" for the pain to happen to, however much the outward performance resembles ours.

I don't have any background in philosophy or biology, so I have no way of judging whether this is a serious position or just a convenient rationalization. What makes it harder to dismiss is that she doesn't act like someone who actually believes it in practice: she's meticulous about minimizing procedures and dosing analgesics generously "just in case." I'd like to know whether there's a genuine, defensible argument behind what she told me, or whether it depends on some sleight of hand — and, if the question of what, if anything, is "going on" behind an animal's reactions really can't be settled from the outside, what that uncertainty should mean for how I treat the animals I handle every week.
