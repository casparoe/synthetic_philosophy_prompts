Me and my friend (she's mid-PhD in a stats-heavy field, I'm faculty in a social psych department) had this two-hour argument over dinner last week that ended with both of us pulling up old papers on our phones at the table, which probably means we need better hobbies. It started because I was complaining about how long our studies take now that we're required to fix sample sizes in advance and preregister everything. She said something like "just switch to reporting Bayes factors, then you can peek at the data as it comes in and stop whenever you want, because a Bayesian doesn't care about your stopping rule, only frequentists do" -- some argument about the likelihood principle meaning the evidential meaning of your data doesn't depend on why you stopped collecting it. I pushed back that this sounded suspiciously like the exact data-peeking that helped get social psych into the replication crisis in the first place, just repackaged with different math, and she insisted no, there's a real asymmetry: optional stopping provably inflates the false-positive rate under a p-value framework in a way it doesn't (or doesn't nearly as badly) under a Bayesian one.

Turns out this wasn't just a dinner argument, because I'm on my department's research-methods and data-integrity committee, and two of our more Bayesian-leaning faculty just formally proposed that we let grad students use optional-stopping designs for thesis studies as long as they report a Bayes factor with a preregistered prior instead of a p-value, mainly to save on subject-recruitment costs. Roughly half the committee thinks this quietly reopens the door to p-hacking under new branding, especially since a couple of our own faculty's studies famously failed to replicate a few years back. I have to circulate a short memo before we vote on this next Wednesday, and I'd like your help drafting it. Can you lay out the actual disagreement my friend and I were having -- why optional stopping is or isn't a problem depending on which framework you're in, and what the likelihood principle has to do with it -- and then have the memo land on an actual recommendation (approve, reject, or approve with specific conditions)? I definately don't want it to just wave off the Bayes-factor argument with "sounds like p-hacking to us" -- the two faculty proposing this are sharp and deserve a memo that engages with their strongest version of the argument, even if it ultimately says no.
