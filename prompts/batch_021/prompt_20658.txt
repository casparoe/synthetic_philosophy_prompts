I've been working through Moore's *Principia Ethica* for a reading group and think I've found a hole in his argument, or else im missing something. At one point he has you imagine two completely lonely worlds, one impossibly beautiful and one incredibly ugly, with literally no mind of any kind ever around to perceive either one, and he says it would obviously be better for the beautiful world to exist -- the whole point being that beauty has real intrinsic value all on its own, totally apart from anyone ever enjoying it. Ok, fine. But then later in the same book he goes and says that personal affection and the appreciation of beauty (i.e., basically, loving your friends and enjoying art/nature) are the two greatest goods a person can have, way more important than just having good things exist "out there" unnoticed.

That seems backwards to me though? If an unwitnessed beautiful world already has pretty much all the value its ever going to have without anyone around to appreciate it, doesn't that mean the "appreciating" part isn't doing much real work value-wise -- like it's just a nice bonus, not one of the two most important things in existence? So shouldn't his own thought experiment actually commit him to saying the beauty (or a friend's good character) is basically where all the value lives, full stop, and whether anyone loves or notices it is secondary? Feels like he wants to have it both ways, unless I'm missing how these two bits of the argument are actually supposed to fit together. Is this a known tension in Principia Ethica, or am I just misreading him?
