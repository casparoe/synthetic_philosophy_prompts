I've spent a long time reading rationalist blogs and forums, and one idea that comes up again and again -- going back to Eliezer Yudkowsky's old "A Human's Guide to Words" sequence -- is that many seemingly deep disagreements dissolve once you notice that the two sides are just attaching different meanings to the same word, so that there's no remaining question except which words to use. I recently learned that there's a much more rigorous version of this idea in academic metaontology: Eli Hirsch's thesis of "quantifier variance," which holds that expressions like "there exists" or "there is" admit of several equally good candidate meanings, so that many disputes in ontology turn out to be merely verbal once each side is charitably interpreted as speaking truly in its own idiom. Ted Sider's response, developed most fully in Writing the Book of the World, is that this proves too much: on his view there is an objectively best, "joint-carving" candidate meaning for the quantifiers -- what Sider calls "Ontologese" -- obtained by extending David Lewis's notion of naturalness from properties to logical structure itself, so that a further, substantive question survives even after both sides have been charitably interpreted as speaking truly.

I'd like to see this debate actually worked through rather than summarized in the usual textbook paragraph, so I'd like you to write me a dialogue between a committed quantifier-variance theorist in Hirsch's mold and a committed joint-carving realist in Sider's mold, arguing about a concrete test case: the dispute between mereological universalists, who hold that any objects whatsoever -- however scattered or gerrymandered -- compose some further object, and mereological nihilists, who hold that composition never occurs and that only simples exist. The Hirschian character should try to show, using the resources of charitable interpretation, how the universalist's and the nihilist's assertions can both be read as true in their own languages, and should argue that once this is done there is nothing substantive left to disagree about; the Siderian character should resist this, and must explain what could make one candidate meaning more joint-carving than another without simply helping themselves to the notion. Both characters are meant to be equally sharp: each should have to face the standard objection to their own side (the quantifier variantist's charity-based criterion threatens to trivialize even disputes Hirsch wants to keep substantive, while the realist's "joint-carving" can look like an unearned metaphysical primitive) and should respond to what the other has actually just said rather than restating their opening position. Something like twelve to eighteen exchanges, formatted as a script with clear speaker labels, would be ideal.
