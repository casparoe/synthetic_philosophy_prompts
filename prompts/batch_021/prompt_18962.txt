i'm taking a philosophy of science seminar this semester and this week we covered structural realism (the Worrall "best of both worlds" stuff, with the classic Fresnel-to-Maxwell switcheroo as the go to example of structure surviving theory change). toward the end of class my professor brought up this old objection from a mathematician named Newman, from a review he wrote of Russell's book back in the late 1920s. the gist, as i understood it: if structural realism just amounts to saying "the world has some structure isomorphic to what our best theory says," that claim is basically guaranteed to be true for any big enough collection of objects, since you can always cook up some relation on the domain that makes the isomorphism hold. so the whole "we can only know the structure, not the nature of the relata" thesis collapses into a boring fact about cardinality, not a real claim about the world.

everyone in the seminar seemed to treat this as a total knockdown that should've killed structural realism right there in 1928, and thought it was kind of embarrassing that Worrall, Ladyman, French, etc. kept writing papers defending fancier and fancier versions of it decades later instead of just admitting defeat. since Newmans argument seems to show the central thesis of structural realism is empty, shouldn't those philosophers have just gone back to regular scientific realism (or full on instrumentalism) instead of inventing stuff like "ontic" structural realism to dodge the problem? i guess what im asking is why this is still treated as a live research program instead of a historical dead end that everyone's too polite to mention.
