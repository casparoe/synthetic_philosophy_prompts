I recently began a habit-tracking experiment modeled on Benjamin Franklin's "project of arriving at moral perfection": thirteen virtues, one tracked per week in a small grid, just as he describes in his autobiography. Mentioning this at dinner set off a debate between two friends, Nadia and Grant, that I couldn't referee.

(1) Grant said the method obviously works, citing Franklin's own verdict: though he "fell far short" of the perfection he aimed at, the effort still left him "a better and a happier man" than he'd otherwise have been -- the way copying a handwriting model improves your hand even though you never quite match it.

(2) Nadia said that holds for a virtue like temperance, but collapses for humility specifically. Franklin added it as a thirteenth virtue only after a Quaker friend told him he was widely thought proud and overbearing, and he later calls pride the hardest passion to subdue, joking that even if he'd fully conquered it, he'd probably just be, in his own words, "proud of my humility." Tracking your own progress on a chart, she said, requires exactly the self-congratulation the virtue forbids.

(3) Grant replied that Franklin saw this coming: he separates the "reality" of humility, which he admits he barely achieved, from its "appearance," which he cultivated deliberately by dropping flat contradiction and words like "certainly" -- and credits that habit with decades of unusual influence over people. So the chart delivered something real, even if not what it advertised.

(4) Nadia answered that this only sharpens her point: Franklin's own moral from his story about the man who wanted an axe ground fully bright and settled for one merely "speckled" is that a benevolent man should allow himself a few faults "to keep his friends in countenance." If that's Franklin's own conclusion, she said, then selling the chart as a route to moral perfection oversells what even he thought it could do.

Please go through this move by move -- who has the better of it at each point, and why, not just who wins overall -- and flag anything about Franklin's actual text that both of them seem to be missing. End with a one-line verdict on which of the two is closer to right, overall.
