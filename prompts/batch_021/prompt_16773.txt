Ok this has been bugging me since my metaphysics of time seminar last week, hoping you can help me actually think it through instead of just nodding along like I did in class. We spent a whole session on the Rietdijk–Putnam argument (from Putnam's old "Time and Physical Geometry" paper, plus the Andromeda paradox version Penrose does in his popular books) and I feel like the whole seminar basically walked away treating it as having settled the presentism vs eternalism debate in eternalism's favor, full stop. Quick recap of the argument as we went through it: take two observers who happen to pass right by each other at some point while moving at different velocities relative to each other. Because of relativity of simultaneity, each observer's plane of "now" picks out a different set of distant events as present. Since neither frame is privileged, and since whatever counts as "now" for an observer has to be granted as fully real (thats sort of the whole point of being a presentist about the present), you end up forced into saying all these different, incompatible sets of "present" events are equally real - which is more or less just eternalism by definition.

So my actual question: is this basically a knockdown argument? Like, has SR essentially made presentism empirically false at this point, so that people who still defend it are just kind of in denial of established physics, the way a couple of my classmates were implying? My officemate (she's in the physics department) thinks this is a non-issue and that philosophers arguing about it are just behind the curve. It feels off to me that a purely physical fact about how simultaneity works could single-handedly settle an ontological question about what exists, but I genuinely can't locate the flaw myself if there is one. I'd rather not just hear "well most physicists lean eternalist" - I want to know whether the argument itself is actually as airtight as everyone in my seminar seemed to assume, or whether it's smuggling in some extra premise that a good presentist could reject.
