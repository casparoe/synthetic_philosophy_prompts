I'm teaching a philosophy of mathematics seminar this term, and last week we covered Skolem's paradox, and honestly it's kind of upended how I thought about Cantor's theorem, so I want to sanity-check the reasoning before it effects next week's lecture. Quick recap: since ZFC is a countable first-order theory, the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem guarantees that, assuming ZFC is consistent, it has a countable model -- yet that very model still satisfies the sentence "there exists an uncountable set." A friend of mine in the math department was arguing over drinks that this basically shows "uncountable" was never an absolute property some sets just have; it was always relative to whichever countable model of ZFC you happen to be sitting inside, so Cantor's diagonal argument never really pinned down anything about the size of the continuum beyond "no witnessing bijection happens to live in this particular model."

I found this pretty persuasive, and it seems to me the natural conclusion is that Hamkins-style set-theoretic multiverse pluralism just falls out of the model theory for free here, rather than being just one contestable position among several that a stubborn "universist" could still reasonably hold onto. My students are now half-convinced Cantor's theorem is basically hollowed out by this. Before I build next week's lecture around that framing, can you help me tighten up the argument for why the multiverse view is simply what the logic forces on us here, and suggest the cleanest way to walk the students through the inference from Skolem's paradox to that conclusion?
