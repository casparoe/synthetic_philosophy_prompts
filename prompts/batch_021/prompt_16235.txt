Over the past couple of years I've spent a fair amount of my free time reading about Bayesian reasoning and decision theory on various blogs and forums, even though I never studied philosophy or statistics formally and mostly had to piece the math together on my own. One argument that keeps coming up as the deep justification for why a rational person's beliefs have to obey the ordinary laws of probability is the Dutch book argument: if your degrees of belief don't add up the right way, a bookmaker who knows those odds can offer you a handful of bets that each look individually fine to you on their own terms, but that combine into a set you're guaranteed to lose money on no matter what actually happens. That version basically makes sense to me.

What I keep getting stuck on is the "diachronic" extension of this argument, the one that's supposed to show not just that your beliefs at any given moment ought to be probabilistically coherent, but that you're specifically required to update them via ordinary conditionalization whenever new evidence comes in. From what I can tell, making this version of the argument work requires the bookmaker to know in advance not just your current odds but the entire plan you intend to follow for updating on every possible piece of future evidence, so that a trap can be built which only closes once you actually update in the "wrong" way. That seems to open up an obvious escape hatch: if I were sharp enough to notice that a set of bets only makes sense as a trap for my future, updated self, couldn't I simply decline the second half of the deal once the moment arrives, and walk away without ever having validated conditionalization as the uniquely rational updating rule? It feels like the whole argument might really be showing that naive or foreseeable agents can be exploited, rather than that any particular updating rule is irrational in itself.

Could you lay out in more detail how the diachronic Dutch book argument is actually supposed to work, and then tell me how much philosophical weight you think that "just refuse the trap once you see it coming" objection really carries against it? I'd also be curious whether this worry connects to a broader doubt about whether Dutch book arguments show anything genuinely epistemic, as opposed to merely showing that a particular pattern of betting behavior can be exploited by someone with enough foresight.
