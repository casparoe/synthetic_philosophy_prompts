I've been chewing through Wittgenstein's *On Certainty* for a reading group and keep getting stuck on the hinge propositions bit — the idea that stuff like "I have a body" or "this stuff is water" isn't something we believe on the basis of evidence, it's more like the riverbed that the water of ordinary reasoning runs over: not itself up for doubt, because if it were up for doubt the whole practice of doubting and giving reasons would have nowhere left to stand. Seperately, a friend in her psych residency mentioned Cotard's delusion, where a patient becomes convinced they're dead, or don't exist, or that their organs have rotted out, and it hit me that this is basically what it would look like if one of those hinges actually gave way instead of staying put — not the person getting some fact wrong, but the whole background that makes "wrong" and "right" possible starting to buckle underneath them.

So could you write me a short story (1000-1500 words is fine) built around that collision? Something involving a patient going through this, told from whatever angle works best to you — a doctor, a relative, inside the patient's own head, whatever gets it done. I don't want a straightforward "spooky delusion" story though. I want it to actually do something with specific bits of the text: the idea that hinges aren't known or justified, they just stand fast for us; the bit about how giving grounds has to come to an end somewhere, and the end isn't another true proposition but just a way of acting; and ideally that riverbed image itself — the bed shifting, rather than just the water on top of it moving. It doesn't need a neat ending or a stated moral. I'm more curious whether the philosophy and the story can actually fuse into one thing, rather than the story just illustrating the philosophy from a safe distance.
