After forty years as an experimental physicist, I retired two years ago and have finally had the leisure to read the philosophical and theological works I always meant to get to. Lately this has meant working through Pascal's Pensées in the Krailsheimer translation, partly out of professional kinship: it is hard not to feel some pride in a fellow scientist who worked out the physical principle behind the hydraulic press, built an early calculating machine to help his father with tax accounts, and corresponded with Fermat on the foundations of probability, before turning to theology in his later years.

What has brought me up short, though, is the cluster of fragments on justice and force. Pascal writes that because justice is inherently open to dispute while force is plain for everyone to recognize, humanity has never managed to make what is just strong, and so has instead made what is strong count as just—a bargain that at least buys us peace. A little further on he adds that a mere river can turn justice into injustice, since what passes for law on one side of the Pyrenees counts as a crime on the other. Read together, the fragments seem to say quite bluntly that "justice" is nothing but a flattering name we give to whatever custom or power happens to have prevailed in a given place, which sounds less like Christian moral theology than something out of Hobbes, or even Nietzsche. It is a strange thing, too, for someone who spent a career treating force as a precisely defined, conserved quantity measured in newtons, to watch the word do such murkier work here.

When I raised this with a friend from my old department, now retired herself and reading Pascal alongside me, she agreed the passages sound cynical but could not say exactly why that reading might be too quick. Since the rest of the Pensées is so transparently in earnest about sin, grace, and a real moral order beneath all the corruption, I am left wondering whether Pascal secretly held a thoroughly deflationary, might-makes-right view of justice and merely dressed it in religious language for public consumption, or whether there is a way of reading the justice-and-force fragments that keeps them from quietly undermining everything he says elsewhere. I would value help thinking this through.
