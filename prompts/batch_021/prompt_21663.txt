I've been reporting on the long afterlife of the ENCODE genome project's "junk DNA" controversy for a piece on how biologists define their key terms, and the more I read, the more it looks like a philosophical dispute wearing a biochemist's lab coat. When ENCODE researchers announced that a striking majority of the human genome shows some form of "biochemical function," evolutionary biologists pushed back hard, arguing that such a permissive standard would count almost anything as functional -- one memorable rebuttal compared it to calling a broken, unplugged television still "functional" because it keeps doing things, such as gathering dust and reflecting light, for as long as it exists. Digging further, I found that this fight is really a rerun of a longstanding disagreement in the philosophy of biology between two rival accounts of what a biological function even is: the etiological account, originating with Larry Wright and later refined by Ruth Millikan and Karen Neander, on which a trait's function is fixed by the selective history that explains why the trait exists or persists; and the causal-role account, associated with Robert Cummins, on which a trait's function is simply whatever it currently contributes to some capacity of a containing system, with no essential appeal to history at all.

For the piece, I'd like to move past a simple "both sides have a point" framing. Could you write an essay laying out the strongest considerations for each account -- for instance, the etiological view's apparent edge in explaining malfunction, vestigial traits, and disease, against the causal-role view's advantages for working biologists doing systems-level functional analysis without staking their explanations on uncertain evolutionary history? Rather than ending on a diplomatic fence-sit, please defend an actual verdict, even a qualified one (say, that one account governs biology proper while the other suits engineering or medicine, or that one account is ultimately parasitic on the other), and justify it. Feel free to test your view against the junk DNA dispute itself, the function of a vestigial structure like the human appendix, or another concrete case that sharpens the disagreement.
