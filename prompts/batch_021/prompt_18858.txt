I attend a Jewish day school, and in our Machshava (Jewish Thought) elective we just spent two weeks working through sections of the Rambam's Guide for the Perplexed, in particular the chapters where he gives reasons for the commandments. Our teacher assigned the part of Book III, chapter 32 — in the old Friedländer translation that's in our school library — where Maimonides explains why the Torah commands animal sacrifice in the first place. I have read the sacrificial portions of Vayikra plenty of times and always just found them tedious to get through, but this passage confused me in a completely different way, and since I have to present on it next class, I wanted to work through it somewhere other than just nodding along with my chavruta, who thinks the whole theory basically reduces the Temple service to an ancient anthropology lecture with a few verses stapled on. That feels too quick to me, but I cannot yet say exactly why.

Here is the part I keep rereading:

"Many precepts in our Law are the result of a similar course adopted by the same Supreme Being. It is, namely, impossible to go suddenly from one extreme to the other: it is therefore according to the nature of man impossible for him suddenly to discontinue everything to which he has been accustomed... But the custom which was in those days general among all men, and the general mode of worship in which the Israelites were brought up, consisted in sacrificing animals in those temples which contained certain images, to bow down to those images, and to burn incense before them... It was in accordance with the wisdom and plan of God, as displayed in the whole Creation, that He did not command us to give up and to discontinue all these manners of service; for to obey such a commandment it would have been contrary to the nature of man, who generally cleaves to that to which he is used; it would in those days have made the same impression as a prophet would make at present if he called us to the service of God and told us in His name, that we should not pray to Him, not fast, not seek His help in time of trouble; that we should serve Him in thought, and not by any action. For this reason God allowed these kinds of service to continue; He transferred to His service that which had formerly served as a worship of created beings, and of things imaginary and unreal, and commanded us to serve Him in the same manner."

What I can't work out is exactly how much weight the "impossible to go suddenly from one extreme to the other" idea is supposed to carry, and two things about it bother me. First, if sacrifice is fundamentally a concession to a people already habituated to pagan sacrificial worship, that seems to explain only why the Torah tolerates and redirects an existing practice, not where the practice came from in the first place. Our teacher mentioned in passing that this is basically Nachmanides' objection to the whole theory: Cain, Abel, and Noah are all described offering sacrifices in Genesis long before there is any Israelite idolatry around to be weaned off of, which is presumably why Nachmanides thought the theory was serious enough to warrant accusing Maimonides of "making the table of the Eternal polluted." Second, if sacrifice really is scaffolding for people too spiritually immature for pure, actionless worship, I don't see why the Rambam's own law code still describes a rebuilt Temple with restored sacrificial service as part of the Messianic future, as though the scaffolding is supposed to go back up once the building is already finished. Could you help me work out what Maimonides is and isn't actually committed to in this passage, and how someone defending his account might respond to both of these objections?
