I'm working on my IB Extended Essay — the 4,000-word independent research paper we have to write in the IB Diploma — and I've chosen to write mine on the sorites paradox. As background reading, my supervisor had me start with the Stanford Encyclopedia's entry on vagueness, which is where I first came across epistemicism and supervaluationism as two of the leading modern responses to the paradox, and she then pointed me to Bertrand Russell's 1923 essay "Vagueness," since it's apparently treated as the paper that kicked off the modern debate. I can follow most of it: he opens with the old puzzle about the man who gradually goes bald, and uses it to argue that vague predicates like "bald" and "red" don't really obey the law of excluded middle. But I keep getting stuck on the paragraph where he tries to explain why vagueness is a genuinely different thing from mere generality, which seems to be doing a lot of the argumentative work for him. Here is the passage:

"It follows that every proposition that can be framed in practice has a certain degree of vagueness; that is to say, there is not one definite fact necessary and sufficient for its truth, but a certain region of possible facts, any one of which would make it true. And this region is itself ill-defined: we cannot assign to it a definite boundary. This is the difference between vagueness and generality. A proposition involving a general concept — e.g. 'This is a man' — will be verified by a number of facts, such as 'This' being Brown or Jones or Robinson. But if 'man' were a precise idea, the set of possible facts that would verify 'this is a man' would be quite definite. Since, however, the conception 'man' is more or less vague, it is possible to discover prehistoric specimens concerning which there is no, even in theory, a definite answer to the question 'Is this a man?' As applied to such specimens, the proposition 'this is a man' is neither definitely true nor definitely false."

Could you help me work through this slowly? Specifically, I don't understand why "This is a man" being made true by Brown, or by Jones, or by Robinson counts only as generality and not also as vagueness, since both notions seem to involve a single word covering a whole range of different individual cases — so what exactly is the extra ingredient that Russell thinks turns a merely general term into a vague one? And once you've walked me through his reasoning, I would love to know how a present-day epistemicist and a present-day supervaluationist would each assess this passage: would either of them grant that Russell has actually located a real difference between vagueness and generality, or would they say he has quietly smuggled his conclusion in through the choice of example?
