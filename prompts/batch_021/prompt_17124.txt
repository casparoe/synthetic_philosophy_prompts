I don't have any formal training in philosophy or computer science, but for the past year or so I've spent a lot of my spare time reading about the debate over whether large language models genuinely understand the language they produce, as opposed to being extremely sophisticated pattern-matchers with nothing going on "inside" (I gather people sometimes call this the "stochastic parrots" debate). That reading eventually sent me down a rabbit hole into philosophy of mind, where I kept running into the word "intentionality," meaning roughly a mental state's capacity to be directed at, or "about," something. Tracing the term back, I landed on Franz Brentano, the nineteenth-century Austrian philosopher who argued, in his 1874 book on psychology, that this "aboutness" is the one mark that reliably separates the mental from the merely physical. He also made the much stranger claim that a mental act can be genuinely directed at an object that doesn't exist at all, which he called "intentional inexistence": you can think about a unicorn, and your thought is still "about" the unicorn, even though there's nothing out there for it to be about.

Brentano died in 1917, long before anyone had built anything resembling a computer, so obviously he never said a word about machines or language models, and I haven't come across anyone who has seriously worked out what someone committed to his specific arguments would say about them. So here's what I'd like your help with: reasoning as carefully as you can from Brentano's actual position (not just the one-line slogan version of his thesis), what do you think he would conclude about whether a large language model's outputs display genuine intentionality in his sense, or whether they would strike him as, at best, the sort of merely derivative "as-if" intentionality that Searle later contrasted with the real thing? I'd also be curious whether you think his odd notion of "intentional inexistence" gives us any genuine angle on what's happening when a model confidently "hallucinates" something about an object or fact that doesn't exist, or whether that's reading far too much into a nineteenth-century idea it was never meant to bear. Please commit to an actual answer rather than just listing considerations on both sides, and flag clearly wherever you think you're extrapolating well past anything Brentano himself was in a position to settle.
