For a philosophy of religion seminar I am taking this term, we recently spent two class meetings reconstructing Gödel's modal ontological argument in Dana Scott's tidied-up version: the definition of a God-like being as one possessing all positive properties, the axiom that positive properties are closed under strict (necessary) entailment, the axiom that for any property either it or its negation is positive but never both, and the further axiom that necessary existence is itself a positive property. Given these, and working within a modal logic at least as strong as S5, one can derive first that a God-like being is possibly exemplified and then, via the characteristic S5 axiom moving from possible necessity to necessity, that a God-like being necessarily exists. I followed this derivation reasonably well on its own terms, and I recall reading that when researchers a few years ago fed a version of the axioms into an automated theorem prover, the software confirmed the argument was formally valid, which briefly made for a wave of headlines suggesting computers had proven that God exists.

What I am still stuck on is the standard objection that this same axiom set entails modal collapse: not merely that God exists necessarily, but that every actually true proposition whatsoever turns out to be necessarily true, erasing the distinction between the contingent and the necessary across the board rather than just for theological claims. I can see in outline why this would be considered a devastating cost, since it would mean the modal apparatus is doing something far stronger, and far less plausible, than advertised. What I do not yet have a good grip on is exactly how the derivation gets from "God has all of His properties essentially" to "every true proposition is a necessary one" — which definition or axiom is really carrying the weight — and why the fixes people have proposed, such as tinkering with the definition of essence or of necessary existence, tend either to fail to block the collapse or to drain the argument of its original force.

Could you walk me through, in fairly intuitive terms rather than a fully formalized derivation, how the collapse actually follows from the axioms, and say something about why it has proven so hard to patch without either reintroducing the collapse or giving up on reaching necessary existence? I would also be curious whether you think this is a defect peculiar to Gödel's particular axiomatization, or whether it reflects a more general problem with running any Anselm-style ontological argument inside a modal logic as strong as S5.
