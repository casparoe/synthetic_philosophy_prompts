I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on formal epistemology and multi-agent decision theory, and for the last month my department's AI-safety reading group has been stuck on Daniel Kokotajlo's "commitment races problem." The worry, put roughly, is that a sufficiently sophisticated bargaining agent has an incentive to lock in a binding commitment as early as possible, before it has had time to reason things through, because the folk theorem shows that any status quo can be sustained by mutual grim-trigger threats -- so whoever manages to be "earlier in logical time" with a credible commitment gets to set the terms, and hesitating doesn't keep you safe, it just cedes that move to whoever commits first. Half of us think the rational response is to build agents that self-modify into updateless commitment devices as fast as possible, since that looks like the only real protection against being exploited; the other half thinks that is exactly the reasoning that turns a solvable bargaining problem into mutual disaster once it becomes common knowledge that everyone reasons this way, and wants to invest instead in mitigations like surrogate goals and safe Pareto improvements, which researchers at the Center on Long-Term Risk have proposed as ways of getting some of the benefits of commitment without the same tail risk. We keep going in circles in these meetings without anyone changing their mind, so I'd like to see the disagreement staged as an actual argument rather than as two position statements.

Could you write me a dialogue between two decision theorists, each privately convinced that caution is on their side, cast as the two parties in a standoff over how to split a windfall that neither can obtain without the other's cooperation -- set in the moments before either one decides whether to broadcast an irreversible commitment? One should make the case that committing early to a scrupulously fair demand is the prudent move precisely because refusing to commit is not the neutral, low-risk option it looks like. The other should grant that hesitation carries a real cost, but argue that racing to commit is what manufactures the disaster in the first place, and press the mitigations above as a way of getting out of the race rather than winning it. I don't want either side handed an easy victory: each should be forced to answer the specific objection the other has just raised, including the uncomfortable point that "always deliberate before committing" is itself a commitment that a sufficiently fast opponent could exploit, and I'd like the exchange to end with both sides conceding something genuine about the weakness of their own position rather than one side clearly converting the other.
