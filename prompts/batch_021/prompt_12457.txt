Two colleagues of mine on the science desk, Marisol (an investigative reporter) and Callum (our research editor), spent twenty minutes arguing at a fact-checking meeting last month over a single-source story: a former employee alleging, in convincing detail and with no obvious motive, that a mid-sized biotech firm had buried an unfavorable trial result. No documents, no second witness, just her account.

(1) Callum said we couldn't run it on her word alone: something you're told isn't knowledge until it's checked against something independent — a document, a second witness — and right now we just had her assurance. (2) Marisol replied that "something independent" is almost always somebody else's word too — the second witness, the archivist who vouches for the document — so if testimony only counts once it's backed by non-testimony, we can never start, since almost everything we call independent confirmation is more testimony; someone coherent, detailed, and with nothing to gain is owed belief by default, revisable the moment something specific counts against her. (3) Callum said he wasn't asking for anything non-testimonial, just a second, independent testimony — that's why the rule is always two sources, because we've learned, case by case, which lone accounts pan out — and here there was something specific: she'd been let go eighteen months ago and was suing the company. (4) Marisol said she'd disclosed the lawsuit in her memo, that a motive to lie isn't a lie, and that if grievances disqualified people from belief, no whistleblower would ever be believed, since almost anyone willing to blow one has already been alienated; she added that "we've learned case by case" still can't explain the first tip either of them ever acted on, before either had any track record to generalize from.

Neither budged. I recognized the shape of it: an old dispute between Hume and Reid over whether testimony only counts once it's backed by independent, non-testimonial evidence, or is itself a basic source of justified belief — reductionism versus anti-reductionism about testimony, replayed as a newsroom fight. Please adjudicate move by move: say who has the better argument at each of the four moves above, flag anything both of them are missing, and end with a verdict on its own line, in exactly this format: \verdict{Marisol} or \verdict{Callum}.
