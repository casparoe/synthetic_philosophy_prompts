I am a novelist, and for several years I have watched the space between a book and its reader fill with intermediaries that would have astonished the eighteenth century: an aggregate star rating on Goodreads, a "readers also enjoyed" algorithm trained on millions of purchase histories, and now AI reading assistants that will, on request, summarize a novel and pronounce it a "4.5-star must-read" before anyone has opened its cover. In "Of the Standard of Taste," David Hume confronted a version of this problem for his own age: because sentiments of beauty vary from person to person, he argued, only the joint verdict of what he called "true judges" — critics who combine delicacy of sentiment with practice, comparison, good sense, and freedom from prejudice — amounts to a genuine standard of taste. He defended the reality of such delicacy with the story of Sancho Panza's kinsmen, ridiculed for detecting a taste of iron and leather in a hogshead of wine that others had pronounced simply excellent, until the cask was emptied and an old key on a leather thong turned up at the bottom.

Suppose you had to explain to Hume, in terms comprehensible to him, what a recommendation algorithm and a generative AI reading assistant are and how each arrives at its verdicts. Would he grant either one standing as a modern proxy for a true judge, or would he conclude that both are, at best, a highly efficient version of the crowd that mocked Sancho's kinsmen before the cask was opened? Reach a definite verdict for each of the two technologies, and defend it by engaging closely with the specific criteria Hume lays out for a true judge, rather than gesturing at his empiricism in general.
