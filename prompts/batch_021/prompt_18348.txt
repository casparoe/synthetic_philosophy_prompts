I spent most of my career as a comparative neurophysiologist -- recorded from squid and octopus ganglia for a good chunk of it -- and now that I'm retired I've fallen into philosophy of mind fairly hard, mostly because a friend from my old department keeps handing me papers. The one that's been stuck in my head lately is the multiple realizability arguement against type-identity theory: pain (or whatever mental state you like) can't just be some particular physical/chemical state, because octopuses, birds, and hypothetical silicon creatures can all be "in pain" too, despite sharing basically no physical structure with us or each other. Then I ran into a couple of philosophers -- Bechtel and Mundale, I think -- arguing this is partly a trick of description: that once you match the grain of your psychological categories to the grain of your neuroscientific ones, instead of comparing one coarse mental category against a needlessly fine physical one, a lot of the apparent multiplicity disappears. Having spent thirty-odd years staring at just how differently a cephalopod's nervous system is wired up compared to a vertebrate's, I find myself sympathetic to both sides and genuinely unsure how the disagreement is even supposed to get settled.

Could you write me a dialogue between a committed functionalist and a "new-wave" reductionist armed with that grain-matching move, working it out using octopus versus vertebrate nociception as their running example instead of the usual Martians-in-pain thought experiment? I want the reductionist to really press on how the functionalist decides two states count as "the same" functional kind across such different architectures without just helping themselves to the answer, and I want the functionalist to have an actual comeback rather than just restating Putnam -- something about the reductionist picking whatever grain happens to make the reduction come out right, maybe. If the exchange naturally drifts toward Kim's causal exclusion worry and the idea of a species-by-species "local" reduction as an uneasy truce between them, let it go there, but don't force it in if it doesn't fit. Neither side should be a pushover -- I want to see real blows landed on both over a dozen or so exchanges, not two people reciting position papers past each other.
