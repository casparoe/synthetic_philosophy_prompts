So I've gotten really into poker over the past year and went down a rabbit hole reading about bankroll management, which is how I first ran into the Kelly criterion (bet a fraction of your bankroll proportional to your edge, supposedly the "optimal" size). Anyway it caused an argument at poker night between my friend Jonah, who bets full Kelly on everything including his options trades, and his housemate Erin, who did some stats years ago. I was half-listening while dealing cards and honestly can't tell who won, so here's the exchange as best I remember it:

(1) Jonah opened by saying betting the Kelly fraction isn't just a reasonable choice, it's THE correct one: do it over enough bets and you're basically guaranteed to end up richer than under any meaningfully different strategy.

(2) Erin said that "basically guaranteed" only kicks in as the number of bets goes to infinity -- over any real, finite stretch, the strategy with the higher expected bankroll at the end is often one that bets bigger than Kelly, it just also carries a much higher chance of busting along the way. So calling Kelly "the" correct size already assumes you care about the likely outcome over the average one -- that's a assumption, not a proof.

(3) Jonah's comeback was that plain expected value is the wrong yardstick once you're reinvesting winnings, because your stack multiplies each round instead of adding, so the expected-value math gets dominated by some vanishingly unlikely run of luck -- kind of like how the St. Petersburg paradox's "expected value" is dominated by absurd jackpots nobody will ever actually hit. Once you're compounding, he said, what you actually want to maximize is your long-run geometric growth rate -- expected log-wealth -- and that's exactly what Kelly does.

(4) Erin granted that but said swapping to log-wealth is itself a preference -- a pretty specific, fairly aggressive taste for avoiding the typical-case disappointment -- and someone who's genuinely fine with a real shot at busting in exchange for a much bigger possible payout isn't breaking any law of probability by betting more than Kelly. So "mathematically proven," she said, really means "correct if you already want what Kelly happens to optimize for," which isn't a proof, it's an assumption wearing a lab coat.

(5) Jonah's last word before they got called back to the table: it's stronger than a mere preference, because it's not just that Kelly gives a better typical outcome -- if he and someone playing a noticeably different strategy both bet forever, the probability he ends up ahead approaches 1, not just "more likely than not." Certainty in the limit, he said, is a different and more objective kind of winning than anything that depends on a utility function.

Nobody got a comeback in because the next hand started. Can you go through moves 1 through 5 and say who's got the better of it at each one, not just an overall winner? And then tell me straight: is Jonah basically right that Kelly betting is objectively "the" rational bet size, is Erin basically right that it's smuggling in a preference, or are they both missing something about how a "certainty in the limit" argument like Jonah's stacks up against an ordinary expected-value argument like Erin's?
