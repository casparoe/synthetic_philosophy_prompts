Question: "A classmate says: 'Intuitionistic logic is basically just classical logic minus excluded middle -- intuitionists hold that P ∨ ¬P is simply false.' Assess this claim. Your answer should address: (a) what the BHK interpretation says about why P ∨ ¬P isn't provable as a general schema, (b) whether ¬(P ∨ ¬P) could coherently be added to intuitionistic logic as an axiom, (c) the history behind the 'B', 'H', and 'K' in 'BHK', and (d) whether giving up excluded middle also commits you to giving up the law of non-contradiction."

Student's answer: "This is a common mistake, but it's not right. On the BHK interpretation, a proof of P ∨ Q is a proof of P together with a flag saying which disjunct got proved, or a proof of Q with the same. So a general proof of P ∨ ¬P would be a uniform method that, for any P, either proves P or refutes it -- and for propositions we haven't settled (Goldbach's conjecture is the standard example) no such method is available, so P ∨ ¬P isn't a theorem schema. That's different from being false, though. Actually ¬¬(P ∨ ¬P) is a theorem: assume ¬(P ∨ ¬P); assuming P gives P ∨ ¬P, which contradicts that, so ¬P; but ¬P gives P ∨ ¬P too, another contradiction; so the assumption is itself absurd. That means adding ¬(P ∨ ¬P) as an axiom would make the system inconsistent, so intuitionists can't coherently call excluded middle false, only unprovable in general.

As for 'BHK': Brouwer built the underlying philosophy of mathematics as mental construction and, in the 1920s, put it into the formal system of axioms and inference rules we still call intuitionistic logic today. Heyting, his student, is mostly remembered for popularizing this system in the years after. Kolmogorov, arriving independently at more or less the same logic a bit later, gave it a probabilistic gloss, reading each proposition as a claim staked to some degree of certainty rather than a flat assertion.

None of this touches non-contradiction, by the way -- ¬(P ∧ ¬P) is still a theorem, shown the usual way, by assuming P ∧ ¬P and deriving absurdity straight from the two conjuncts. So what's actually given up is excluded middle as a general law (and double negation elimination along with it), not non-contradiction -- and it's given up for proof-theoretic reasons, not because some statements are secretly neither true nor false."
