I've spent years reading both traditional philosophy of science and the more Bayesian epistemology common on rationalist blogs and forums, much of which leans on algorithmic information theory, and one tension between the two has never quite resolved for me. The Duhem–Quine thesis holds that evidence underdetermines theory choice: any theory can be saved from a recalcitrant observation by revising some auxiliary hypothesis instead, so no finite body of evidence picks out a unique theory. The standard rationalist rejoinder is that this is largely dissolved once rival hypotheses are weighted by a simplicity prior in the style of Solomonoff induction, so that "equally consistent with the evidence" stops meaning "equally probable." But that simplicity ranking is only well-defined relative to a choice of universal Turing machine (or description language), and the invariance theorem meant to make this choice not matter only guarantees that any two machines' complexity assignments differ by no more than some fixed additive constant — a constant that can dwarf any realistic body of evidence, leaving the choice of machine itself unconstrained by that evidence.

Write a dialogue between a committed Duhem–Quine holist and a Bayesian epistemologist defending the simplicity-prior response, in which they debate this directly, each giving their strongest version of the argument. The Bayesian should have a genuine answer to the machine-relativity worry rather than simply restating the invariance theorem, and the Quinean should have a real rejoinder to that answer in turn, so the exchange reads as substantive back-and-forth rather than two people talking past each other.
