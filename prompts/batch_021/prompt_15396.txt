My friend Priya and I got into a proper arguement at dinner last night over that old Taurek paper, "Should the Numbers Count?" — the one where he says that if you can only save one stranger or five other equally-situated strangers, the mere fact that there's five of them isn't actually a reason to save them over the one, and that flipping a coin respects everyone involved better than just automatically going with the bigger group. Priya thinks this is obviously nuts ("you don't need a philosophy degree to know five is more than one," her exact words) and I ended up defending it way more than I meant to, mostly because I couldn't find the hole in the "there's no one for whom the death of the five is worse than the death of the one, since its five separate people each losing their own one life" move. We didn't resolve anything, which is bugging me today.

Anyway, I help run my university's public philosophy events, and next month we've got a philosopher coming to give a talk who has built a good chunk of her career defending basically this anti-aggregative position, and she applies it to actual triage and organ-allocation debates, not just toy rescue cases with interchangeable strangers. I'm moderating the Q&A afterward and I really don't want it to be forty minutes of her repeating the "separate losses can't be added up into one bigger loss" line while everyone nods along — I want to actually corner her a bit, the way Priya nearly cornered me. Could you draft me 8-10 questions for the Q&A, each with the answer she's most likely to give and then a follow-up that pushes past it? I specifically want one question that makes her say whether the coin-flip conclusion survives at absurd ratios like one life against a million, one on why a flat fifty-fifty coin flip is more defensible than the "obvious" compromise of a lottery weighted by group size, one that puts Scanlon's attempt to rescue "save the greater number" through some kind of tie-breaking role for numbers to her directly and asks why that isn't just aggregation wearing a disguise, and one that gets at whether her whole position secretly depends on a separateness-of-persons claim that she'd need to defend on totally different grounds than the rescue case itself.
