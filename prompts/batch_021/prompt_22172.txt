Last night a friend and I fell into a long, not fully resolved argument about whether pointing out someone's hypocrisy is a genuine philosophical move or simply a polite way of dodging the substance of what they said. It began with a televised debate in which a commentator's argument was waved aside purely on the grounds of his own past conduct, and it grew into a broader dispute about the "standing to blame" literature: my friend insists that the whole apparatus philosophers such as R. Jay Wallace and G. A. Cohen have built around this idea is just a dressed-up version of the tu quoque fallacy, while I tried, with only partial success, to convince him that there is a genuine distinction between denying someone's standing to blame and denying that what they said is true.

As it happens, I am serving as a teaching assistant for a graduate seminar this term that covers exactly this material, and one of the take-home exam questions bears directly on our argument. The question reads:

"In 'Hypocrisy, Moral Address, and the Equal Standing of Persons,' R. Jay Wallace argues that hypocritical blame is objectionable for reasons that go beyond simple inconsistency. G. A. Cohen, in 'Casting the First Stone,' argues that condemning others requires satisfying a non-hypocrisy condition. (a) In about 150 words, explain the difference between denying that someone has the standing to blame and denying that their blame is warranted. (b) Drawing on either Wallace's or Cohen's account, explain why a hypocrite's blame might still be worth taking seriously even though the hypocrite lacks the standing to issue it. (c) Give one example that brings out the difference between being a hypocrite and merely being inconsistent."

Below is one student's answer, reproduced verbatim. I would like your help grading it before I settle on a final mark, and I would rather have a rigorous, part-by-part check than a general impression, since a fluent answer can glide past real errors:

"(a) These are two different questions. Standing is about whether this particular speaker is well positioned to blame someone else -- whether they have the moral authority to demand an account from them -- while warrant is about whether the target actually did something wrong in the first place. A blamer can lack standing while being completely right that the act was wrong (a burglar can truly say that the man who mugged him did wrong, even though, as a burglar, he's an odd person to lecture anyone on property rights), and conversely someone with impeccable standing can simply be mistaken about whether the act was wrong at all. So calling someone a hypocrite is not, by itself, a rebuttal of their claim.

(b) Cohen's discussion in 'Casting the First Stone' explains this well: what makes hypocritical blame distinctively objectionable is not just the double standard, but the fact that the hypocrite implicitly claims an exemption for himself from the very demand he is issuing to someone else, and so fails to treat the two of them as moral equals. This is why we can still take a hypocrite's blame seriously: a torturer-turned-human-rights-activist who condemns torture is, on this picture, not entitled to make that demand of others, so his blame is both standingless and, strictly, unwarranted -- yet most of us would still agree that torture is wrong, so there is a sense in which what he says is worth hearing even if he isn't the one who should be saying it.

(c) Hypocrisy is a special case of inconsistency, not the same thing. A hypocrite exempts himself from a rule he enforces on others -- for instance, a manager who fires an employee for lateness while habitually strolling in late himself. But you can be inconsistent without being a hypocrite in this sense: a critic who writes furious columns about one senator's affair while barely mentioning an ideologically congenial senator's identical affair is being inconsistent, even unfair, but she isn't exempting herself from anything, since she isn't claiming any special immunity for her own conduct. So all hypocrisy involves inconsistency, but not all inconsistency is hypocrisy."

Could you go through (a), (b), and (c) separately and flag anything that is imprecise, unsupported, or mistaken?
