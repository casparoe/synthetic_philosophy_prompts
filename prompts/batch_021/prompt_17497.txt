I've been following the semaglutide discourse on ACX and LessWrong, and one detail keeps drawing my attention: GLP-1 drugs like Ozempic do not so much strengthen willpower as delete the appetite that willpower would otherwise have had to contend with — an effect now widely described as the quieting of "food noise." This strikes me as a genuinely novel kind of intervention on the self, and I'd like to know how Nietzsche might have judged it.

I don't mean this at the level of Übermensch-grade generalities. I mean the actual physiologist of "Why I Am So Clever" in Ecce Homo, who treated nutrition, climate, and recreation as "the whole casuistry of selfishness," who attributed the torpor of the German intellect to greasy, beer-soaked cooking, who called alcohol and Christianity Europe's two narcotics, and who talked himself out of the vegetarianism he had flirted with in his twenties once Wagner disabused him of it. This was a thinker who took his own digestion as seriously as his metaphysics, and whose treatment of the ascetic ideal in the Genealogy of Morals makes clear that self-imposed hunger can be either a technique of strength or a symptom of life turning against itself, depending on who is doing the starving and why.

Given this, would Nietzsche have welcomed the pharmacological silencing of appetite as a legitimate technology of self-mastery — clearing away physiological static so the will can direct itself toward higher creation, much as he engineered his own diet — or would he have classed it with alcohol among the narcotics, one more comfortable shortcut that anesthetizes a drive rather than disciplining it? Please pick a side and argue for it, grounding your case in his own stated commitments rather than in the popular caricature of him.
