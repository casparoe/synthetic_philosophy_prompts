The 'Death in Damascus' case (guy meets Death in Damascus, flees to Aleppo, but Death was headed to Aleppo the whole time) usually gets trotted out as a problem case for causal decision theory: neither 'stay' nor 'flee' ends up ratifiable, since whichever one you're sure you'll do, that's exactly the info that puts Death in that city, making the other option look better. Gibbard and Harpers fix is that the rational move is to randomize (flip a coin), since that's the one option that IS ratifiable. Write an essay that takes an actual stand on this rather than just describing it: is randomizing a genuine solution, or a dodge -- does it even make sense to call Death a 'perfect predictor' if a coin flip he couldn't foresee is what saves you half the time? Does the coin flip really rescue causal decision theory here, or does the case show something's broken in CDT (or in ratifiability as a rationality requirement) that no amount of randomizing patches over? Give an actual answer and defend it, don't just lay out considerations on both sides.
