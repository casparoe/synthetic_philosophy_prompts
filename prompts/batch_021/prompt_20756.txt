For my school's forecasting club, we submit predictions that are graded with a Brier-style scoring rule, which our coach explained is "proper" because you cannot raise your expected score by reporting anything other than your honest credence. She mentioned, almost as an aside, that some epistemologists use this very fact to argue that rational credences must obey the probability axioms even outside of any competition -- something called an accuracy-dominance argument, associated with the philosopher Jim Joyce. I do not see how you get from "honesty is the best policy under this one scoring rule" to "incoherent credences are always epistemically defective, full stop" -- that feels like a much bigger claim than the scoring-rule fact can support. Could you walk me through the actual structure of that argument, including what has to be true of a scoring rule for it to count as measuring genuine accuracy and why that is supposed to force coherence, and then tell me where philosophers think the argument is most vulnerable?
