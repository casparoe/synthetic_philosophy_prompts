I'm a backend engineer, and a few weeks ago a coworker made an offhand joke about AI systems that can never fully trust their own future code changes "for Gödel reasons." That sent me down a rabbit hole into Löb's theorem and what people in the AI-alignment literature call the Löbian obstacle: roughly, the result that a formal system cannot in general adopt a reflection principle along the lines of "whatever my proof system proves is true" for an arbitrary statement without either already being able to prove that statement outright, or, in the worst case, becoming flatly inconsistent. That turns out to be a real practical headache for what people in this area call the "tiling agents" problem: getting a self-modifying agent to certify that a successor version of itself, possibly running on a different or stronger proof system, is safe to hand control to.

I run a small interview-style podcast for other engineers curious about the more formal and philosophical corners of AI safety, and next week's guest is a logician who works on AI alignment -- invented for our purposes, so nobody has to worry about misquoting a real person -- who has built her reputation defending the position that the Löbian obstacle is a solvable bookkeeping problem rather than a genuine limit on self-trust. Whenever a host raises it as something deep, her go-to move is some version of: "you're smuggling in the assumption that an agent needs to trust something as strong as itself -- once you let each agent in the chain certify only a strictly weaker successor, the obstacle just evaporates." I'd like you to draft eight to ten interview questions, increasing in difficulty, that keep her from resting on that line. Please make sure at least one question forces her to explain, in terms a competent programmer without formal logic training could follow, why it is specifically Löb's theorem -- rather than some fuzzy worry about circularity -- that rules out the naive "unconditionally trust my successor's proofs" schema; at least one that asks whether stepping down through a hierarchy of successively weaker systems is a genuine solution or just an infinite regress that quietly abandons the original goal of unbounded self-trust; at least one on whether moving to probabilistic approaches such as logical induction really dissolves the obstacle or just trades a hard impossibility result for a softer one; and a closing question asking her to name the one result or thought experiment that would actually change her mind about her "bookkeeping, not a limit" framing. For each question, add a short parenthetical noting the answer or deflection she is likely to reach for, plus a follow-up question that keeps her from retreating to it.
