I've been re-reading the closing sections of Cicero's *De Senectute* for a course on ancient ethics, and I'd like your help extending one of its arguments to a live contemporary question. There, Cicero has Cato meet the traditional complaint that old age is undesirable because it lies so close to death by embracing the proximity rather than denying it: he likens a natural death in old age to ripe fruit dropping gently from the branch, as opposed to unripe fruit wrenched off by force, and argues that nature has set a due measure for life, just as for each of its earlier stages, so that reaching one's natural end is fitting rather than something to lament. He adds the further, broadly Socratic point that death is either annihilation, in which case nothing is lost, or a passage to a better state among the great souls who came before. Given this picture of a ripe, well-timed death as the fitting conclusion to a life well spent, how do you think Cicero would respond to the contemporary longevity movement's ambition to treat aging itself as a disease to be indefinitely deferred or even cured? Could his own premises actually be marshaled in favor of that project, as a rational extension of the lifelong cultivation of mind and virtue he otherwise prizes, or do they commit him to viewing such efforts as a refusal of nature's due order? Please work through both readings using his own arguments and imagery rather than settling for a quick verdict.
