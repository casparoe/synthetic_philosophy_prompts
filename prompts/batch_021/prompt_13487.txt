I'm a philosophy major currently taking a seminar on social convention and coordination problems, in which we've spent the last few weeks working closely through David Lewis's *Convention*. My older sister is getting married next month, and she's asked me to give the toast at the reception. She and her fiancé are both in a linguistics PhD program, and the story they tell about how they got together is distractingly on-topic for my seminar: for most of a semester, they were the only two people who kept arriving early to the same visiting-speaker series and claiming the same two seats by the radiator, without ever once discussing it, until the week those seats were taken and they ended up sharing a windowsill instead — and that was that. They've started calling this "their coordination equilibrium" as a running joke, and my sister has told me, half-seriously, that she would love the toast to put Lewis's actual apparatus to work — common knowledge, salience, precedent, the requirement that some other regularity could have served just as well had it caught on first — rather than gesturing at "convention" as a vague synonym for "the thing people happen to do."

Here is my actual difficulty. Roughly a third of the guests are people from their programs who have read the book closely enough to notice if I use the vocabulary sloppily or get a clause wrong, while the rest are relatives who've never heard of David Lewis and shouldn't have to before enjoying a wedding toast. But I'm also uneasy about more than tone. Lewis's own definition makes arbitrariness constitutive of a convention: for a regularity to count as one at all, there has to be some other regularity that almost everyone would have preferred, had it become established instead. I don't want the toast to end up implying, even as a joke, that my sister and her husband are together because they happened to land on one equilibrium among several equally good ones that any other two people in that room could have filled — and this worry isn't something I can dodge just by writing carefully, since it looks like a genuine consequence of the official definition rather than a sloppy misreading of it. Could you write me a three-to-four-minute toast that puts Lewis's machinery to work precisely enough to reward the people who've read the book, stays warm and clear for the people who haven't, and finds some honest way of leaning on his notion of salience or precedent to explain why this particular equilibrium is the one that held, without quietly pretending the arbitrariness worry isn't there?
