Michael Dummett's *On Immigration and Refugees* (2001) argues that the moral case for tightly restricting immigration is far weaker than most public debate assumes, and that British immigration law in particular has long been shaped by unacknowledged racial prejudice rather than by the neutral administrative concerns officially invoked to justify it. Dummett died in 2011, several years before the UK's "hostile environment" policy produced the Windrush scandal, in which numerous Black Britons of Caribbean origin who had lived in the country legally for decades were wrongly detained, denied services, or deported as illegal immigrants. Given the specific arguments Dummett develops in his book, how do you think he would have assessed this episode: would he most likely have treated it as a straightforward vindication of the diagnosis he offered in 2001, or does some feature of the Windrush case sit awkwardly with his framework?
