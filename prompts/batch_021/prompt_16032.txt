I've been following the periodic re-litigation of Peter Singer's drowning-child argument on the EA Forum and LessWrong for years now, and something about the standard framing has started to bother me. Those debates usually proceed as though the only question is where, exactly, to draw the line between obligatory and merely-supererogatory giving -- but the deeper problem, it seems to me, is that a fully consistent maximizing consequentialism doesn't obviously leave room for such a line at all. If the right act is always whichever available act produces the most good, then the best available act simply is the obligatory one, and anything we're tempted to praise as saintly or heroic "going beyond duty" should collapse straight back into duty.

I'd like an essay that works through this properly rather than just gesturing at the tension. Specifically: can maximizing act consequentialism coherently accommodate a genuine, non-illusory category of supererogatory action, or does preserving supererogation (as something distinct from duty, and specially praiseworthy) require giving up maximization somewhere? Please engage seriously with at least the following moves, and say why each does or doesn't work: Slote-style satisficing consequentialism, on which rightness only requires a "good enough" outcome rather than the best one; Scheffler-style agent-centered prerogatives, which permit (without requiring) agents to give extra weight to their own projects and thereby fall short of the impartially optimal act, leaving room for further self-sacrifice to count as above and beyond; and Portmore-style "dual-ranking" consequentialism, which tries to preserve a recognizably consequentialist structure while building in agent-relative options. You should also take seriously the harder-nosed alternative associated with Kagan, on which our confidence in a robust category of supererogation is better read as evidence that ordinary morality understates its own demands than as evidence against maximizing views. Feel free to use the standard Urmson-style hero/saint cases (the soldier who smothers a grenade, the doctor who stays behind in a plague-stricken city) as intuition pumps along the way, but don't let the essay end on "it depends" -- I want a real verdict on whether, and how, supererogation survives.
