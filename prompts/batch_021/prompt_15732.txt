I'm writing a piece on what happens to a philosopher's reputation when private writings surface revealing something morally repugnant, and I've settled on Gottlob Frege as my case study. The diary he kept toward the end of his life, which only surfaced decades later when scholars combed through his papers, is full of virulent antisemitism, contempt for parliamentary democracy, and a longing for an authoritarian strongman — material that Michael Dummett, who did more than anyone to canonize Frege for twentieth-century analytic philosophy, later wrote about discovering in starkly personal terms, given his own history as an anti-racist campaigner. For readers who know Frege only as the founder of modern quantificational logic, I need a precise account of what specialists actually disagree about: is there a serious, non-question-begging case — of the sort some contextualist historians, like Hans Sluga, have pressed — that commitments central to his technical philosophy, such as his anti-psychologism, his realism about thoughts and truth-values, and his insistence that logical truth admits no relativizing to a particular people or standpoint, connect to the diary's politics in some non-accidental way? Or is the more defensible view that the diary is biographically damning but leaves the philosophy untouched? Lay out the strongest version of each side and tell me where you come down.
