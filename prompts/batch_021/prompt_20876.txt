So for my AI ethics elective we had to read part of Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (the one where he comes up with the imitation game/Turing test), and honestly the part that stuck with me wasn't even the robot stuff, it was that the very last objection he deals with -- like literally the 9th one in his whole list -- is about ESP, and he takes it dead serious. He straight up says "the statistical evidence, at least for telepathy, is overwhelming," and works out this whole scenario where the interrogator asks what suit a card is, the telepathic guy gets 130 out of 400 right while the computer only gets 104, so he concludes you'd need to stick everyone in a "telepathy-proof room" for the test to even be fair. My teacher said this was probably him getting influenced by those J.B. Rhine card-guessing studies out of Duke that were a big deal back then, which is kinda wierd when you think about how rigorous of a mathematician Turing was in literally every other part of the paper.

Obviously that whole research program aged really badly -- Rhine's results never held up, Princeton actually had a whole ESP lab (PEAR) that finally got shut down in 2007 after 28 years of not really finding anything, and Daryl Bem's 2011 "feeling the future" precognition study is basically the textbook example people bring up now when they talk about the replication crisis in psychology. Given all that, what do you think Turing would've actually concluded if he'd lived to see it play out? Like, is there something about how he handled evidence in that paper that means he'd have been slower to give up on ESP than you'd expect from someone that logical, or would he have dropped it right away? And seperately, do you think his actual specific worry -- that a genuinely telepathic person could beat the imitation game in a way no computer ever could -- still tells us something interesting about the limits of behavior-based tests for intelligence, or does the whole thing just collapse once you're confident ESP isn't real?
