I recently agreed to serve on my local party's executive committee, and within weeks I was asked to publicly defend a policy I privately doubt, in the name of what everyone at the table calls "party unity," months before we have even contested an election. The experience sent me back to Simone Weil's 1943 essay calling for the outright suppression of political parties, which she portrays as organizations engineered to manufacture collective passion and enforce conformity among their members, and whose only real, unlimited goal is their own growth, crowding out anyone's independent judgment about truth and justice. Her diagnosis fits my committee uncomfortably well, yet it sits oddly against Madison's argument in Federalist No. 10, where the causes of faction are rooted in human nature itself, so that removing them would require either destroying liberty or imposing a uniformity of opinion that is simply not achievable, leaving control of faction's effects through the machinery of a large representative republic as the only remedy Madison considers sound. Compare Weil's case for abolishing parties outright with Madison's case for merely managing faction. Does Weil's proposal actually escape the dilemma Madison poses, perhaps because her target, the organized party as a self-perpetuating machine, is narrower than his target, any group united by a common passion or interest, or does suppressing parties simply relocate the Madisonian problem rather than solve it? Please justify your answer with reference to the specific mechanisms each writer describes, rather than generic claims about the dangers of factionalism.
