I spent close to forty years as a geologist, most of it drilling and reading old sediment and ice cores, and now that I'm retired I've been going to a weekly philosophy reading group at the community collage, mostly just for fun - lately we've been slogging through some epistemology, rational belief, that sort of thing. Something the instructor said a couple weeks back is still bugging me, because it collides with a real situation from my old career. He claimed that if two honest, careful people fully understand each other's reasoning on some question - not just hear the arguments once, but actually know the other has taken them in, and know that the other knows that, and so on up - then they logically can't end up in genuine, lasting disagreement. Any leftover gap between their views, he said, has to come from someone being not-quite-rational or not-quite-honest, rather than the evidence itself being genuinely ambiguous.

That claim runs headlong into my thirty year working relationship with Marta, my old field partner. We drilled the same cores and poured over the same data for decades, and to this day we disagree about what caused a particular cooling signal that shows up across a bunch of our old records - I've always leaned one way, she's always leaned the other. We must have argued it out a hundred times over the years, in person, at conferences, in long letters back before email got big; we trust each other's integrity completely, and by now I honestly believe we each know the other's whole case cold, there's no argument left that would be news to either of us. So is my instructor actually onto something real here, and if so, does that mean one of us has secretly been irrational or dishonest with the other this whole time? Or is he overstating things, and if so, where exactly does the reasoning break down for two people like me and Marta?
