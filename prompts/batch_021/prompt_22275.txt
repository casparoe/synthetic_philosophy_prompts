For about two weeks last spring, I kept to something like four hours of sleep a night, racing to finish the climactic chapter of my second novel after my publisher moved the deadline up by two months. Against my own expectations, I emerged from that stretch with a chapter I was thrilled with, and on every rereading since—tired or rested—it has struck me as the best writing I have done. When I mentioned this to my agent, she was strangely unmoved: citing something she had read on the psychology of creative work, she told me that writers who push through severe sleep deprivation are reliably poor judges of their own pacing and tone, and tend to feel most confident about exactly the passages that later prove to have the flaws such exhaustion typically produces. I raised the point over dinner with two friends—one a literary critic, the other a cognitive scientist—and we spent most of the evening arguing about it, one of them convinced the whole worry was confused and the other convinced I was fooling myself, without anyone landing anywhere conclusive.

What I cannot quite get past is this: I do not feel impaired, and the chapter has not read as sloppy or self-indulgent on any of the five or six occasions I have returned to it, including after a week of ordinary sleep. But presumably that is exactly how impaired judgment feels from the inside, so I am not sure I can exempt myself from my agent's claim merely because I cannot detect any way in which it applies to me—and it occurs to me that my confidence on the later, well-rested readings might simply be the original verdict hardening into habit rather than an independent check on it. I am left holding two things at once: a vivid, repeated impression that the chapter works, and a fairly good argument that I was in no position, at the time, to trust that very impression. Should one of these give way to the other, and if so, on what grounds? Or can both be held rationally, with neither required to yield?
