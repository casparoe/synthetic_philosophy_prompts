Dutch book arguments come up constantly on LessWrong and in the wider rationalist blogosphere as the standard knockdown case for Bayesian probabilism, but I've noticed that people tend to talk about "the" Dutch book argument as if it were a single piece of reasoning, when there actually seem to be at least two importantly different versions. There is the synchronic argument, going back to Ramsey and de Finetti, which shows that if your credences at a single moment violate the probability axioms, a bookie can construct a set of bets that guarantees you a loss regardless of how the world turns out. And there is the diachronic argument, usually traced to an argument of David Lewis's that Paul Teller published in the early 1970s, which is meant to justify conditionalization itself by showing that an agent who plans to update in some other way can be Dutch-booked across time. I brought this distinction up in a discussion thread recently and got pushback along the lines of "they're really the same argument, just applied at different points in time," which I'm not convinced is right.

Could you compare these two arguments in some depth? I'm especially interested in whether the diachronic version smuggles in extra, more contestable assumptions that the synchronic version doesn't need – for instance, that the agent's future credences have to be fixed and knowable in advance for the bookie to exploit them, or that failing to conditionalize is the only alternative to exploitation, as opposed to simply revising one's updating plan when new considerations come in. I'd also like your take on how the standard general objections to Dutch book reasoning – e.g., that it only establishes "pragmatic" rather than "epistemic" irrationality, or that it presupposes an agent who evaluates a sequence of bets one at a time rather than assessing and rejecting the whole losing package – bear differently on the two arguments. Finally, since van Fraassen's Reflection Principle is often presented as a descendant of the diachronic argument, I'd be curious whether it inherits the diachronic argument's weaknesses or manages to sidestep them. On balance, which of the two Dutch book arguments do you think is on firmer philosophical ground, and why?
