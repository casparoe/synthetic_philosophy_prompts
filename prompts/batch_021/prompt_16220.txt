A friend and I spent most of last night's dinner arguing over Ernest Adams' well-worn pair: "If Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, someone else did" against "If Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have." She insisted that anyone who accepts the first while rejecting the second is simply being inconsistent, since the two sentences differ only in grammatical mood; I argued that the divergence is exactly what a good semantics of conditionals needs to explain, not explain away. We never quite settled it, which is probably fitting, since I'm now marking take-home exams for a philosophy of language course and one student's answer addresses precisely this contrast. I'd like a second, disinterested opinion on how to grade it.

Here is the question as set:

"Consider the pair (i) 'If Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, someone else did' and (ii) 'If Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have.' Most competent speakers accept (i) while rejecting (ii), even though the two sentences appear to share the same antecedent-consequent content. Drawing on at least two of the theories covered in this course — the material conditional analysis, Stalnaker's and Lewis's possible-worlds semantics, and Adams' probabilistic account — explain why this divergence arises. Then briefly discuss what Lewis's triviality results imply for the prospect of a single, unified truth-conditional semantics of the sort Adams' Thesis might seem to invite."

And here is the student's answer, verbatim:

"Sentence (i) is an indicative conditional and (ii) is a counterfactual, and the puzzle is that despite sharing a common core, ordinary speakers accept the first while rejecting the second. On the material conditional analysis, (i) comes out true simply because Oswald did kill Kennedy, which makes the antecedent false and the whole conditional trivially true regardless of content; this fails to capture why we accept (i), since we accept it precisely because we are unsure who did it, not because we are sure Oswald did. Stalnaker and Lewis instead hold that a conditional is true at a world just in case the consequent holds at the closest world(s) where the antecedent holds; applying this to (ii), the closest world in which Oswald does not kill Kennedy is not one with a conveniently placed backup assassin, so the consequent fails there and (ii) comes out false, matching our intuitions. Because (i) is indicative rather than subjunctive, Stalnaker and Lewis's semantics does not apply to it at all, so we instead turn to Adams' Thesis, on which the assertability of a conditional equals the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent; since we are already certain someone killed Kennedy, the conditional probability that someone else did it, given that Oswald did not, is high, so (i) is assertable. This shows that indicative and subjunctive conditionals need entirely different treatments: indicatives are handled probabilistically and have no truth conditions at all, while subjunctives get classical truth conditions from similarity semantics. Lewis's triviality results confirm that this division was necessary, since they proved that no proposition's probability can equal the relevant conditional probability except in trivial cases, showing that Adams' Thesis was ultimately untenable and explaining why the field largely moved on from it after 1976."

Could you grade this out of 20?
