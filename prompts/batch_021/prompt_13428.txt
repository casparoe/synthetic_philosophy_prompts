So I sit on my town's Historic District Commission, and after our meeting this past Thursday a few of us went out for beers, which is how I ended up in a two-hour argument with my friend Priya (she's finishing up a philosophy PhD) about a case that's on next Tuesday's agenda. Our ordinance requires commission approval for any "substantial alteration" to the exterior of a contributing structure, but it never actually defines "substantial" beyond a couple of example photos from the 90s. The application in question wants to strip and replace the original wood clapboard on about 45% of the facade with fiber-cement siding milled to look almost identical, resize two windows to match a non-original addition from the 60s, and rebuild the porch railings to meet current code. Taken feature by feature none of it seems like a big deal, but add it up and half the commission thinks its obviously substantial while the other half thinks we're just inventing a line that isn't in the ordinance -- and to make things worse, we approved a similar project last year at around 30% replacement on a different house, and there's already a 60%-replacement application in the pipeline for spring that everyone assumes will just point back to whatever we decide here.

Priya's whole argument was that this is basically the sorites paradox wearing a hard hat -- that there's a real philosophical literature on exactly this kind of problem, predicates that seem fine to apply after any one small change but not once the changes add up. She threw around terms like epistemicism and supervaluationism (and something about fuzzy logic, I think?) and said most of the "solutions" people actually reach for by default, like just picking a percentage and defending it, have known problems that philosophers have been arguing about for decades. I don't know that literature well enough to tell if she's onto something or just being a smug philosophy person about my zoning meeting, but I'd rather not write a memo that boils down to "40% felt right to me" if there's a more defensible way to approach this.

Could you draft the actual decision memo I'd circulate to the rest of the commission before Tuesday? I need a real bottom line on this specific application -- approve, deny, or approve with conditions -- plus a proposed standard or procedure we could point back to for future cases so we're not refighting this from scratch every time something like this comes up. And please be honest about the strongest case for whichever side you don't end up recommending, since at least two commissioners are going to push back hard no matter which way I go.
