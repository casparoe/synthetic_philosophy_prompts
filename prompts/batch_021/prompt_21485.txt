My grandmother once told me, quite matter-of-factly, that a few weeks after my grandfather died she "saw" him sitting in his usual chair by the window. It came up when I visited her last spring; I pressed her on the details rather more than politeness really allowed, since I'm partway through a novel with a recently widowed character and had been struggling to write a scene like this honestly. She was adamant that she hadn't been dreaming or dozing, and that she knew even as it was happening that he was gone; what she insisted on was that the experience was nothing like remembering him or picturing him in her mind's eye, but was in every respect just like seeing him, except that there was, of course, nothing there to see.

I described this to a friend of mine who's finishing up a PhD in cognitive neuroscience, hoping for something I could use in the book, and he thought the puzzle dissolved fairly easily: as far as the brain is concerned, he said, a vivid hallucination and an actual perception draw on more or less the same visual machinery, so there is no deep difference in kind between really seeing your husband in his chair and merely seeming to, with total conviction — the two cases differ only in whether anything answering to the experience happens to be sitting there. That answer is tidy, but it also seems to dissolve the very thing I'm trying to write about, and something in it bothers me: if genuinely perceiving the world and merely having an experience indistinguishable from perceiving it are, at bottom, the same kind of mental event — one that happens to be anchored to reality and one that isn't — then it becomes hard to see what, if anything, is special about actual perception, or why we should trust it any more than we trust a hallucination for as long as we're having it. So is my friend right that there's no principled difference here — that seeing and merely seeming to see, with total conviction, are fundamentally the same kind of mental event — or is there a genuine difference in kind between the two, even when you can't tell from the inside which one you're in?
