Question: Robin Hanson's futarchy proposal is often summed up as "vote on values, but bet on beliefs." (a) In your own words, explain how a futarchy would actually decide whether to adopt some proposed policy. (b) Give one serious objection to the scheme and explain why it's a problem.

Her answer: "Futarchy is Robin Hanson's idea for running a government using prediction markets instead of normal politics. The slogan is 'vote on values, but bet on beliefs' -- democratic voting is still used to decide on the big-picture goal, like maximizing GDP or some broader measure of national welfare, but once that goal is set, elected officials step back and traders in a prediction market decide everything else. For any proposed policy, people buy and sell shares betting on whether the policy will turn out to be a good one, and whichever policy the market ends up betting on the most is the one that gets adopted. Since prediction markets have such a good track record (they're famously more accurate than pundits at stuff like predicting elections), we should expect a futarchy to pick better policies than ordinary democracies most of the time, because it's really just moving that same well-tested forecasting machinery into government.

One objection is that rich people or interest groups could manipulate the market by placing huge bets to push up the price of a policy they want, tricking the system into adopting it even though it's actually bad. Hanson's response is that this isn't really a problem, because manipulators just become a source of free money for informed traders, who bet against them and correct the price, so attempted manipulation tends to make the market more accurate rather than less. Still, for a small or thin market on some niche policy there might not be enough informed traders around to correct a manipulator, so that defense doesn't fully hold up."
