Okay so this is kind of a weird one but hear me out. In my English class we've been doing a unit on how people mean more than just the literal words they say (my teacher keeps bringing up "Grice's maxims" for conversation), and one day it got kind of derailed when a classmate brought up this thing called Moore's paradox, like the sentence "it's raining, but I don't believe it." Everyone agreed it sounds insane to say out loud, but nobody could actually explain why, since both halves of the sentence could totally be true at the same time (it really could be raining while I happen to not beleive it — that happens to people, like when someone's in denial about something obvious). I went down a wikipedia hole about it that night and it turns out philosophers don't even fully agree on why it's absurd — there's basically two whole camps, and they don't actually agree with each other, they just both happen to explain why the sentence sounds crazy.

One camp (I think this is closer to the Grice-y stuff from English class, but don't quote me) says the weirdness is basically a fact about the RULES of talking to people — like when you assert something you're automatically representing yourself as believing it, so tacking on "but I don't believe it" undercuts your own move in the conversation, kind of like a self-destructing speech act, even though there's no actual logical contradiction in the content itself. The other camp thinks that's not deep enough, and says the real issue is about what belief even IS — something about how you can't peek inside your own head to check what you believe, you check by asking whether the thing is true, so actually believing (not just saying) "P and I don't believe P" is basically impossible to pull off honestly, whether or not anyone's listening.

Can you write me a dialogue between two philosophers, one for each camp, who are both genuinely sharp and defend their own view well (not like a strawman where one obviously wins), and who actually respond to each other's specific points instead of just repeating themselves? I want them to wrestle with two test cases I think any good theory has to handle: (1) does the weirdness disappear if I write "it's raining and I don't believe it" in a private diary that literally nobody will ever read, and (2) why is it totally normal, zero absurdity, to say "it's raining, but he doesnt believe it" about someone else. Bonus points if by the end they even disagree about whether they're arguing about the same phenomenon, or if each theory secretly only explains half of it.
