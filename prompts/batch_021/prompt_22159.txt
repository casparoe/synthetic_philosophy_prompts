The setup, as I understood it: imagine a person who's created instantly, fully grown and rational, but suspended in midair -- blindfolded, ears plugged, limbs spread out so they can't even touch each other, basically zero sensory input, like the worlds most extreme isolation tank. Avicenna's claim is that even in that state, this guy would still know "I exist," but he'd have no way to know his body exists at all, and he wants to use that gap to conclude that the self (or soul) is literally a different thing from the body, not just that we happen to be more certain about the self than the body. I gather this is supposed to be a kind of ancestor to Descartes' cogito, except apparently not quite the same move, and honestly the part that's bugging me is that last step -- going from "I can conceive of myself existing without my body" to "therefore my self and my body are actually different substances" feels like a pretty big leap, the kind of thing a modern philosopher would call out immediately. But people have clearly taken this seriously for like a thousand years, so I assume I'm missing something.
