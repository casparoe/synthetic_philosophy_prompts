My older brother's parole hearing is in a few weeks and I've fallen down a total rabbit hole about whether "responsibility" even means what we act like it means. He had a genuinely brutal childhood (foster care, a stepdad who should've been locked up himself, all of it) and part of me thinks that basically explains why he ended up where he is, but then I feel like I'm just making excuses for him when I say that out loud to my mom. A friend doing a philosophy PhD sent me Galen Strawson's "Basic Argument" and it kind of broke my brain: you do what you do because of the way you are, but to be truly responsible for what you do you'd have to be responsible for the way you are, and you can't be, because that would mean shaping your own character before you existed with any character to do the shaping — it's a regress that never gets off the ground, and Strawson's whole point is that this holds even if the world isn't deterministic, since random indeterminism obviously doesn't hand you control either. Then I found out the almost funny/awful part: his dad, P.F. Strawson, wrote basically the classic modern defense of ordinary moral responsibility, the "reactive attitudes" essay about resentment and gratitude — so the son spent his career taking apart something close to his own father's view.

I don't need a summary of the argument, I've pretty much got it memorized at this point from reading it wrong three times before it clicked. What I actually want is to watch the fight happen. Could you write me a dialogue between someone who fully bites the bullet on the Basic Argument (no ultimate responsibility, ever, for anyone, guilty or not) and a sharp compatibilist defending real moral responsibility along Strawsonian reactive-attitudes or reasons-responsiveness lines? I want them to actually engage each others strongest moves — the regress in premise 3, the luck objection, whether reactive attitudes can just be brute facts about human life that don't need metaphysical backing, whether reasons-responsiveness is enough or is secretly smuggling back in the very thing that was supposed to be impossible — rather then each one just restating their own side. It's fine, maybe even better, if it doesn't resolve neatly, I don't think it's supposed to, but I want both people to land real hits on each other by the end, not one of them quietly winning because the other's a pushover.
