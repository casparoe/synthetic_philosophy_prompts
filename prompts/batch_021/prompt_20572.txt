I'm a final-year philosophy major, and this term I'm taking a seminar on self-knowledge that has just spent two weeks on the chapter of Timothy Williamson's Knowledge and Its Limits where he argues against "luminosity" and, via that, against the KK principle -- the thesis that if you know P, you know that you know P (Kp → KKp). A condition is luminous, in his technical sense, if whenever you're in it, you're automatically in a position to know that you're in it; the traditional picture treats states like feeling cold or being in pain as paradigm cases of this. His argument against luminosity has you imagine a stretch of time during which the temperature changes so gradually that you clearly feel cold at the start and clearly don't by the end, even though no two neighboring moments in between feel any different to you. He then invokes a margin-for-error requirement on knowledge -- roughly, that you can't know something on a basis that could just as easily have produced a false belief in a case indistinguishable from the actual one -- to argue that if feeling cold were luminous, this would force you to still feel cold at the very last moment, which is absurd, so feeling cold isn't luminous after all. He generalises the pattern to argue that no interesting condition is luminous, including the condition of knowing itself, which is what does the damage to KK.

After the seminar, two people in the reading group, Naomi and Callum, kept arguing about this over coffee while I mostly listened. (1) Callum opened by saying the argument is basically airtight: since our self-discrimination is coarse-grained somewhere, and margin-for-error is just part of what knowledge requires, there can be no sharp last moment of knowing-you-feel-cold, so luminosity -- and KK along with it -- has to go. (2) Naomi replied that this is a Sorites paradox wearing a lab coat: a chain of indiscriminable steps running from a clear case to its opposite is exactly the structure of the ancient heap paradox, and nobody thinks that paradox shows heaps don't exist -- we think some premise in the chain is defective even if we can't say which, so by parity we should treat the margin-for-error premise the same way rather than swallow the wild conclusion. (3) Callum came back that the parity fails: ordinary Sorites arguments trade on the vagueness of a predicate -- borderline cases where "heap" just doesn't determinately apply or fail to apply -- whereas his argument needs no indeterminacy at all; at every instant it can be perfectly determinate whether you feel cold, and all the work is done by your inability to discriminate neighboring instants, which is a fact about your discriminatory powers, not about the semantics of "feels cold." (4) Naomi granted that but pressed a different worry: indiscriminability of neighboring cases is an empirical claim about creatures with limited memory and coarse introspection, like us, not a conceptual truth about knowledge or about mental states as such -- so at most the argument shows that we, given our specific cognitive limits, fail to be luminous introspectors, not that luminosity or KK is untenable for any possible knower, and Williamson needs the stronger claim to get the result he wants. (5) Callum answered that this concedes too little, since KK was never proposed as a constraint on logically omniscient reasoners with perfect discrimination -- it's discussed as a principle for agents like us, so restricting the conclusion to realistic epistemic agents isn't a retreat, it's the target the argument was aimed at from the start. (6) Naomi's last word was that even granting that, the chain-of-cases setup obviously works for states like feeling cold, which vary continuously and which we track with a genuinely coarse-grained faculty; it's a further, unfinished step to extend the same argument to something like knowing that 2+2=4, where there's no obvious neighboring case in which the same belief, formed on the same basis, would come out false -- so even restricted to realistic agents, the claim that no interesting condition is luminous is not yet secured by anything said so far.

I'm especially interested in whether Callum's reply in (3) actually defuses the Sorites-parity charge from (2), whether Naomi's empirical-versus-conceptual distinction in (4) is doing the work she needs it to do given Callum's response in (5), and whether her closing scope objection in (6) marks a genuine, unfinished gap in Williamson's argument or something he has independent resources to close. Please also flag anything the two of them seem to be talking past each other on throughout, and end with a one-line verdict on who ends up with the stronger overall position -- or say explicitly if you think it's a genuine wash.
