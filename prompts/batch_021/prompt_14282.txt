I cover science and research culture, and I'm working on a piece about a decision every science desk wrestles with: whether to hold a story on a striking new finding until independent replication or more data comes in, or to run it now on the strength of the original study alone. Editors often defend "wait for more evidence" as simply the rational move, on the intuition that information can only help a decision, never hurt it. Before I repeat that line in print, I want to test it against I.J. Good's 1967 theorem on the value of information rather than just assert it as self-evident. Please lay out precisely what the theorem does and doesn't establish, and then describe its most important implications for this newsroom dilemma — above all, the specific ways in which real editorial decisions quietly fail to meet the conditions the theorem actually requires.
