The student's answer:

"In Knobe's famous chairman vignettes, a company's vice-president tells the chairman that a new program will increase profits but will also have a side effect, either harmful or helpful, on the environment. The chairman says he doesn't care at all about the environment, he just wants the extra profit either way, and the program goes ahead regardless. Knobe found a striking asymmetry: about 82% of people said the chairman intentionally harmed the environment, but only around 23% said he intentionally helped it — even though the chairman's own attitude toward the side effect (pure indifference) was identical across the two stories.

Knobe's own reading of this is that people's moral reactions are contaminating what's supposed to be a neutral, psychological judgment: because harming the environment is bad, we're quick to blame the chairman, and that blame gets read back into his mental states as intentionality, even though nothing in his actual psychology differs between the two cases. So for Knobe, the moral has basically infected our folk concept, which means our intuitions about 'intentionally' in cases like this one are a fairly unreliable guide — a philosopher trying to give a real analysis of intentional action should be suspicious of intuitions built on morally loaded cases and lean instead on cleaner, morally neutral ones.

A rival explanation is the trade-off hypothesis: the profit and the harm are bound together as a single package the chairman is choosing (you can't get the one without the other in this scenario), whereas in the help case the profit and the environmental benefit aren't tied together in the same way — the benefit is just a lucky bonus he'd have gotten regardless. On this view, what matters isn't blame directly but the cost/benefit shape of the decision, so it genuinely competes with Knobe's story, since it explains the same asymmetry without needing to invoke the psychology of blame at all. That said, since this result was built entirely around one particular business/profit scenario, my guess is the asymmetry is at least partly an artifact of that specific framing, and it's still an open question whether it would show up in vignettes that don't involve a callous businessman figure."
