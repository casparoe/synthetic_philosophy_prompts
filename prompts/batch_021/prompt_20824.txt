I'm reporting a feature on the ethics of advance directives for patients with advanced dementia, and I keep running into the fact that the whole debate is shadowed by Derek Parfit's work on personal identity. Parfit uses the teletransporter thought experiment — and especially its "branch-line" variant, in which the original body is not destroyed and survives for a few days alongside the newly created replica — to argue that strict numerical identity is not actually what matters to us when we care about our own survival. On his view, what matters is "Relation R": psychological continuity and connectedness of the right kind, which can hold to any degree, unlike identity, which is all-or-nothing. He makes a structurally similar point with his psychological spectrum cases, imagining a person's psychology altered by ever-larger degrees until almost nothing connects the two end points.

That second point is what got me thinking about dementia, since progressive dementia looks uncomfortably like a real-world psychological spectrum: memories, values, and personality erode by degrees rather than all at once. Bioethicists have already drawn on this territory. Ronald Dworkin's discussion of "precedent autonomy" in Life's Dominion, built around the real case of an Alzheimer's patient known as "Margo," and the pushback it drew from critics such as Rebecca Dresser, both turn on whether a healthy person's prior wishes should bind the very different person who exists once the disease has progressed; Allen Buchanan's earlier essay on advance directives and the personal identity problem takes this Parfitian question as its explicit starting point.

I'd like you to work through what you see as the most important implications of Parfit's account of what matters in survival for this specific dilemma. I'm especially interested in whether the claim that "Relation R comes in degrees" gives families, clinicians, and courts any real, usable guidance for deciding how much weight to give an advance directive as dementia progresses — or whether it mainly succeeds in making the underlying metaphysics more honest while leaving the practical question of what to actually do just as unresolved, or even more fraught, than before.
