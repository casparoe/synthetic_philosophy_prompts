I'm working on a piece about how hospitals and disaster-relief teams make triage calls when they can't save everyone, and while chasing down the philosophical backstory I fell into a total rabbit hole with John Taurek's old paper "Should the Numbers Count?" His argument still kind of messes with my head: he says that if you can only save one person — David, in his example, I think? — or a different group of five instead, you're not obviously doing something worse by picking David, because there's no single person for whom five deaths add up to a bigger loss than one death: each of the five only looses their own life, exactly like David looses his. He even suggests just flipping a coin instead of automatically saving the bigger group. I brought this up with a philosopher friend at a dinner party and she just said "oh, the coin-flip guy" and moved on, which did not exactly clarify things for me. Parfit wrote a well-known reply pushing back on all this ("Innumerate Ethics," if I've got the title right), and more or less everyone since has sided against Taurek, but honestly, reading through the back-and-forth, I don't think I could reconstruct either side's strongest version of the argument from memory, let alone tell you who actually wins.

Could you write me a dialogue between two equally sharp people hashing this out — stuck arguing it out right inside Taurek's thought experiment, one dose of the drug left, no way to split it or delay the decision, with one of them (call her the Taurek side) channeling his position and the other (call him the numbers-count side) defending the "obviously you save the five" view?

Neither should be a pushover, and I want them actually responding to each other rather than just restating their own case. The Taurek side needs to answer the charge that her view quietly abandons the idea that death and suffering are just bad, full stop, regardless of whose they are, and that it leads to absurd results once you scale the numbers up (1 versus a thousand, say). The numbers-count side needs to answer her challenge about what exactly "adding up" harms across seperate people who never jointly experience anything is supposed to be doing, and whether that isn't just glossing over the seperateness of persons to land on the answer we already wanted. Get them arguing about the coin-flip idea too, since I genuinely can't tell if it's a real solution or just a dodge dressed up as fairness. Keep it fairly tight and conversational, more back-and-forth than monologue, and let one of them actually shift their position a little if the argument genuinely earns it, rather than both just digging in no matter what.
