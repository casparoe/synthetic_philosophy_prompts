Ok, random one, but I've been down a Leibniz rabbit hole for my early modern seminar and can't stop thinking about his beef with Pufendorf over the foundations of justice -- feels like it never gets airtime compared to all the theodicy/best-of-all-possible-worlds stuff. Pufendorf's view is basically that moral entities like obligation and rights don't exist "in nature," they're imposed onto physical facts by a superior will, so natural law only binds us because God commands it, not because of anything intrinsic to the acts themselves. Leibniz thinks this gets it backwards: justice and goodness track necessary, eternal truths about fittingness and perfection that even God's own will doesn't invent (his understanding just perceives them), which is part of why he keeps defining justice as "the charity of the wise." Could you write me a dialogue between a Pufendorfian and a Leibnizian actually hashing this out -- I want the Pufendorfian pressing on how we're suppose to have reliable epistemic access to these "eternal truths of morality" without it secretly just being theology dressed up as logic, and the Leibnizian pressing back on how a pure imposition/command theory can't tell real justice apart from whatever the strongest party happens to want. Neither one should be a pushover, and I want them actually responding to each others specific points rather than just restating their own view past one another.
