In my work on AI alignment, I've recently had to think hard about how, or whether, one can aggregate many different people's preferences into a single training objective for a model -- for instance, when combining many raters' feedback via reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Two results that colleagues keep invoking seem to be in real tension. Arrow's impossibility theorem is cited to argue that no reasonable rule for aggregating individual preference orderings into a social ordering can satisfy a small set of seemingly minimal fairness conditions without collapsing into dictatorship. Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, on the other hand, is cited as showing almost the opposite: if both individual and social preferences over lotteries satisfy the expected-utility axioms and a mild Pareto condition holds, the social preference is forced to take the form of a weighted sum of individual utilities -- apparently vindicating a kind of utilitarian aggregation. Could you explain why these two results don't actually contradict one another, focusing in particular on what work interpersonal comparability of utility is doing, or not doing, in each? I would also value your judgment on whether Harsanyi's theorem genuinely earns its utilitarian conclusion, or whether it covertly presupposes just the kind of interpersonal comparison that Arrow's framework, and Robbins-style skepticism about comparing utility across people, were meant to avoid.
