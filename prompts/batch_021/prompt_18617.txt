So there's this old argument -- Bradley's regress -- that's supposed to sink the idea that a proposition (or a fact) is just its parts glued together by a relation: if 'Desdemona loves Cassio' is unified by the loving-relation connecting Desdemona and Cassio, you need a further relation connecting that relation to each of them, and then another relation to connect THAT one, forever. This is basically the guts of the 'unity of the proposition' problem. Frege's fix was to say concepts are 'unsaturated' so they don't need a seperate glue-relation, Russell tried a couple of different moves (something about relations working differently when they're actually relating vs. just being named as terms, then later the multiple relation theory of judgment), and some people, like Armstrong, just posit a primitive 'tie' that isn't itself a further relatum needing its own connector. Write an essay that spells out the strongest version of the regress, decides whether it's genuinely vicious or just a harmless infinite regress, and defends whether any of these escape routes actually work -- or whether propositions/facts just can't be unities built out of separately existing parts at all. Don't just survey the positions -- pick one and argue for it.
