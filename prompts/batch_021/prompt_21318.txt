As a PhD student in philosophy of religion, I've spent this term working through the contemporary Molinism literature, and one particular objection strikes me as better suited to fiction than to another footnote. Molinists hold that God's middle knowledge — his scientia media — consists of true counterfactuals about what every possible free creature would do in every possible circumstance, standing logically (if not temporally) prior to any decree to create. Because this knowledge precedes the decree, God can use it to select which circumstances to actualize; mere foreknowledge of what a creature will actually do, by contrast, arrives too late in the order of explanation to guide any choice. Dean Zimmerman has sharpened this asymmetry into what he calls the "Extreme Manipulation Objection": once you're choosing someone's circumstances because you already know what they would freely do in them, you start to look less like a respectful witness to their freedom and more like a casting director who already knows, before the audition, exactly what each actor will improvise, and books them accordingly.

Write a short story, roughly 800–1200 words, that dramatizes this exact tension rather than explaining it. Somewhere in the story, a character who possesses this sort of prevolitional, counterfactual knowledge of someone else must decide whether to arrange that person's circumstances precisely because of what those circumstances are already known to elicit from them. Don't use the words "Molinism," "middle knowledge," or "God," and don't let any character deliver the argument above in dialogue; the plot and its unresolved discomfort should do that work instead.
