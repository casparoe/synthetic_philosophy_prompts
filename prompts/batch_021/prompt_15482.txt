I hold the non-scientist, unaffiliated seat on my university's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee — the seat that, in practice, tends to go to a science journalist. A cognition lab has submitted a protocol to test whether a common analgesic blunts injury-guarding behavior in octopuses, which means briefly exposing one arm to a mild noxious stimulus before and after dosing. Octopuses fall outside the federal Animal Welfare Act's definition of a covered animal, so nothing obliges us to impose the refinement and endpoint conditions we'd require automatically for an equivalent rat protocol; anything further has to be justified on our own authority. Our veterinarian wants to approve the protocol as submitted, on the grounds that the evidence for octopus sentience remains genuinely contested and that a committee with neither the expertise nor the charter to settle that dispute has no business inventing welfare standards on the fly. Another member wants us to impose rat-level safeguards anyway, arguing that scaling precautions to the probability and severity of possible suffering is the only defensible response to real uncertainty, not a reason to default to inaction. Please draft the memo I'll circulate before Thursday's vote. It should recommend specific, concrete protocol conditions, or explain in equally concrete terms why none are warranted, and it should take the veterinarian's institutional-competence worry seriously rather than treating it as mere foot-dragging.
