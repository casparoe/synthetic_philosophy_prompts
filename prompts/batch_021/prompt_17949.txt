I'm teaching a graduate seminar on vagueness this term, and we've just spent a couple of weeks on Timothy Williamson's epistemicism: the view that predicates like "heap," "tall," or "red" have a perfectly sharp, fact-of-the-matter extension—there is some exact number of grains at which a non-heap becomes a heap—that we are nonetheless in principle unable to know, because, on Williamson's margin-for-error reasoning, the coarse, use-based way our words acquire their meanings could never underwrite beliefs precise enough to count as knowledge of exactly where the cutoff falls. I chose epistemicism over rivals like supervaluationism precisely because it is the hardest position to make sympathetic: positing a fact with no evidence even possible for its exact location strikes most people as a piece of metaphysical extravagance rather than serious philosophy of language, and one of my sharper students said as much in class last week. I would like a piece of fiction that makes the view feel less like a logician's trick and more like a way the world could actually be for someone.

Could you write me a short story, something in the 1000-1800 word range, in which the plot genuinely turns on epistemicism being true of some vague predicate that matters to the characters—legal, medical, military, romantic, whatever setting you think carries the idea best—rather than the stock heap-of-sand or bald-man cases, which telegraph the punchline before the story even starts? I want at least one character to be gripped by the thought that there is a real, determinate answer to some "borderline" question bearing on their life, even though no amount of evidence or refinement of method could ever reveal it. If the story can also gesture, even lightly, at higher-order vagueness—the way the line between the "knowably clear" cases and the "unknowably borderline" ones is itself exactly the kind of thing epistemicism says we cannot know—that would be a welcome bonus, though I would rather not have the story feel like it is checking boxes off a syllabus. Ideally it should leave a reader who already understands the Sorites paradox unsettled in some new way, rather than simply re-explaining the paradox to them.
