I'm partway through a novel with two recurring secondary characters, a married couple named Ines and Thomas who teach at the same university: she is a social psychologist who works on moral behavior, and he is a philosopher in the Aristotelian tradition who has spent his career defending the reality of the virtues. Their marriage runs on one long-standing argument that resurfaces, in slightly different clothes, at every dinner party and faculty reception in the book, and I have finally reached the chapter where I need to render it in full rather than merely gesture at it from the outside. I have done my own reading to get the shape of the dispute right — Doris's Lack of Character and Harman's papers on the "nonexistence" of character traits for her side, Kamtekar's reply in Ethics and some of the virtue-as-skill literature for his — but I still can't hear how the exchange would actually move, sentence by sentence, between two people who have spent a decade sharpening their objections to each other in particular rather than to a position in the abstract.

Could you draft that scene as a dialogue between Ines and Thomas, roughly 700–900 words, with light stage direction at most, so that the argument itself carries the scene? I would like Ines to open with the empirical case situationists actually lean on — Milgram's obedience results, Isen and Levin's dime-in-the-phone-booth study, Darley and Batson's experiment with the seminarians in a hurry, and the famously weak cross-situational consistency in Hartshorne and May's old honesty studies — in order to press the claim that "character," in the cross-situationally robust sense virtue ethics needs, is largely an empirical fiction. Thomas should not simply concede ground or change the subject: he needs substantive replies, touching on why global consistency was never the relevant test for an Aristotelian, on practical wisdom as itself a kind of situation-sensitivity rather than its opposite, and on what a forced-choice laboratory paradigm can and cannot be taken to measure. But Ines should have live rejoinders to each of those points as they come, not merely to his opening remarks, and I don't want either character flattened into someone who states a position once and then repeats it with more feeling. Let the exchange end without a clean winner; that is the honest state of this debate, and it also happens to be the more useful outcome for my chapter, since I genuinely don't yet know, in my own authorial opinion, which of the two of them has the better of it.
