I've been reading about "evidential cooperation in large worlds" (ECL) on some effective altruism and rationalist blogs, and I can't shake the sense that it's a strange descendant of Kant's ethics that nobody involved seems to frame that way. The idea, roughly: if the universe (or multiverse) is vast or infinite, there are countless agents causally cut off from us whose reasoning nonetheless correlates with ours, because we face structurally similar decision problems. On this view, if I act cooperatively toward the values of others — including agents I'll never meet and whose values differ from mine — that choice is itself evidence that relevantly similar reasoners elsewhere will do the same with respect to my values, even with no causal link between us. It's grounded in evidential rather than causal decision theory (the same family of reasoning behind one-boxing on Newcomb's problem), and its usual justification is broadly consequentialist: this kind of cooperation is supposed to raise expected value across the whole universe compared to everyone reasoning in a purely local, parochial way.

Suppose someone explained ECL to Kant. Would he recognize it as an unexpected, empirical vindication of the Formula of Universal Law and the kingdom of ends, or would he dismiss it as a fundamentally different and, by his own lights, confused sort of argument, given that its force comes from expected-value calculation and correlated dispositions rather than from the a priori autonomy of the rational will? Please argue for your best reconstruction of his verdict, drawing on the actual structure of his moral philosophy — the autonomy/heteronomy distinction, his rejection of consequentialism, his account of what gives a maxim moral worth — rather than just the "universal law" slogan.
