So I've been rereading Gregory Kavka's old toxin puzzle after a friend at my rationalist meetup brought it up in the context of functional decision theory, and honestly I want someone to actually commit to an answer instead of just restating the setup for the millionth time. Quick recap in case its fuzzy: an eccentric billionaire tells you that if, by midnight tonight, you form the sincere intention to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow at noon (it'll make you painfully sick for a day, no lasting harm, definitely won't kill you), a million dollars shows up in your bank account tonight -- and crucially, you don't have to actually drink the stuff to get paid, you just have to really intend to, somehow verifiably. The classic puzzle is about whether you can even form that intention in the first place, since you already know that once the money's sitting in your account, you'll have zero forward-looking reason to go through with drinking something that just makes you sick with no further benefit to show for it.

Here's the part I actually want litigated: say you somehow manage to form the intention (let's bracket whether that's even psychologically possible) and wake up a million dollars richer. Noon rolls around, toxin in hand. Do you drink it? My gut says obviously not -- drinking it now can't retroactively cause the money to have appeared, so it's a pure cost with zero remaining upside, which I think is just the standard causal-decision-theory answer. But then I keep thinking about how structurally close this is to Parfit's hitchhiker, and to all the stuff Yudkowsky and Soares write about functional decision theory and about McClennen's (resolute? sophisticated? I always mix up his terminology) choosers who stick to a plan precisely because being the kind of agent who sticks to plans is what got them the reward in the first place. So which is it: is drinking the toxin just a dumb, gratuitous cost, or is refusing to drink it basically two-boxing with extra steps? Write me an essay that actually weighs the causal-decision-theory objection against the resolute-choice/FDT-style objection and picks a side -- I don't want a "well it depends which decision theory you subscribe to" non-answer, I want to know which side is actually getting the rationality of the situation right, and why.
