A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether Graham Priest's dialetheism genuinely solves the Liar paradox or merely relocates it, and I would like to work our disagreement into a short essay. Below are my notes toward that essay; I would welcome your comments before I develop them further.

My central claim is that the dialetheic strategy—treating "This sentence is not true" as both true and false, within a paraconsistent logic that blocks explosion—looks successful mainly because it is tested against sentences built on negation and self-reference together. Curry's paradox suggests this success is narrower than it appears. Let C be the sentence "If C is true, then P," for an arbitrary and even absurd P. Using nothing but the unrestricted T-schema, modus ponens, and the standard rule that lets you discharge an assumption once it has done its work, one can derive P outright, with no negation and no explicit contradiction appearing anywhere in the proof. A theory that only adds a third or fourth truth value for negated self-reference, while leaving modus ponens, contraction, and the T-schema untouched, is therefore still committed to full triviality via Curry's paradox—a far worse fate than the dialetheism Priest is prepared to accept. My tentative conclusion is that the real fault line among theories of truth is not gaps versus gluts, Kripke versus Priest, but whether one is willing to give up classical structural rules such as contraction, and that the popular framing of the Liar debate in terms of gaps and gluts obscures this.

My friend's objection, which I could not fully answer, is that a sufficiently careful dialetheist can simply adopt a separate, weaker conditional for which contraction fails, restricted to contexts involving the truth predicate, leaving ordinary reasoning classical everywhere else. On this view, the dialetheist is doing exactly what Tarski does by segregating languages, or what Kripke does by withholding a classical truth value from ungrounded sentences: paying a local, well-defined cost rather than a global one. If that is right, Curry's paradox does not specially embarrass dialetheism relative to its rivals; it merely shows that every theory of truth needs an independent answer to it.

I would like your comments on two things: whether my central argument is accurate and fair to Priest's actual position, or whether I am overstating what Curry's paradox shows about the significance of the gap/glut distinction; and whether my friend's proposed rebuttal genuinely defuses the objection or merely restates it in more technical vocabulary.
