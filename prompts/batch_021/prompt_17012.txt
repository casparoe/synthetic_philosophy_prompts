I'm a philosophy PhD student finishing a dissertation chapter on Leibniz's Principle of Sufficient Reason, and this semester I've also been sitting in on the math department's intro set theory course to shore up my formal background (long story, my advisor thinks historians of philosophy should actually know some real math). Last week we covered the axiom of choice, and somethings been bugging me since. AC gets you a well-ordering of literally any set, including the reals, but the existence proof is totally non-constructive — nobody has ever produced an actual well-ordering of the reals, or even a rule for picking representatives out of the cosets you use to build a Vitali set, and by all accounts no such rule could ever be written down in general. My professor's attitude was basically "it's just picking one item per bin, who cares if there's no rule," but it struck me that this is exactly the kind of "difference for which there could be no sufficient reason" that Leibniz spends half the Clarke correspondence railing against — his whole argument against Newtonian absolute space is that God could never really face a choice between two indiscernible arrangements (the material universe placed here vs. shifted five feet east) because a choice with literally no reason behind it is exactly what PSR forbids.

So there seems to be a genuine collision between Leibniz's mature metaphysics and 20th century set theory, and I can't work out how it should go. On one hand, a "choice function" that provably exists but that nothing — no rule, no formula, no procedure — could ever pick out looks like precisely the kind of brute, unreasoned selection PSR exists to outlaw, which would make Leibniz a proto-constructivist about all this well before Brouwer. On the other hand, his whole theory of contingency is built around reasons that are perfectly real but not finitely surveyable: contingent truths do have sufficient reasons, it's just that the analysis needed to see them may run to infinity, so only God can grasp it in one go. That seems to leave room for him to say a "ruleless" choice only looks ruleless to us, and there's no PSR violation at all, just a proof whose reason is beyond any finite mind. And then there's the extra wrinkle that Leibniz was weirdly cagey about actual infinite totalities to begin with — he flatly denied there's a coherent "infinite number" even while insisting there are infinitely many monads — so I'm honestly not sure he'd even grant the background picture (an infinite collection of nonempty sets, treated as one completed object) that Zermelo's axiom is stated in terms of.

Could you actually reconstruct how you think Leibniz would rule on the axiom of choice, if you brought him up to speed on Zermelo, the well-ordering theorem, and Zorn's lemma? I don't want the cheap answer ("he's a rationalist, of course he'd hate non-constructive proofs") — I want you to actually work through the tension between his PSR-based hostility to unmotivated distinctions and his infinite-analysis theory of contingency, tell me which of his own commitments you think would actually win out, and defend it.
