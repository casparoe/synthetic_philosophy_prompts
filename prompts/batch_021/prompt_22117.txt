I am a PhD student in comparative philosophy, currently writing a dissertation chapter on Roger Ames and Henry Rosemont Jr.'s reconstruction of Confucian role ethics. Ames and Rosemont insist that a Confucian person simply is their constitutive roles and relations—father, ruler, elder sibling, and so on—all the way down, with no residual autonomous self standing apart from those roles to serve as an independent evaluative vantage point. Yet the tradition they draw on also valorizes remonstrance (jian) against wrongdoing kin and superiors, and Mencius goes so far as to deny that killing Jie and Zhou, the deposed final rulers of the Xia and Shang, counted as regicide at all, on the grounds that a ruler who abandons ren and yi thereby forfeits the very title of ruler. Please argue that role ethics, on Ames and Rosemont's own terms, cannot make sense of this normative resource: justifying remonstrance or the deposing of a tyrant requires exactly the kind of role-transcending standard that their relational account of the self rules out by fiat, so the position must either quietly reintroduce an independent self or lose its ability to explain why a role-partner's failures need not simply be endured.
