I chair a five-person jury for a regional literary fiction prize — I've served on it on and off since my second novel was a finalist, on the theory that former contenders make reasonably careful readers — and this year's deliberation has left me with a genuine puzzle rather than just an awkward split vote. Two of our most experienced judges, both of whom have sat on this panel for over a decade and plainly respect each other's taste, spent three full days walking each other through their reasoning for the top prize: a spare, hundred-page interior monologue against a sprawling, multi-generational family saga. By the end, each could restate the other's case as fluently as their own, and neither could name a single consideration about either book that hadn't already been raised and answered by the other. And yet one remains adamant that the monologue deserves the prize, the other equally certain it's the saga. A friend of mine who teaches game theory once told me, half in jest, about a result showing that two honest, rational people who start from the same basic outlook can't remain in genuine disagreement once their reasoning has become fully known to each other — that disagreement surviving full disclosure is itself a sign that somebody isn't reasoning as well as they think, or that the two of them were never really starting from the same place to begin with. Watching my two judges, each too sharp and too fair-minded to dismiss, I can't decide whether that idea is a comfort or an indictment.

We reconvene in ten days to finalize things before the announcement goes to press, and our sponsor, in thirty years of funding this prize, has never allowed it to be split — they want one book, one cheque, one name in the release. I need a short memo to circulate to the whole jury beforehand, recommending one concrete way to arrive at a single winner that the two judges themselves could accept as fair rather than as a chair's ruling by fiat. But I don't want the memo to simply plow past the harder question: it should also take seriously, rather than brush aside, the strongest case for thinking that no such mechanism is really warranted here — that if these two are as rational and well-informed as they appear, their unresolved disagreement may be less a problem for the jury to fix than a sign that literary merit doesn't behave the way a tie-breaking procedure has to assume it does.
