I'm a student representative on my university's Accessibility Services Advisory Board, and this term I'm also taking a seminar on philosophy of psychiatry, where we recently spent two weeks on Ian Hacking's work on classification. His idea, roughly, is that many categories of person — unlike categories in the natural sciences — are subject to a "looping effect": being classified as a certain kind of person changes how people so classified think about and present themselves, which can in turn change the very criteria experts use to pick out the kind, which changes the population again, and so on, so that human kinds end up being, in his phrase, "moving targets" in a way that quarks or rocks are not. One of his central examples was autism, especially how the testimony of the self-organized adult autistic community about masking, sensory experience, and social difficulty has visibly reshaped diagnostic criteria over the last two decades, rather than those criteria simply being checked against some fixed, independently given fact of the matter.

This turns out to bear directly on a live dispute on my board. Our accessibility office currently requires a full clinical diagnostic assessment — costly, and with a 12-to-18-month wait in our area — before it will grant autism-related accommodations such as extended exam time or reduced-stimulation testing rooms. A growing number of students instead arrive with a well-documented self-identification, developed through online autistic communities and validated self-report screening instruments, and ask to be accommodated on that basis; they argue, in terms that echo what we read in class, that clinicians trained on older, externally observed, male-centered behavioral criteria are demonstrably worse than the community itself at recognizing adult and especially non-male presentations. Other board members worry that dropping the clinical requirement invites strategic self-labeling for exam advantages and leaves us with no principled way to adjudicate hard cases. What I can't stop thinking about is that both sides seem to assume a formal diagnosis is simply a more reliable detector of some fixed underlying fact that self-identification approximates less well — and Hacking's picture suggests that assumption itself might not survive if the category is partly constituted by the classified population's own self-understanding.

Could you draft the short memo I'll circulate before our meeting in three weeks? It should recommend one concrete policy for how the office should treat self-identification going forward — keeping the status quo, replacing it outright, and some staged alternative are all fair game — with a realistic timeline for phasing it in, and it should take the strongest version of whichever side it doesn't adopt seriously rather than dismissing it.
