So I'm about a third of the way into a novel where my protagonist, a woman in her fifties, looses her husband suddenly and starts using one of those "griefbot" apps that have been popping up -- you feed it his old texts, emails, and voicemails, and it lets her keep "texting" with a simulation of him. This isn't even really speculative on my part -- there was a whole write-up a few years back about a guy who did basically this with an early GPT-3 project after his fiancée died, and I know at least one company markets an app where you record interviews while you're alive specifically so your kids can "talk" to a chatbot version of you once you're gone. Anyway, I keep getting stuck on how to end her arc, and it occurred to me over dinner with my writing group that Sartre would have Opinions about this, given how obsessed Being and Nothingness is with death, the Look, and bad faith -- even though obviously nothing like a griefbot existed anywhere near his lifetime.

Here's what I'm hoping you can help me reconstruct. Sartre has this argument, against Heidegger, that death isn't really "my" possibility the way we like to think it is -- it's just an absurd, external fact that hands my life over entirely to other people's interpretations of it, whether I like it or not. Given that, would he see a griefbot as an especially aggressive new way of finishing that job, freezing the dead person into a fixed, manipulable object even more totally than death alone already does? And separately: is my widow, by choosing to keep talking to the bot instead of sitting with the anguish of a freedom she never asked for, a pretty clean textbook case of bad faith -- or does his framework actually leave room for a more sympathetic reading of what she's doing? I don't need a full lit review, just your best reconstruction of the position he'd stake out, and if you can, close with the single strongest objection to that position that you don't think he ever adequately answered.
