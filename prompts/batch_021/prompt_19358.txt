I'm a philosophy PhD student in a cross-listed seminar on practical rationality this term, and this week's syllabus paired Herbert Simon's foundational work on bounded rationality with Michael Slote's *Beyond Optimizing* — a juxtaposition that has left me wanting a more careful comparison than either the seminar discussion or the secondary literature I've tracked down actually offers. Simon introduced "satisficing" to describe how real decision-makers, facing genuine limits on time, information, and computational capacity, adopt an aspiration level and stop searching once some option clears it, on the grounds that computing the true optimum would itself be too costly relative to the expected gain from finding it. Slote borrows the same term for a much more ambitious claim: that an agent can be fully rational, and even morally blameless, in choosing an option she knows to be merely "good enough" rather than the best one available, even in cases where nothing — no informational gap, no computational bottleneck, no cost of any kind — stands between her and the better option. On its face this looks like a very different animal from Simon's satisficer, since Slote's whole point is supposed to survive the stipulation that every one of Simon's limiting conditions has been lifted.

Could you compare the two notions of satisficing in enough depth to settle a question my advisor raised, somewhat dismissively, in office hours: does Slote's satisficing consequentialism actually inherit anything substantive from Simon's account beyond the label, or is the shared name mostly a matter of borrowing the intuitive appeal of bounded rationality for a position that needs a wholly independent justification once cost and ignorance have been stipulated away? In working this out, please engage with what I take to be the strongest version of Slote's reply — that a disposition to always squeeze out the marginally better option is itself a kind of low-grade obsessiveness that a reasonable agent is entitled to avoid — and tell me plainly whether that reply gives satisficing consequentialism a rationale that is genuinely continuous with Simon's, or whether it just smuggles a cost (the cost of avoiding "obsessiveness") back in through the side door, in which case the vindication would belong to Simon's framework rather than to any independent thesis of Slote's.
