Over a long career in visual psychophysics I administered the Müller-Lyer illusion to more subjects than I can now recall, so now that I've retired and started reading philosophy of perception (Martin on naive realism, Ayer on the older sense-datum view), the disjunctivism debate feels close to home. I understand the basic shape of the disagreement: naive realists deny that veridical perception and illusion share any fundamental common nature, while defenders of the argument from illusion hold that something like a sense-datum or representational content must be common to both. What I haven't found is a source willing to push the exchange past its opening moves. Could you write me a dialogue between a naive-realist disjunctivist and an intentionalist, arguing specifically over the Müller-Lyer case? Have the intentionalist press the point that nothing goes wrong at the level of mechanism: the same visual system, using the same cues, produces both the illusory judgment and an accurate one in ordinary cases, and since the two experiences are introspectively indistinguishable, positing a fundamental difference in mental kind between them looks unmotivated. The disjunctivist should have a real answer to that, including to the harder question of how a subject could ever tell, from the inside, which state she's in, and not just a restatement of naive realism -- each side should keep pressing the other rather than settle for a polite truce.
