A senior colleague of mine — who taught Leibniz's Theodicy every year for three decades and treated its central claim, that this is the best of all possible worlds, as a live thesis rather than a historical curiosity — died last month after a long illness he discussed, right up to the end, in the same measured terms he used in seminar. I am giving his eulogy at a memorial service full of family and old friends who knew him simply as generous and quick to laugh, plus several colleagues who will notice at once if I flatten his views into Panglossian cheerfulness. Please write this ten-minute eulogy so that it comforts the grieving non-philosophers in the room without having him endorse anything a careful Leibnizian would reject: no fatalism smuggled in under the principle of sufficient reason, no claim that his death specifically was "for the best" (he always insisted the thesis concerns the whole created order, not any single event within it), and no picture of him now watching over us that ignores what pre-established harmony actually implies about whether departed souls keep in touch with the living. If possible, let his own definition of justice as "the charity of the wise" do some real work in the eulogy, rather than just supply its closing line.
