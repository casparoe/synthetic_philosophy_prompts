Over the past year I've been teaching myself Jain philosophy in my spare time. I have no formal background in philosophy or Sanskrit, so I've been piecing things together slowly from secondhand translations of the Tattvartha Sutra and whatever essays and old lecture notes on Jain logic I could track down. The part that has really held my attention is anekāntavāda, the "many-sidedness" doctrine, and specifically the formal machinery built around it: syādvāda's sevenfold predication (saptabhangi), where a single judgment gets qualified into forms like "syat asti" (in some respect, it is), "syat nasti" (in some respect, it is not), their conjunction taken one after another, and then the harder fourth case, "syat avaktavyam," where asserting both at once is supposedly impossible to put into words, because speech unfolds in sequence and simultaneous being-and-non-being does not. The standard way of keeping all this consistent is to index each predication to substance, place, time, and mode, so that a jar "is," relative to its own clay, and "is not," relative to a piece of cloth, without any contradiction.

What I can't get past is an objection that keeps surfacing in the more skeptical secondary reading I've done, and it works on two levels. On the level of any single judgment, there's a dilemma: either the substance/place/time/mode qualifiers genuinely pick out different respects, in which case there was never a real contradiction to tame in the first place and syādvāda hasn't earned any special logical status beyond ordinary careful predication, or the qualifiers don't fully do that work, in which case the doctrine is smuggling a real violation of non-contradiction in under cover of "syat." On the level of the doctrine as a whole, there's a nastier version of the same worry: Jain philosophers present anekāntavāda itself as simply, unconditionally true, as the correct account of how reality is -- so if unqualified, one-sided (ekānta) claims are supposed to be suspect, the doctrine looks like it is quietly exempting itself from its own rule, and if it is instead only true "in some respect," a rival philosopher seems free to just decline that respect and walk away unrefuted.

I'd like to see two people who actually know this material argue it out, rather than read one more summary that ends with the elephant parable. Could you write me a dialogue -- between a Jain philosopher and a rival logician in the mold of the classical Naiyāyika critics of Jainism, still going at it after a public debate has technically ended -- in which the Jain figure has to answer the objection on both levels using the real technical resources (the substance/place/time/mode qualifiers, the sequential-versus-simultaneous distinction, the avaktavya clause, the nayavāda theory of partial standpoints) rather than just repeating that everyone only sees part of the picture, and the logician has to do more than restate the law of non-contradiction and call it a day? I want each of them genuinely responding to the other's last point every time, with neither one obviously winning, so that I'm left to work out for myself who actually has the stronger position.
