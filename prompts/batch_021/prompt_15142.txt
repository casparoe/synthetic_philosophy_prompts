Since retiring a few years ago, I have finally had the leisure to read philosophy properly, something my working life as a biochemist never left much room for. I recently described an old episode from that working life to a small reading group I have since joined, expecting a quick consensus, and instead got a fairly even split of opinion, which has left me wanting a more careful verdict than any of us could manage over coffee.

Early in my career, I was the senior author on a paper with a very capable postdoc who wanted our abstract to state plainly that a particular enzyme was responsible for a metabolic shift we had observed in two of three animal cohorts. The statistics were suggestive rather than conclusive, and we had no mechanistic pathway yet to underwrite the causal claim. I insisted on softening this to something like "is associated with" until the picture was clearer. She thought this was needless cowardice: in her view, once you privately believe, given the weight of the evidence, that something is overwhelmingly likely to be true, to say anything more cautious in print is its own species of dishonesty, a way of protecting your own reputation at the expense of the field's understanding. I thought, and still think, that stating a causal claim you cannot yet back up is its own species of recklessness, whatever the eventual outcome. As it happened, the outcome favored her: a decade later, an independent group established the very mechanism she had assumed all along, and her flat statement turned out to have been exactly right.

That resolution unsettles me rather more than it settles anything. It feels wrong to say she was simply vindicated just because things eventually broke her way, yet I find it hard to pin down what, exactly, would have been wrong with what she did at the time, given that her reading of the evidence had been the correct one from the start. I would value your view on whether she was at fault in stating things as flatly as she did and, if so, what precisely the fault consisted in -- and, more generally, what ought to license a scientist, or anyone, to state something outright rather than merely venture it.
