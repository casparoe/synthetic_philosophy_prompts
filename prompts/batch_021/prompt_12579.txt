So this is going to sound like a wierd way to process something but bear with me. Three weeks ago I donated a kidney to a total stranger — not a family member, no sob story, just signed up through a nonprofit that matches non-directed donors to whoever's next on the transplant list. Everyone in my life started calling me a hero/saint, which felt nice for about a week, until a friend who's deep in the effective altruism world told me, basically, "don't get a big head, the math says you were kind of supposed to do that." That comment has been eating at me, so I went down a rabbit hole on the philosophy of supererogation and wrote up my confusion as an essay instead of, like, a normal person. Pasting it below — genuinely want to know if I'm butchering the concepts, and separately whether this question is actually interesting or just me overthinking a nice thing I did.

Urmson basically invented the modern category of supererogation in his 1958 "Saints and Heroes" paper, pointing out that a morality with only three boxes — obligatory, permissible, forbidden — can't make sense of the guy who jumps on a grenade. That act isn't required (we don't blame the soldiers who don't jump), but it's also not "permissible" in the thin sense that ordering the soup instead of the salad is permissible. It's praiseworthy precisely because it wasn't demanded of him.

Here's the paradox I can't get past though: if jumping on the grenade really is the best available act, more good done than any alternative, why isn't it required? Any theory that tells you to always do whatever has the most value (basically textbook utilitarianism) seems to swallow supererogation whole — "optional but best" collapses into "the one obligatory act," and there's no room left for moral extra credit. Scheffler patched this with agent-centered prerogatives, basically permission to weigh your own life and plans more heavily than the math says you should, which lets the best act stay non-obligatory. Fine, except that move feels like it was built backward from wanting supererogation to exist, not from any independent motivation for it.

Which is where the kidney thing actually bites. Singer's old drowning-child argument says if you can prevent something very bad at small cost to yourself, your obligated to, not just encouraged to. A kidney, statistically, costs the donor something like a slightly elevated long-term health risk — smaller than plenty of stuff we treat as no big deal. So my friend has a point: by the logic I already claim to believe, this wasn't supererogatory at all, it's just what the argument requires, and I only get to feel special because most people don't follow their own stated principles that far.

So which is it, am I the grenade guy, or did I just pay a bill that turned out smaller than advertised? And if nobody can agree where the "beyond duty" line sits even for a case this concrete and personal, is that a sign the category is doing real philosophical work, or a sign it's mostly a polite fiction we use so we don't have to call non-donors negligent?

Anyway, be honest, especially about the Scheffler bit — I feel like I'm missing something there.
