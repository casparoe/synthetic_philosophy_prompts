I'm taking a philosophy elective this semester, and we recently spent two class periods on the First Essay of Nietzsche's *Genealogy of Morality*, where he traces "good" and "evil" back to the ressentiment of a powerless priestly caste against the nobles it envied. Our teacher had to run to a meeting right when class ended, so when two people in my discussion group kept arguing about it in the hallway afterward, nobody was around to referee, and I've been turning their exchange over in my head ever since without being able to decide who actually had the better of it.

It started when Aisha argued that Nietzsche's story -- where the priestly caste, unable to act out its hostility toward the strong, relabels the nobles' pride and spontaneity as "evil" and its own patience and humility as "good" -- amounts to a debunking argument: since our modern good/evil vocabulary descends mostly from that reactive relabeling rather than from any clear-eyed insight, we have good reason to distrust it. (1) Marcus shot back that this is a textbook genetic fallacy: explaining where a value came from doesn't tell you whether it's worth keeping now, any more than showing that the modern theory of evolution grew out of Malthusian anxieties about overpopulation would show the theory is false. If Nietzsche wants to show pity or equality are bad, he needs an actual argument against them, not an origin story. (2) Aisha answered that this assumes Nietzsche is trying to win the same kind of argument -- to show a claim like "pity is good" is false the way you'd refute a scientific claim -- when he explicitly denies there are moral facts of that sort to get right or wrong in the first place. What the genealogy attacks is the sense that morality speaks with one unconditional voice handed down by reason or God, rather than being one contingent evaluation among others; you don't have to refute a claim to loosen its grip on someone, just show them clearly how they came to hold it. (3) Marcus replied that this concession costs Nietzsche more than Aisha thinks: if merely tracing a value to its contingent, power-driven origin is enough to strip it of authority, that should undercut Nietzsche's own favorites too -- self-overcoming, honesty about one's drives, strength -- since by his own account every evaluation, including his, expresses some will to power. So either the debunking is completely general, and his values lose their grip as well, or he owes us some further reason, beyond mere contingency of origin, for why slave morality alone should lose its hold on us. (4) Aisha answered that Nietzsche never claims to speak from outside all perspectives -- that's exactly what he says nobody can do -- so the real comparison isn't "my values have no origin story and yours do," it's that a perspective which can recognize and affirm its own contingent, willed character is healthier and more honest than one that has to disguise its contingency as necessity in order to function at all.

Could you go through moves (1) through (4) and say, for each one, who has the better of the argument and why, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I would also like to know whether either of them is relying on a common misreading of the Genealogy, and whether there is some further consideration -- one that neither of them raised -- that you think should actually settle, or at least reframe, the disagreement.
