Write a dialogue between two decision theorists arguing about Kavka's toxin puzzle -- not the basic setup (assume they both already know it), but the harder question buried in it: can a fully rational agent who knows their own rationality actually form the intention to drink the toxin, or is that intention necessarily a kind of fiction? One character is a resolute-choice type: you settle on the plan at midnight, and a truly resolute agent then goes ahead and drinks the toxin the next day too, because backing out would undermine the very policy that made the intention rational in the first place. The other thinks intending requires actually expecting you'll follow through, and since a rational person can see perfectly well they'll have zero reason to drink once the money's already locked in, they know they won't do it -- so they can't really form the intention, at best their kidding themselves. Push them to engage each other's strongest move instead of just restating their own view -- e.g. does the resolute-choice move just quietly redefine what counts as a "reason" to make the puzzle disappear, and does the skeptic's view accidentally prove that almost no future-directed intention is ever fully rational.
