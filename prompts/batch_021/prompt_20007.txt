A friend and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about constitutional precommitment, and I don't think either of us persuaded the other. We'd both just worked through Jon Elster's discussion of Ulysses and the Sirens for a seminar on constitutional theory, and my friend pushed the analogy further than I was willing to follow. Her claim was that the only thing that ever legitimates an entrenched constitutional constraint — a bill of rights, a supermajority threshold for amending the constitution, an independent central bank insulated from ordinary electoral politics — is that it functions exactly as the ropes on the mast did for Ulysses: protecting a polity's settled, long-run judgment from some passing fit of panic, prejudice, or enthusiasm.

From this she concluded that any such constraint forfeits its justification the moment the majority it blocks is behaving calmly and deliberately rather than impulsively. If, say, a supermajority spent years rationally and unhurriedly deliberating before deciding to strip some minority of a property right, then on her view a constitutional provision standing in the way would have nothing left to appeal to, since there is no Siren song to protect anyone from, and the constraint would just be one generation imposing its will on another under cover of Ulysses's name. I couldn't put together a clean rebuttal at the table, though something about the argument has kept nagging at me since. Is my friend's account of what justifies constitutional entrenchment basically right, and does her conclusion about calm, deliberate majorities really follow from the Ulysses analogy?
