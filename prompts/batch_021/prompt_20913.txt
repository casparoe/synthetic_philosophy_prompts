I'm a PhD student in social psychology currently auditing a graduate seminar in epistemology, partly to get a firmer grip on the methodological debates about replication that keep coming up in my own field. Last week the seminar worked through the dogmatism paradox -- the argument, usually traced to an unpublished lecture by Kripke and given its canonical statement in print by Gilbert Harman, which seems to show that if I know that p, I am thereby entitled to know in advance that any future evidence against p would be misleading, and so entitled to discount that evidence before I have even seen it. The discussion turned personal rather quickly, because I am currently in more or less exactly the situation the paradox describes: my advisor wants me to pre-register a large direct replication of a finding from our subfield that I would say, pre-theoretically, I already 'know' to be true, and to commit in writing beforehand to exactly how much a null result should count against it. Two of my labmates think the paradox is a neat piece of logic-chopping with no real bearing on this, since the whole point of a pre-registered commitment is to treat the not-yet-collected data as a genuine test rather than to pre-judge it; a third labmate thinks Harman's argument points to a real, underappreciated tension between taking yourself to already know a hypothesis and simultaneously promising, in good faith, to let evidence you haven't seen yet count against it.

Could you describe the most important implications of the dogmatism paradox for this kind of pre-registration commitment, and more generally for how someone who takes themselves to know that a hypothesis is true should decide, in advance, how much evidential weight to assign to results that haven't come in yet? I'd be especially interested in your take on whether the standard escape routes from the paradox -- defeasibility or sensitivity-based accounts of knowledge, or the more recent literature on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia -- actually dissolve this tension for a working scientist, or merely relocate it somewhere else.
