Three months ago, at forty-one, I was formally diagnosed as autistic, after my daughter's own diagnosis sent me back through old school reports and journals looking for an explanation of my own history. A friend finishing a philosophy PhD keeps urging me to read Ian Hacking on "looping effects" and "interactive kinds," and I find I can no longer decide whether the diagnosis has uncovered something that was quietly true of me all along, or has partly manufactured the person I have since become, someone who now reaches for words like masking, stimming, and sensory load that never occurred to me to use before. I would like a dialogue that does justice to both sides of this, written by people who actually know the literature rather than a pop-science gloss on it.

Please write a dialogue between two philosophers of psychiatry: one who defends autism as a genuine natural kind that diagnosis merely discovers, and one who, following Hacking, argues that it is a paradigm interactive kind whose looping effects run all the way down. They should engage directly with each other's strongest evidence — heritability and neurodevelopmental findings on one side; the DSM-5 dissolution of Asperger's into the broader spectrum, the recent surge in adult self-diagnosis, and phenomena such as the "double empathy problem" on the other — rather than talking past one another, and at least one of them should concede a genuine weakness in their own position before the dialogue closes.
