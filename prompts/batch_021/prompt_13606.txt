So this happened at a dinner thing a few weeks ago. My friend Owen, who's finishing an econ PhD, likes to spring "decision theory party tricks" on people after a couple drinks, and this time it was basically the actual Allais paradox experiment. Round 1: pick between (A) a guaranteed $1 million, or (B) an 89% chance of $1 million, 10% chance of $5 million, and 1% chance of nothing. Round 2: pick between (C) an 11% chance of $1 million (89% chance of nothing), or (D) a 10% chance of $5 million (90% chance of nothing). Basically everyone at the table definately picked A then D, same as most people apparently do in the real studies, and it kicked off a whole side-argument between two of my friends, Mara and Diego, that outlasted dessert.

Diego's opening move (1) was that picking A-then-D is just inconsistent: both rounds reduce to the exact same underlying choice -- a sure $1 million versus a shot at $5 million -- just diluted with an extra dose of some shared outcome that isn't supposed to affect anything (a sure $1 million in round one, a sure nothing in round two). So preferring A over B locks you into preferring C over D, and picking D anyway just means your gut is bad at this, not that you have some deeper preference. Mara (2) said that "diluting" move quietly assumes the very axiom that's actually in dispute -- Allais's whole point was that dropping from 100% certainty to anything less is a different kind of change than moving between two risky probabilities, so caring specifically about eliminating risk isn't confused, it's a value the axiom just has no slot for. Diego (3) came back with what he called the money-pump version: picture a two-stage lottery where 89% of the time the whole thing ends immediately with nothing, and 11% of the time you're walked to a second table and asked, fresh, to choose between a sure $1 million or the $5-million-ish gamble -- and whichever you pick there is exactly what determines whether you end up living inside option C or option D. He said someone with Mara's preferences would, if asked in advance, plan to take the sure thing at that second table, since that's what they picked in round one -- but that locks them into C, even though they'd just told us they actually prefer D, so somewhere between planning and arriving they'd have to flip, and a patient enough bookie could charge you once to lock in the plan and again to let you break it. Mara (4) answered that this only works if you grant that your choice at the second table has to match some "context-free" version of that same choice, and she doesn't grant that -- a rational person can resolve in advance to stick with the plan precisely because how a gamble arose is allowed to matter to them, and its refusing to honor your own earlier resolution that makes you exploitable, not having Allais-style preferences in the first place. Diego's closing line was that "sticking to a plan for no reason except that you made it" is just dynamic inconsistency wearing a trenchcoat.

Nobody at the table actually settled it, we just drifted off to coffee, and it's been rattling around in my head since. I've got zero background in this stuff, which is exactly why I can't tell whose logic actually holds up. Could you go through the exchange move by move -- (1) through (4) -- and say who comes out ahead at each specific step, rather than just handing down one overall winner? I'd also genuinely like to know if there's some consideration you think both of them are completely missing.
