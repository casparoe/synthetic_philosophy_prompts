Last night a friend and I had a long argument about buck-passing accounts of value—the view that for something to be good just is for there to be reasons to favor it in some way. She invoked the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem, using the standard case of an evil demon who threatens to torture your family unless you admire him: since this clearly gives you a reason to admire the demon without making him any more admirable, she took the case to show that fitting-attitude analyses simply cannot succeed, and that this is by now a settled, fatal objection that no serious metaethicist still bothers trying to answer. I wasn't convinced that a single thought experiment could sink an entire research program, but I couldn't quite pin down where her reasoning went wrong. Is she right that the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem amounts to a decisive refutation of buck-passing theories, or is the actual state of the debate more complicated than that?
