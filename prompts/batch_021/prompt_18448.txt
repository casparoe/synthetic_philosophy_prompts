I'm a software engineer working mostly on ML infrastructure, with no formal philosophy training beyond books and podcasts consumed on my commute. I've been chewing on Fodor's language-of-thought hypothesis and how it holds up against modern deep learning, and wrote up my thinking below to pressure-test it before bringing it to a work reading group. I'd like your comments specifically on three things: whether I'm representing Fodor and Pylyshyn's actual argument fairly; whether the inference I draw from transformer behavior to a philosophical conclusion is legitimate or a well-known kind of mistake; and whether the worry I raise at the end is a real, recognized problem in this literature or something I'm overstating out of inexperience. Please don't just tell me it's an interesting attempt -- I want to know exactly where the argument is weakest.

Working title: Do transformers refute the systematicity argument?

Fodor's language-of-thought hypothesis holds that propositional thought is the manipulation of symbols in an internal system with a compositional syntax and semantics -- "Mentalese" -- so that a complex thought's content is built from the contents of its parts together with how they're combined. The centerpiece of the case Fodor and Pylyshyn made for this, in their well-known 1988 attack on connectionism, was systematicity: a mind that can think "John loves Mary" can, as a matter of psychological law, also think "Mary loves John," even without ever having encountered that sentence before. On their account this is a mystery for any architecture lacking constituent structure, since nothing but shared internal structure explains why the two abilities always travel together; a plain associative network trained to represent one sentence would have no principled reason to also represent the other, so systematicity would be a lucky accident rather than the rule it obviously is. From this they concluded that any system exhibiting real systematicity must implement a classical, symbolic architecture underneath, whatever its surface form.

This struck me as exactly the kind of claim current language models should let us stress-test, because transformers plainly produce and use novel combinations of things they were never trained on verbatim, and they do it with no discrete symbol tokens or classical variable-binding at all -- just matrix multiplications over dense, superposed vectors mediated by attention. That looked to me like an existence proof that systematicity doesn't require a Fodor-style architecture, just a sufficiently rich distributed one, which would falsify the "must implement classical structure" claim rather than merely complicate it.

But I've talked myself out of being sure this settles anything. First, the empirical picture is messier than "transformers do it": compositional-generalization benchmarks like SCAN show plenty of sequence models failing badly at exactly this kind of recombination, and even transformers need specific training tricks to do well on the harder splits, so "transformers" is not the clean, single counterexample I wanted. Second, and this is the part I can't get past: I don't see a principled way, from outside the network, to tell whether a set of attention-weighted vectors "has constituent structure" in whatever sense the argument needs, rather than merely behaving as if it did on the input-output profile. Smolensky's old tensor-product proposals tried to build classical constituent structure directly inside a connectionist vector space, which makes me suspect "distributed" and "compositional" were never cleanly opposed to begin with -- so maybe what large models show isn't that Fodor and Pylyshyn were wrong, but that the classical/connectionist dichotomy their whole argument depends on was never as sharp as the 1988 paper needed it to be. And I don't know how to settle that without a criterion for "constituent structure" that isn't just gerrymandered to fit whichever side you already wanted to win.
