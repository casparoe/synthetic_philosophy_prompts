So this went down in the #off-topic Slack channel at work and it's been stuck in my head since. Two people on my team, Priya and Marco, got into it about 'surrogate goals' as a defense against acausal threats -- the idea, from AI-safety/rationalist circles, that a sufficiently good predictor (like a future misaligned AI) could threaten to hurt something you value without ever causally interacting with you, just by simulating you well enough. The proposed fix is you deliberately get yourself (or an AI) to also genuinely care a bit about some made-up decoy goal, so if a threat ever comes in, you can give in on the decoy instead of your real values and lose nothing that actually matters.

Priya said this basically solves the problem: its cheap insurance, no real downside.

Marco said its circular: the trick only works if the threatener accepts the decoy as worth threatening, but anything smart enough to threaten you acausally in the first place is smart enough to notice the decoy isn't your real value and just threaten that instead -- so it only protects you from threateners too weak to matter anyway.

Priya came back with: it doesn't need to fool anyone completely, it just has to make going after the real goal costly or uncertain enough that the decoy becomes the rational target.

Marco said that move can rescue pretty much any half-baked precommitment scheme, and that 'it shifts the expected value a bit' stops being a real argument at some point.

Neither budged, and it kind of ate the channel for an hour. Can you go through this move by move, say who's got the better of it at each point -- not just who wins overall -- and flag anything important you think both of them are missing?
