And here's the student's answer:

"(a) The argument turns on the trichotomy thesis: given some covering value, any two options must be better than, worse than, or equally as good as one another. In Raz's case, the lawyer (L) doesn't seem better than the clarinetist (C), nor worse. They can't be equally good either, because if they were, a slightly sweetened version of the law job (L+, say with a modest raise) would have to come out better than C, and intuitively it doesn't. So L+ is not better than C, not worse than C, and not equal to C: none of the three trichotomy relations hold, and Raz concludes that L and C are incomparable.

(b) Chang resists this by denying that better/worse/equal exhausts the field. She adds a fourth relation, parity: L and C, and L+ and C, can simply be on a par with each other. This is essentially Griffin and Parfit's idea that some options are only roughly or imprecisely equal in value — close enough that a small enough improvement doesn't automatically flip who wins. Because L+ and C are on a par rather than unrelated, the argument's last step, from 'no trichotomy relation' to 'no relation at all,' doesn't go through.

(c) I'd say the two terms pick out the same underlying fact rather than two different ones: if there's no common scale for weighing a legal career against a musical one, then nothing can make L better than, worse than, or equal to C, and 'nothing can make one better, worse, or equal' is just what it is for two things to be incomparable. So the reason Raz uses the words interchangeably is that in cases like this, incommensurability (no shared scale) and incomparability (no value relation) come to the same thing — you get the second because you have the first. Chang's parity relation doesn't really challenge that link; it just gives us a more fine-grained description of the resulting incomparability, seperating cases where the items are 'close' (on a par) from cases where they're simply unrelated."
