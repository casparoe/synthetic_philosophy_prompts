quick recap in case i'm misremembering the standard version. imagine a raffle, 1000 tickets sold, exactly one winner guaranteed. for any single ticket, say #457, the chance it wins is basically nothing, so it seems totally reasonable to just believe "457 is gonna loose." but that logic applies to every ticket equally, so if you go around believing all 1000 of those "will lose" statements, you've committed yourself to believing nobody wins - which you also know for a fact is false, since somebody has to win. 1000 individually reasonable beliefs, contradictory as a group.

the preface paradox is the same shape but feels totally different in spirit. somebody spends years writing a big nonfiction book and checks every single fact carefully before it goes to print. they believe every sentence, because they actually did the legwork on each one. but in the preface they write the standard humble line, something like "given how big this project was, i'm sure a mistake or two got through" - and honestly they're probably right to believe that too. so again: a pile of individually solid beliefs (every sentence, plus "not every sentence is true") that can't all be true together.

what's been bugging me is the raffle setup reminds me a ton of something i half remember from reading about court cases - some hypothetical about a bus company that owns something like 80% of the buses in a city (might be butchering the actual number), somebody gets hit by a bus but can't tell which company's bus it was, and the question is whether "80% of buses in this city belong to company X" is good enough on its own to make company X pay up. from what i remember the usual answer is no, courts don't like that kind of bare statistical reasoning, even when the odds are way higher than "more likely than not." felt to me like literally the same structure as the raffle - a fact about the whole group that doesn't actually pick out anything special about the one case in front of you.

so here's my attempted fix, at least for the raffle/bus version: maybe the actual mistake is forming the belief "457 will lose" in the first place. all you really have is a fact about the group as a whole, nothing that distinguishes ticket 457 from ticket 892 - swap the number and your "evidence" is word for word identical. if that's your situation, maybe you're only allowed to believe the group-level fact (999 out of 1000 tickets lose), never the specific one, and the paradox never gets started because you never actually collect 1000 contradictory beliefs in the first place.

where i get stuck: that fix doesn't seem to touch the book case at all. the evidence for each sentence isn't some anonymous fact about a group - the author actually went and checked that particular sentence, it's not interchangeable with the evidence for some other random sentence the way ticket 457 is interchangeable with ticket 892. so my "only believe group-level facts" rule has no bite there, yet the exact same contradiction shows up anyway. so either the book needs a totally seperate kind of fix, or my "fix" was never doing the work i thought it was, and it just happened to feel right for the raffle. genuinely don't know which and it's bugging me.
