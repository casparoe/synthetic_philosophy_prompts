"Sharon Street's Darwinian Dilemma is an argument against realist theories of value, i.e., the view that there are evaluative truths that hold independently of what anyone happens to think or value. Street's starting point is the empirical observation that our evaluative attitudes have been massively shaped by natural selection: ancestors who valued their offspring's welfare, who felt gratitude toward reciprocators, who feared certain genuine dangers, tended to leave more descendants than ancestors with different attitudes. Given this, Street thinks the realist owes us an account of how this evolutionary influence relates to the independent evaluative truths that realism posits.

She argues there are two possible accounts of this relationship. On the tracking account, natural selection favored ancestors who could perceive the independent evaluative truths, because correctly perceiving them was itself fitness-enhancing (e.g., it was adaptive to recognize that one's offspring's welfare really does matter). On the adaptive link account, there is no such relationship: selection pressures shaped our evaluative attitudes for reasons that have nothing to do with whether those attitudes are actually true.

Street thinks the tracking account is a non-starter, essentially for the reason Mackie identifies with his argument from queerness: if moral facts really did shape the trajectory of natural selection by being perceived by our ancestors, they would have to be strange entities unlike anything else recognized by biology, and our faculties for detecting them equally strange. Since we shouldn't multiply queer entities beyond necessity, the tracking account should be rejected, leaving only the adaptive link account. But on the adaptive link account we have no non-circular reason to think our evaluative judgments are reliably true, so realists are stuck with a global skepticism about their own moral views. Since both horns are unacceptable, Street concludes, like Mackie, that realism is false and that our moral judgments are all systematically false, since there is no evaluative reality for them to correspond to.

One prominent realist response is David Enoch's 'Third Factor' reply. Enoch accepts that natural selection shaped our evaluative attitudes without tracking independent truths, but denies this leaves realism defenseless. He proposes that some third factor could explain both why we have the evaluative attitudes we have and why, coincidentally, those attitudes line up with independent evaluative truths, without either one causing the other -- for instance, if survival and reproduction are (at some very general level) genuinely good, it's unsurprising that creatures shaped by selection for survival and reproduction would also come to believe this, without the goodness of survival needing to have caused the selection pressure itself. Enoch is careful to note that this move works best for very general and almost trivial evaluative truths, and is much harder to run for specific, contested first-order moral claims."
