I spent my working life as an experimental physicist, and only in retirement have I started reading philosophy in any depth. The split-brain literature has become something of a fixation for me -- it feels like the closest thing philosophy of mind has to a genuine controlled experiment bearing on a metaphysical question, namely the unity of consciousness. What I can't settle for myself is the disagreement between two positions that each seem to accommodate the evidence equally well. The duality view, in Puccetti's mold, holds that commissurotomy simply unmasks two streams of consciousness that were there all along, ordinarily coordinated well enough to pass for one. Bayne's "switch model" denies this: there remains only a single stream, realized first by one hemisphere and then the other, switching according to whichever hemisphere currently has control of behavior. Could you write me a dialogue between a proponent of each view? I'd like them to be equally sharp, genuinely responding to each other's arguments rather than restating their own, and I'd especially like them to fight it out over the chimeric-figures tests, the reports of faster, apparently parallel performance on bilateral visual search tasks, and the cross-cuing behavior sometimes invoked to explain away apparent hemispheric independence. Ideally each should end up conceding which piece of evidence is hardest for their own position to accommodate.
