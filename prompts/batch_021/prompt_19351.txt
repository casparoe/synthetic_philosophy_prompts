I've spent the last few weeks working through Alfred North Whitehead's *The Aims of Education and Other Essays* with a reading group that grew out of a rationalist-adjacent forum I've followed for years, and I keep noticing how much of the real argument lives in his more idiosyncratic machinery rather than in the quotable slogans. Everyone remembers "inert ideas," but the actual argument depends on the full cycle he lays out in "The Rhythm of Education" — Romance, Precision, and Generalization — and on his repeated insistence that curricula fixed by external examining bodies are fundamentally at odds with genuine intellectual growth. Whitehead died in 1947, long before anything resembling the current testing-and-accountability apparatus in Anglophone schooling, or anything like adaptive AI tutoring software, existed, and I would like your help working out how his own framework applies to both.

On one hand, there is the accountability regime that now governs a great deal of public education: state tests tied to school funding and teacher evaluation, PISA-ranking anxiety, and "teaching to the test" as an openly acknowledged practice — exactly the kind of externally imposed, uniform syllabus Whitehead spent much of the book attacking. On the other hand, there are adaptive AI tutoring systems that promise something Whitehead insisted on and thought ordinary school systems structurally could not deliver: genuinely individualized pacing, letting each student move through romance, precision, and generalization on their own schedule rather than a class's lockstep one. These two developments seem to sit in some tension with each other, and I'm not convinced they resolve the same way inside his framework.

Rather than the usual shallow exercise of guessing whether a historical figure would have "liked" or "disliked" something, I'd like you to argue this through the internal logic of his actual position. Would he read adaptive tutoring as finally solving the problem he blamed on external examinations, or would he suspect that a system engineered to produce correct answers efficiently is just a more sophisticated way of manufacturing inert ideas — precision produced without the romance that's supposed to precede it, and no real generalization at the end? And does his critique of external examinations straightforwardly transfer onto modern standardized testing, or does the argument depend on features of 1920s British schooling that no longer hold?
