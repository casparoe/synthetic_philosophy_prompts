I've been trying to work out what it would take for a mental state to be "about" something - to have genuine content - on a purely naturalistic picture of the mind, and I keep running into a stand-off between two camps that I haven't been able to adjudicate on my own.

Teleosemantic theorists hold that a state's content is fixed by the biological function it was selected to perform: a frog's "fly-detector" represents flies, and only flies, because responding to flies is what that mechanism was selected to do, so a state triggered by a BB pellet flicked past the frog's eye counts as a misfire rather than evidence that the content was "fly-or-BB" all along. Their opponents, who ground content in current causal or counterfactual dependencies rather than evolutionary history, think this gets the order of explanation backwards, and they like to press the case of "Swampman": a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a person that is spontaneously assembled when lightning strikes a swamp, with no evolutionary or learning history whatsoever, who nonetheless seems to recognize his friends and reason about his surroundings just as fluently as the original would have.

Could you write me a dialogue between a committed teleosemantic theorist and a causal/counterfactual theorist of content, in which each uses Swampman and the frog-and-BB-pellet case to press the other, and each has to actually engage with the specific difficulty the other's case raises for their own view, rather than simply restating their own position?
