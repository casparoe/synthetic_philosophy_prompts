Here's the question as it was given on the sheet:

"Fitch's paradox purports to derive, from two apparently modest premises, the wildly implausible conclusion that every truth is actually known. (a) State the knowability principle and the non-omniscience premise using K for 'somebody knows that' and ◊ for metaphysical possibility, and walk through the derivation of the contradiction that results from applying the knowability principle to the instance p ∧ ¬Kp. (b) Name the two structural principles governing K that are doing the work in the derivation, and say exactly where each one gets used. (c) A friend of yours says: 'the very last step of the derivation is only classically valid, so an anti-realist who's already independently committed to intuitionistic logic gets a free pass out of the whole paradox.' Is your friend right? (d) Briefly present one serious strategy from the literature for saving something like the knowability thesis, and say whether you think it succeeds."

And here's the student's answer, copied over verbatim, notation and all:

"(a) KP: for all p, if p then it's possible that someone knows p — formally ∀p(p → ◊Kp). Non-omniscience: some truth is unknown — ∃p(p ∧ ¬Kp). Instantiate KP with the witnessing instance (p ∧ ¬Kp) itself: if (p ∧ ¬Kp) is true then ◊K(p ∧ ¬Kp). Since (p ∧ ¬Kp) actually is true (that's just non-omniscience), we get ◊K(p ∧ ¬Kp). But K(p ∧ ¬Kp) is impossible: if someone knew both conjuncts, they'd know p and know that p is unknown, and since knowledge implies truth, knowing ¬Kp makes ¬Kp true — but they also know p, so Kp is true too. Contradiction. So K(p ∧ ¬Kp) is necessarily false, which gives ¬◊K(p ∧ ¬Kp) — directly contradicting what KP gave us two steps back. So KP plus non-omniscience is inconsistent, which means KP by itself forces the negation of non-omniscience, i.e. ∀p(p → Kp): every truth is actually known.

(b) The two structural principles are factivity (Kp → p) and distribution of K over conjunction (K(p ∧ q) → (Kp ∧ Kq)). Factivity gets used twice in the step above: once on ¬Kp, so that 'knowing ¬Kp' gives you actual ¬Kp, and once implicitly on p itself. Distribution is what lets you split K(p ∧ ¬Kp) into the pair Kp, K¬Kp in the first place, so the two halves can be collided into a contradiction.

(c) My friend is right, and I think this is actually a clean escape for the anti-realist. The step that gets you from 'there's no unknown truth' to 'every truth is known' is a quantifier-shift (from ¬∃x¬A(x) to ∀xA(x)) and that inference needs double-negation elimination, which intuitionistic logic simply doesn't grant. Since verificationists like Dummett are independently committed to intuitionistic logic anyway, they can run the derivation as far as it goes and then just decline to take that last step — the paradox never actually lands on them, it's really a classical-logic artifact that's gotten more mileage in the literature than it deserves.

(d) The strategy I find most promising is the restriction strategy, due to Dummett: rather than letting the knowability principle range over every proposition whatsoever, restrict it to what he calls 'Cartesian' propositions — roughly, propositions p such that supposing p is known doesn't itself generate a contradiction. Since p ∧ ¬Kp is exactly the sort of proposition that can't coherently be supposed known, it falls outside the restricted principle and the paradox can't even get started. I think this mostly works, though it leaves the anti-realist owing an independent, non-question-begging account of which propositions count as 'Cartesian' — otherwise the restriction looks tailor-made to dodge exactly the sentences Fitch's proof needs."
