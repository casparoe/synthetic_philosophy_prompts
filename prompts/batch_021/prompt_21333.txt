I've followed the debates about discounting the future that recur in rationalist and effective-altruist circles, and one point that usually gets treated as settled has always puzzled me. Following Sidgwick, and echoing Frank Ramsey's remark that such discounting is merely a failure of imagination, Parfit devotes much of Reasons and Persons to arguing that pure time preference — discounting future well-being simply because it is future — is indefensible: there's no more reason to discount someone's well-being for lying fifty years ahead than for lying on the other side of the world. This is close to a consensus among philosophers and economists who have written on the ethics of discounting. Given that, I'm puzzled why economists still treat the size of the social discount rate as a live controversy, and why prominent critics of the Stern Review's very low rate, such as Nordhaus, weren't simply committing an elementary philosophical error by pushing for something substantially higher. If the argument against pure time preference is this decisive, shouldn't something close to zero simply be the correct rate for evaluating climate and existential-risk policy, with continued resistance just a sign that economics hasn't caught up with the philosophy?
