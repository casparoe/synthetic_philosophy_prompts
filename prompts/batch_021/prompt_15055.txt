For the past few weeks, I've been working through some philosophy of mind on my own for the Extended Essay component of my IB Diploma, and I've run into a dispute inside higher-order theories of consciousness that I can't find a clean explanation of anywhere, even in the secondary literature I've been able to get my hands on. I started with David Rosenthal's higher-order thought theory: the idea that a mental state is conscious only when there is a distinct, roughly simultaneous, non-inferential higher-order thought representing the subject as being in that state. What's been bothering me and a couple of friends in my school's philosophy club is the so-called targetless higher-order thought case: what happens if the higher-order thought fires — say, representing you as seeing red — even though there is no actual lower-order state for it to be about? Rosenthal seems willing to just accept that the subject would still seem to themselves to be having the experience, purely because of how the higher-order thought characterizes things, and that has always struck us as a strange thing to accept, since it seems to conjure a real conscious experience out of nothing at all.

When I looked further into how people have responded to this, I found that self-representationalists such as Uriah Kriegel deny Rosenthal's picture of the higher-order thought as a separate, only contingently connected state, and instead hold that the higher-order content has to be built into the very same complex state as the lower-order content it is about. This is supposed to make a targetless misfire much harder, or even impossible, to get off the ground, but I don't fully understand why building the two contents into one state is supposed to help, rather than just pushing the very same problem down a level.

Could you write me a dialogue between a proponent of Rosenthal's version of higher-order thought theory and a proponent of self-representationalism, arguing specifically about the targetless higher-order thought problem? I would like the higher-order thought theorist to actually defend Rosenthal's willingness to bite the bullet here rather than caving immediately, and I would like the self-representationalist to explain in some detail why fusing the two contents into a single state is supposed to block the problem, with the higher-order thought theorist pushing back on whether that really solves anything or just relocates it. I don't want either side to come across as an obvious strawman, since I'm trying to work out for myself which view seems more defensible before I commit to a position in my essay, so please have them genuinely respond to each other's specific points rather than talk past one another.
