After nearly thirty years running an experimental lab, I spent my last few years before retiring on my university's faculty research-integrity panel. One case from that period has stayed with me, especially now that I've started reading philosophy in earnest.

Under our policy, as at most research institutions, a misconduct finding requires three separate things to each be established: that the conduct was a significant departure from accepted practice, that it was intentional or reckless, and that the case is supported by a preponderance of the evidence. All three elements are individually necessary, so a single failure blocks a finding.

Our panel of five voted element by element, and each of the three requirements was affirmed by four members — though not the same four each time; every element had exactly one dissenter, and the three dissenters were three different colleagues. On that tally, misconduct was clearly supported. Yet when our chair also polled each of us for a personal, all-things-considered verdict, only two of the five actually said "misconduct" — the other three each rejected a different one of the three elements and so, in their own minds, could not vote that way.

The official finding followed the element-by-element tally, as our procedures required, and the researcher was found to have committed misconduct. But I have never been sure this was really the more defensible way to combine our judgments, rather than trusting the simple poll of final verdicts. Both seem like reasonable ways of aggregating the very same underlying opinions, yet they point in opposite directions. Is there a principled reason to prefer one over the other, or is this simply a structural hazard that any group decision built from individually reasonable judgments can fall into?
