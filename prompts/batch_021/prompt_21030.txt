I've spent the past few weeks working through Richard Jeffrey's treatment of "probability kinematics" in The Logic of Decision, after years of only ever encountering Bayesian updating in the tidy form usually presented on LessWrong and adjacent forums, where you always condition on some proposition you've become certain of. What sent me down this path was a disagreement in my reading group about cases like Jeffrey's own example of judging a piece of cloth's color by candlelight: you never become certain the cloth is green rather than some other color, you just end up more confident of green than you were, without your credence in the alternatives dropping to zero. Strict conditionalization has no way to take input of that kind, which is presumably the whole point of Jeffrey's generalized rule, but the more I read about it, the less confident I am that it is simply an unproblematic generalization of the standard one.

Two things in particular are bothering me. First, Jeffrey's rule is apparently non-commutative in a way strict conditionalization isn't: if you have uncertain evidence bearing on two different propositions, updating on the first and then the second leaves you with different final credences in both of them than updating in the other order — even though the rigidity condition is satisfied at each step regardless of which one you do first. Second, I understand there is a diachronic Dutch book argument for the rule, originally due to David Lewis, meant to vindicate it in the same way Dutch book arguments vindicate strict conditionalization, but that argument seems to have to assume in advance that the rigidity condition will hold for the specific experience you are about to undergo — which feels like it presupposes exactly the thing that makes an update "Jeffrey-appropriate" in the first place, rather than establishing that you should update that way.

Could you compare Jeffrey conditionalization with strict Bayesian conditionalization along these two lines — order-dependence, and the strength of their respective Dutch-book vindications? And could you then tell me which position you find closer to the truth: that Jeffrey conditionalization is a genuine generalization of Bayesian updating, with strict conditionalization as the special limiting case where the evidence happens to be certain, or that these two problems show it is actually a shakier, more situational norm that merely coincides with conditionalization when the evidence is sharp? I would like your own assessment and reasoning, not just a summary of the debate.
