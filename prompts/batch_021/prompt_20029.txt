I spent thirty-five years running an experimental physics lab before retiring, and I have been using some of my newfound free time to finally read philosophy properly. Working through Plato's Meno recently, I was struck by the passage near the end of the dialogue where Socrates compares a man who merely has a correct opinion about the road to Larissa with a man who actually knows it: both will guide a traveller there equally well, and yet Socrates insists that knowledge is somehow worth more, because true opinions are flighty things that need to be tied down by working out the reason for them, rather like the statues of Daedalus, which, according to legend, would wander off unless tethered. A little further reading told me that this passage is the ancestor of what is now called the value problem in epistemology, and that people are still not fully agreed on what, if anything, knowledge has over mere true belief once the truth itself is secured.

This turns out to bear directly on something I still do in retirement: the occasional peer review for my old journal. I was recently sent a paper proposing a deep-learning model that predicts which new compounds will turn out to be superconducting, and which reportedly outperforms the physics-based simulations, grounded in the microscopic theory of superconductivity, that people in my subfield spent decades refining, all while offering no mechanistic story for any individual prediction. I found myself unsure how to weigh this: the model's outputs seem to be reliably true, in something like Meno's sense, without ever being tied down to anything a physicist would recognize as an explanation. Could you set out the most important implications of the Meno problem, i.e., of the debate over why knowledge should be more valuable than mere true belief, for how someone in my position ought to evaluate this kind of work relative to slower, mechanistically grounded predictions that are less reliable but can at least say why they are right? I would especially value your view on whether any of the standard responses to the value problem supply a principled reason to rate explanatory, mechanistic science more highly than accurate yet opaque prediction, or whether my hesitation about the paper is really just an inherited prejudice from an older style of doing physics.
