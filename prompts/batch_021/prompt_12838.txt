This past year, in the gap between the end of my semester abroad and the start of classes, something happened that I still have not fully worked out, and it feels connected to some of the puzzles about practical rationality we have been dissecting this term in my decision theory seminar, even though nothing on our syllabus maps onto it exactly. I was traveling alone through a rural part of the country I had been studying in when my bag, containing my phone, cards, and passport, was stolen while I waited for a connecting bus. The only transport to the regional city that day was a shared van leaving within the hour; missing it meant being stranded for two more days with no money, no phone, and no way of reaching my consulate. A woman selling food near the station saw what had happened and offered to drive me the four hours herself, but she made clear this would cost her a day of business and fuel she could not really spare. She agreed to take me only after I promised that, once I reached the city and had access to my accounts again, I would transfer her a sum we settled on for her trouble. She had no real way to compel this: thanks to the language barrier we had barely spoken beyond logistics, she knew me only by my first name, and once she dropped me at the consulate, there was no realistic way for either of us to find the other again if I simply kept the money.

I did pay her as soon as I could, but for most of the four-hour drive I caught myself rehearsing the opposite argument, and I still cannot fully dismiss it. By the time I would be in a position to pay, the ride would already be behind me; nothing I did afterward could change whether I had reached the city, and she would have no further dealings with me and no means of tracking me down either way, so a payment at that point looked like pure loss with nothing to show for it.

When I described this to a classmate from the seminar as a sort of thought experiment, she thought that, strictly speaking, keeping the money would have been the rational course once I was safe, and that whatever sense I had of owing the driver anything was just a residual feeling with no real backing once you looked at the facts squarely. That conclusion still strikes me as wrong, but I cannot locate the flaw in the reasoning behind it, especially since the driver's whole willingness to help seemed to depend on her correctly judging, before we ever set off, that I was not someone who would reason that way once safe. I would appreciate your take on where exactly my classmate's argument goes wrong, if it does, and on whether there is a principled way of saying what made it rational, or irrational, for me to pay once no further benefit to me hung on it.
