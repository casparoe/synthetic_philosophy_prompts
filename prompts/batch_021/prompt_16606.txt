In rationalist-adjacent discussions of Gödel, his private argument for personal immortality is often folded together with his formal ontological proof of God's existence, as if the two were stages of one continuous deductive project. Both lean on a broadly Leibnizian principle of sufficient reason: the ontological proof formalizes the classical argument that a being possessing all positive properties must necessarily exist, while the letter to his mother contends that a genuinely rational world would not permit a fully developed human personality to be extinguished for no purpose. Given that Gödel was notoriously unwilling to circulate even the ontological proof until he judged it watertight — sitting on it for years before confiding it to Dana Scott — doesn't it follow that the afterlife argument should count as an implicit corollary of the ontological proof, rigorous in the same sense as the incompleteness theorems, rather than merely a biographical footnote, as most historians of logic currently treat it?
