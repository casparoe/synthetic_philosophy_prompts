I've been listening to Douglass's autobiography on my commute and got completely derailed by a part of his life I never learned about in school. For years he was fully Team Garrison on the idea that the Constitution was rotten to the core on slavery -- Garrison's own line for it was a "covenant with death and an agreement with hell." Then around 1851 Douglass does a total 180, breaks with Garrison over it (partly, I think, under the influence of Gerrit Smith and a lawyer named Lysander Spooner who'd argued slavery was already unconstitutional under existing law), and starts arguing the opposite: that if you just read the actual text -- which never once uses the words "slave" or "slavery," only euphemisms like "other persons" -- the Constitution, properly interpreted, is a "glorious liberty document," and its the slaveholders and their courts who are misreading it. His method basically boils down to "ignore what individual framers privately intended, ignore custom and precedent, and go with what the actual words plus stuff like the Preamble commit the country to" -- which, not gonna lie, sounded very familiar to me, it's basically the "go by what the spec literally says, not what you think the original author meant" fight I have at code review all the time, just with unimaginably higher stakes.

That resemblance is what's been bugging me, because it sounds a lot like the textualism/originalism you hear about from the Supreme Court now, and as far as I know that wasn't even a named legal philosophy until Bork and Scalia basically built the modern version of it in the 70s/80s, a century after Douglass died. So: if you dropped Douglass into the present and showed him how originalism actually gets used in real opinions -- say Shelby County v. Holder gutting the Voting Rights Act, or the recurring fights over birthright citizenship -- would he recognize it as his own move, just grown up and put in robes? Or is there real daylight between "read the bare text against the framers' private intentions, in favor of liberty" and what today's originalists actually do? Don't just give me a vibes-based "he'd love it" or "he'd hate it" -- I want the actual case for where the two methods would agree and where they'd quietly part ways, and your best guess at which side he'd land on.
