Here's the question, worth 25 points, from a unit on moral worth:

"Kant distinguishes between acting 'in conformity with duty' and acting 'from duty,' and argues that only the latter has moral worth. (a) Explain this distinction using Kant's own examples, and say why he thinks it matters. (b) Raise one serious objection to Kant's picture from the more recent literature on moral worth. (c) Explain how a proponent of Julia Markovits's 'Coincident Reasons Thesis' would respond to the objection you raised in (b)."

And here's the student's answer, copied over exactly as submitted:

"For Kant, there's a big difference between merely doing what duty requires and doing it because duty requires it. His shopkeeper case makes the point: a shopkeeper who doesn't overcharge an inexperienced customer only because word might get around and hurt his business is acting in conformity with duty — his action matches what morality demands — but he isn't acting from duty, since his motive is just self-interest. Kant says this action has no moral worth, even though it's the 'right' action in the sense of external conformity. Someone who refrains from overcharging because they recognize it would be dishonest, and who acts for that reason alone, is acting from duty, and that action does have moral worth.

Kant pushes this further with the sympathetic person case: someone who is naturally warm-hearted and helps people because it delights them to do so is not, according to Kant, acting from duty — so oddly, their kind act has no more moral worth than the shopkeeper's. Only the person who does good with no inclination to, or even against inclination, shows that they're acting purely from respect for the moral law. This leads to the strange result that the more you have to grit your teeth to do the right thing, the more moral credit you get, and the more naturally decent you are, the less credit you get for it.

This is basically the objection Bernard Williams raises against Kant: requiring the agent's motivating thought to be 'I am doing this because it is my duty' is a kind of moral fetishism, since it makes the agent's relationship to morality itself, rather than to the people morality is actually about, the object of concern. Williams' Huckleberry Finn case shows why this matters: Huck helps Jim escape even though he knows, deep down, that it's the right thing to do, because he's scared of getting caught and punished for it. His moral worth comes from doing the right thing under pressure, not from citing a maxim about duty.

A defender of Markovits's Coincident Reasons Thesis would say Huck's action has moral worth because the reason he's actually motivated by — Jim needs help, Jim is a person and not property — is the same reason that makes helping Jim right, whether or not Huck has an explicit thought about duty in his head. This lets Markovits agree with Williams that you don't need the 'because it's my duty' thought while still keeping the Kantian idea that reasons, and not just any old motive, are what matter for moral worth. So really her view just restates Williams' objection in more Kant-friendly language, which is why it counts as a compromise position rather than a rejection of Kant."
