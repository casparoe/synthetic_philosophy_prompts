A friend of mine — who works in mathematical statistics but treats philosophy mostly as an after-hours amusement — and I spent much of a long dinner last week arguing about van Fraassen's Reflection Principle, which I had just encountered in "Belief and the Will" for a seminar on formal epistemology. Her contention, which I could not manage to rebut on the spot, was that Reflection is not really an independent constraint on rational belief at all, but simply the law of total probability wearing a diachronic disguise: once you let "my credence in A at noon tomorrow will be r" stand for one cell of a partition over epistemically possible futures, the identity that your present credence in A must equal the weighted average of your possible future credences is forced on you the moment you accept the axioms of probability, in exactly the way that a sequence of conditional expectations relative to a filtration is guaranteed to form a martingale. On her reading, van Fraassen's Dutch Book argument for Reflection is idle machinery, since no coherent Bayesian could ever violate a theorem, and the various counterexamples one meets in the literature — cases of anticipated memory loss, foreseen fits of irrationality, and so on — must be quietly importing some further assumption under Reflection's name rather than genuinely refuting anything.

I went along with this for most of the evening, but it has left me uneasy, since I distinctly remember Reflection being taught as a substantive and even controversial principle, one that needs its own defense and admits of real counterexamples, rather than as a disguised bit of arithmetic. Could you help me sort out who is right here? Is my friend correct that Reflection collapses into an instance of the law of total probability, so that the Dutch Book argument and the whole counterexample literature are answering a question that cannot actually arise for a coherent agent? Or is there some further, independent assumption doing the real work in Reflection that the bare probability calculus does not already hand us for free — and if so, what precisely is it, and how does it explain why the principle can fail?
