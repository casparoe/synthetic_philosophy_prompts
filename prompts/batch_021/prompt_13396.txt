I've been reading about money-pump arguments for why a rational agent's preferences must be transitive, usually as part of a broader case for expected-utility maximization. The setup is that if your preferences over three goods—call them A, B, and C—cycle, a trader can exploit you by offering a sequence of trades you'd willingly accept at each step, leaving you back where you started but poorer; and since nothing about your preferences has changed in the process, the whole loop can then be run again and again for further profit. This has always struck me as too easy to defuse. The moment you notice you've been traded from A to B to C and are being offered the trade back to A, you can simply decline it and keep C, which halts any further losses and blocks additional looping, so a sufficiently attentive agent is never pumped for more than a bounded, one-time cost. That makes the argument look to me like it's really about diachronic exploitability, a fact about what happens to inattentive agents across a sequence of choices, rather than about any defect in the preferences themselves at a given moment. Is that the right way to draw the line, or does my proposed escape quietly assume something that an agent with genuinely cyclic preferences wouldn't actually have available to it?
