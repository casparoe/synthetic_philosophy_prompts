A close friend and I spent much of a long drive last weekend arguing about whether two honest, sufficiently rational people can ever legitimately go on disagreeing once they have laid all their evidence and reasoning out on the table. I had recently come across Aumann's agreement theorem in some reading on formal epistemology, and I argued that it settles the question rather decisively: because the theorem shows that two Bayesian agents who share a common prior cannot have common knowledge of differing posterior probabilities for the same event, it seems to follow that any two sufficiently rational, candid people who still disagree after a full and open exchange of views — two colleagues assessing the same evidence differently, say, or the two of us arguing about a policy question — must be falling short somewhere, whether in updating properly on each other's reasoning or in being fully honest about their actual credences. My friend found this much too strong a conclusion to draw from a result about idealized agents, but neither of us could pin down exactly where the inference goes wrong, if it does.

On reflection, I suspect part of the problem might lie with the "common prior assumption" that the theorem quietly relies on, which I had been treating as little more than a harmless idealization — surely any two sufficiently rational people would start out assigning roughly the same probabilities to the relevant possibilities, differing only once they receive different evidence? But I have a nagging sense that this assumption is doing far more work than that, and that it might not be nearly as innocent, or as forced upon us by rationality alone, as I'd assumed. Could you help me work out how much weight Aumann's theorem can actually bear when applied to disagreements like the one my friend and I were having, and clarify just how substantive — and how contested — the common prior assumption really is?
