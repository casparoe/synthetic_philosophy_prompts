The question was:

"State the knowability principle that anti-realists about truth are committed to. Reconstruct Fitch's argument against it as a step-by-step reductio, making explicit which general principles governing knowledge (as opposed to contingent assumptions) are doing the work at each step. Say exactly what conclusion the reductio forces, why that conclusion is meant to be unacceptable, and which premise is standardly given up in response, including at least one specific proposal from the literature for how it is given up."

The submitted answer was:

"Anti-realists about truth (Dummett is the central figure here) hold that truth cannot outstrip what we are capable of knowing, at least in principle. This is usually formalised as the knowability principle (KP): for every true proposition p, it is possible for someone to know that p, symbolised p → ◊Kp.

Fitch's argument is a reductio that starts from KP together with the modest-looking assumption that not every truth is actually known — call this non-omniscience (NO): there is some p such that p is true and p is not known, i.e. ∃p(p ∧ ¬Kp).

Take such a p. Since p ∧ ¬Kp is, by hypothesis, itself true, we can instantiate KP with it directly: (p ∧ ¬Kp) → ◊K(p ∧ ¬Kp). Since the antecedent holds, we get ◊K(p ∧ ¬Kp): it is possible for someone to know both that p and that nobody knows p.

This turns out to be impossible, using only two very weak-looking structural facts about knowledge. First, knowledge distributes over conjunction: if you know a conjunction you know each conjunct, so K(p ∧ ¬Kp) would give us both Kp and K¬Kp. Second, knowledge is factive: whatever is known is true, so from K¬Kp we can infer that ¬Kp is actually true. Putting these together, anyone who knew p ∧ ¬Kp would have to be such that ¬p was known and ¬Kp was true at once — a direct contradiction. So K(p ∧ ¬Kp) is impossible in any circumstance, and since this was shown using nothing but the logic of K, it is necessarily impossible: ¬◊K(p ∧ ¬Kp).

That flatly contradicts what we got from KP two steps back. So the reductio is complete: NO must be false, and its negation is that every truth is known — an absurd 'omniscience thesis,' since there obviously are truths nobody happens to know.

Since distribution and factivity both look constitutive of what knowledge even is, the pressure falls on KP, which is exactly the principle the anti-realist cannot easily give up. The most influential way of saving something in its vicinity is Dummett's proposal, in The Taming of the True, to restrict KP so that it only governs 'Cartesian' propositions — propositions for which supposing they are known does not lead to contradiction on logical grounds alone. Since p ∧ ¬Kp is precisely a non-Cartesian proposition, restricting KP this way blocks the substitution move at the very first step, while leaving the knowability of ordinary truths untouched."
