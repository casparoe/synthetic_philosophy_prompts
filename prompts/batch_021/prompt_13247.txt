I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology, and I've been asked to give the toast at the wedding of two close friends from my department, Aaron and Ines, who met arguing about exactly the kind of question the rest of us spend too much time on: whether it is ever permissible to believe something on less than sufficient evidence. Aaron wrote his qualifying paper defending a fairly hardline Cliffordian evidentialism -- he can recite the shipowner passage from 'The Ethics of Belief' from memory -- while Ines works on James and pragmatism and has spent the better part of three years telling him, in seminar and out of it, that he is wrong about what a 'genuine option' actually demands of a person. Roughly a third of the guests are philosophers who will catch every move; the rest are family and old friends who know Clifford and James only as the surnames of two people who are, as far as they're concerned, simply and finally getting married.

I'd like you to draft this toast (roughly five to seven minutes, spoken). Its comic and emotional engine should be the observation that Aaron, in proposing, has just done the one thing his own dissertation says nobody has the right to do: commit himself, with everything at stake, to a proposition -- 'we will be all right' -- for which no one standing at an altar could possibly have Cliffordian-grade sufficient evidence. Treat this as an affectionate victory for Ines rather than a referendum on whose thesis is correct, and make sure the toast actually uses the structure of their disagreement -- living/dead, forced/avoidable, and momentous/trivial options, the resulting 'genuine option', and James's point that the duty to seek truth and the duty to avoid error can pull in different directions -- rather than gesturing vaguely at faith versus reason. I'd also like you to find a way of working in Clifford's shipowner, since he's become a running joke between the two of them, without making the obvious and rather ominous move of comparing a marriage to a ship that goes down; I'd rather you turn that image on its head than simply avoid it. Above all, the philosophers in the room should feel the argument land precisely, while everyone else should just experience a warm, funny toast about two people they love getting married.
