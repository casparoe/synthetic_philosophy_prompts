A colleague of mine in the economics department, who consults part-time for an effective-altruism-aligned grantmaking foundation, recently asked me to help settle an internal disagreement. The foundation's practice has been to rank competing grants by expected value alone, but one program officer has begun invoking Lara Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility (REU) theory to argue that a funder can be rationally risk-averse — and not merely averse in the ordinary, diminishing-marginal-utility sense that standard expected utility theory already accommodates — when preferring a portfolio of well-evidenced global-health grants over a single higher-expected-value but extremely high-variance bet on reducing existential risk. I understand REU's basic apparatus well enough (the risk function r operating on cumulative rather than outcome-wise probabilities, and its capacity to treat Allais-style preferences that violate the independence axiom as fully rational rather than as errors) to see that this is not simply a relabeled concave utility function, but I have not worked through what actually follows from taking the theory seriously in a case like this one.

For a talk I have been asked to give at our philosophy department's colloquium, could you describe the most important implications of risk-weighted expected utility theory for this kind of cause-prioritization dispute? I am especially interested in whether an REU-based defense of the risk-averse program officer can be reconciled with the standard "shut up and multiply" rejoinder that expected-value reasoning is precisely what rationality demands once the stakes are large enough, and whether there is any principled, REU-internal limit on how much weight the risk function may place on variance before the theory shades from an account of rational risk-attitudes into a vehicle for smuggling in substantive value judgments it was meant to avoid.
