I'm taking a graduate seminar on self-knowledge this term, and for the last two sessions we've been working through Timothy Williamson's anti-luminosity argument in Knowledge and Its Limits: first the general case against luminosity in chapter 4, built around the example of someone who feels freezing cold at dawn and warms up so gradually and imperceptibly that they're hot by noon, and then its extension to the KK principle in chapter 5. As I understand it, the core move is a safety-style margin-for-error premise — roughly, that if you know how you feel at a given moment, your belief couldn't easily have been false, so you must still feel much the same way a millisecond later — which, combined with the assumption that feeling cold is luminous (i.e., that feeling cold guarantees knowing that you feel cold), yields a contradiction once you run the reasoning all the way from dawn to noon.

I find this pretty persuasive, but a friend in the seminar, who wrote her undergraduate thesis on Hintikka and is quite sympathetic to his original defense of the KK principle, thinks it can be resisted, and we've been arguing about it by email all week without either of us budging. Her best case against the margin-for-error premise involves a scenario in which a true belief still seems to count as knowledge even though it could very easily have been false in some indistinguishable nearby case: you drink a glass of water; standing next to you is someone who has just won the lottery and would have spitefully poisoned your water with an undetectable toxin had she lost — but she won, so she didn't, and you still seem to know perfectly well that you're drinking clean water. She takes this to show that the margin-for-error premise is simply false, not something a defender of KK is independently obliged to accept.

Could you write me a dialogue of around 800 words in which two philosophers who've stayed behind after a seminar session like this one work through the disagreement properly? One should be defending the KK principle and should feel free to reach for whatever resource seems most promising at whatever point the argument looks weakest — denying the margin-for-error premise along the lines above, retreating to something like Hintikka's stronger "conclusive evidence" notion of knowledge (for which KK might hold even though it fails for our everyday concept), or suggesting that states like feeling cold might be "self-presenting" in a way that closes the gap between believing and being true. The other should be a convinced Williamsonian who has to do more than restate the original argument: in particular, they should have something substantive to say about why safety-violating cases like the water/lottery one don't actually rescue the margin-for-error premise, and about why even a concession that some sensory states are luminous after all would leave the KK principle exactly as exposed as before. I want real back-and-forth — each of them should respond to what the other has just actually said, concede a point when they're genuinely stuck, and press an advantage when they have one, rather than talking past each other or having either side collapse into a strawman the other can trivially dispose of. Ideally, I should finish the dialogue about as unsure of who has the better of it as I am right now.
