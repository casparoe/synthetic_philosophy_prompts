I've been working through the Transcendental Dialectic of Kant's first Critique with an informal reading group that grew out of a few rationalist forums I've followed for years, and we recently reached his classic objection to the ontological argument: that existence adds no content to a concept, which he illustrates with the example of a hundred merely possible thalers containing exactly as much conceptual content as a hundred real ones. Someone in the group then brought up Gödel's modal ontological argument, which strikes me as having been constructed with something like Kant's objection already in mind.

Rather than treating bare existence as a perfection to be added to God's other attributes the way Anselm or Descartes did, Gödel instead builds a notion of "necessary existence" out of a logic of essences: a property counts as an essence of a thing just in case it necessitates every other property that thing has, and the thing necessarily exists just in case that essence is instantiated in every possible world. Gödel then simply stipulates, as an axiom, that necessary existence in this technical sense is itself a "positive" property that any maximally perfect being must possess, after which the rest of the derivation goes through in ordinary S5 modal logic. As I understand it, when logicians later ran the argument through automated theorem provers, they confirmed that the derivation is indeed formally valid given the axioms, which only sharpens rather than settles the underlying philosophical question.

What I haven't been able to settle, even after some back-and-forth with the group, is what Kant himself would most plausibly conclude if this specific maneuver were put in front of him. Would he treat Gödel's essence-based construction of necessary existence as a genuine technical advance that escapes his 1781 objection, on the grounds that it no longer smuggles in "exists" as an ordinary first-order predicate, or would he see it as simply relocating the very same mistake into the axiom that necessary existence is "positive" — so that the argument's real content reduces to a single conditional, namely that if you grant this one modal axiom, everything else follows? I'd like you to reconstruct Kant's most likely verdict as carefully as you can from his actual critical philosophy, rather than from stock summaries of "existence is not a real predicate." Please state the verdict itself in a sentence or two up front, then reconstruct the reasoning you think Kant would genuinely give for it, and close by flagging the point or points at which you are least confident that your reconstruction tracks Kant's own commitments rather than a modern gloss placed on them.
