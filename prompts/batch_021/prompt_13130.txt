I'm putting together a session on bargaining-theoretic approaches to justice for my graduate seminar on rational choice and contractarianism, to run alongside our discussion of the relevant chapter of Gauthier's Morals by Agreement. Gauthier's principle of minimax relative concession, meant to pick out the unique fair outcome of a hypothetical bargain over the terms of social cooperation, is -- as several of his commentators have noted -- essentially the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution to the two-person bargaining problem in another guise. What I want my students to feel is that the choice between Kalai–Smorodinsky and the more familiar Nash solution is not a free technical parameter: the two solutions agree on Pareto optimality, symmetry, and invariance to affine transformations of utility, and diverge only because Kalai and Smorodinsky replaced independence of irrelevant alternatives with individual monotonicity -- a substitution forced on them by the fact that, given the other three axioms, no solution can honor both. My students tend to nod along with whichever axiom gets introduced first and never really feel the force of that trade-off, so I'd like something livelier than another paragraph of exposition.

Could you write me a dialogue between a Gauthier-style contractarian, who wants to fix the fair division of some cooperative surplus using minimax relative concession, and a bargaining theorist who thinks dropping independence of irrelevant alternatives is too high a price and that Nash's solution is the only defensible one? Put the two of them in front of a shared, fairly simple two-person bargaining diagram -- a disagreement point and an asymmetric Pareto frontier for splitting the gains from some joint venture -- so their disagreement has to cash out in an actual disputed split rather than in competing slogans. I want the Nash defender to press the contractarian on how a solution that gives up IIA can avoid being manipulable by throwing in extra options that nobody would ever actually choose, and I want the contractarian to hit back with the standard monotonicity complaint, ideally by altering the diagram so that one party's best-case payoff improves while the other's is untouched, and showing what that does to the Nash split. Neither character should get to simply restate an axiom and declare victory: the Nash defender should have to say something about what makes an alternative "irrelevant" in the first place, and the contractarian owes an account of why relative concession, once IIA is gone, isn't just as arbitrary as the solution it replaces. Leave the exchange unresolved -- I'd rather have my students adjudicate the argument themselves than hand them a winner.
