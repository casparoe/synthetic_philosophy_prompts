So two people in my game theory study group (we're all econ PhD students grinding through quals) got into it over a problem set question, and honestly I still can't tell who came out on top. The setup: player 1 can play T, M, or B, and player 2 can play L or R. Player 1's payoffs are all we need here: 4 if T meets L, 0 if T meets R; 1 either way if they play M; 0 if B meets L, 2 if B meets R. The question asked which of player 1's strategies survive iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies, where you're allowed to check domination by mixed strategies too, not just pure ones.

Josh said M survives: T beats it under L but loses under R, and B is the mirror image, so no pure strategy dominates M.

Mei said not so fast - mix 1/3 T with 2/3 B and you get 4/3 either way, which beats M's guaranteed 1 in both columns. So M is strictly dominated after all, just not by anything pure.

Josh said that feels like a dodge - nobody would actually go play that specific 1/3-2/3 mix, it's not even a best response to anything player 2 might do. So how does pointing at a strategy no rational player would pick show that M is the irrational one?

Mei said it doesn't matter whether the mix ever gets played. "Dominated" just means M can't be a best response to any belief about what player 2 does - there's always something else, here that mix, doing strictly better. Whether that something else is itself ever optimal is a seperate question.

Josh conceded M is out, but then asked whether the elimination order matters - like if removing M first changes what's dominated for player 2 down the line, could a different order get you a different final answer?

Mei said no, not for strict dominance - same surviving strategies no matter the order, or even if you remove everything dominated in one pass. Order only becomes an issue once you switch to weak dominance.

Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's got it right (and who doesn't, and why) at each point, rather than just an overall winner?
