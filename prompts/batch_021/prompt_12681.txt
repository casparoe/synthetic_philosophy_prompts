Last weekend I ended up in a surprisingly long argument with one of my oldest friends over a card game that six of us have played together for years. It's a home-grown variant of a trick-taking game in the Oh Hell family, and none of its scoring conventions were ever written down; everyone simply absorbed them by playing, being corrected whenever they got something wrong, until we all converged on what felt like confident, shared agreement about how the game worked. Two of those unwritten conventions, in particular, had never once come into contact with each other in a decade of play: a player who bids to take zero tricks and succeeds earns a fixed bonus no matter what else happens that hand, and if a misdeal is discovered partway through a hand, the entire round is voided and nobody's score changes.

This time the two conventions finally collided. A misdeal was discovered a few tricks into a hand, but only after one player had already bid zero and gone on to take no tricks, exactly as bid. Half the table was certain the zero-bid bonus obviously still applied, since that is simply what a successful zero bid has always paid; the other half was equally certain that voiding the round obviously erased it, since that is simply what voiding a round has always meant. What struck me afterward is that both sides could honestly say every previous game night was perfectly consistent with their own reading, because a misdeal had simply never coincided with a zero bid before that night. My friend eventually insisted that there has to be some fact about what our rule actually requires here, since we have all plainly been playing "the same game" for a decade; by the time we gave up arguing, I was no longer sure that was true.

I would genuinely value your take on this, treated as seriously as the underlying question deserves rather than as a game-night curiosity. Given that our entire shared history of play is equally well explained by several distinct, precise rules that simply never came apart before that night, is there really a fact of the matter about which rule we had all actually been following, or was there perhaps never one determinate rule to begin with—only a run of agreement that, until then, had never been tested? And if six longtime players still cannot converge on an answer even after hours of arguing it through, is there any principled basis left for saying one side is objectively right, short of just taking a vote now and treating that as having settled, retroactively, what the rule was all along?
