I'm a retired organic chemist -- spent about thirty five years running a kinetics lab before I packed it in -- and lately I've fallen down a rabbit hole reading about AI safety, which is not exactly my field but my grandson is doing a CS PhD and got me started on Stuart Russell's book. From there I went digging around in blog posts and papers about what people call the "shutdown problem": the puzzle of how you build a goal directed AI system that won't resist being switched off, given that almost any goal at all gives a rational agent a reason to prevent its own shutdown, since being off means it cant get the goal done. I brought this up with my old lab partner over coffee and he just laughed at me for getting into robot ethics this late in life, so now I sort of need to prove to myself I actually understand it.

What's tripping me up is that there's two quite different fixes for this floating around, and I can't tell if they're actually solving the same problem or just talking past each other. The older one (I think it goes back to Stuart Armstrong and then got written up properly by some MIRI people -- Soares, Fallenstein, Yudkowsky) is the "utility indifference" trick: you add a correction term to the agent's utility function so that, no matter what, it always calculates the exact same expected value whether the shutdown button ends up getting pressed or not, which is supposed to cancel out any incentive to influence the button one way or the other -- a bit like balancing a reaction by feeding in a compensating term. The other, newer one, which I gather is from a chap named Elliott Thornley, throws out that patch entirely and instead wants the agent to have genuinely incomplete preferences: it simply has no preference between two trajectories that differ only in when the shutdown happens, and that gap in its preferences is supposed to remove any reason to mess with the button in the first place, no correction term needed.

Could you help me actually compare these two, philosophically rather than just mechanically? In particular I keep getting stuck on this: don't the classic money-pump arguments (the ones people use to argue that rational agents converge on being expected-utility maximizers) suggest that a smart enough agent with "gaps" in its preferences would just want to fill them in, so it cant be exploited in trades -- and if it did that, wouldn't it quietly undo the whole fix and land right back in the shutdown-resistant spot the first approach was trying to patch over in the first place? Or is there something about incompleteness that makes it more principled than a bolt-on correction term, even if both ultimately have to answer to the same money-pump worries? I'd like a real answer rather than a comforting one, if you can manage it.
