Ok so this is a research question for a novel I'm about a third of the way into, and I could use a sanity check from someone who actually knows the philosophy properly. The book is a counterfactual biography, kind of -- I've taken Frank Ramsey, the Cambridge prodigy who helped translate the Tractatus as a teenager and more or less invented subjective probability, and just let him live past 26. In my version the 1930 operation doesn't kill him and he potters around King's College for decades, brilliant and increasingly cranky, watching whole fields of philosophy develop off the back of stuff he wrote in his twenties. I got hooked on this after reading Misak's biography of him last winter (the "sheer excess of powers" one) and being kind of floored that someone that young left behind that much unfinished business.

The scene I'm stuck on is set in 1976, so Ramsey's 73. A younger philosopher comes to visit and shows him David Lewis's new paper, the one where Lewis proves you can't in general have the probability of "if A then B" equal your conditional probability of B given A, except in degenerate cases. That's a big deal for Ramsey in particular because that equation is more or less the formalization of a two-line footnote he tossed off in 1929 about how people actually evaluate conditionals -- add the antecedent to your stock of beliefs hypothetically, see what you then think of the consequent. My philosopher friend tells me there's a fork after Lewis: some people keep conditionals truth-apt and just restrict or give up the neat equation, others (Adams, Edgington) bite the bullet and say indicative conditionals were never stating propositions to begin with, that all the "probability of the conditional" talk was doing something else the whole time.

Here's the bit I actually want your help with, and its the reason I picked Ramsey and not some other logician: my friend also pointed out that Ramsey is on record mocking the end of the Tractatus, the idea that there's meaningful stuff that can't be said, only shown -- something about not being able to whistle what you can't say. So is there something a little awkward for him in the Adams/Edgington move, which also says there's a whole class of ordinary, meaningful-sounding sentences that were never in the fact-stating business? Or is that a superficial parallel and he'd have been fine biting that bullet, given how he thought about probability and belief generally? I don't need a tidy verdict so much as I need him actually reasoning it out loud to this visitor -- give me a page of that if you can, his voice working through it, not just a one-sentence conclusion, since that's the part I actually have to write.
