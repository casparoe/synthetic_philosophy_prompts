So this is going to sound like a weirdly specific thing to be hung up on, but here goes. My startup got acquired last month and they collapsed two overlapping "Senior Engineer" roles into one new "Staff Engineer" slot — me and a guy named Wasim were the two people in that overlap. HR's review process had us tied on literally every metric they tracked, so instead of picking, our new VP flipped an actual coin over Zoom, live, in front of both of us, to decide who got the title and the pay bump. I lost, for what its worth, and I'm still a little sore about it, which is exactly why I don't trust my own read on what happened next and want an outside opinion.

I told this story at a friend's birthday dinner and two people there, Noor and Callum, ended up in this whole side-argument about whether the coin flip even made sense, and it went way further into the weeds than a dinner conversation has any right to go. Roughly, in order:

(1) Noor, who did a master's in econ and never misses a chance to bring up decision theory, said the coin flip was actually irrational and that you can basically prove it. There's a real result, Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, that says: if every individual is an expected-utility maximizer, and whoever is choosing on the group's behalf is also one, and that group-level ranking has to respect unanimity (if everyone individually prefers option X to option Y, the group can't rank Y above X), then the only consistent social ranking is some fixed weighted sum of the individual utilities. Since me and Wasim were tied, our weights come out equal, and because a weighted sum is linear, the expected value of "flip a coin between giving it to him and giving it to me" comes out mathematically identical to just handing it to either one of us outright. So the coin flip added real risk — somebody was always going to end up disappointed — for zero expected gain. Not a neutral tiebreak, she said, just a gamble wearing a fairness costume.

(2) Callum, who does health-policy consulting, said that's a known trap, not a discovery. He brought up a famous comeback to Harsanyi from an economist named Diamond (the sixties, he thought), built on almost this exact case: two identical people, one scarce, indivisible good. Straight utilitarian aggregation really is indifferent between a coin flip and just favoring one of them outright, because expected value only tracks the sum, not how it got produced. But nearly everyone's actual intuition is that the coin flip isn't merely equal to favoritism, it's better, because it honors both people's equal claim instead of arbitrarily picking a favorite. If the VP had just handed the title to Wasim with no coin flip at all, Callum said, I'd have had a genuine grievance that "the expected value was identical either way" doesn't make go away.

(3) Noor said that grievance is a procedural complaint, not a utility complaint, and that's precisely her point: Harsanyi's theorem is about aggregating welfare, it was never pretending to be a theory of fair procedure, so it's not a mark against it that it stays quiet there. She also pushed Callum to say why a coin specifically is required rather than any other tiebreak — seniority, alphabetical order, whoever spoke up first are procedures too. If his real answer is "because a coin gives both people an equal chance," fine, she said, but that's a substantive fairness principle he's bringing in from outside, not something the math itself forces on anyone.

(4) Callum said seniority and the rest aren't actually in the same category, because they resolve a tie by smuggling in a criterion that has nothing to do with what made it a tie in the first place — they dissolve the tie instead of honoring it. A coin flip is the one procedure that treats a genuine tie as a tie. And he said the entire reason people reach for Harsanyi's theorem in real allocation fights — kidneys, vaccines, apparently now staff titles — is to argue that expected-utility aggregation is what rationality demands once uncertainty's involved, so if it genuinely can't tell a coin flip apart from flat-out favoritism in a case like this, that's a real cost of leaning on it, not a complaint about the wrong target.

Neither of them budged and I just stood there holding my drink like an idiot. Could you referee this round by round, 1 through 4, and tell me who had the better of it each time, rather than just declaring one overall winner? Also, if there's some consideration neither of them raised that actually matters here, I'd like to hear it. Finish with a one-line verdict: on balance, did Noor or Callum come out ahead, or is it a genuine wash?
