For a term paper in my seminar on the history of utilitarianism, I've been working through Bentham's unpublished writings on sexual morality: the essay conventionally known as "Offences Against One's Self" (c. 1785) and the later manuscripts collected as "Of Sexual Irregularities" (written in the 1810s but not published until 2014). Across both texts, Bentham works through the standard objections to "irregular" and non-procreative sex — that it weakens men, threatens marriage, depletes the population — and argues that none survives utilitarian scrutiny; by the later manuscript he goes further, suggesting that, given contemporary anxieties about a growing population, sex that produces no child is if anything a net good rather than a loss. I'd like to know what you think Bentham would make of today's "pronatalist" movement, which holds that developed countries facing below-replacement fertility should adopt policies to encourage childbearing. Reasoning from the actual structure of his argument rather than just his general commitment to the greatest happiness principle, would the reversal in demographic circumstances force him to abandon his position, or is there a deeper commitment in his reasoning that would survive the switch from a world anxious about overpopulation to one anxious about underpopulation?
