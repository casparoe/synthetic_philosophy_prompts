Question: Explain how Marx can maintain that exploitation occurs under capitalism even when workers are paid the full market value of what they sell to capitalists. Your answer should identify the relevant distinction Marx draws regarding what is actually exchanged in the labor market, and should connect this account of exploitation to at least two of the forms of alienation Marx describes in the 1844 Manuscripts.

Student's answer: According to Marx, the wage contract looks like a fair exchange between equals: the worker sells their labor to the capitalist, and the capitalist pays whatever the going rate for that labor happens to be. Since both sides get what the contract specifies, there is no cheating at the level of exchange itself. Exploitation instead arises from what happens afterward, in production. The value that labor creates in a working day is greater than the value the worker needs to be paid to cover their own subsistence and that of their family, so once they have worked long enough to cover that cost, everything they produce beyond it is surplus labor, and the value it creates -- surplus value -- is simply retained by the capitalist. Because this holds regardless of how competitive or generous the wage is, exploitation for Marx is compatible with a technically fair market exchange.

This same structure explains two of the forms of alienation from the 1844 Manuscripts. First, the worker is alienated from the product of their labor, since what they make belongs to the capitalist and confronts them as something foreign and even hostile. Second, the worker is alienated from their own species-being, since competition among workers for scarce jobs turns what should be humanity's cooperative, defining activity into a battle of each against all.
