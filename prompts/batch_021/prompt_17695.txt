I'm taking a decision theory seminar this term (cross-listed between the economics and philosophy departments), and something from my own life has been nagging at me ever since our unit on choice under uncertainty, which so far has only equipped us with expected utility theory and the standard story about risk aversion via a concave utility function. I don't think that story actually covers what's going on in my situation, and I'd like a second opinion.

I have to choose between two research assistant positions for the summer. The first is with Professor Alvarez, who has advised dozens of undergraduates over fifteen years; our department archives exit surveys, so I know almost exactly what I'm getting into — roughly three-quarters of her past RAs rated the experience highly and came away with a strong letter, and that fraction has barely moved in a decade. The second position is with Professor Voss, who just arrived from industry and has never supervised an undergraduate before. There are no surveys on file, no former students to email, nothing to go on. He might turn out to be an unusually generous mentor, or he might be absent and disorganized, and I have no real way of estimating which is more likely — though if you forced me to name a number, I'd probably guess something at least as good as three-quarters, given how enthusiastic he seemed in our interview. Even so, I find myself strongly favoring Professor Alvarez's position, to the point that I suspect I'd still favor it even if a mutual friend swore Professor Voss's "true" odds were somewhat better. I don't think this is just the ordinary aversion to risk that we covered in week one — I signed up for a genuine lottery for lab placements last year without hesitating, so variance or chance as such doesn't seem to be what's bothering me here. When I laid all this out for my roommate, she said the whole preference is incoherent: if my own best guess about Professor Voss is at least as favorable as Professor Alvarez's track record, then favoring Alvarez can't be tracking the odds at all, and I'm just letting nerves masquerade as reasoning. Is she right that there's no defensible basis for treating the known three-quarters differently from my own, equally optimistic guess about the unknown position, or is there a genuine asymmetry between the two cases that her argument is missing — and if so, should that actually change how I pick between the two labs?
