I'm several chapters into a novel that pulls George Berkeley — the Bishop of Cloyne, better remembered now for "esse est percipi" than for anything he wrote about coinage — out of his own century and drops him into a present-day Dublin dinner party, disoriented but quick to recover his taste for argument. One scene I keep circling back to has him cornered by a young venture capitalist eager to explain Bitcoin to him, confident that a philosopher famous for denying the existence of matter will be charmed by a currency that isn't backed by anything material either. I want to write Berkeley's side of that exchange honestly, from his actual economic commitments, rather than just having him deliver a knowing wink at the coincidence.

What I remember from working through *The Querist* for background reading is that Berkeley was already, for his time, unusually radical on the nature of money: he pressed the question of whether money needs to be identified with gold and silver at all, and seemed to favor treating it instead as a kind of ticket, counter, or record of a power to command goods and labor — a view he used to argue that Ireland's chronic shortage of specie could be solved by a national bank issuing paper credit, since what mattered was public trust in the record-keeping, not the intrinsic stuff being recorded. That looks, on its face, like it should make him sympathetic to a currency that is explicitly not a commodity. But my hunch is that this resemblance is mostly superficial, since his whole proposal seems to lean on some accountable public or sovereign authority standing behind the credit, guaranteeing it and answerable for it — precisely what a decentralized, trustless, permissionless system is designed to do without.

Could you reconstruct, as carefully as you can from his actual arguments rather than from vibes, how Berkeley would most likely reason his way through an encounter with Bitcoin or cryptocurrency more generally? I'd like you to work through where his anti-bullionist, credit-based view of money would incline him to find something congenial, or even vindicating, in a currency detached from metal, and where his apparent insistence on public accountability and circulation for productive ends would incline him to distrust or condemn it instead — and I'd like your honest sense of which pull wins out, rather than a diplomatic tie. If you think the parallel to his immaterialist metaphysics is doing any real philosophical work here, rather than being a cute coincidence of vocabulary, I'd be curious to hear why; if it's a red herring, feel free to say so and set it aside.
