I'm putting together the session of my graduate seminar in formal epistemology that pairs van Fraassen's discussion of the Reflection Principle with Lewis's Principal Principle, and before I commit to the analogy in my lecture notes I'd like to pressure-test it. On the surface the two look like instances of a single deference schema: Lewis holds that your credence in A, conditional on the objective chance of A at t being x together with any admissible evidence, should equal x; van Fraassen holds that your credence in A now, conditional on your own credence in A at some later time t' being x, should likewise equal x. Both tell you to set your opinion equal to some credence-fixing quantity you haven't yet directly consulted — the world's chances in one case, your own future self's opinion in the other — and both have been motivated by something in the neighborhood of a diachronic Dutch book, or a "you shouldn't be able to predict the direction of your own belief revision" style argument.

What I'm less confident about is how far the analogy survives once you look at what each principle needs in order to avoid triviality or outright counterexample. Lewis's principle depends entirely on a notion of "admissible" evidence to have any bite beyond the trivial case, and it famously needed patching — Lewis's own "New Principle" — once critics pointed out that on a Humean view of chance, facts about how the future actually unfolds can retroactively bear on what the chance at t already was, undermining the very ascription the principle was meant to license. Van Fraassen's principle, for its part, needs the proviso that you currently regard your future opinions as issuing from a process you trust; without it, anticipated brainwashing, memory loss, or sheer non-evidential drift would generate obviously absurd deference requirements. My worry is that these two escape clauses aren't doing the same kind of work: admissibility can at least be characterized without presupposing which verdicts it needs to protect, whereas "defer to your future self, except when you shouldn't" can look uncomfortably close to writing the desired answer into the antecedent, which would make Reflection much the more fragile of the two.

I'd like your help thinking through this carefully. First, are these genuinely the same underlying norm in different guises, or is the surface parallel doing more rhetorical work than philosophical work? Second, in which specific respects — justificatory, formal, or in how each accommodates its exceptions — is the Reflection Principle a harder principle to defend than the Principal Principle, and is there any respect in which it is actually the more secure of the two? And third: suppose I currently have a well-defined chance to defer to, but I also have good independent reason to expect my own future, fully rational credence to diverge from it — not through any defect in me, but because the same undermining-style considerations mean there may be no single settled fact about the current chance for the Principal Principle to have latched onto in the first place. In a case like that, does Reflection quietly end up doing the real work while the Principal Principle becomes inapplicable, or is there a principled way to keep both operative at once?
