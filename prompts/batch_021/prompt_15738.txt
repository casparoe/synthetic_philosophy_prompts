So I'm a backend engineer, but I've gotten pretty into the online forecasting scene lately (Metaculus, Manifold, that whole world), and last week two friends from that scene, Leah and Theo, got into a whole thing over drinks about whether the "Dutch book" argument actually proves anything about how your beliefs have to behave. I wasn't really part of it, just half-following along, and afterward I wrote down the actual moves because I honestly couldn't tell who was winning.

It went roughly like this. (1) Leah opened with the standard pitch: if your degrees of belief don't obey the probability axioms -- say you think there's a 60% chance of rain tomorrow and separately a 50% chance of no rain -- a bookie can construct a set of bets that each look individually fine to you but that guarantee you a loss no matter what happens, so incoherent credences are just irrational. (2) Theo said that only bites if you're forced to accept each bet on its own; if you're allowed to look at the whole slate of bets as one package before agreeing to any of it, you'd spot the trap and decline the lot, so all this really shows is that one weird betting protocol punishes incoherence, not that incoherence itself is bad. (3) Leah replied that Theo's missing what "fair odds" means here -- your fair betting quotient basically *is* your credence, on the usual story, it's not some separate offer you get to accept or reject, so the guaranteed loss is just a formal readout of an inconsistency that was already sitting in your head. (4) Theo came back that if belief is just defined as a betting quotient then the whole argument is circular: sure, a thing defined that way has to be coherent, but that doesn't tell us our ordinary notion of confidence has to match that defintion, which was supposed to be the actual substantive claim. (5) Leah said fine, but the moment Theo actually uses his confidence levels to do anything -- price insurance, make bets, put out forecasts he gets scored on -- the incoherence shows up as him loosing value in a way a coherent person wouldn't, whatever you want to say about definitions. (6) Theo's last word was that this move quietly needs the assumption that money has roughly constant marginal value to you at whatever stakes are involved, which is an extra empirical assumption about utility and not something logic alone hands you -- and he pointed out Ramsey himself apparently needed small stakes for exactly this reason.
