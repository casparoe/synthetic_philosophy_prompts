Here's the question, more or less as it was given to the students:

"Consider a three-judge appellate panel deciding a breach-of-contract suit. The relevant legal rule says the defendant is liable if and only if (i) a valid contract existed between the parties, and (ii) the defendant breached it. The judges' individual views on the two premises are: Judge A says the contract was valid and there was a breach; Judge B says the contract was valid but there was no breach; Judge C says the contract was not valid but there was a breach. (a) Work out the panel's collective verdict under a conclusion-based procedure (each judge votes directly on liability, majority rules) versus a premise-based procedure (the panel votes seperately on each premise, then applies the legal rule to whatever the majority says on each premise). Show your work. (b) The two procedures disagree. Explain why that's an instance of List and Pettit's discursive dilemma rather than just a coincidence of this particular case, and say how it connects to the 'doctrinal paradox' from the legal literature. (c) A commentator claims this is 'basically Condorcet's paradox in different clothes.' Is that a fair characterization?"

And here's the student's answer, typos and all:

"(a) Under the conclusion-based procedure, each judge votes on liability directly. Judge A supports liability (she said yes to both premises), while Judges B and C both vote against it (B because there's no breach on her view, C because there's no valid contract on his view). That's 2-1 against, so the panel finds the defendant not liable. Under the premise-based procedure we instead vote on each premise separately. On contract validity, A and B say yes and C says no, so the panel finds by majority that the contract was valid. On breach, A and C say yes and B says no, so the panel finds by majority that a breach occurred. Applying the rule (valid contract + breach = liable), the premise-based panel concludes the defendant IS liable. So the two procedures give opposite verdicts even though no individual judge changed their mind about anything -- only the voting procedure changed.

(b) This is exactly the discursive dilemma that List and Pettit first identified: propositionwise majority voting can turn perfectly consistent individual judgments into a collective outcome that's either inconsistent or dependent on an essentially arbitrary choice of procedure. List and Pettit actually coined the term 'doctrinal paradox' for this after noticing that real U.S. appellate panels sometimes hand down opposite results on identical facts depending on whether they voted issue-by-issue or voted on the bottom line -- 'discursive dilemma' is really just their broader, non-legal name for the same underlying structure. What makes it a genuine dilemma and not just a curiosity is that both procedures are individually reasonable and even satisfy some obvious fairness conditions (anonymity, one-issue-one-vote), yet they can't both be right, and nothing in the setup tells you which one to trust.

(c) I'd push back on that a little, though I get where it's coming from. Condorcet's paradox is about cyclical majority preferences -- A beats B, B beats C, and C beats A in pairwise votes over three options, even though each individual's own ranking is perfectly transitive. The discursive dilemma has the same flavor of surprise: individually rational inputs, run through a seemingly fair aggregation rule, produce a collectively irrational output. Since List and Pettit explicitly modeled their result on Arrow's theorem, and later work shows preference aggregation can be represented as a special case of judgment aggregation, I'd actually say the commentator is basically right that it's the same paradox wearing different clothes -- Condorcet cycles are just what you get when you specialize the discursive dilemma to the particular case of preference rankings."
