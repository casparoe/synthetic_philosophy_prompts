Got into it with my friend Jo last night about whether "coherent" agents basically have to be expected-utility maximizers, and we never landed anywhere. Her point: incomplete preferences -- where there's just no fact about how you rank some pair of options, not even a tie -- get you money-pumped almost as easily as cyclic ones do, so anything smart enough to survive being exploited over and over (a person, a company, or eventually an AI) ends up behaving as if it has complete, transitive preferences whether or not it really does on the inside. I said the standard pump only works on a myopic chooser evaluating each trade in isolation, and that an agent with foresight could just see the whole sequence coming and refuse to step onto the escalator in the first place (resolute choice), no ranking of the original options required. She came back that this doesn't get you out of anything, it just relocates the loss: at the last step the resolute agent is knowingly picking the option it likes less by its own current lights, so its still leaving value on the table, just for a different reason. I honestly don't know if that's a knockdown or just a relabeling of what I said. Can you write a dialogue between a completeness hardliner and someone defending incomplete preferences, where they actually engage each others best moves instead of just restating their opener, and try to settle whether resolute choice really escapes the money pump or just hides the cost somewhere else?
