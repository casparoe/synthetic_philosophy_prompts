I am currently at work on a novel that braids two timelines together: a fictionalised account of Pascal's last years, moving between Port-Royal and Paris, and a present-day plot following a young prosecutor at the International Criminal Court. The device holding the two strands together is that every contemporary chapter opens with a fragment from the Pensées, and my prosecutor — who studied a little Pascal long before she studied law, and never quite shook him off — keeps circling back to the note where he writes that "la justice sans la force est impuissante; la force sans la justice est tyrannique," and to the bleaker corollary that followed from it: that since we cannot make what is just strong, we have generally settled for making what is strong pass for just.

In the chapter I am drafting now, she has just watched the Court issue an arrest warrant against a sitting head of state whom everyone in the room privately knows will never be surrendered, because the states with the actual power to compel him have no interest in doing so. I want her interior monologue to reconstruct, as carefully as it can be reconstructed, what Pascal himself would say about a body like this — not the undergraduate "might makes right" reading he sometimes gets reduced to, but something that takes in the fuller architecture of his thought: his notion of the "raison des effets," his account of how ordinary people, the merely "demi-habiles," and the genuinely wise all end up endorsing the same established order, each for entirely different reasons, and his deep suspicion, formed in a France still raw from civil war, of anyone who would unseat a working arrangement in the name of a justice that has no force behind it. My own Pascal is a single comparative-literature seminar from many years ago, so I would rather build the scene on something sturdier than my own half-remembered gloss of him.

So: reasoning strictly from Pascal's own premises, would he regard an institution like the ICC as a doomed attempt at exactly the move he diagnoses as impossible — trying to make justice strong from the outside instead of making force just — or might he, on his own terms, see it as a fledgling "effect" that could, given enough time and enough habitual compliance, harden into a new establishment and thereby simply come to count as just, or is there some third reading that fits him better still? Could you work through this with some genuine care, in roughly 500 to 600 words? I would like it pitched as a briefing note to me rather than as dialogue, since I will be the one who eventually turns it into her voice.
