"The Lucas-Penrose argument tries to use Gödel's incompleteness theorem to show that mechanism -- the view that the mind just is a Turing machine, or could in principle be perfectly simulated by one -- is false.

Start with Gödel's first incompleteness theorem: for any consistent formal system F that is rich enough to represent elementary arithmetic, there's a sentence G(F), built by a diagonal/self-referential trick, which says of itself, in effect, that it is false. Because of the way G(F) is constructed, F can prove G(F) only if F is inconsistent; so if F is consistent, G(F) is undecidable in F -- neither it nor its negation is a theorem of F -- and moreover G(F) is true, since it correctly asserts that it has no proof in F.

Gödel himself saw the philosophical import of this in his 1951 Gibbs lecture, where he argued that the theorem shows the human mind cannot be equivalent to any single Turing machine: whatever formal system F we propose as a model of human mathematical reasoning, a mathematician can, by reflecting on F, see that G(F) is true, and thereby know something F cannot prove. Since this works for any F, no F can capture everything a human mathematician can, in principle, come to know, so minds outrun machines and mechanism is false. Lucas (1961) and later Penrose (in The Emperor's New Mind and Shadows of the Mind) developed this into a fuller argument, with Penrose additionally speculating about what kind of physical process in the brain could realize a non-computable form of reasoning.

The strongest objection, due to Putnam and pressed by many since, targets the move from 'F is consistent' to 'we can see that G(F) is true.' By Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, F itself cannot prove Con(F), its own consistency statement. Lucas's reply is that the human can simply step outside F and recognize its consistency directly, and it's exactly this stepping-outside that no machine can do. But the mechanist can respond that this just relocates the problem: for the argument to actually defeat a specific mechanist hypothesis 'the human is F,' the human has to know, of that very F, both that it's consistent and that it's the right model of them -- and nothing guarantees a human always has, or could have, this knowledge. If humans are occasionally inconsistent, or simply don't know which F (if any) models their own reasoning, the argument never gets off the ground against that F, so it doesn't show that no F models the mind, only that no F we can currently certify as consistent and self-identify with does.

On balance, I think the objection wins: the argument shows something real -- that any formal system we can fully trust and identify with our own reasoning is one we can also outrun -- but it doesn't show that some machine, perhaps one whose consistency we could never certify, isn't quietly running the show. So mechanism survives, though in a more chastened form than its defenders might like."
