I spent forty-two years running an experimental condensed-matter physics laboratory, and I confess I always regarded metaphysics as the sort of thing physicists grow out of. Retirement has humbled me on that score: for the past year, a small reading group made up of former colleagues and one very patient philosopher friend has been working through Leibniz on the identity of indiscernibles, and we have spent an embarrassing number of sessions stuck on Max Black's 1952 dialogue positing a universe that contains nothing but two qualitatively identical iron spheres, related to each other in every respect and to nothing else, offered as a counterexample to the Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles (PII): two numerically distinct objects that do not differ by so much as a single property. It is a clean enough puzzle on the page, but the secondary literature responding to it turns out to be a thicket.

What keeps drawing me back in is that the various "solutions" on offer don't so much compete on the same ground as change the subject entirely. Hacking, if I have followed the argument correctly, tries to dissolve the case by insisting that under a genuinely relational view of space the "two spheres" story is not clearly distinguishable from a "one sphere, encountered twice" story. Adams is content to grant the spheres a bare, unanalyzable thisness and leave the matter there. And a line running through French and Redhead, and later Saunders, tries to rescue a weaker version of PII by pointing out that an irreflexive relation, rather than any monadic property, can still discern one thing from another -- with entangled fermion pairs offered as the physically real case that a thought experiment about spheres can only gesture at. That last move is the one I keep worrying at, because I spent a career around genuinely indistinguishable particles, and I am not convinced that bosons and fermions are as interchangeable for this purpose as some of the philosophy papers seem to assume.

I would like a second opinion that takes the metaphysics and the physics equally seriously.
