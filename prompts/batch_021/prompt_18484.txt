I had a long argument with a friend last night about effective altruism, and I've come around to a conclusion of theirs that I can't quite shake. Their claim, drawing on the cluelessness objection to consequentialism (a line of argument I gather traces back to the philosopher James Lenman), was that because the downstream effects of any action ramify indefinitely and unpredictably, we can never really know its net effect on the world — and so cost-effectiveness comparisons between charities, of the kind GiveWell publishes in terms of cost per life saved, carry no more rational authority than picking a cause at random. If we grant that, what should someone who wants to give effectively actually optimize for, if not expected impact?
