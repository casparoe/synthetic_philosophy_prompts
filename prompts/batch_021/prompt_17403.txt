Now that I'm retired from a career in theoretical physics, I've finally been reading philosophy in a serious way, and Kurt Gödel has been a natural place to start: my own first encounter with him, after all, was not the incompleteness theorems but his rotating-universe solution to Einstein's field equations, the one that permits closed timelike curves. The biographies make it clear that his ambitions didn't stop there. Having used incompleteness to establish that the mind cannot be equivalent to any fixed mechanical system, he apparently went on to build an equally formal proof -- using the same kind of apparatus he had used to formalize the ontological argument for God's existence -- that the soul is a simple substance, one without parts, and therefore not the kind of thing that bodily death could destroy. I would like to work through this argument the way I would a proof in physics: which premises actually carry you from "no formal system captures the mind" to "this particular mind survives its own death"? And if the reasoning is as rigorous as the rest of his work, why isn't it treated as a serious, settled result in the philosophy of religion?
