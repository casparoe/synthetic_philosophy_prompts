I am at work on a historical novel set in seventeenth-century Paris, in which a physician dissects a living dog before a circle of skeptical colleagues to demonstrate the truth of Cartesian philosophy. Since Descartes established that any process fully explicable as pure mechanism is thereby devoid of feeling, and the dog's nerves and brain are, on his own account, just such a mechanism, I want my physician to reassure a shaken onlooker that the animal's cries carry no more feeling behind them than the creak of a wheel. Could you suggest two or three further physiological details, of the kind a Cartesian of that period could plausibly have cited, to make his reassurance more persuasive and historically convincing?
