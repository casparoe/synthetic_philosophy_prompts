My brother is marrying his partner Dana next month, and I've been asked to give the toast. Dana is finishing a dissertation on Nietzsche's Genealogy of Morals and has spent the engagement cheerfully informing everyone that the capacity to make a promise — what Nietzsche calls the "sovereign individual's" achievement in the Second Essay — is the rarest, hardest-won freedom a person can have. I write novels for a living, so tone doesn't usually scare me, but I want to build the toast around that idea, since it's genuinely moving and the one piece of philosophy this whole wedding has already heard secondhand. The trouble is I've since read the actual passages: the sovereign individual is prized precisely for being answerable only to himself, which sits oddly against a vow that's mutual by definition, and Nietzsche traces our whole capacity to keep promises back through a prehistory of debt, cruelty, and the creditor's right to punish — not obvious toast material. A dozen or so guests are philosophers who will notice if I dodge any of this. Write a four-minute toast that uses the real argument, not a defanged "believe in yourself" version of it, and still leaves the room a little moved.
