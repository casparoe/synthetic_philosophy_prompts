I'm a PhD student in philosophy, currently drafting a dissertation chapter on whether collective and vicarious punishment can be justified in secular law -- corporate criminal liability, sanctions that fall on a state's population for its regime's conduct, strict liability offences, and the punishment of family members under various customary legal systems. A few weeks ago, in a reading group on theories of punishment, one of the faculty members mentioned, almost as an aside, that David Lewis -- yes, the modal realist -- once wrote a short and unusually earnest paper called "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" (Philosophical Papers, 1997), in which he takes the Christian doctrine that Christ's suffering discharged the punishment sinners deserved fully seriously as a philosophical puzzle about punishment and justice, rather than dismissing it as a theological curiosity. I finally read it properly this week. As I understand his central worry: a monetary debt can be settled by anyone, since the creditor is made whole regardless of whose money changes hands, but punishment does not seem to work this way -- if retributive justice requires that the guilty party suffer precisely because they did wrong, then substituting a willing, innocent sufferer looks less like paying off a debt on someone else's behalf and more like a straightforward category error. Lewis tries a couple of ways of easing this worry by trading on different accounts of what punishment is even for -- strict desert versus something closer to deterrence or the maintenance of a credible legal and moral order -- without, as far as I can tell, ever quite declaring the puzzle solved.

I have no need to relitigate the theology; I want to borrow the argument. Setting the doctrine of the atonement aside entirely, please describe the most important implications of Lewis's debt/punishment disanalogy for the secular cases I actually work on. I'm especially interested in where the disanalogy bites hardest against existing practices of collective or vicarious punishment, where it merely relabels a worry the legal-philosophical literature already has its own tools for, and whether the legal fictions that treat corporations or states as single punishable "persons" should be read as genuine answers to Lewis's puzzle or as ways of quietly sidestepping it.
