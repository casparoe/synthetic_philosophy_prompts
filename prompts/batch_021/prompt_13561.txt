For the past couple of years I've spent a lot of my free time reading about decision theory and probability puzzles as a hobby, even though I never studied mathematics or philosophy formally. It started with the St. Petersburg paradox, the coin-flipping game where the expected payout works out to infinity, so that a rational agent is apparently committed to paying any finite fee to play it. That puzzle always felt containable to me: the number it spits out is absurd, but it's at least a definite number, and the real argument is over whether that number should govern anyone's actual willingness to pay.

A few months ago, a forum thread I was reading linked to a paper by Alan Hájek and Harris Nover describing a much stranger relative of that game, which they call the Pasadena paradox. It uses the same basic coin-flipping setup, but the payoff, if the first heads occurs on toss number n, alternates between a positive and a negative amount whose size is roughly 2^n divided by n. Multiply each payoff by its 1-in-2^n probability and add up the results in the obvious order — toss 1, then 2, then 3, and so on — and you get the alternating harmonic series, 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + …, which converges to a perfectly ordinary number, ln 2 (about 0.69). The catch, which I had never run into before, is that this series is only "conditionally" convergent rather than "absolutely" convergent, and by a result called the Riemann rearrangement theorem, a conditionally convergent series can be reordered to add up to any real number at all, or to positive infinity, or negative infinity, or fail to settle down to anything at all. When I brought this up with a friend who works with statistics, she waved it off, insisting the expected value is "obviously" ln 2 and that I was overthinking a technicality — but that response just assumes that summing toss by toss is the one privileged order, when nothing about the coin, the payoffs, or the probabilities seems to force that choice on us.

What I'd like from you is an essay that actually takes a side on what this shows, rather than a survey that lists reactions and stops there. Please lay out the strongest version of the view that the Pasadena game exposes a genuine, previously hidden defect in expected-value reasoning as a norm of rational choice — one that might, for all we know, be lurking in other, more ordinary-looking gambles — and weigh it against the strongest version of the view that this is ultimately just an artifact of conditional convergence that can be fenced off by some principled restriction (such as requiring absolute convergence, treating temporal or causal order as canonical for reasons that don't just beg the question, or denying that the scenario even specifies a legitimate gamble in the first place), leaving expected-value reasoning otherwise intact. Please end with a genuine verdict on which side is closer to right and why, rather than concluding that reasonable people can just disagree.
