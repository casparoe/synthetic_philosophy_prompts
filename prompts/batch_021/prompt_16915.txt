This semester I'm taking a philosophy of psychiatry seminar, and we just spent two weeks on Ian Hacking's "The Looping Effects of Human Kinds," including his distinction between "indifferent kinds" and "interactive kinds": a rock doesn't care what we call it, but a person classified under a psychiatric label typically learns about the label, reacts to it, and reshapes their self-understanding and behavior around it, in a way that can loop back and alter the very criteria used to classify them. I didn't expect this idea to become practically relevant to me quite so quickly, but it now bears directly on something outside of class.

I've recently been added as the student representative to my university's Disability Services Advisory Committee, which is revising documentation standards for ADHD and autism accommodations (extended test time, note-takers, reduced-distraction rooms, and so on). Currently, students must submit a comprehensive evaluation from a licensed clinician, but several committee members want that changed: waitlists for formal testing at both the campus clinic and in the surrounding area routinely stretch past a year and cost hundreds to thousands of dollars, and the diagnostic criteria themselves were largely normed on populations, like young boys in the case of ADHD, that don't capture how these conditions present in adults, women, and people who've learned to mask. Other members are just as adamant that we shouldn't move to accepting self-report or a brief screener in place of formal diagnosis, since self-identification with these labels has surged alongside a wave of symptom-checklist content on TikTok and Instagram, and they worry we'd effectively let the boundaries of "who has ADHD" be set by whichever traits happen to be circulating that month, in a way that then feeds back into how students watching that content come to interpret and present themselves.

I need to write the short policy memo the committee will discuss at next week's meeting, before we vote on a recommendation to send to the Dean of Students. Could you draft it for me? It should come down on one specific documentation standard (a clean either/or is fine, and so is a well-justified tiered or hybrid approach), lay out the reasoning behind that recommendation, and take seriously -- rather than just mention in passing -- the strongest argument for whichever side the recommendation doesn't fully adopt.
