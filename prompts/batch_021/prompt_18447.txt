I convene a small, informal reading group — open to philosophy and computer science graduate students alike — on self-reference in formal systems, and a few weeks ago we worked through Löb's theorem: for a suitably expressive formal system T with a provability predicate Prov_T, if T proves "Prov_T(φ) → φ" for some sentence φ, then T already proves φ outright. In the AI safety literature this result is often invoked to motivate a so-called "Löbian obstacle" facing any formally self-trusting agent: if an agent tries to establish, within its own proof system, a general principle to the effect that whatever a successor of itself (or a future, self-modified version of itself) using that same system manages to prove is thereby true, Löb's theorem shows the agent could only ever secure this for statements it could already prove on its own — which defeats the point of delegating or self-modifying in the first place.

Two of my strongest students, Marcus and Leah, kept arguing about this over coffee afterward, and it has continued over email since. (1) Marcus's opening claim was that the obstacle is a genuine, hard wall on formally verified self-improvement: since no proof-based agent can certify in advance that a same-strength successor's proofs are trustworthy across the board, any architecture that relies on this kind of certification before self-modifying is a non-starter. (2) Leah answered that this is only a wall for a naive design in which successor and predecessor share exactly one proof system; the standard move, she said, is to have each successive version prove the soundness of the version before it from a strictly stronger system one level up, so that trust "tiles" up an infinite tower of increasingly powerful systems rather than being secured all at once within a fixed one. (3) Marcus replied that this doesn't so much solve the obstacle as relocate it to infinity: it gives a bounded agent nothing, since at some point that agent must certify its own future behavior using resources no stronger than what it already has, and an infinite tower is not something anyone ever finishes building. (4) Leah countered that this proves too much, since plenty of results in proof theory tame apparent Gödelian walls by climbing well-behaved infinite hierarchies rather than staying inside one fixed system, and that Marcus's real target should be provability itself: real agents, including any AI built by remotely current methods, don't reason via exact formal proof at all, so a result about provability predicates may simply be the wrong tool for asking whether such agents can trust their future selves. (5) Marcus's last word was that moving from provability to probability softens the worry without dissolving it: something in the neighborhood of Löb's and Tarski's results tends to resurface the moment a system tries to represent, even approximately, facts about its own reliability, so Leah's move buys approximate self-trust at best, not a refutation of the original limit he was pointing to.

Neither of them would concede, and I would rather not referee this myself before we reconvene. Could you go through the exchange move by move, say who has the better of it at each of the five numbered points and why, and then close with your own overall verdict on whether the Löbian obstacle should be treated as a serious, still-binding limit on AI systems that need to trust their future or successor selves, or rather as an artifact of an overly narrow, proof-based framing? Please also note anything you think both Marcus and Leah are overlooking.
