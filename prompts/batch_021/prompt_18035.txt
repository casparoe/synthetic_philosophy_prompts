My aunt Marion, a political philosopher who spent most of her career working on Rawls and religion, died three weeks ago at seventy-nine, and I've been asked to give the eulogy at her memorial service this coming Saturday. She was a genuinely devout Episcopalian her entire adult life -- choir, vestry, the works -- which her colleagues always found a little rich, since she was also the person everyone in her department went to for the sharpest reading of Rawls's "proviso." The paper of hers that gets cited most argues that sympathetic and unsympathetic readers alike tend to get Rawls backwards on religion: that the proviso isn't a demand that believers keep quiet in politics, but a demand that, sooner or later, they back up whatever they're advocating with reasons that don't themselves depend on their religion being true -- a considerably less exclusionary bar than it's usually taken to be. She liked to point out, half as a party trick, that Rawls himself had planned on the Episcopal priesthood before the war changed his mind.

Before she got sick, she told my mother -- her sister -- that she didn't want her own funeral to do the thing she'd spent thirty years watching politicians and, occasionally, priests do badly: stand up and assert, as if it settled the matter for everyone present, that she is "with God now" or "watching over us," when a good third of the room -- her graduate students, her closest collaborator, my own thoroughly atheist father -- couldn't be expected to accept that as a premise they shared. What she left as more of a private joke than an instruction, though, is that she knew perfectly well a funeral isn't the kind of forum her own theory constrains in the first place: eulogies belong to what Rawls calls the background culture, not the public political forum, so strictly speaking nothing in political liberalism stops me from preaching her straight into heaven if I felt like it. Whether she thought the eulogy should hold itself to the stricter standard anyway, or was quietly hoping someone would let her off the hook for once, she never said, even to my mother.

I have to actually write and deliver this in four days -- something like five or six minutes -- for a room that will be perhaps half parish and family who loved her and just want to grieve, and half philosophers who will notice immediately if I collapse the overlapping consensus she cared so much about into a mere modus vivendi, or muddle the proviso in either direction. Could you draft this eulogy for me? It should be honestly comforting to the people grieving a woman of faith, it should take that faith seriously rather than quietly editing it into something more secular and safe, and it should not resolve, on her behalf, the question she deliberately left open -- all without turning into a philosophy lecture.
