Here's the full answer:

"Ghazali's target in the Seventeenth Discussion is the philosophers' claim -- he has Avicenna chiefly in mind -- that natural causes act of necessity: put fire and cotton together and burning simply must follow, because that just is what it is for something to be fire. Ghazali's central move is to point out that we never actually observe this 'must.' What experience gives us is only that burning is regularly found alongside contact with fire, not that the fire itself brings the burning about, and concluding from mere constant accompaniment that one thing necessitates the other is something the philosophers have assumed rather than demonstrated. He backs this up with the Quranic story of Ibrahim coming out of the fire unharmed: since there's no contradiction in supposing fire without burning, the connection between the two can't be a necessary one, only a habitual one.

In place of necessity, Ghazali puts God's custom ('ada): the things we call cause and effect are just two events God is in the habit of creating together, or one right after the other, and he's free to break that habit, as he did for Ibrahim. Since this rests on the broader Ash'arite occasionalist physics of his school, on which the world is really just a series of momentary atoms and accidents that God re-creates at every instant, fire itself turns out to have no efficacy whatsoever on this view -- it's exactly as inert as the cotton is -- and the real agent behind the burning, as behind every event whatsoever, is God alone, acting moment to moment.

It's tempting to fold this straight into an early version of Hume, since both refuse to let reason certify any necessary connection between cause and effect. But I'd resist treating the two moves as the same: Hume's 'custom' is a fact about us, the mind's tendency to form an expectation after enough repetitions, and he stays agnostic about what, if anything, grounds the regularity out in the world. Ghazali's 'custom' belongs to God, not to us -- it's a full metaphysical claim about who's really doing the causing, not a description of a psychological tic that might be tracking nothing at all. So the two thinkers end up equally unwilling to call the connection necessary, but for almost opposite reasons: one of them deflationary about the world, the other about as metaphysically committal as it's possible to be.

Ibn Rushd's reply in the Tahafut al-Tahafut, for what it's worth, doesn't really engage this argument so much as reassert against it that fire obviously burns cotton and always will, which amounts to begging the question Ghazali raised in the first place."
