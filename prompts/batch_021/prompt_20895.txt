So I got into it with my friend last night about Popper and evolution and I want to firm up my side for round two. Basically Popper spent years calling natural selection "almost tautological," just a "metaphysical research programme" and not real science, and then in that later Darwin lecture he recants and admits it does make specific testable predictions after all. My friends take is that this basically vindicates his whole "evolutionary epistemology" thing, since now the biology backing the science-as-natural-selection analogy is on solid ground. I think that lets him off too easy -- the recantation only fixes the emptiness problem, it does nothing for the much bigger issue that mutation is blind/undirected in a way that Popper's own picture of how scientists actually come up with conjectures (rational, creative, aimed at solving a specific problem-situation, not randomly generated and tested) never was. So even a perfectly non-tautological, fully testable theory of natural selection would still be a bad model for the "variation" half of the analogy, since real conjecture-formation was never blind in the relevant sense to begin with. Can you actually build this out into a proper argument, and deal with the obvious comeback that Popper (and Campbell after him) only ever meant "blind" as "not prescient of success," not "random or undirected" -- I want to know if that comeback actually rescues the analogy or just quietly gives up the thing that made it interesting in the first place.
