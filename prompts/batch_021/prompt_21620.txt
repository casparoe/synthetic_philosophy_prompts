I sit on the grants committee of a small foundation that funds unconventional, high-uncertainty research bets. We are, for better or worse, the kind of organization that asks applicants for explicit credence intervals rather than mission statements, and that reviews every major funding recommendation against subsequent outcomes each quarter. An old friend from a university reading group -- we used to spend hours arguing about Robin Hanson's and Eliezer Yudkowsky's exchanges on when it is rational to defer to a group's judgment over your own -- now serves on the committee with me, and this case has revived that argument in a far less abstract form than either of us expected.

Here is the situation. One of our most senior evaluators is recommending a three-year renewal for a grant supporting pandemic early-warning systems, a program she has championed since the foundation's founding. Our latest retrospective, however, shows that her recommendations specifically within this cause area have been substantially overconfident relative to outcomes, even though her track record everywhere else in the portfolio remains excellent. She has read the retrospective, accepts its methodology, has gone back and independently re-derived her assessment while explicitly checking herself for the exact biases it identifies, and still, on reflection, believes this grant is an outstanding bet -- for reasons she can articulate that are not simply a restatement of the judgment the retrospective flagged as unreliable. The committee has split down the middle: half want a standing rule that mechanically discounts any evaluator's credence toward the portfolio baseline whenever this kind of documented miscalibration exists, on the grounds that anything else means ignoring evidence we spent real money to collect; the other half think such a rule would have vetoed several of our best historical bets, all of which came from evaluators who held firm against the aggregate numbers, and worry that it effectively asks her to defer to a statistic rather than to any actual counter-argument.

Could you draft the short memo I will bring to Thursday's meeting, when a decision has to be made or the earmarked funds revert to the general pool at quarter's end? I need one specific recommendation for this grant -- fund it as proposed, decline it, or some conditional middle path -- rather than a survey of the considerations, and I would like the memo to explain why a reflective, well-informed disagreement like hers is even a coherent position to hold, rather than simply asserting that she should either trust the retrospective or trust her gut. Whatever it recommends, the memo needs to engage honestly with the strongest version of whichever side it doesn't choose: a couple of committee members have made clear they will only be moved by an argument that has clearly grappled with the case against it.
