I spent most of my working life in condensed-matter and statistical physics before retiring five years ago, and I have since been filling the time with philosophy, currently working through the literature on the interpretation of probability. David Lewis's Principal Principle — roughly, that a rational agent's credence in an outcome should equal the known objective chance of that outcome, conditional on nothing but evidence that is "admissible" — struck me at first as close to a truism. That impression has not survived contact with my own field. In classical statistical mechanics we routinely speak of the "probability" that a gas will spontaneously recompress into one corner of its container, or that a puddle will refreeze into an ice cube, even though the underlying microdynamics is perfectly deterministic: fix the exact microstate and the dynamical laws, and the entire future, and for that matter the entire past, are already fixed along with them. On the face of it, that seems to leave no room for objective chance except in a Laplacean sense, where the appearance of chance is just a symptom of our ignorance of the microstate. Yet philosophers I have come to take seriously, notably Barry Loewer and David Albert on what they call Humean objective chance for deterministic systems, maintain that these statistical-mechanical probabilities are chances in exactly Lewis's sense, no less deserving of our deference than the chance of a radioactive nucleus decaying.

This has become a running argument at a philosophy-of-science reading group I joined last year at the local university. A retired mathematician in the group takes the Humean line: once a probability measure earns its place in the best systematic account of the world's regularities, deferring to it via the Principal Principle is simply what rationality demands, whatever the microdynamics beneath it looks like. I keep wanting to answer, and I don't think this is mere scientist's stubbornness, that the maneuver only redescribes our ignorance in fancier language: a demon who knew the exact microstate would rightly assign the "spontaneous recompression" a credence of zero, and a quantity that collapses to zero or one as soon as you know enough was never really a chance to begin with. We have now gone two sessions without either of us managing to state the other's position so well that its owner would accept the summary, so I would like to see the argument staged properly by someone with no stake in who wins.

Could you write a dialogue between two equally sophisticated characters defending these two positions, staged as a real-time exchange in front of a concrete case: the two of them watching a partition slide out of a divided box, gas rushing to fill the vacuum, with some fantastically small but, on the deterministic reading, not obviously zero probability of the whole process reversing itself? I want genuine back-and-forth rather than two prepared speeches — each side pressing the other on what "admissible evidence" could even mean once a demon's knowledge is on the table, on whether calling the measure part of the "best system" changes anything beyond its label, and on why we are willing to run this same probability measure forward in time to predict that entropy will very likely increase, yet recoil from running it backward, which would have that very measure telling us the puddle more likely arose a minute ago from an even more disordered state than from a neatly frozen ice cube. I am less interested in a tidy resolution than in learning, by the end of it, whether the disagreement is really about the physics, about the metaphysics of chance, or about something that only wears the costume of either.
