So Anscombe's pamphlet 'Mr Truman's Degree' basically calls Truman a flat-out murderer over the atomic bombings, and her argument is actually stronger than a normal double-effect violation: her point is that the whole strategic logic of Hiroshima and Nagasaki was to kill masses of civilians on purpose, as the means of terrorizing Japan into surrendering, not just a forseen side-effect of hitting some military target. Write a dialogue, maybe set right after Oxford's vote to give him the degree anyway, between an Anscombe-style absolutist defending that verdict and a sharp defender of the bombings (either a straightforward consequentialist who thinks the net lives saved by avoiding a land invasion justifies it, or someone trying to rescue a double-effect reading where the real target was Japan's capacity/will to keep fighting, with civilian deaths forseen but not intended). Have them actually engage each other's arguments — whether the intention really was to kill civilians as such vs. destroy a legitimate target, what would need to be true about how targets got picked to settle that, and whether Anscombe's rule that no amount of good consequences can justify intentionally killing the innocent is even the right standard to apply here — rather than just each restating their own position.
