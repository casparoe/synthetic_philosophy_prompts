I have noticed that the absent-minded driver problem (Piccione and Rubinstein) and the Sleeping Beauty problem (Elga) tend to be discussed separately in rationalist-adjacent writing on decision theory and self-locating belief, even though they seem to turn on the same underlying feature: an agent's inability to distinguish between multiple positions reached within a single run of a process — successive intersections in one case, successive wakings in the other. Both have also split commentators between an "ex ante" answer fixed in advance and an answer that seems compelling once one actually finds oneself at one of those indistinguishable vantage points: planning-optimal versus action-optimal exit strategies in the driver problem, halfer versus thirder credences in Sleeping Beauty. I would like a direct comparison of these two splits rather than separate treatments of each puzzle. Specifically: is the driver problem's planning/action tension the decision-theoretic mirror image of the halfer/thirder dispute, such that adopting a thirder-style credence should commit one to the planning-optimal exit strategy, or can the two positions be held independently of one another? Relatedly, would an updateless decision theorist — who handles such problems by committing in advance to a single globally optimal policy — dissolve the tension in both puzzles in the same way, or does some feature of their respective structures resist a fully parallel treatment? Please justify your answer with reference to the formal structure of both problems, especially the fact that a later decision node or waking is only reached conditional on what happened earlier, rather than resting on surface analogy alone.
