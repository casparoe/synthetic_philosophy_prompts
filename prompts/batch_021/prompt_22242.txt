I have agreed, perhaps rashly, to give the address at my university's remembrance service this November — the annual ceremony held at the war memorial on the main quad, where the names of alumni killed in both World Wars and every conflict since are read aloud. My department chair proposed me for the honor on the strength of the graduate seminar I teach on Hegel's political philosophy, which strikes me, in retrospect, as a somewhat double-edged qualification. The audience is the usual uneasy cross-section: a handful of genuine combat veterans among the emeritus faculty and groundstaff, a color guard of student cadets who will stand at attention throughout, undergraduates present chiefly because attendance is noted, and, this year, seated in the front row at the family's own request, the parents and younger sister of a graduate killed in action eleven months ago.

I do not want to give the sort of address any chaplain could give — all noble sacrifice and grateful nations — since that would waste the one real advantage of putting a philosopher behind the podium, namely that I might say something Hegel would actually recognize as his own view rather than a euphemism standing in for it. That view, in the Philosophy of Right, is considerably less comfortable than the customary pieties. Hegel denies that war is simply an evil to be regretted and, with sufficient enlightenment, legislated out of existence; he holds that a people which knew only permanent peace would stagnate, and compares such a peace, in a well-known passage, to a sea gone foul for want of the winds that keep it stirred. That claim I cannot use, or not straightforwardly: I am not going to tell a family eleven months into mourning that their son's death served the ethical hygiene of the republic. More usable, I think, is his argument that in calling on citizens to risk their property and even their lives, the state exposes both as something less than absolute — which is his real ground for denying that death in battle is thrown away for nothing. He also has unsentimental arguments against the Kantian dream of a perpetual peace secured by a federation of states, which the philosophically literate quarter of my audience will be watching to see whether I dare bring up.

Could you draft the address itself? Something that reads aloud in about eight minutes. It should contain enough of Hegel's actual position that a colleague in the audience could not fairly accuse me of having smoothed him into mush, it should not say anything to the family in the front row that I would be ashamed to explain to them plainly afterward if asked, and wherever I am simply leaving part of the view out rather than pretending it isn't there, I would rather that omission were a visible and honest choice than a silent one.
