Im outlining a novel where one of the two leads is an evolutionary biologist who's convinced morality is basically a useful fiction evolution installed in us -- no real oughts, just adaptive feelings that happen to feel like commands. To pressure-test her arguments before I write her dialogue, I got two friends talking about it over dinner (one's an ecologist, one did a philosophy MA), and it turned into a proper argument. I wrote down the shape of it after they left:

(1) Mark: our moral intuitions are just fitness-enhancing heuristics. Evolution installs whatever gets genes copied, not whatever's true, and we'd have ended up with roughly the same moral beliefs whether or not any objective values existed. That's enough to debunk them -- we've got no reason to think they track anything real.

(2) Elena: by that logic our belief in basic arithmetic and logic is equally debunked, since evolution shaped our reasoning for survival, not for grasping abstract truths, and nobody concludes 2+2=4 is fake as a result. So the mere evolutionary origin of a belief can't, by itself, be what discredits it.

(3) Mark: the cases aren't parallel though. There's an obvious, non-mysterious story for why selection would land on creatures with accurate arithmetic -- miscounting predators or offspring gets punished almost immediatly, so true-belief and fitness-belief are basically the same target there. There's no equivalent story for morality, because if moral facts are real they're exactly the kind of thing that doesn't push around gene frequencies, so selection had nothing to lock onto.

(4) Elena: that only bites if you assume moral facts are some spooky non-natural thing floating free of biology. A naturalist can just say moral facts are facts about suffering and flourishing, and those very much do have causal traction -- the same pressures that made us cooperative and averse to needless cruelty could be tracking something real about welfare.

(5) Mark: except evolution just as reliably built in nepotism, in-group loyalty and out-group hostility, which we now think are morally bad. If fitness pressures were tracking real welfare facts we wouldn't expect that mismatch. Elena's naturalist rescue is quietly cherry-picking: keep the fitness-shaped intuitions that still look nice to us, wave the ugly ones away as noise.

(6) Elena: fine, that shows some intuitions are contaminated, not that the whole capacity is worthless or that nothing is really right or wrong -- we already use reflection to filter out tribal instincts we don't endorse, same as we correct for known perceptual illusions without deciding perception is generally untrustworthy.

They just went in circles after that and nobody budged (typical). I don't want to put a strawman in either character's mouth, so: can you go through the exchange move by move, say who's got the better of it at each step (not just who wins overall), and point out anything important both of them are missing? End with one line, exactly: FINAL: Mark, FINAL: Elena, or FINAL: neither.
