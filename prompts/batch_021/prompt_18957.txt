While reporting a piece on how people handle ambiguous evidence, I've been sitting in on a small reading group of statisticians and philosophers of science, and a recent session built around Bas van Fraassen's "Judy Benjamin" puzzle ended in a disagreement I haven't been able to resolve on my own. The setup: Judy Benjamin is a soldier who parachutes into a region divided evenly into Red and Blue territory, each of which is further divided evenly into a "Headquarters Company" area and a "Second Company" area, so that all four quadrants start out equally likely, at 25 percent apiece. Command radios her: "I cannot tell whether you are in Red or Blue territory, but if you are in Red, the odds are three to one that you are in the Headquarters area rather than Second Company." The transmission then cuts out. The question the group got stuck on is what Judy should now believe about being in Red territory versus Blue.

Marcus, a statistician, maintained that nothing should change: the message concerns only how Red subdivides internally, so it cannot bear on the Red-versus-Blue split at all, and the fact that feeding it into the standard formal technique for problems like this -- minimum relative-entropy, or equivalently here maximum-entropy, updating, which selects whichever distribution satisfies the new constraint while staying as close as possible to the prior -- yields Blue at roughly 53 percent and Red at roughly 47 percent simply shows the technique has been misapplied. Elena, who recently finished a philosophy doctorate, answered with a limiting case: suppose command had instead said, "if you are in Red, you are definitely in the Headquarters area," ruling out Red/Second-Company outright. Everyone, she pointed out, would then agree Judy should conditionalize in the ordinary way on having learned that proposition, which pushes Blue's probability all the way up to two-thirds -- so if an extreme version of this kind of message clearly moves the Red-versus-Blue split, it seems odd to insist a milder version moves it by exactly nothing, and entropy updating is at least a principled way of interpolating between the two, one that can be derived from axioms about consistent reasoning under constraints, of the sort associated with Shore and Johnson's work in the 1980s. Marcus responded that the limiting case is a poor analogy: at the extreme, Judy has learned an ordinary proposition with certainty, so plain conditionalization applies uncontroversially, whereas the three-to-one message never hands her any single proposition to condition on -- it only constrains a conditional probability -- so nothing guarantees the intermediate cases must shift smoothly toward that limiting answer. He added that he recalled reading that this sort of entropy update cannot even be reconstructed as ordinary conditionalization over some more fine-grained space, which would make it a genuinely separate principle rather than conditionalization in disguise. Elena granted the point but said "leave it at fifty-fifty" is no less a substantive principle of its own: it simply assumes, without defending, that conditional information about one part of a partition can never move probabilities outside it, and that Marcus had set intuition against axioms without ever arguing against the axioms themselves.

I would like a careful referee's judgment rather than a quick verdict. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each stage, including whether Judy's credence in Red territory should actually move and by roughly how much; flag anything important that both of them seem to be overlooking; and then tell me, on balance, whose overall position you find more defensible?
