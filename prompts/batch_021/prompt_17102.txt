I'm several drafts into a novel whose central figure is an analytic philosopher locked in a long-running, increasingly bitter feud with her former doctoral supervisor over the metaphysics of dispositions—fragility, solubility, courage, and the like. One pivotal scene has the two of them sparring over why the simple idea that to have a disposition is just for a certain conditional to be true of you (something like: the glass is fragile just in case it would break if struck) turns out to be so hard to defend. While researching this scene, I've been reading around in the literature and have run into two counterexamples that are clearly meant to be doing different work, though the popular summaries I've found tend to run them together: C. B. Martin's "finkish" dispositions (his case of a wire that is dead, but wired up to a device that makes it live the instant someone touches it, so that it satisfies the relevant conditional without ever really having been live beforehand) and the sort of "masking" or "antidote" case in which a poison is neutralized by something ingested at the very same moment, so that the disposition to kill is retained but never manifests.

For my characters' dispute to have real intellectual bite, rather than just gesturing vaguely at "some problem with conditionals," I need to get this distinction exactly right. Could you compare finkish dispositions with masking/antidotes as objections to the conditional analysis of dispositions, bringing out precisely how the two kinds of case differ in structure and in what each shows is wrong with the simple analysis? I'm especially interested in whether David Lewis's "reformed" conditional analysis, which ties a disposition to an intrinsic causal basis that must persist through the stimulus, genuinely succeeds in defusing one of these problems while remaining exposed to the other, or whether that is too tidy a way of dividing the labor between them. Please reach a definite verdict on this and justify it: it's exactly the kind of exchange my two philosophers would have, and I don't want my supposedly sharpest character to end up making a muddled argument.
