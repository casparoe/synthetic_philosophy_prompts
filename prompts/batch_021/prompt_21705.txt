Here's the question:

Explain Patrick Devlin's "disintegration thesis" and H.L.A. Hart's main objection to it. Then explain Hart's distinction between positive morality and critical morality, and say whether Hart ends up defending an absolute rule that the law should never be used to enforce morality.

And here's my classmate Denise's answer, copied exactly as she wrote it:

"Devlin's disintegration thesis holds that a society is held together by its shared morality, and that if that morality isn't enforced — say, by permitting the private homosexual acts and prostitution discussed in the Wolfenden Report — the society will gradually come apart, the way it would if treason went unpunished. Really this is just a special case of Mill's harm principle: for Devlin, immorality does real, if slow-acting, harm to the social fabric, so the state is entitled to prevent it the same way it prevents any other harm. Hart's objection is that Devlin never shows this harm actually happens — there's no evidence that a society tolerating private homosexual acts is any less stable than one that doesn't.

Hart also distinguishes positive morality (the morality a given society actually holds) from critical morality (the morality that would be justified by rational argument), and argues Devlin is only entitled to appeal to the first, which can't by itself justify legal coercion. Since Hart's whole case rests on Mill, he ends up defending the harm principle in its original, strict form: the law is never justified in restricting conduct that harms no one but the person doing it, and moral disapproval, however widely shared, is never sufficient grounds for making something a crime."
