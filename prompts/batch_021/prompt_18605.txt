I work as a software engineer, mostly on backend and ML infrastructure, and last year my team was asked to build a risk-scoring model for a consumer lending product: given an applicant's data, it outputs a predicted probability of default, which underwriters then use to set interest rates or decline applications outright. Before launch, our compliance team ran the model through a fairness audit and flagged something that puzzled me. The model was well-calibrated within every demographic group we checked — among applicants assigned, say, a 20% predicted default risk, roughly 20% actually defaulted, regardless of group — yet its false-positive and false-negative rates differed noticeably across groups. My first instinct was that this was a bug we could retune away.

It isn't. Digging into it — partly via the older COMPAS recidivism-scoring controversy, which turns out to be the canonical instance of exactly this pattern (ProPublica's investigation emphasized the tool's unequal error rates across race, while its maker, Northpointe, defended it on the grounds that it was equally well-calibrated across race) — I learned that whenever the base rate of the outcome genuinely differs between two groups, it is mathematically impossible for any classifier short of a perfect predictor to be simultaneously calibrated within groups and equalized in its error rates across groups. This is apparently a real, provable impossibility result in the algorithmic fairness literature (I believe the two properties are sometimes called "sufficiency" and "separation"), not just an artifact of sloppy engineering.

That's the part I can't get past. It seems to mean that "make the model fair" cannot just mean "satisfy the fairness metric," because someone still has to decide which notion of fairness is the right target in the first place — and both candidates here seem to track something real: calibration guarantees that a given score means the same thing regardless of who you are, while equalized error rates guarantee that the model's mistakes aren't borne disproportionately by one group. I'd like you to write me an essay that actually takes a side on this, rather than just presenting the trade-off as an unresolvable dilemma. Specifically: setting aside the degenerate cases (equal base rates, or a perfect predictor), which of the two properties has the stronger claim to what we actually mean by a "fair" score, and does that answer shift depending on the stakes of the decision, or on whether the base-rate gap itself plausibly reflects biased measurement of the outcome rather than a genuine underlying difference? You're free to argue that both criteria are the wrong target entirely, if you think that's the more defensible position — but either way, don't leave me with an "it depends, there's no fact of the matter" shrug. Weigh the strongest arguments on each side and commit to a real, defensible verdict, even a conditional one (something like "prioritize X unless condition Y holds" is a perfectly good kind of answer, as long as it's an actual verdict).
