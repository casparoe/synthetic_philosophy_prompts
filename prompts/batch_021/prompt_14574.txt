A friend of mine said over dinner that "verisimilitude is basically an open problem in philosophy of science," and when I pushed him on it he mumbled something about Popper's original definition being technically broken and never properly fixed. I looked into it a bit afterward and it turns out he's right that there's something real there -- Popper's actual formal definition of verisimilitude (built out of the "truth-content" and "falsity-content" of a theory) was apparently shown by Tichý and Miller back in the 70s to not really work, in the sense that it can't do the one job it was invented for: let you say that a false theory like Newtonian mechanics is closer to the truth than a false theory like Aristotelian physics. Every writeup of this I've found just states the result and then dives straight into notation-heavy talk about content classes that loses me completely.

Could you explain, at a gut level and without marching through the full formal proof, why Popper's content-based definition collapses like that -- what's the actual underlying reason two false theories basically can't be ranked against each other under his definition, even in cases where one seems obviously closer to the truth? And seperately, do the later "similarity"/"likeness"-style definitions (I want to say Oddie and Niiniluoto are names attached to this, but I might be misremembering) actually fix the problem, or do they just swap it for a different one? I have some memory of an objection along the lines of the ranking depending on how you carve up the predicates/language you use to describe things, but I don't really get why that would be such a big deal.
