Question: Explain how Arrow's impossibility theorem and Harsanyi's aggregation theorem each treat the problem of interpersonal comparisons of utility, and assess whether Sen's response to Arrow genuinely resolves the tension between the two results.

Student's response:

Arrow's impossibility theorem shows that, once there are at least three social alternatives, no social welfare function can aggregate individual preference orderings into a social ordering while simultaneously satisfying unrestricted domain, the weak Pareto principle, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship. Crucially, the theorem takes as its only input each individual's ordinal ranking of the alternatives: it does not allow the aggregation procedure to draw on any comparison of how much one person prefers an outcome relative to another, or of whether one person's welfare is higher than another's. The impossibility, in other words, is a consequence of confining the informational basis of social choice to non-comparable ordinal preferences.

Harsanyi's aggregation theorem takes up the same problem within the different setting of choice under uncertainty. Provided that each individual's preferences over lotteries satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, and that social preferences over those same lotteries satisfy the axioms as well as the Pareto principle, the social utility function must take the form of an affine combination of the individual utility functions. Because the theorem thereby endows each person with a cardinal utility scale, and cardinal scales are automatically commensurable across individuals, Harsanyi's result amounts to a proof that once cardinal utility information is restored to Arrow's four conditions, they single out utilitarianism as the unique social welfare rule.

Sen's response generalizes this insight while weakening its demands. Rather than requiring the full cardinal comparability that Harsanyi's theorem invokes, Sen showed that weaker informational assumptions, such as being able to rank utility levels across persons without being able to compare utility differences, are already sufficient to escape Arrow-type impossibilities, and that different degrees of comparability recover different rules: level comparability alone recovers something like the Rawlsian maximin, while comparability of utility differences is needed to recover utilitarian summation. Arrow's and Harsanyi's results are therefore not so much competitors as two points on a spectrum of informational assumptions about interpersonal comparison, with the impossibility gradually receding as richer comparisons are allowed.
