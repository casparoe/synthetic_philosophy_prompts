Every other week a few of us get together for board games, and last time the evening detoured into a debate that I haven't been able to let go of. I should say upfront that I'm a backend engineer with no formal background in philosophy, so I'm hoping you can referee this more competently than I could. Part of why it's stuck with me is that I catch myself doing a version of the same thing the argument was about, except with pull requests I keep promising myself I'll review "first thing tomorrow."

The trigger was our friend Jonah, who has spent months trying to stop scrolling on his phone in bed. Most nights around 10pm he'll announce, with complete sincerity, that he's putting the phone down at 11 so he can finally fix his sleep. Most nights around 11:05 he's still scrolling, and by the next morning he's annoyed with himself about it right up until he makes the identical resolution that evening. Two of the other regulars, Maya and Derek, got into an argument about what this actually shows, and it went roughly like this:

1. Maya said this isn't "weakness of will" in any deep sense — it's a textbook case of hyperbolic discounting. People (and plenty of non-human animals, apparently) don't discount future rewards at a constant rate; the curve is steep over short intervals and flattens out further away, so a reward twenty minutes off gets weighted way out of proportion to one eight hours off. Jonah's 10pm self and his 11:05 self are running the same calculation with different inputs, and there's nothing irrational about that — it's just what time preference looks like.

2. Derek, who'd apparently gone down a Wikipedia rabbit hole on this since the last game night, said that's a description, not a defense, and that Maya was just giving the problem a fancier name. The fact that Jonah's preference flips between 10pm and 11:05 with no new information arriving in between is precisely what inconsistency is. He added that "weakness of will" — or akrasia, he informed us, since apparently there's a whole literature on this — was never supposed to mean "violates some outside rule"; it means acting against your own considered judgment, which Jonah does on a nightly schedule.

3. Maya replied that Derek was smuggling in a standard that doesn't obviously apply. At 11:05, continuing to scroll really is what Jonah most wants given how his discounting works in that moment; he isn't defying his own judgment at 11:05, he's just formed a different one than the one he had at 10pm. Changing your mind over time without new evidence might be worth criticizing, she said — call it diachronic inconsistency — but that's a much thinner charge than knowingly doing the wrong thing while doing it, and it's exactly what happens whenever a preference is sensitive to circumstance (nobody calls you irrational for wanting soup on a cold day and salad on a hot one).

4. Derek said the soup comparison doesn't hold, because Jonah isn't sincerely changing his mind — he's told them, in the moment, at 11:05, that he knows he should stop and feels bad continuing, rather than having actually come around to thinking scrolling is the better use of the next twenty minutes. That persistence of "I shouldn't be doing this" running alongside the doing of it, Derek said, is the exact phenomenon the term was coined for, and no amount of relabeling the discount curve makes the felt conflict disappear.

Neither of them budged, and I don't have the background to say who was actually landing the better blows.
