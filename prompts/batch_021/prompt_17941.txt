Here's the question they were given: "In Nietzsche's On the Genealogy of Morality, explain (a) how the 'slave revolt in values' produces the moral vocabulary of good and evil out of the earlier aristocratic vocabulary of good and bad, and (b) how bad conscience originates according to the Second Essay. Your answer should explain what role ressentiment plays in each part of the story, and should use at least one piece of Nietzsche's etymological evidence."

And here's the answer I have to grade, copied exactly as submitted:

"Nietzsche thinks our present moral concepts of good and evil aren't eternal facts but have a history, and that history is basically a power struggle between two types of people, the nobles/masters and the slaves. Originally 'good' just meant noble, powerful, aristocratic, and 'bad' meant common, low, plebeian -- Nietzsche even points to the German word 'schlecht' (bad), which is etymologically almost the same word as 'schlicht' (plain, simple), so 'bad' originally just meant something like 'common' or 'plain' (i.e. not aristocratic), and only later curdled into the stronger, moralized sense of 'bad' we have now. This was the masters' own self-affirming judgment ('we the powerful are good'), and 'bad' was just an afterthought, a way of saying 'not like us.'

Slave morality inverts this. The slaves can't act out their hostility toward the masters directly, so they harbor a kind of impotent hatred that Nietzsche calls ressentiment, and this ressentiment becomes creative: instead of just being angry, the slaves invent an entirely new value, 'evil,' which is really just a moralized description of the masters' strength and pride, and then define 'good' as the opposite of that -- meekness, humility, poverty. So where master morality starts from a 'yes' to itself, slave morality starts from a 'no' to the other.

Bad conscience is really this same ressentiment, just turned inward instead of outward. Since the slaves can't discharge their hostility onto the masters, they turn that cruelty against themselves instead, and this self-directed cruelty is what Nietzsche calls bad conscience -- basically ressentiment with nowhere left to go, so it attacks its own possessor. This is also where guilt comes from: Nietzsche notes that the German word for guilt, 'Schuld,' comes from 'Schulden,' meaning debts, so guilt originally meant being in debt to the community for a wrong you committed. Later the ascetic priest exploits this bad conscience by linking it to sin and God, but the raw material -- cruelty with no outlet -- is supplied by ressentiment."
