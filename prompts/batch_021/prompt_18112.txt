I've been chewing on evidential cooperation in large worlds (the old "multiverse-wide superrationality" framing Oesterheld used) for a discussion post and want a sanity check before I put it up, since my decision theory background is basically two grad seminars and a lot of forum lurking. Heres the core of the argument as I've currently got it written down:

"ECL asks us to imagine the universe (or multiverse) is so vast that there exist, elsewhere, agents whose reasoning is correlated with ours -- not causally connected in any way, but correlated in the sense that if we're the kind of agent who'd find this very argument compelling, so are they, the same way two mathematicians who never met can independently hit on the same proof. Because our decision is evidence about theirs and vice versa, each of us should act as though we'd struck a bargain: shade our behavior a bit away from our own parochial values and toward a compromise aggregate of the values held across the whole correlated set, since doing so is evidence that our distant counterparts are doing the same for us. The whole scheme only gets off the ground under something like evidential decision theory -- under a causal theory there's no channel for the 'cooperation' to run through, so you'd just quietly defect and keep the gains for your own values."

My hang-up, which I genuinely can't tell is a real problem or just me missing something, is that this sounds like it smuggles in Kant's universalizability test through the back door: "act as the sort of agent whose decision-procedure you'd want instantiated widely" doesn't seem that different from "act only on maxims you could will to be universal law," just re-derived from decision theory instead of practical reason (and I'm pretty sure Hofstadter himself drew that comparison for superrationality generally). If that's a fair parallel, shouldn't ECL inherit the classic objections to universalizability arguments, like the problem that almost any maxim can be redescribed at whatever level of generality makes it come out universalizable? Or does the correlation-based framing actually dodge that bullet in a way plain Kantian universalizability doesn't? Curious whether my summary of the argument is fair to the literature, whether the Kant comparison actually holds up or is a red herring, and whether there's a more standard objection to ECL I'm skating past by fixating on this one.
