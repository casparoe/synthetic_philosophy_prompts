Ok so I went down a rabbit hole this week reading about epistemic game theory (I guess that's what the field is called), starting from a wikipedia article on the centipede game and ending with me skimming Aumann's 1995 paper on backward induction. Thing that got me: Aumann apparently proved that if its common knowledge both players are rational, the whole game unravels immediately -- the first player just grabs the pile of money right away instead of passing it along, even though cooperating for a few more rounds would leave both of them way better off. Kind of reminded me of a race condition, the outcome depends entirely on what everyone assumes everyone else will do at every future step. But then I found out this is actually super contested -- people like Binmore, Reny, Bicchieri and Stalnaker pushed back hard, arguing the proof smuggles in something incoherent: if both players are always rational, most nodes of the game should never even get reached, so what does "commonly known rationality" even mean once somebody actually deviates and everyone's stuck figuring out what to believe next? Stalnaker apparently gets the literal opposite conclusion out of basically the same setup, just different assumptions about how beliefs get revised after a surprise move. Also found out Aumann and Binmore had an actual back-and-forth about this in the same journal (a "note" and a "reply"), which cracked me up -- two grown men journal-fighting over a hypothetical pile of money.

Anyway, can you write me a dialogue, maybe 600-800 words, between two epistemic game theorists sitting across the table from each other mid-way through an actual centipede game (real pile of money between them, deciding whether to take it or pass it along)? One of them is an Aumann-style true believer who thinks backward induction just is what common knowledge of rationality entails, no way around it. The other is a Binmore/Stalnaker-style skeptic who thinks the whole argument collapses once you ask what a player should believe after an "irrational" move actually happens. I want real back-and-forth on that specific crux, not both of them just restating their conclusions -- like, the believer needs an actual answer to the "ok but what happens off the path" objection, not just a re-statement of the theorem.
