I'm preparing this week's session for a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, in which we work through probabilism and the main arguments offered on its behalf, and I've run into a point I'd like to present more carefully than the standard textbook gloss allows. Everyone in the room readily grants the Dutch Book Theorem itself: if a credence function fails to satisfy the probability axioms, one can always construct a finite set of bets, each of which looks fair (or better than fair) by the agent's own lights, whose combination guarantees her a net loss no matter how things turn out. What I've never managed to convey crisply is the further inferential step, from that purely mathematical fact to the substantive normative conclusion that incoherent credences are thereby irrational. Sure enough, when I floated this last term, one of my sharper students raised the old complaint that exposure to a sure loss seems to depend on contingent facts about the agent's willingness to bet and on there being a bookie both clever and motivated enough to assemble the relevant book -- neither of which seems like it should bear on whether her credences are rational in the first place.

I know that Christensen and others working in what's sometimes called the "depragmatizing" tradition have tried to relocate the real defect: not in the possible loss of money, but in some kind of purely doxastic or evaluative inconsistency that mere vulnerability to a book is supposed to reveal rather than constitute. Could you walk me through, at a fairly granular level, how that move is meant to work -- what the underlying inconsistency is supposed to consist in once the betting apparatus has been bracketed off -- and why some formal epistemologists remain unconvinced that it actually rescues the argument rather than simply relocating the same difficulty one level up? I'd rather not have the mechanics of the theorem itself rehearsed again, since I'm confident I can present that part on my own; what I'd find useful is help with the more contested territory of what, if anything, susceptibility to a Dutch Book is supposed to show about the rationality of an agent's credences.
