I've been down a rabbit hole with Pascal's Pensées lately and keep getting stuck on that fragment on justice and force (298 in the old Brunschvicg numbering, I think) — the one where he says something like justice without force is powerless and force without justice is tyrannical, and since we cant make justice strong we've just made strength "just" instead. Derrida picks this exact fragment apart in Force of Law, pairs it with a very similar move he finds earlier in Montaigne, and uses both to argue that all legal and political authority ultimately rests on a "mystical foundation" — a founding act of violence that can never be justified by law itself (there's no prior law to appeal to) and just gets dressed up as legitimate after the fact. What I can't settle is whether Pascal is actually making that radical, proto-deconstructive move himself, or whether Derrida is reading way more into a guy whose real project was to humble readers into Christian faith by showing that merely human justice is arbitrary next to God's. Write me an essay that lays out both readings on their own terms, actually engages with what Pascal says elsewhere in the Pensées about custom, power, and the "three orders" of flesh, mind, and charity, and then commits to a verdict instead of just splitting the difference. I want an actual answer, not a "well, it's complicated."
