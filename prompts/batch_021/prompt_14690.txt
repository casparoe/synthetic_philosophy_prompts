I've spent a long time reading LessWrong, Overcoming Bias, and their various offshoots, and one thing I've noticed is that Wittgenstein's private language argument gets invoked all the time in those circles — usually to make some point about meaning-as-use, the publicity of concepts, or why "I just know what I mean" doesn't settle anything on its own — but almost always at one remove from the actual text of the Philosophical Investigations. That eventually bugged me enough that I sat down and read the relevant stretch of the book (roughly §243 through §315) properly, rather than just absorbing the folklore version. It's now paying off in an unexpected way: a friend who teaches a graduate seminar on Wittgenstein has roped me into helping grade take-home exams, on the theory that a well-read non-specialist is a decent stress test for whether an answer would hold up with someone who isn't already inclined to give it the benefit of the doubt.

Here is the question, worth 20 points:

"Wittgenstein imagines someone who tries to keep a diary of a recurring sensation by associating it with the sign 'S' and writing 'S' in a calendar on every day the sensation occurs, where, by hypothesis, there is no external, publicly checkable criterion for whether 'S' has been applied correctly on any given occasion (§258). (a) [10 pts] Reconstruct the argument Wittgenstein builds around this example, and explain precisely why he thinks it shows that such a diary could not amount to a private language in his sense. (b) [5 pts] Wittgenstein later illustrates a related point with the 'beetle in a box' example (§293). What additional work does this example do, beyond what the diary example alone establishes? (c) [5 pts] Some popular expositions take the private language argument to establish that there is simply no such thing as private, inner experience. Is that an accurate summary of Wittgenstein's actual conclusion? Explain."

And here is the answer I've been asked to grade:

"(a) The diarist wants 'S' to name a private sensation, and every time the sensation recurs he writes 'S' in his calendar. Because the sensation is private, no one else can check whether he's using 'S' consistently — but crucially, neither can he: all he can appeal to is his memory of what he meant by 'S' before, and that memory would itself need some independent check to count as a genuine criterion, which by hypothesis isn't available here. So there's no gap left between 'S' merely seeming correct to him and 'S' actually being correct, which is precisely the point at which, as Wittgenstein puts it, we can no longer really talk about being 'right' at all. Since following a rule — and meaning something by a word is a case of following a rule — requires that a mistake be possible in principle, and that possibility has been defined out of existence here, the diary can't be a language in any real sense. Read this way, the argument is fundamentally about solitude: Wittgenstein is arguing that an individual with no linguistic community at all — a Crusoe figure — could never get a language off the ground in the first place, since there would be no one else around to correct him if his usage drifted.

(b) In the beetle-in-a-box case, everyone has a box only they can look into, and everyone calls whatever's inside it a 'beetle.' The point is that if the meaning of 'beetle' depended on each person's private object, that object could change, or vanish, or differ from box to box, without making the slightest difference to how the word gets used — so the private object is doing no work and simply drops out of consideration as irrelevant. This reinforces the diary argument: you can't secure a word's meaning by pointing it at something only one person can inspect, because whatever's inside can't be what fixes correct use.

(c) So the upshot is that there's no private, inner fact that could fix the meaning of a sensation-word like 'S' or 'pain' — what fixes it instead is publicly available circumstance and communal agreement, which is essentially the reading Kripke develops in Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language: a word means what it means because the community would endorse that use, not because of any inner state the speaker is separately tracking. So yes, the private language argument does show that 'inner experience,' in the sense of some inner object language could simply refer to, isn't doing any real work — the popular gloss has this basically right."
