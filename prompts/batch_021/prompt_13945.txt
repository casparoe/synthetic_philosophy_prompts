Back when I was still working in statistical mechanics, I used to burn through my lunch breaks reading Douglas Hofstadter's old "Metamagical Themas" columns, and his idea of "superrationality" -- that two identically-reasoning agents playing a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma should both cooperate, because whatever argument convinces one of them to defect or cooperate is, by the symmetry of the situation, exactly as convincing to the other one -- always struck me as one of the slicker pieces of reasoning I'd run into outside of actual physics. Now that I'm retired and getting properly into philosophy (slowly, and probably not always correctly), I keep running into a newer-sounding idea out of the effective altruism/longtermist world called "evidential cooperation in large worlds," which from what I can tell takes basically the same superrationality move and blows it up to the scale of the whole universe, or multiverse if its infinite: the thought being that in a big enough world there are bound to be huge numbers of agents totally cut off from us causally, who nonetheless reason in ways correlated with ours, even if they're not exact copies of us and dont share our values.

I mentioned this to an old colleague of mine over coffee and we ended up arguing about whether there's anything genuinely new here, or whether it's just superrationality wearing a bigger coat. So I'd like you to properly compare evidential cooperation in large worlds with Hofstadter-style superrationality. Where does the large-worlds version really need correlation-without-identity and evidential decision theory to carry weight that plain superrationality never had to carry, and where is it secretly still riding on that same old assumption of near-perfect symmetry between agents? And while you're at it, I'd like your honest take on how serious a problem it is for the large-worlds argument that nobody can actually specify who counts as "correlated enough with me" to be part of the deal, given that Hofstadter got to just stipulate perfect symmetry in his toy examples and the large-worlds crowd doesn't get that luxury.
