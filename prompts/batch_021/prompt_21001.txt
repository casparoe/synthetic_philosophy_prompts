Since a bout of insomnia this spring I've been keeping a dream journal, and a couple of unusually vivid, borderline-lucid dreams have left me with a lingering, faintly unsettling difficulty telling, in the first few seconds after waking, which details belonged to the dream and which to the day before. I mentioned this at a monthly reading group I belong to, where we happened to be partway through the Inner Chapters of the Zhuangzi in translation, and it set off a real argument between two of the other members about how to read the famous butterfly dream passage that closes the second chapter (the "Qiwulun," often translated "Discussion on Making All Things Equal") -- the one where Zhuang Zhou dreams he is a butterfly, wakes up, and can no longer tell whether he is a man who dreamed he was a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming he is a man.

Owen opened by claiming the passage is essentially a Chinese Descartes: since a dream can feel exactly as vivid and coherent as waking life while it is happening, Zhuangzi's point must be that we can never be certain, at any given moment, which state we are in, and that is exactly the moral of Zhuang Zhou's confusion on waking. Naomi pushed back, noting that the very next line has Zhuangzi insist there must, in fact, be a real distinction between Zhou and the butterfly, and that what he calls this whole business -- "the transformation of things" (wu hua) -- sounds like a claim about the fluidity of identity, not a claim that we lack any reliable test for reality; a skeptic who had just spent a passage arguing that dream and waking are indistinguishable would be undercutting himself by then flatly asserting that the two are, in fact, different. Owen replied that Zhuangzi makes the stronger epistemic claim explicitly a little earlier in the very same chapter, where he says that while dreaming we do not know we are dreaming, that only a "Great Sage" who might not appear for ten thousand generations would truly grasp that this whole waking life is itself one "Great Dream," and that on this basis he tells his interlocutor flatly that both he and Confucius are dreaming right now -- which is not a claim about identity at all, but a direct denial that anyone present can currently tell wakefulness from sleep. Naomi granted that this earlier passage sounds skeptical taken by itself, but pointed out that Zhuangzi immediately adds that his own claim that they are dreaming is itself just another dream -- a self-undermining move no one mounting a general skeptical argument would need to make -- and that he is needling Confucius by his given name, a real breach of etiquette, rather than advancing a thesis meant to apply evenhandedly to everyone; on her reading, the target is the specific overconfidence of the rival schools of his day, not the reliability of perception as such. Owen's rejoinder was that carving out that exemption is convenient but arbitrary: if the argument is strong enough to deflate Confucius's certainty, nothing in it stops it from deflating ours too, and the self-undermining twist might just be Zhuangzi conceding the point every sophisticated skeptic eventually has to concede, rather than proof that he was never doing epistemology in the first place.

Neither of them budged, and I have gone back and forth on it since without settling the question myself. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the stronger position at each stage, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also be curious whether there's something about what the passage is actually doing that you think both of them are overlooking.
