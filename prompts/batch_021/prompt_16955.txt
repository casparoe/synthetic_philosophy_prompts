I'm a retired field geologist (almost forty years of it, a lot of that in pretty remote parts of the world) and about a year ago I fell into a small philosophy discussion group that meets at our local library - mostly other retirees, plus one ex-lawyer who runs it like it's still a seminar. This month I'm the one who has to bring a topic, and I've settled on something that's been nagging at me since we did a session on ethics generally: why exactly does making a promise generate a real obligation, rather than just being a nice-to-have?

From my reading so far there seem to be two quite different families of answer on offer. Hume, in the bit of the Treatise called "Of the obligation of promises," seems to think there's nothing natural about the bindingness at all - its basically a useful convention society sets up because everyone benefits from being able to rely on each other, so the "obligation" is just whatever we've collectively attached to the act of promising, a bit like the rules of a game that only really bind once enough people are playing along. Then much later you get someone like Scanlon in What We Owe to Each Other, who grounds the obligation directly in the wrong of manipulating someone's trust and expectations in the moment you promise them something - he's got a whole "Principle F" for it, if I'm remembering the name right - and on that view you don't seem to need any background social practice at all.

Here's the test case I want to bring to the group, and I'd like your help thinking it through first. Suppose I'm the last survivor of an expedition, stuck somewhere with zero chance of rescue, and my dying colleague asks me to promise I'll get word to their family, who's waiting back home, if I somehow ever make it out alive - both of us knowing full well that not a soul will ever find out whether I keep it or not, and that there's no future "practice" of promise-keeping left for either of us to protect. Does Hume's convention-based story actually predict that I'm bound in a case like this, or does it just quietly stop applying once you strip away the social payoff? And does Scanlon's account really do any better, or does it just move the mystery somewhere else? I'd like a proper side-by-side of how the two theories would handle this specific scenario, rather than the usual one-line "convention vs. trust" contrast, which is about as far as I've gotten on my own.
