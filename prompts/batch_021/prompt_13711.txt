For the past year I have been drafting a novel built on a single point of divergence in American history: in my version, Oswald's rifle jams in Dealey Plaza and Kennedy survives the drive through Dallas. In trying to get the counterfactual scaffolding of the plot right, I fell down a rabbit hole of philosophy-of-language reading on conditionals, and I have run into an example that has bothered me far more than it probably should.

The literature keeps setting up a contrast between two sentences: "If Oswald did not kill Kennedy, then someone else did," and "If Oswald had not killed Kennedy, then someone else would have." Apparently most people judge the first to be true, on the reasoning that Kennedy was shot by somebody, so if it wasn't Oswald it must have been another gunman, while judging the second false, since there is no reason to think a second assassin was waiting in the wings. When I brought this up over dinner, a friend who teaches logic just said, "ah, the Adams pair," smiled, and changed the subject, which has only made me more determined to work out for myself why this is supposed to be puzzling at all.

My own suspicion is that the whole thing is a trick of English grammar rather than a genuine logical divergence. Both sentences concern exactly the same pair of events — what Oswald did, and whether Kennedy was shot — and differ only in swapping indicative "did/didn't" for subjunctive "had/would have," much as "he will go" and "he would go" are the same claim relativized to a different point of view. Since the underlying content is identical, any difference in how true the two sentences feel seems to me like a psychological quirk that careful readers should train themselves out of, rather than a real difference in what the sentences mean. Am I right that this is fundamentally one conditional dressed in two grammatical costumes, and that treating the indicative and counterfactual versions as logically distinct is a case of philosophers over-reading an artifact of English tense?
