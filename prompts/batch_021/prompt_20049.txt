In my epistemic game theory course, we have spent the last two weeks on Aumann's proof that common knowledge of rationality (CKR) in a finite game of perfect information forces the backward-induction outcome, together with the objections raised by Binmore and Bicchieri and the related "backward induction paradox" due to Pettit and Sugden. I follow the formal result itself, but I am still unsure who actually wins the underlying philosophical argument. Write a dialogue between two game theorists debating a concrete four-move centipede game: one defends Aumann's conclusion that CKR at the very start of the game already settles that the first player will take rather than pass, while the other presses the objection that reaching any node past the first already falsifies the assumption of CKR, so nothing about what rationality requires there can legitimately be inferred from it. Have them engage directly with Aumann's distinction between "substantive" and "material" rationality, the question of what a player is entitled to believe upon reaching a node that CKR implies should have probability zero, and whether Aumann's theorem is best read as a claim about real-time strategic reasoning or merely as a static consistency condition on the players' dispositions. Neither side should concede too easily or talk past the other's actual argument.
