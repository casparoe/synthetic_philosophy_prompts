So I never studied philosophy or theology in school, I just read old texts for fun sometimes, and I went down a rabbit hole recently on Augustine and lying. I already knew church guys from way back were against lying in general, but I didnt expect him to take it this far. He's got this early treatise called De Mendacio where he basically sorts every possible lie into a list from worst to least bad, and even the most sympathetic case on the list -- lying to save someone's life -- he still says its a sin, just a smaller one than the others. Then later on, in another book (Contra Mendacium, which I think he wrote to some guy named Consentius who wanted to use deception to infiltrate and expose a group of heretics), he doubles down and says no, you still can't do it, not even for that. And what really got me stuck is he has to deal with Rahab from the book of Joshua, who lies straight to the king's men to protect the Israelite spies and then gets praised for it later in scripture, plus the two midwives in Exodus who lie to Pharaoh -- and his way of handling it is basically "God rewarded them for their faith and mercy, not for the lie, they'd have gotten an even bigger reward if they'd found a way to help without lying." Which sounds like a dodge, but the deeper I read the more I noticed his real argument isn't about who gets hurt or helped at all (which is basically what every modern debate about "is it ok to lie to save a life" assumes the argument must be about) -- it's that deceiving someone with speech corrupts what speech is for in the first place, so it damages your own soul regardless of the consequences to anyone else.

What I want is an essay that actually engages with that specific argument instead of just re-running the generic "is it ever okay to lie" debate. Lay out the real case for Augustine's position (the stuff about speech having a purpose that gets violated, not just a harm based argument), and the strongest objections to it (the save-a-life cases, the Rahab problem, the fact that pretty much no other major ethical tradition goes this far -- Kant's the closest, and even his reasons for it are completely different). I don't want a wishy washy "there's merit on both sides" ending -- actually tell me, once you take Augustine's own reasoning on its own terms rather than strawmanning it, whether it holds up or where exactly it breaks down, and defend that.
