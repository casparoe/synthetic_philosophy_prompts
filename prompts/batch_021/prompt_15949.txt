I've been drafting a post for my blog connecting two corners of Berkeley's philosophy that don't usually get discussed together, and I want a sanity check before I publish it, because the argument has exactly the kind of clean, satisfying shape that makes me suspicious of it. Here's the draft in full (working title: "Money Without Matter"):

Everyone who has taken an introductory philosophy course remembers Berkeley for one move: esse est percipi, the claim that to be is to be perceived, and the argument that a mind-independent material substratum is an idle wheel we can subtract from our account of tables and trees without losing anything. Almost nobody remembers that the same Berkeley spent much of the 1730s writing obsessively about money.

The Querist, issued in three installments while he was Bishop of Cloyne, is a long sequence of rhetorical questions about the stalled Irish economy, and it keeps circling back to one claim: that the wealth of a nation is not the gold and silver inside its borders but the productive "industry" of its people, and that money is best understood not as a valuable substance but as a counter for reckoning and transferring shares in that productive activity. A well-run system of paper credit, backed by nothing more than trust in a competently administered bank, could in principle do everything specie does — which mattered enormously to a country too short of bullion to finance its own development any other way.

That isn't, I think, a new idea for Berkeley; it's an old one redeployed. A few years earlier, in the Alciphron, he had defended the meaningfulness of religious language against the freethinkers by pointing out that a word can direct our conduct and combine correctly with other words without our forming a distinct idea of its object every time we use it, and he reached for exactly the same image — a counter in an account book — to make the point. (I'm working from memory on the Alciphron passage and would genuinely like to be corrected if I'm misremembering where or how explicitly he puts it.) In both places the point is the same: a token can do real work — securing an inference, moving goods — purely by being embedded in a rule-governed practice, without needing some further intrinsic worth or determinate idea behind it to justify that work.

Once you see it that way, the money talk starts to look like the immaterialism, run a second time on a different domain. In the Principles, sensible qualities don't need a material substratum, because their reality is exhausted by their conforming to the lawlike, shared pattern of ideas that God's continuous perception guarantees. In the Querist, money doesn't need a metallic substratum, because its value is exhausted by its role in a lawlike, shared pattern of credit and exchange that a well-administered bank guarantees. Substance drops out of both accounts for the same reason: positing an inert something underneath the phenomena we actually care about adds nothing to their reality.

But the parallel has a crack in it, and I can't decide how serious the crack is. Berkeley's idealism has always had to answer for what keeps the pattern of ideas from collapsing into whatever any one perceiver currently feels like imagining, and his answer is theological: God's perceiving is the fixed point the rest of us are answerable to. The Querist doesn't give money a fixed point anywhere near that strong; its guarantee is a fallible human institution that could be mismanaged or simply fail. So either Berkeley thought a merely conventional, human-administered regularity was secure enough to carry the reality of value even though nothing short of God was secure enough to carry the reality of matter — a real asymmetry in his system, and one worth explaining — or there's a sturdier anchor for money in his account than I'm giving him credit for.

I'd welcome comments of three kinds: whether the reading of The Querist here is fair to Berkeley, or whether I'm projecting a modern, functionalist story about money — the kind of thing anyone who's read Nick Szabo on the origins of money is primed to reach for — onto what was really just an argument for a particular bank; whether the Alciphron connection is right, and if not, what I'm misremembering; and whether the asymmetry I flag in the last paragraph is the sharpest problem with the parallel, or whether there's a more basic objection to the whole exercise that I'm skating past.
