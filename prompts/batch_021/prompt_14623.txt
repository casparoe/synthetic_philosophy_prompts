I'm outlining a novel whose two central characters are linguists on a joint fieldwork posting, and I want to draft a scene where an argument that has clearly been simmering between them since the trip began finally breaks into the open. That evening, replaying the day's recordings, they reach the moment their informant, watching a rabbit dart across the clearing, said "gavagai." Write their exchange as a dialogue that could plausibly appear in the novel.

Elena, who wrote her dissertation on Quine, should press the case that no amount of further fieldwork could ever settle whether "gavagai" is properly glossed as "rabbit," "undetached rabbit part," or "rabbit stage" -- not because the fact is hard to get at, but because there is no fact to get at, since all three glosses agree on every stimulation that would prompt assent or dissent, and even asking whether "this gavagai" is the "same" as "that one" only presupposes a translation of the native speaker's apparatus of identity and individuation rather than testing it.

Marcus, who has never accepted Quine's conclusion but knows the argument at least as well as she does, should resist her without simply insisting that meaning must be determinate somehow -- have him challenge either the austerity of the evidence Quine allows himself, or press the idea that some interpretive principle can do real disambiguating work rather than just asserting an answer by fiat. The two should engage each other's actual moves rather than talk past one another, and the scene should end without resolution, each still convinced the other has missed the point.
