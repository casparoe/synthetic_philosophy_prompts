This semester I am taking a graduate seminar on theories of well-being, and we recently spent two weeks on idealized-preference (or "full-information") accounts of a person's good: the family of views associated with Richard Brandt and later refined by philosophers such as Peter Railton, on which what is good for someone is fixed by what a fully informed, vividly imagining, and procedurally rational version of that person would want for their actual, non-idealized self. The most damaging objection we covered was the one due to David Sobel and pressed further by Connie Rosati: if the idealization is mild, it may not filter out enough of the ignorance and inconsistency that motivated appealing to an idealized standpoint in the first place, but if the idealization is severe enough to actually correct these defects, the resulting idealized counterpart's values and cares may have drifted so far from the actual person's that it is no longer clear whose good is being tracked at all — we are no longer obviously hearing from a wiser version of the same person, but from a distinct agent whose verdicts have lost their authority over the original.

In office hours, my professor mentioned that Eliezer Yudkowsky's proposal that a superintelligent AI be aligned with humanity's "coherent extrapolated volition" (CEV), rather than with our present desires, is structurally the same idealizing move applied at the level of an entire species, and suggested it could make an interesting term-paper topic. As I understand the proposal, CEV is meant to capture what we would want if we knew more, thought faster, and were more the people we wished we were, with the extrapolation itself carried out in whatever direction we would currently wish it to go; where individual extrapolated volitions diverge too much — their "spread" is too high — the procedure is supposed to stay silent on that point rather than force a resolution, and a designated "last judge" is kept in reserve as a safeguard, able to halt the whole process if its output looks clearly mistaken. Before I commit to this as my thesis, I would like to see the strongest version of the argument that this apparatus does not actually save CEV from the Sobel/Rosati-style objection.

Please argue for the position that CEV cannot escape the Sobel/Rosati objection to idealized-preference accounts of a person's good, and that extending the idealizing move from a single individual to humanity's collective volition makes the underlying problem worse rather than better. I would like the argument to engage directly with CEV's specific mechanics — extrapolation, coherence, spread, and the last-judge safeguard — rather than resting on more generic worries about paternalism, aggregation across individuals, or whose preferences ultimately get counted.
