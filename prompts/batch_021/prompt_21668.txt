I'm a novelist currently assembling a linked collection of short stories about the strange intimacy between diagnosis and identity, and I keep circling back to Ian Hacking's work on what he calls the looping effects of human kinds. His central observation, if I'm remembering my reading correctly, is that classifying people is not like classifying rocks or chemical elements: when a new category of person is invented, named, or medicalized, the people who fall under it become aware of the label, absorb it into how they narrate their own lives, sometimes organize around it or push back against it, and thereby change the very behavior that clinicians and researchers set out to study, so that the category itself eventually has to be revised, which sets off another round of self-understanding, and so on. He illustrates the idea with real historical cases, such as the nineteenth-century diagnosis of multiple personality, or the "feebleminded" classifications used in early intelligence testing, but for this particular story I want to steer well clear of retelling any of his own textbook examples.

Could you write me a short story, somewhere in the range of 900 to 1,300 words, built around a wholly invented psychiatric or behavioral diagnosis of your own devising, one with no real-world counterpart? I'd like it to follow a single character through at least two full turns of the loop: an initial moment of being named or diagnosed, a visible shift in the character's self-understanding or conduct that follows directly from learning the label (through a support group, a book, an offhand remark from a clinician, or whatever you find most natural), and then some downstream consequence in which the diagnostic category itself is questioned, narrowed, or rewritten in light of how people like your character actually turned out to behave once labeled. I'd rather the mechanism stayed implicit in the scene-work and interiority than have any character pause to explain the theory out loud, and I'd like the piece to stay in a grounded, realist register rather than drifting into satire or science fiction.
