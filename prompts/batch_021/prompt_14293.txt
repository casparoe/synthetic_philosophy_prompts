I spent about 30 years as a health physicist, mostly setting radiation exposure limits at a nuclear plant, and now that I'm retired I've been reading philosophy for fun. A while back I ran across that old thought experiment about torture vs. dust specks -- you know, would you rather one person get tortured for years on end, or some absurdly huge number of people each get one barely noticeable speck of dust in their eye. It hit way too close to home, because half my career was spent arguing about what's called "collective dose," where you add up a mountain of tiny, individually undetectable radiation risks spread across a huge population and treat the sum as equivalent to some number of actual cancer deaths. Could you describe the most important implications of the torture/dust-specks aggregation problem for whether that kind of collective-dose reasoning really holds up, or if its just a statistical trick that lets regulators feel like they've quantified something they havent?
