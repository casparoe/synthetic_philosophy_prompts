A friend and I spent most of a long dinner arguing, of all things, about Rousseau's *Letter to d'Alembert on the Theatre*, after the conversation drifted from a mutual friend who had just quit her job to try to make it as a full-time streamer to whether Rousseau would have had anything useful to say about that way of life. We ended up further in the weeds than either of us expected. D'Alembert's "Genève" article for the Encyclopédie had proposed the city finally get a theatre; Rousseau's reply wasn't the simple "theatre corrupts" line I half-remembered from a survey course, but a fairly specific argument that plays don't reform an audience's morals so much as flatter the ones it already has back to it -- his complaint about Molière's Misanthrope, that it makes the blunt, sincere Alceste look ridiculous next to the smooth, accommodating Philinte, was apparently meant to illustrate exactly this. My friend also produced the detail, which I hadn't known and couldn't really answer, that Rousseau himself had written a genuinely successful opera, Le Devin du village, performed before Louis XV, years before he wrote the Letter -- which she took as evidence that his later position was less a principled stand against dramatic performance than special pleading on behalf of Geneva specifically.

That was really the crux of our disagreement. My friend's view is that the whole argument is too tightly fitted to its occasion to travel: Rousseau's case rested on Geneva being a small, still-virtuous republic that could least afford the luxury and idleness theatre would import, in contrast to a Paris he considered already so corrupt that a theatre there could do little further damage -- so treating this as a general theory of anything is a kind of category error. I think there's a portable mechanism underneath the Geneva-specific packaging: something about performers being habitually rewarded for feelings and personas not quite their own, and about spectatorship crowding out the sort of direct mutual engagement he wanted from the civic festivals he proposed instead, where citizens were meant to be their own spectacle rather than paying to watch someone else's.

I'd like your best reconstruction of how Rousseau would actually reason through the case of someone who has built a career out of broadcasting a curated version of their life to strangers online for money and attention, engaging seriously with my friend's objection along the way rather than just assuming I'm right. I'm especially interested in whether this practice counts, on his terms, as theatre proper or could instead be redeemed as something closer to his festival ideal; whether his "Paris was already lost, Geneva wasn't" move changes the verdict depending on what kind of audience and society is doing the watching; and how much weight his own operatic career should really be allowed to carry against him.
