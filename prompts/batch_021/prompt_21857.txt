For my philosophy club, we have been working through some introductory material on panpsychism, and last week we got stuck on what's called the combination problem: even if every fundamental entity (an electron, a quark, or whatever physics eventually settles on) turns out to have some primitive form of experience, it's genuinely unclear how countless tiny "micro-subjects" could ever combine into a single unified "macro-subject," like the conscious mind of a human being, rather than just remaining a disorganized heap of unrelated micro-experiences. One of the other members mentioned that the term itself is fairly recent, apparently coined by a philosopher named William Seager sometime in the 1990s, even though the underlying worry is often traced back to William James's objections to so-called "mind-dust" theories decades earlier. That got me wondering whether much older panpsychist-adjacent systems already had the resources to deal with this problem, even if they never phrased it this way, and Leibniz's monadology seemed like the obvious test case.

Leibniz treats monads as simple, "windowless" substances that cannot causally interact with one another, with all apparent coordination between them supplied entirely by pre-established harmony, and he explicitly denies that mere aggregates (bodies, machines, or even an organism's body considered apart from its "dominant monad") count as genuine unities in their own right. That already looks like it gives him strong grounds to resist the idea that a macro-subject could ever literally arise out of many micro-subjects fusing together. Yet his remarks on petites perceptions complicate this a little: his famous point that we hear the roar of the sea as a single sound even though we never separately notice the contribution made by each individual wave sounds almost like a proto-solution to the combination problem, even though, on reflection, it seems to be a claim about unnoticed perceptions aggregating within one monad's own perceptual state, not about many distinct subjects merging into one.

Could you reconstruct, as carefully as possible from Leibniz's actual commitments, what his most plausible response would be if a contemporary philosopher pressed the combination problem on him directly? I am especially interested in whether he would treat it as a real difficulty that his system still owes an answer to, or whether he would simply reject the assumption shared by both micro-panpsychist "phenomenal bonding" solutions and cosmopsychist alternatives, namely that composite subjects are things which get built up (or broken down) at all. It would also help if you could flag which parts of your answer follow fairly directly from things Leibniz actually says and which parts are more speculative extrapolation on your part, since I'm hoping to turn this into a short paper for the club and would rather not present an educated guess as settled Leibniz scholarship.
