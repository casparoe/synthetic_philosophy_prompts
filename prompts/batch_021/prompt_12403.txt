I spent much of last evening in a friendly but increasingly stubborn argument with a friend over George Berkeley's Querist, prompted by an essay she had been reading that traced a line from eighteenth-century Irish pamphleteering to contemporary Modern Monetary Theory. Her contention was that Berkeley, writing over a century and a half before Georg Friedrich Knapp coined the term "chartalism," had already arrived at its central insight: that money is not a commodity possessed of intrinsic worth but an artifact of social convention — what she kept calling his "ticket-or-counter" view — whose value depends on the institutional uses a community makes of it, most concretely in his proposal for a national bank to supply paper currency to a coin-starved Ireland. I argued that this imports far too much of our own monetary theory back into a text whose actual concerns were narrower and more moralized: reviving "industry" in an idle population, stemming the drain of specie and rents to absentee landlords, and addressing a specific local crisis, rather than developing anything resembling an account of taxation, sovereign currency issuance, or fiscal capacity. We reached no agreement, and the dispute mostly came down to how much interpretive weight one should place on the Querist's recurring concerns with industry, credit, and the passions, as against the handful of lines that sound strikingly "nominalist" about the nature of money itself.

I would like your considered judgment on this. Please write an essay assessing whether Berkeley's Querist is best read as a genuine forerunner of chartalist or state theories of money — and, by extension, of ideas now associated with MMT — or whether this is substantially an anachronistic projection onto a text with rather different aims and a much thinner theoretical apparatus. In making your case, engage with the actual substance and query-form of the text (its treatment of money, credit, banking, and industry) rather than resting mainly on Berkeley's better-known immaterialism, though you're welcome to note briefly whether his nominalism about money strikes you as continuous with, or in tension with, his broader metaphysical commitments about the mind-dependence of ideas. Please don't simply lay out both sides and stop there: I would like you to defend an actual verdict, even a heavily qualified one, and to say what would have to be true of Berkeley's text and intentions for my friend's reading, or mine, to be the more defensible one.
