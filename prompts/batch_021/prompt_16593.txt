So this has been living in my head rent free for a few weeks and I want to actually work through it properly instead of just going in circles with myself. Basic question: is it ever actually rational to believe something while at the same time believing that your own belief isn't well supported by your evidence? Backstory - me and my buddy were both grinding through the same nasty probability puzzle, we landed on different answers, and even though i went back through my own steps like five separate times and couldn't find anything wrong, just knowing that he's at least as sharp as me and got a different number made my confidence in my own answer tank. Which feels weird, because either my reasoning was good or it wasn't, and him disagreeing doesn't retroactively change the actual math I did. So was I being appropriately humble, or was I just being a pushover and letting "evidence about my evidence" push around the actual evidence?

That sent me down a rabbit hole and apparently there's a whole corner of epistemology about exactly this, under terms like "higher order evidence" and "epistemic akrasia" (pretty sure I've got the terms right, correct me if not). From what I can piece together there are basically two camps. One side says rationality requires your beliefs to always stay "in sync" with what you think your evidence supports overall - so the second you think, all things considered, that your evidence doesn't support P, you literally can't go on rationally believing P anymore, no exceptions (I've seen names like David Christensen come up a lot around here, and some kind of "non-akrasia" rule). The other side points to cases like this: a doctor gets told, with good reason to believe it, that she just took a drug known to mess with people's arithmetic. She redoes an important dosage calculation super carefully anyway, and it still checks out perfectly, step by step, as far as she can tell. Does she really have to distrust her own math there, just because of a rumor about her own brain, even when she can't find a single actual flaw in the reasoning itself? Seems like that's demanding way too much.

Can you write me an essay that actually wrestles with this properly instead of just defining a bunch of terms and shrugging? Lay out the strongest version of the "no akrasia allowed, always defer to the higher order evidence" side and the strongest version of the "sometimes you get to (or even have to) stick with your first order reasoning" side, and then actually pick a side instead of giving me a wishy-washy "both have good points" ending. A qualified verdict is fine, like "defer in cases like A but not cases like B" - I just want you to commit to something I could go argue with my buddy about, not a neutral summary of the debate.
