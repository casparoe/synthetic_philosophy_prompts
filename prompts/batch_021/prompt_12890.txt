Two close friends of mine, Elena and Marcus, are getting married next month, and they've asked me — the one person in their circle who isn't an academic philosopher, though I've interviewed both of them over the years for pieces on moral psychology — to give the toast. The complication is that they have spent much of the last decade in print disagreeing with each other about "moral worth": Marcus defends something close to the orthodox Kantian line that an act has genuine moral worth only when it is done from duty rather than inclination, and still cites the honest shopkeeper approvingly; Elena works on the opposing, broadly sentimentalist side of the debate, drawing on Michael Smith's charge of "moral fetishism" against duty-first views and on Nomy Arpaly's reading of Huckleberry Finn, and holds that acting from love or sympathy can itself be exactly what gives an act its worth, rather than something that has to be stripped out before the act counts morally. Their colleagues find it both touching and a little theoretically awkward that two people so committed to opposite answers are, by all accounts, obviously happy together — and I want to get the tone right, because with this crowd a toast that leans toward the wrong side of the debate will be noticed before the champagne is poured.

I keep coming back to Bernard Williams' old remark that a husband who pauses, mid-rescue, to confirm that saving his drowning wife rather than a stranger is morally permitted has had "one thought too many," which seems like exactly the right mix of wedding-appropriate and needling for this particular crowd — I'd like it to be the spine of the speech. Could you write the toast itself: about five minutes long, for a reception where roughly half the room will catch every reference above and the other half just want something warm and a little funny about two people they love? I don't want it to quietly resolve the disagreement in either direction, or to flatten it into a list of citations — I'd like the fact that two people who cannot agree on why kindness matters have nonetheless spent years being remarkably kind to each other to do the actual work of the speech, so that the disagreement supplies the joke and the closing lines supply the real warmth.
