I've spent a good number of years reading rationalist blogs and forums, and in that world "hyperbolic discounting" has become the default, almost reflexive explanation for akrasia -- procrastination, failed diets, broken resolutions, all of it gets filed under time-inconsistent discount functions, usually illustrated with the same well-worn "Ulysses and the sirens" framing. Recently, partly out of dissatisfaction with how flattened that story felt, I went back to some of the original philosophical material on weakness of will: Aristotle's discussion in Book VII of the Nicomachean Ethics, and especially Donald Davidson's essay "How Is Weakness of the Will Possible?", alongside some secondary discussion of George Ainslie's Picoeconomics. Doing so left me with a nagging worry that the rationalist-blogosphere version of the story quietly changes the subject rather than explaining the original puzzle.

Here is the worry, as precisely as I can state it: Davidson's puzzle is that the akratic agent seems to act against their own considered, all-things-considered judgment at the very moment of choice -- which looks almost contradictory, since intentional action seems to presuppose the belief that what one is doing is, in some sense, the thing to do. But the hyperbolic discounting model doesn't obviously reproduce that structure. On that model, when I take the donut over the diet, I'm not violating my judgment at the moment of choosing the donut; I'm simply applying a discount function, operative right then, under which the donut really does come out ahead. My earlier self's judgment gets overruled, but nothing internal to the choosing self at the moment of choice is being contradicted. So it starts to look like economists have swapped out a genuinely paradoxical phenomenon (synchronic irrationality) for a much tamer one (diachronic preference reversal), while quietly keeping the old vocabulary of "weakness of will" for this new, tamer thing.

Is this a real tension, or am I missing something that dissolves it? And if it is real, how have people working at the intersection of decision theory and philosophy of action tried to handle it -- do they argue that time-inconsistency secretly involves a form of synchronic irrationality after all, that Davidson's original puzzle was somewhat overstated, or something else entirely? I'd appreciate a genuinely detailed treatment of the conceptual issue itself, rather than another walk-through of precommitment devices and Beeminder-style solutions, which I've already encountered more times than I can count.
