In my normative ethics seminar this term, we've been reading Taurek's "Should the Numbers Count?", and the discussion turned into a running argument between two classmates that I'd like an outside opinion on, since I keep flip-flopping about who's right.

The case they were arguing about is a version of Taurek's own: a doctor has a fixed supply of a drug that can either go entirely to one badly injured person, or be split five ways to save five other people, each of whom faces an equally serious threat to their life. Taurek argues it's not obvious the doctor is required to save the five, and suggests that flipping a coin between the two options might be fairer than simply doing the arithmetic.

(1) Ingrid defended Taurek: she said the instinct to "just save the five" quietly assumes that five deaths sum to something five times as bad as one death, but there's no one who actually bears that summed loss — each of the six people stands to lose only their own life, exactly as much as any of the others. Treating the five as outweighing the one, she said, substitutes bookkeeping about total welfare for what should be equal concern for six separate people.

(2) Mateo replied that this argument proves too much: it would apply just as well to one person against a thousand, and no one thinks a doctor should flip a coin between saving one patient and saving a thousand. Since we obviously should save the thousand, he said, something in Ingrid's argument has to be wrong, even if he couldn't yet say what.

(3) Ingrid answered that "obviously" is exactly the problem: the intuition that we must save the thousand could just be the aggregative assumption she's questioning, dressed up as common sense, and Taurek would rather revise that intuition than give up treating each person's claim as complete in itself.

(4) Mateo then proposed a middle path: instead of a straight fifty-fifty coin flip or simply saving the greater number outright, run a lottery weighted by group size, so the lone person gets a one-in-six chance and the group of five gets a five-in-six chance. That way, no one's claim is simply overridden by a headcount, but numbers still make a statistical difference. Ingrid hadn't responded to this by the time the thread died.
