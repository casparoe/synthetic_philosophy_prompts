So this is a weird combo but bear with me. I've had a chunk of my savings in crypto for about a year now (started as a joke with my roommate, hasn't stayed a joke) and to feel a bit smarter about what I'm actually doing with my money I picked up an old "history of economic thought" reader over the summer. Buried in it was a chunk of Berkeley's Querist -- yes, that Berkeley, the "esse est percipi" guy from intro philosophy -- and I had genuinely no idea he spent the 1730s writing this long weird sequence of numbered rhetorical questions about Ireland's money supply. His basic pitch seems to be that gold and silver aren't really wealth at all, real wealth is just a country's industry and produce, and money is more like a "ticket" or counter for keeping track of who's owed what than a valuable thing in itself -- which is why he thought Ireland should stop panicking about its shortage of coin and just set up a national bank issuing paper credit backed by land instead. For a bishop writing three centuries ago that sounds shockingly close to stuff modern MMT people argue.

Now I can't stop chewing on how he'd size up my portfolio. Part of me thinks Bitcoin basically proves him right -- there's no intrinsic value there any more than in a coin, its just a shared ledger everyone's agreed to treat as valuable, which is pretty much his ticket/counter idea. But the whole selling point of Bitcoin for most people is the fixed, unforgeable supply, which sounds a lot like exactly the gold-fetish thinking he was trying to argue Ireland out of. So which way do you think he'd actually land -- does Bitcoin vindicate his theory of money or repeat the mistake he diagnosed? And separately, what do you think he'd make of something like a central bank digital currency, which honestly sounds a lot closer to the actual national bank scheme he was proposing? I'd rather you engage seriously with the arguments he actually makes in the Querist than just riff on the immaterialism stuff, which I already know about from school.
