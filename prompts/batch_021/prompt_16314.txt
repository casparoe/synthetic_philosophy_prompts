I'm working on a feature about the points where Bayesian updating runs into trouble as a normative theory of belief revision, and I keep coming back to Bas van Fraassen's Judy Benjamin puzzle as the sharpest illustration. In the original setup, a soldier is dropped into a region divided evenly into four zones — Red Headquarters Company area, Red Second Company area, Blue Headquarters Company area, and Blue Second Company area — each initially equally likely. She then receives a radio message stating only that, conditional on her being in Red territory, she is three times as likely to be in the Headquarters Company area as in the Second Company area; the message says nothing at all about Blue, or about Red versus Blue. Yet when that conditional is incorporated by choosing the posterior that maximizes entropy (equivalently, minimizes cross-entropy relative to her uniform prior) subject to this new constraint, her credence in Red drops below 50 percent, with the displaced probability flowing into Blue. Could you give me an intuitive, gut-level explanation of why a message that is ostensibly silent on Red versus Blue nonetheless shifts credence toward Blue under this updating rule, and why philosophers treat this as a genuine defect in the rule rather than a merely surprising but correct consequence of updating properly?
