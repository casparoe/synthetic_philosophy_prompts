I write a column on the odder corners of analytic philosophy, and I have lately been stuck on a short, strange paper that David Lewis -- yes, the modal realist -- published in 1997 under the title "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" In it, he takes the Christian doctrine that Christ was punished in the sinner's place seriously enough to ask a surprisingly technical question: is punishment even the sort of thing that can be handed off to a willing substitute, the way a debt can be paid off by a third party? The doctrine has been dogged since Socinus by the objection that guilt, unlike a sum of money owed, sticks to the person who incurred it, so that "punishing" an innocent volunteer instead discharges nothing -- it just adds one more unmerited harm to the world's ledger. What struck me, reading the paper for a piece on philosophers who wander into theology on their day off, is how precisely Lewis tries to locate exactly where that old objection bites and where it might not, leaning on the contrast between a fine (which plainly can be paid by anyone) and a prison sentence (which intuitively cannot be served on someone else's behalf) to test how much of our resistance to substitution is conceptual bedrock and how much is just an artifact of how we happen to have built our criminal courts.

For a sidebar, I would rather dramatize this disagreement than summarize it for readers. Could you write a dialogue between two philosophers of religion, sharing a drink after they have each just delivered a paper on the topic at a small workshop: one who thinks Lewis's strategy actually rescues the coherence of penal substitution, and one who has read the very same paper and thinks it quietly concedes the Socinian objection without ever admitting as much? I would like them to argue with real specificity about Lewis's own moves -- the debt-versus-desert distinction, the fine-versus-imprisonment contrast, whatever conditions he thinks a legitimate transfer of punishment would have to satisfy -- rather than trading the generic pro- and anti-atonement talking points you would find in a systematic theology textbook. Neither should be easy to beat: I want each of them to have a genuine, non-strawmanned answer to the other's sharpest point, so that by the end I, as the reporter transcribing all this, still cannot honestly tell you which one won.
