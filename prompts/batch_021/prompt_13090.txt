I was at my local rationalist meetup a couple weeks back arguing with a friend (she's a PhD student in immunology, so not exactly her home turf) about whether acausal trade is a real decision-theoretic insight or just Newcomb's-problem intuitions dressed up with a multiverse backstory, and it bugged me enough that I went back down the rabbit hole rereading the old Yudkowsky "timeless decision theory" era posts on LessWrong plus whatever I could track down of Caspar Oesterheld's stuff on multiverse-wide superrationality / evidential cooperation. I ended up writing up a worry I've apparently been sitting on for years without ever putting into words, and before I turn it into an actual post I want someone to sanity check it, partly because my decision theory background is strictly amateur-hour and there's a decent chance I'm just re-deriving something that got settled in a comment thread a decade ago.

Here's the core of it. The whole case for cooperating with some agent you'll never meet or causally affect rests on a correlation claim: your decision and theirs count as "the same" in whatever sense makes subjunctive dependence hold, so choosing to cooperate is choosing what they do too. But nobody actually pins down how coarse or fine the reference class of "relevantly similar agent" is supposed to be, and I think the argument needs it both ways at once. Make the class narrow -- exact or near-exact logical duplicates -- and the correlation is basically guaranteed, but then the "trade" is trivial, you're just coordinating with clones of yourself. Make it wide enough to be interesting, like "any sufficiently rational consequentialist," and you loose the guarantee, because two agents can both fit that description and still land on totally different terms, or decide the deal isn't worth the trouble at all. And the standard reply to "what stops the other side from defecting, there's no enforcement" -- some version of "if you'd defect, so would they, so defecting buys you nothing" -- only actually works in the narrow, trivial case. In the interesting case it just presupposes the correlation that was supposed to be established, which feels like smuggling the trivial case back in through the reply. (It reminds me a bit of the maxim-individuation problem people raise against Kant's universalizability test, where you can describe the same act narrowly or broadly to get whatever verdict you want out of it -- probably a tangent, but it made me suspicious this is a structural issue with tests like this generally, not something specific to decision theory.)

So, two things I'd love comments on: does that argument actually hold together as I've laid it out, or is there a gap I'm not seeing? And separately -- assuming it's sound -- is it actually saying something new, or is it just the "subjunctive dependence is fuzzy" concession that's already sitting right there in the FDT paper, and I'm just repackaging it to look like a discovery? I'd rather find out from you than in the comments section.
