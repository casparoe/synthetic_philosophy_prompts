I spent the better part of four decades doing experimental work in quantum optics before retiring, which is presumably why Roger Penrose's writing kept crossing my desk over the years — first for the physics, and only much later, in retirement, for his case against computationalism. Over the past few months I have finally read *The Emperor's New Mind* and *Shadows of the Mind* with philosophical rather than physics-adjacent intent, alongside Lucas's original 1961 paper and enough of the surrounding literature — Putnam, Benacerraf, and a few more recent commentators — to have formed a real opinion rather than the vague impression I started with.

The objection I keep returning to is not quite the one I expected going in, namely that we can never be sure our own "formal system" is even consistent, but a subtler point I associate with Putnam: that even granting there is some fixed, sound formal system F that fully captures a given mathematician's arithmetical competence, that mathematician could never be in a position to know that F is the relevant system, still less to certify its soundness from the inside, in the way Lucas's move of "I can see that G(F) is true, and the machine cannot" seems to require. This seems to me to recast the whole debate: the interesting conclusion on offer is not that minds are provably not machines, but only that no mind, mechanical or otherwise, could ever be in an epistemic position to certify itself as one particular sound machine rather than another, or as sound at all.

I would like your help working this out properly rather than just trading impressions with my old reading group. Please construct the strongest version you can of the argument that Gödelian anti-mechanist reasoning — both Lucas's original version and Penrose's more careful reconstruction in *Shadows of the Mind* — establishes at most this epistemic limitation, rather than the metaphysical conclusion Lucas and Penrose actually want, namely that minds are, as a matter of fact, not equivalent to any Turing machine. Please engage specifically with how Penrose tries to close off exactly this escape route through his discussion of a putative sound machine M and its associated Gödel sentence, rather than resting on the more familiar "perhaps we are simply unsound" objection, and tell me honestly whether the distinction I am drawing between the epistemic and the metaphysical reading actually survives scrutiny.
