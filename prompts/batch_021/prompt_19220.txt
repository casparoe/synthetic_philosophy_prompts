In our seminar meeting last week, two classmates, Sofia and Daniel, got into a genuinely sharp back-and-forth about whether the position even makes sense, and a few of us kept arguing about it over coffee afterward without settling anything.

(1) Sofia opened by defending illusionism roughly as Frankish states it: introspection is not a transparent window onto the mind but a substantive, fallible representational system, and what it represents when it represents "the felt redness of red" or "the raw sting of pain" is not some extra, non-physical ingredient but a garbled readout of complicated physical and functional goings-on. The redness we seem to find in experience isn't really there; it's only represented as being there.

(2) Daniel objected that this can't be the whole story, because calling something an "illusion" doesn't get you outside the phenomenal realm — it just relocates you within it. If Sofia is under the illusion that her experience has some ineffable quality, there has to be something it is like for her to undergo that very illusion. The seeming itself is a conscious episode, so illusionism has, at best, renamed the phenomenal residue rather than removed it.

(3) Sofia replied that Daniel was conflating the representing with the represented: introspection can misrepresent an experience as having some intrinsic quale without the introspective representation itself possessing that quale, in just the way a visual system can represent a stick in water as bent without the representation itself being bent. The "something it is like" he kept invoking, she said, is itself just further introspective representation, not an independent phenomenal fact sitting outside the theory and demanding its own separate explanation.

(4) Daniel pushed back that this only postpones the question, on two fronts. First, even granting that the introspective state's content is false, we still need to explain why entertaining that false content feels like anything at all rather than nothing — why there is any subjective glow to the misrepresentation, however faint. Second, when Sofia added, in the moment, that this lingering pull is exactly what illusionism predicts, since an introspective system built to represent certain states as simple and irreducible will keep doing so even once we know the representation is inaccurate, he objected that this move makes the theory suspiciously unfalsifiable: it treats the strongest evidence against illusionism, the sheer vividness of the seeming, as if it were confirmation instead.
