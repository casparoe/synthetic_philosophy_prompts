In my decision theory seminar this term, we spent a week on Douglas Hofstadter's concept of superrationality: the idea that two perfectly rational, relevantly identical agents facing a one-shot, symmetric Prisoner's-Dilemma-style choice should recognize that whatever one of them decides, the other—reasoning the same way—will decide too, and should therefore both pick whichever option is jointly best rather than whichever is individually dominant. The idea came up again over lunch, when two people from my study group, Elena and Marcus, ended up arguing about whether it justifies bothering to fill out end-of-term course evaluations.

Elena said she always fills hers out in detail, even though it takes a good twenty minutes and her one form obviously won't shift the average by itself. Her reasoning: if she decided her form didn't matter and skipped it, every similarly thoughtful student would reach the same conclusion by the same reasoning, and the department would end up with no useful feedback at all—so she should act as she'd want everyone relevantly like her to act, and fill it out. Marcus objected that this smuggles in a kind of magical thinking: whether Elena personally spends the twenty minutes has zero causal effect on what anyone else decides. Her filling out her own form doesn't cause other people to fill out theirs; it's simply one more form in the pile. Treating "people like me will reason the same way" as a reason for her own choice, he said, is really just self-administered guilt dressed up in decision-theoretic language.

Elena replied that if Marcus's causal argument were the whole story, it would apply equally to every conscientious student, predicting that careful, detailed evaluations should almost never get submitted—yet they clearly do, routinely. So either the causal argument is missing something about why relevantly identical reasoners tend to land on correlated choices, she said, or the real disagreement is actually about whether two people "running the same algorithm" on a structurally identical problem can give each other reasons for action at all, which is a different question from whether one person's choice causes the other's. Marcus answered that the fact that detailed evaluations get submitted proves nothing of the kind: people fill them out for all sorts of reasons—duty, habit, wanting to vent—and plenty of widely performed actions are backed by outright fallacious reasoning (the gambler's fallacy being the standard example), so an action's popularity is no evidence that the reasoning behind it is sound.

They asked me to settle it, and I don't think I can. Could you go through the exchange turn by turn and say who has the better of it at each point, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I would also like to know whether there is a version of the superrationality argument that actually survives Marcus's causal objection, and what, if anything, both of them are missing about how this connects to the more familiar debate between evidential and causal decision theory.
