I had this whole back-and-forth with my friend Ravi over text last week and its still bugging me. Ravi cancels plans constantly — like, last minute, multiple times a month — and never apologizes for it. This was maybe the fourth time this month he'd bailed on something, so I was already annoyed when I brought it up again, expecting the usual "sorry, I'll try to do better."

Instead he said he genuinely doesn't feel bad about canceling, so an apology would just be a performance, and faking remorse he doesnt actually have would be more dishonest than staying quiet. I told him that however he currently feels, he still has a real reason to be more considerate of people's time, because it actually affects them, whether he feels it or not. He shot back that this is exactly the kind of thing people say that doesn't actually mean anything: if he has zero desire to change, and nothing he currently wants points toward changing, then in what sense is there a "reason" there for HIM specifically, as opposed to it just being a reason for me, since I'm the one who's bothered by it? His whole point was that reasons don't float around out there independent of what a person actually wants (or what they'd come to want if they thought it through carefully) — no hook into your actual desires means no reason, full stop, and everything else is really just other people's preferences dressed up as though they're binding on you too.

We went back and forth on this for way longer than two grown adults should argue over text, and honestly some of his comebacks were sharper than I expected going in. I couldn't tell if he was just being contrarian for sport or if he'd landed on something real about how "you have a reason to X" can be kind of empty unless it connects to something the person already cares about, deep down, even if they won't admit it to themselves. I don't want you to just take my side here — I want to know who's actually got the stronger position. Is Ravi right that there's no reason for him to be more considerate unless it already hooks into something he wants? And if he's wrong, what's the actual comeback — one that doesn't just quietly boil down to me saying "well, I think you should care about this"?
