I teach the philosophy of computability as part of a graduate logic sequence, and an exchange with a sharp student this term has left me genuinely unsure what to say the next time the topic comes up. The standard story I give is this: Turing's machine model, Church's lambda calculus, and the general recursive functions developed by Gödel and Kleene were each motivated independently, then proved extensionally equivalent, and Turing additionally supplied a direct argument — via his analysis of what a human clerk mechanically following fixed instructions, equipped with only finitely many recognizable symbol-configurations and states of mind, could accomplish — for why no wider notion of an "effective procedure" could escape this class. My student's response was that this is a strange thing to still call a "thesis." On her view, once three independently motivated formal systems converge on a single extension, and you additionally have an argument, like Turing's, that engages the informal notion directly rather than just cross-checking formalisms against one another, you have exactly the kind of triangulation that mathematics treats as a proof everywhere else. She takes the continued insistence that the Church-Turing thesis remains a well-supported conjecture, forever short of the status of a theorem, to be either an empty terminological scruple or a holdover from before Turing's argument was fully appreciated.

I would genuinely like to know why she is mistaken, if she is. Kleene, in coining the term "Church's thesis," clearly meant to mark it off from anything provable, and most subsequent writers on the philosophy of computability have followed him in denying that such a proof exists or could exist — but on the spot, I could not reconstruct a principled reason for this that did not simply restate the fact that "effectively calculable" is an informal notion, which sounds uncomfortably close to declaring the thesis unprovable by definitional fiat. Is there a real argument, distinct from Turing's own, for why extensional convergence plus Turing's analysis still falls short of a mathematical proof of the thesis? And does that argument also explain why no future analysis of this kind, however careful, could ever close the gap?
