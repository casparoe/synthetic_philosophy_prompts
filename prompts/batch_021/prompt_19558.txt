So I'm maybe a third of the way into a novel set in Vienna in the late 1920s, and one of my side characters is loosely based on the logical positivist crowd that used to meet around the university -- I've been picturing him as a Schlick type, stiff, very committed to the idea that most of philosophy is just people confusing themselves with words. To get his voice right, a friend lent me this old anthology of meaning-of-life essays, and it completely threw me to find Schlick himself in there, with a piece literally titled "On the Meaning of Life" from 1927. That seems backwards to me. I thought the whole positivist project was built on showing that questions like "what is the meaning of life," "does God exist," "is the Absolute perfect," and so on, are pseudo-questions -- not false, just meaningless, since there's no conceivable way to verify an answer either way. So why would their own guy sit down and write a sincere essay under that exact title, instead of a short paper politely explaining why the question dissolves under analysis?

I brought this up with the friend who lent me the book, and she said something like "oh yeah, isn't his whole point that the question doesn't really mean anything, thats basically the answer" -- which honestly doesn't sound like it would take a whole essay to say, so now I'm even more curious what's actually in there. Before I put words in this character's mouth I'd like to understand it properly: what is Schlick actually doing in that essay, and does it genuinely fit with the rest of what he believed, or is it in tension with it?
