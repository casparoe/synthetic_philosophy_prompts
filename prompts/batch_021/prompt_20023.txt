Had a long, kind of ridiculous argument with my friend Jae last night about whose values an aligned AI is even supposed to be tracking once it's smart enough that this actually matters. It started because I said something like "just aggregate everyone's preferences, weight them, done," and Jae shot back that Arrow's theorem already tells you "aggregate everyone's preferences" isn't a real proposal, it's a wish, since no non-dictatorial rule can satisfy the properties you'd want from it anyway. From there it spiraled into whether alignment should look more like Rawlsian public reason (build the thing to only act on whatever's in the overlapping consensus of every reasonable comprehensive doctrine, and stay neutral on the rest) vs. something like Yudkowsky's coherent extrapolated volition (extrapolate one composite volition of what humanity would want if it were smarter and more the people we wish we were) vs. just dropping the aggregation question entirely and making the system corrigible to whoever's actually operating it. Neither of us landed a knockout blow, we just ended up scrawling a longer list of proposals on a napkin, and now I cant stop thinking about it and Jae's asleep so I can't keep arguing with her.

1. Coherent extrapolated volition (CEV) -- extrapolate a single idealized composite of what humanity would want if it knew more, thought faster, and was more the people it wishes it were.
2. Corrigibility to a single principal -- skip aggregation across humanity entirely; align to whoever's operating/deploying the system and keep it correctable by them specifically.
3. Rawlsian overlapping consensus / liberal neutrality -- only encode norms endorsed across all reasonable comprehensive moral, religious, and philosophical doctrines; stay silent on whatever's reasonably contested between them.
4. Social welfare function aggregation -- formally combine individual utility or preference functions (Harsanyi-style summation, Nash bargaining, etc.) into a single function the system maximizes.
5. Debate as a proxy for correctness -- have two instances of the system argue opposing sides in front of a human judge, and treat whichever position survives cross-examination as the aligned answer, so you never have to specify substantive values up front.
