After fifteen years as a corporate litigator, I recently moved to part-time work so that I could spend more time with my newborn son, and watching him lose himself completely in play that has no goal beyond the playing itself led me to start reading more widely on philosophical treatments of work and play. That is how I came across Moritz Schlick's essay "On the Meaning of Life," in which he locates meaning in precisely this kind of self-contained, playful activity and treats youth as its purest expression. What I have not been able to reconcile is that Schlick was a founding figure of the Vienna Circle and one of the chief architects of the verifiability criterion: a statement counts as cognitively meaningful only if it can in principle be empirically confirmed or refuted, and any metaphysical question that fails this test is to be dismissed as a pseudo-problem rather than answered. "What is the meaning of life?" looks like exactly that kind of question, and yet here he is earnestly offering a substantive answer to it. Was Schlick simply failing to live up to his own standards, or is there a way of reading the essay that dissolves the apparent tension with his logical positivism?
