For as long as I can remember I have felt slightly embarrassed about my own taste in art: I would rather sit through a simple hymn sung none too well by my local church choir, or reread a folk tale I loved as a child, than get much out of most of the things I am told are masterpieces. A friend who studied music got tired of hearing me say this and, half as a joke, told me to read Tolstoy's late essay "What Is Art?", since he apparently arrived at something like the same position himself, only far more radically. Reading it turned out to be unsettling in a good way. Tolstoy argues that the whole purpose of art is to "infect" an audience with a feeling the artist has genuinely lived through, and that for a work to count as good art, let alone great art, it has to be sincere, clear, and, above all, universally shareable: capable of moving a peasant and a professor in the same way, rather than requiring years of training or refined taste to appreciate. On this basis he dismisses huge parts of the accepted canon, including, if I followed the argument correctly, a pointed attack on Beethoven's Ninth Symphony as failing to produce any real fellow-feeling in an ordinary listener despite its famous "Ode to Joy," plus most of Shakespeare and Wagner, and, most startling of all, nearly everything he himself had written before his religious conversion, "War and Peace" included.

What I would like is for you to apply this theory as consistently as Tolstoy claimed to, rather than treat it as just a rhetorical flourish on his part. Below are ten things, deliberately mixing acknowledged masterpieces, simple or popular works, and one item from Tolstoy's own fiction. Using only the criteria Tolstoy himself sets out in "What Is Art?", and not your own aesthetic judgment or the standard critical consensus, please sort them into a tier list running from S (comes closest to Tolstoy's own ideal of good, universal art) down to D (the sort of "counterfeit" art he reserved his greatest contempt for). The ten items are: a full concert performance of Beethoven's Ninth Symphony; Shakespeare's "King Lear"; a scene from Wagner's "Ring" cycle; a traditional hymn sung by an ordinary church congregation; a folk fairy tale told to children at bedtime; a catchy three-minute love song currently playing on the radio; an abstract expressionist painting hanging in a major museum; Dickens's "A Christmas Carol"; a big-budget superhero blockbuster film; and Tolstoy's own "War and Peace."
