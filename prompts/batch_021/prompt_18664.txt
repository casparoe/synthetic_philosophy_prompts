Judy Benjamin is dropped somewhere in a region split into four equally likely areas, each starting with credence 1/4: Red-Headquarters, Red-Second-Company, Blue-Headquarters, Blue-Second-Company. Command radios her: "we don't know exactly where you are, but if you're in Red territory, it's three times as likely you're in the Headquarters area as the Second Company area." (a) Explain why she can't update by strict conditionalization on this message. (b) Explain why Jeffrey's rule (probability kinematics), in its usual form, doesn't straightforwardly settle the case either. (c) Sketch the "minimize relative information relative to the prior" proposal for extending Jeffrey's rule to handle this, and explain the surprising result van Fraassen gets out of it.

my classmate's answer:

"Strict conditionalization requires that the evidence be some proposition the agent now assigns probability 1 to, like 'I am in Red territory.' But the message doesn't say that — it doesn't locate her in Red or Blue at all, it just gives a conditional odds claim, P(HQ|Red) = 3/4. Since nothing has simply become certain, there's nothing to strictly conditionalize on.

Jeffrey's rule generalizes conditionalization for when the evidence directly hands you new probabilities for a partition (e.g. "P(Red) is now 0.6"), and works by holding fixed every other conditional probability given each cell of that partition. That's not what's happening here: the new information comes as a conditional probability rather than a new unconditional weight on Red, Blue, or any cell of the four-way partition, so it's not obvious which partition to run Jeffrey's rule on or what the new unconditional weights even are.

The standard fix is to take, among all distributions over the four regions consistent with P(HQ|Red)=3/4, the one closest to the flat prior by minimizing relative information — which, since the prior is uniform, amounts to maximum entropy subject to the constraint. Van Fraassen's punchline is that this doesn't just redistribute the mass inside Red 3:1 while leaving P(Red)=P(Blue)=1/2 like you'd expect — it actually pushes P(Blue) above 1/2, even though the message said nothing comparing Red to Blue at all. He takes this as vindicating Jeffrey conditionalization over strict conditionalization, since it shows only the probability-kinematics approach can handle evidence that isn't a flat-out certainty."
