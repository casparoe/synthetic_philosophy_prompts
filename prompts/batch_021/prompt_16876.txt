I help produce a small podcast on the philosophy of science and medicine, and next month's episode features a philosopher of psychiatry whose forthcoming book extends Ian Hacking's account of "looping effects" to the recent explosion in certain psychiatric diagnoses. Her central claim is that the sharp, sustained rise in autism and ADHD diagnoses among children and adolescents over the past two decades is not primarily a story about improved detection of some fixed underlying condition, but an instance of the same dynamic Hacking described for multiple personality disorder in Rewriting the Soul: naming and classifying a kind of person changes how those who might fit the label come to understand and present themselves, which then feeds back into diagnostic practice and reshapes the category itself -- something that, on her view, cannot happen to an "indifferent kind" like a quark. I've spent a fair amount of time on rationalist blogs and forums arguing about whether the rise in these diagnoses is "real," and I'd like the interview to get well past the point at which that kind of discussion usually stalls.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions for her? I want them to press on the actual mechanics of the looping-effects argument rather than inviting a restatement of it. Specifically, please include: one question that forces her to say what observation would actually distinguish a genuine looping effect from mere improved or differential ascertainment -- that is, what she would accept as evidence against her own view; one that presses on the disanalogy between multiple personality disorder, whose diagnoses collapsed once the recovered-memory and satanic-ritual-abuse panic behind it was discredited, and autism and ADHD, whose numbers have kept climbing across countries with very different clinical cultures; one on whether her argument slides illegitimately from "this category is partly constituted by how people classified under it come to see themselves" to "this category is not a real, objective kind" -- a move Hacking himself was careful to resist -- and, relatedly, whether she means the weak claim that classification changes the behavior of people who already fit some pattern, or the much stronger claim that classification creates a way of being a person that could not have existed before it; and one asking whether an interactive kind could survive the discovery of a robust biological marker, or whether that discovery would simply convert it back into an indifferent kind. For each of these, please add a short follow-up question keyed to the answer she's most likely to give.
