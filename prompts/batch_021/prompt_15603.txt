I recently started work as a research assistant in a comparative cognition lab, and after weeks of watching our corvids bend wire into hooks and our octopuses solve multi-step latch puzzles, I've found myself thinking less about our experimental protocols and more about a puzzle from my undergraduate ethics seminar. In the Metaphysics of Morals and the Lectures on Ethics, Kant grounds moral standing entirely in rational autonomy, denies that we have any direct duties to animals, and treats apparent kindness toward them as an indirect duty to humanity: cruelty is wrong, on his view, not because it wrongs the animal but because it dulls the very sympathies our duties to other human beings depend on. I'd like your best reconstruction, working strictly from Kant's own premises rather than a modern animal-rights gloss on him, of how he would respond to the kind of behavioral evidence labs like mine now generate -- flexible planning, tool manufacture, apparent mirror self-recognition. Would this evidence pressure him toward granting some animals direct moral status, or is his criterion set up so that no amount of demonstrated behavioral intelligence could ever count as the right kind of evidence, given that what grounds personhood for him is a categorically different capacity for self-legislation under the moral law, not a difference of degree in cleverness?
