And here's the student's answer, in full:

"(a) The basic idea is that a signal can only be trusted if it's expensive enough that faking it isn't worth it. If advertising your quality cost nothing, every male, weak or strong, would just advertise the maximum, so a receiver would get no information out of the display -- it would be what economists call cheap talk. Zahavi's fix is to make the signal a genuine handicap: something that actually reduces the bearer's chances of survival, like a huge, unwieldy tail that's expensive to grow and makes you slower and more visible to predators. Crucially, the cost has to be the same, in absolute terms, for every male who displays it, weak or strong -- that shared, flat cost is what keeps the system honest, because it means a low-quality male can't get away with mimicking a high-quality male's tail any more cheaply than the high-quality male himself paid for it. So the tail still ends up separating the population by quality even though the tax on growing it is identical for everyone.

(b) This is different from Fisher's runaway model, where an arbitrary female preference and the male trait it targets just get dragged along together by genetic correlation, generation after generation, regardless of whether the trait says anything about the male's underlying quality. In the Fisherian story you could in principle get sexy but useless (or even harmful) traits fixed in a population purely because of the feedback loop between preference and trait; nothing there guarantees the trait is an honest indicator of anything. Zahavi's account, by contrast, is specifically about why cost can certify quality.

(c) Zahavi's proposal from the mid-1970s was pretty quickly put on solid mathematical footing by Maynard Smith, whose game-theoretic modelling showed that handicap signalling could be an evolutionarily stable strategy, which is largely why the handicap principle overtook Fisherian runaway as the mainstream explanation for ornaments like the peacock's tail. Since then it's become one of the standard tools in the sexual-selection literature, alongside 'good genes' models more generally.

(d) A nice human parallel is Michael Spence's job-market signalling model, where workers signal their ability to employers by getting a costly education, even in fields where the content of the degree is irrelevant to the job itself. This works as a genuine handicap-type signal rather than an index signal, because nothing about a diploma physically or logically tracks ability the way, say, a deep voice necessarily tracks body size -- it's only the differential cost of getting the credential (easier for high-ability people to earn good grades) that keeps the signal informative. Interestingly, Spence's paper actually predates Zahavi's, so the economic version of the idea arrived slightly earlier than the biological one."
