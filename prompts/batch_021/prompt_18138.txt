I'm working on a piece about Kurt Gödel's stranger philosophical commitments — not the famous modal-logic proof of God's existence, but a much less-discussed argument for an afterlife that he laid out in letters to his mother late in his life. His reasoning ran roughly like this: because the world is fundamentally rational rather than arbitrary, it would be absurd for the decades-long ripening of a person's intellectual and moral capacities to be cut short by death just as that person starts to make real use of them, so some further existence has to follow. Separately, in his 1951 Gibbs lecture, Gödel argued that either the human mind's mathematical powers exceed anything a Turing machine could achieve, or there exist mathematical questions no mind or machine could ever resolve — and he made clear that his own sympathies lay with the first horn, i.e., that mind is not reducible to formal, mechanical procedure. Holding both commitments fixed, how do you think Gödel would have judged today's "digital immortality" proposals: whole-brain emulation, say, or training a language model on a dead person's letters and presenting the output as their continuation? Would his own premises let him count either as the further existence his rationalism demanded, or would his anti-mechanism commit him to rejecting them as category errors — simulations of a man's outputs rather than a continuation of the man himself?
