I'm taking a political philosophy seminar this term organized around democratic theory and its critics, and we recently spent two sessions on Robin Hanson's proposal for futarchy. His slogan is that a polity should "vote on values, but bet on beliefs": ordinary democratic politics settles on a welfare metric, while conditional prediction markets — traders betting on what that metric would turn out to be under policy A versus policy B — actually choose the policy, on the theory that markets aggregate dispersed information better than voters or legislators can. Our professor spent a while on the 2003 Policy Analysis Market episode, where a DARPA-backed plan to run markets on Middle Eastern political events, including the odds of specific attacks, was killed within about a day of becoming public — apparently more because betting on atrocities struck nearly everyone as grotesque than because of any narrowly technical flaw — and used it to introduce the standard objections to futarchy: that a workable welfare metric is hard to specify without inviting gaming, that markets can be moved by wealthy or well-placed traders, and that the whole scheme needs markets liquid enough to be trustworthy.

Outside of class, a few of us who help run the campus forecasting club have started wondering whether a much smaller version of the same idea could replace some of our own internal votes. Right now we decide things the ordinary way, at meetings of about three dozen people, plenty of whom have not read whatever is on that week's agenda. The proposal on the table is to run small conditional markets, settled against our own post-semester satisfaction survey, to decide a handful of recurring questions: which speaker to invite with our modest budget, whether to switch the platform we use for practice tournaments, and how many forecasting "reps" to require of new members.

Before we take this any further, I would like to understand, independently of whether we should actually do it, what the most important implications of shrinking futarchy down to this scale are. In particular: does meeting weekly and knowing exactly who is trading against whom make manipulation and insider trading easier or harder to sustain than in Hanson's anonymous, nation-sized version; does a satisfaction survey among three dozen friends suffer from the same gameability problems as GDP, or different ones; and does replacing a vote with a market at this scale raise the same kind of moral-discomfort objection that reportedly sank the Policy Analysis Market, just in miniature — would it feel different, and worse, to bet against a friend's proposal than against a stranger's? I would appreciate a fairly systematic walk-through rather than just a list of scattered pros and cons.
