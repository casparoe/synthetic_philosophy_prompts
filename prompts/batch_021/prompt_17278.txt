A friend and I spent much of last night arguing about cryptocurrency, and the argument kept circling back to George Berkeley. She maintained that because Berkeley reduced money in The Querist to a mere "ticket or counter" with no intrinsic worth -- consistent with his broader view that material objects are nothing but bundles of perceived qualities -- he would have recognized Bitcoin as the purest realization of his own theory of value, and would have welcomed a currency accountable to no state. I argued the opposite: that Berkeley stripped money of intrinsic value precisely in order to make the case for a national bank, whose credit would be trustworthy only because a public, answerable authority stood behind it, and that this points toward something much closer to a central bank digital currency than to a system built to need no issuer at all. Neither of us gave ground. Setting the anachronism aside, and reasoning from what Berkeley actually argued in The Querist about credit, circulation, and the dangers of unaccountable paper rather than just from his metaphysics, which of the two -- Bitcoin or a central bank digital currency -- would his own arguments actually commit him to, and why? I'd like you to engage with the strongest version of my friend's reading rather than dismiss it out of hand.
