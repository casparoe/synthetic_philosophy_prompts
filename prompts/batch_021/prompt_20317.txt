I recently moved from a philosophy PhD into an interpretability team at an AI lab, and in my first week a colleague dismissed our entire project as conceptually confused, insisting that Donald Davidson's account of radical interpretation already settles that it's meaningless to ask whether a language model has beliefs. On Davidson's view, an interpreter can only assign beliefs and meanings together, holistically, constrained by a Principle of Charity that treats the speaker as largely rational and largely right about a shared world. In his later work this is sharpened into the triangulation argument: genuine belief requires two communicating creatures jointly responding to shared objects, since it is only that triangular relationship that supplies the concept of objective truth, and therefore of error, that belief presupposes.

Large language models never occupy that triangular position in real time with a perceptible shared world, even though they are trained on the residue of countless human triangulations and do converse with users turn by turn. Reasoning from the actual structure of Davidson's arguments rather than a loose gloss on "charitable interpretation," would he say the question of whether an LLM has beliefs is foreclosed before it even starts, or is there some deflected or derivative form of triangulation already at work here that would keep radical interpretation in play?
