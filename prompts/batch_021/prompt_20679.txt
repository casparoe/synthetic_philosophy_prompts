Since I retired from field biology a couple of years ago (spent most of my career studying alarm calls in vervet monkeys, of all things) I've finally had time to read philosophy properly instead of just picking up bits secondhand. An old friend from grad school who ended up in a philosophy department pointed me toward David Lewis's book on convention, and from there toward Brian Skyrms' work on signalling games, since apparently thats the bridge between philosophy and the kind of animal communication work I used to do. I've been chewing on this for weeks and think I've worked out what's really going on, but I'd like someone who knows the material better than me to tell me if I've got it right.

Here's my read: Lewis's "convention" is basically just a formal redescription of an ordinary evolutionary equilibrium. You've got a recurring coordination problem, some arbitrary solution gets locked in because deviating doesn't pay once everybody else is already doing it, and that's your convention -- exactly the same logic that explains why vervet alarm calls differ by predator, why honeybees all read the waggle dance the same way, or why we all drive on the same side of the road. If that's really the content of the theory, I don't see what all the fuss over "common knowledge" is for -- agents needing beliefs about other agents' beliefs about their beliefs, and so on -- since the exact same equilibrium story works fine for monkeys and bees that definately aren't reasoning about anybody's mental states. So am I right that the common-knowledge apparatus is basically just decorative for the human case, and if so, why do philosophers still write as if it's doing indispensable conceptual work, instead of just admitting "convention" is a label for a certain kind of stable equilibrium, full stop?
