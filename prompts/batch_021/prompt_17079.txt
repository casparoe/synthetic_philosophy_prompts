My epistemology seminar just spent two whole class periods on the Meno problem -- the bit where Socrates points out that a guy who correctly guesses the road to Larissa gets there just as well as a guy who actually knows the road, so why should we value knowledge over mere true belief at all. After class a few of us were still arguing about it in the group chat, and I said I honestly don't get why people still treat this as some big unsolved problem in epistemology.

Like, basically every post-Gettier account says knowledge is just true belief plus SOMETHING -- justification, reliability, safety, sensitivity, whatever condition you like best. So knowledge obviously has more going for it than plain true belief does, and it feels almost trivially true that "true belief + extra stuff" beats "true belief" on its own, kinda like how a burger with bacon on it just straightforwardly beats the same burger without the bacon. My professor pushed back on this pretty hard and brought up some example about coffee from a reliable vs. a busted expresso machine, but I honestly couldn't follow why that was even supposed to be relevant. Am I missing something real here, or does this whole "problem" just dissolve the second you commit to any standard analysis of knowledge?
