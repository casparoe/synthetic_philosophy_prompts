I'm a retired biochemist, and about two years into retirement I stumbled into a small philosophy reading group at the local library -- best decision I've made since I stopped pipetting for a living. This month we worked through the Hart-Fuller exchange (Hart's 1958 Harvard Law Review piece and Fuller's reply, plus chunks of Fuller's Morality of Law), and one bit really snagged in my brain: Fuller's list of eight ways a system can fail to even count as law, regardless of what the rules actually say. As I understood it: there have to be rules at all rather than just one-off decrees (generality), the rules have to be published (publicity), they can't be retroactive (prospectivity), they have to be at least somewhat clear (clarity), they can't flatly contradict each other (non-contradiction), they can't demand the impossible (possibility of compliance), they can't change so often nobody can plan around them (constancy), and the rules on the books have to match what officials actually do (congruence). Fuller calls this whole package the "inner morality of law," and his big claim against Hart is that a regime that's egregiously bad on enough of these axes isn't really a legal system at all, whatever you think of its substance -- Hart, naturally, thought this was just efficient-poisoner morality dressed up as the real thing, not a genuine bridge between law and morals.

What's been bugging me since group is that these eight don't seem to be pulling equal weight. A couple of them feel like genuine conceptual floors -- I can barely imagine calling something "law" if it fails hard enough at generality or publicity -- while others (constancy, maybe congruence?) feel more like marks of a well-run system that a shakier one could still limp along without and still plausibly be "law," just bad law. So here's my ask: rank Fuller's eight principles from most to least indispensible to a system's bare claim to BE law (as opposed to just being good, well-crafted law), using Fullers own logic as best you can reconstruct it rather than my hand-wavy paraphrase above. Bucket them into tiers rather than a strict 1-through-8 ordering, formatted as a JSON block like this:

```json
{
  "essential": ["...", ...],
  "important": ["...", ...],
  "minor": ["...", ...]
}
```

with one or two sentences per principle explaining the placement. And if you think this whole "which ones are essential" framing actually misreads what Fuller was up to, say so -- but give me the ranking anyway rather than dodging it.
