Last month, at a workshop on rational choice, I watched two speakers — an economist and a philosopher — spend a whole dinner arguing over a decision-theory puzzle called "Death in Damascus," which decision theorists trace to a 1978 paper by the philosophers Allan Gibbard and William Harper. In it, a man in Damascus is told by Death, a flawless predictor, that Death is coming for him tomorrow; wherever he ends up the next day, Damascus or Aleppo, Death will already be there too, since Death predicts his choice and shows up accordingly. He has to decide, tonight, whether to stay or flee.

(1) The economist said the answer is straightforward: the man's decision tonight can't reach back and change where Death has already resolved to be, and, apart from that decision, Death is equally likely to be in either city, so staying and fleeing carry identical expected utility — he might as well flip a coin. (2) The philosopher objected that this can't be the whole story: the instant the man settles on staying, he acquires evidence that Death probably chose Damascus too, which makes staying look like the losing move; settle on fleeing instead, and Aleppo suddenly looks like the trap. Neither option can be settled into without immediately looking like the wrong one, which is a strange thing to say about a "rational" choice. (3) The economist replied that this is just unwelcome evidence the man can't do anything about: his decision doesn't cause Death's location, so the correlation it reveals isn't a causal lever, and that is precisely the lesson of Newcomb's problem — you don't treat a correlation as a reason to act differently just because it delivers bad news after the fact. (4) The philosopher answered that Death in Damascus isn't simply Newcomb's problem again: in the classic Newcomb case, asking which act can survive this kind of self-scrutiny actually singles out one answer, so the self-scrutiny test and the causal reasoning agree; here, neither act survives it, and calling that "just bad news" doesn't explain why a method for choosing should be allowed to endorse an act that falls apart the instant it's adopted.

Neither speaker gave ground, and I couldn't tell who had the better of it. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move rather than declaring an overall winner?
