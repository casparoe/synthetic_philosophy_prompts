I write historical short fiction, and I've been assembling a small cycle of stories about canonical scientific figures whose private convictions would have scandalized the institutions that housed them — the premise being that the tidy popular image of these men as purely rational actors conceals a much stranger interior life. The one I can't yet get right concerns Isaac Newton, who spent decades on unpublished biblical scholarship and arrived, privately, at a conclusion that would have shocked most of his contemporaries: that the doctrine of the Trinity was a corruption grafted onto the early church, centuries after Christ, by the very councils usually credited with defining orthodoxy. He held that Christ was divine but not co-eternal with the Father, and that the two verses most often invoked to prove otherwise — the "three heavenly witnesses" of 1 John 5:7, and the disputed wording of 1 Timothy 3:16 — were later interpolations rather than authentic scripture. Around 1690 he wrote this argument up as a careful piece of textual criticism and sent it to John Locke, meaning to have it published anonymously on the continent; at the last moment, he asked Locke to suppress it, and it would not appear in print until long after Newton's death. All the while he held his fellowship at Trinity College, Cambridge, on terms that assumed exactly the doctrine he had privately decided was a fraud.

Could you write me a short story, somewhere in the range of 1,000 to 1,800 words, set in the days around Newton's decision to call the manuscript back from Locke? I'd rather not have Newton simply narrate his theology at the reader; I want the actual substance of his heterodoxy — what he thought had been altered, and why it mattered enough to risk everything and then not risk it — to surface indirectly, perhaps through a second character (a copyist, a servant, a young relation) who catches fragments of the argument without ever being shown the whole of it. If it doesn't feel forced, I'd also be glad to see some use made of the resonance between this episode and the rest of Newton's life's work: the same conviction that a true, hidden order underlies a corrupted appearance runs through both his biblical criticism and his physics. Invent whatever minor characters and domestic detail you need, but I'd like the theological content and the basic sequence of events — the letter to Locke, the loss of nerve, the long silence that followed — to be recognizable to anyone who knows Newton's biography.
