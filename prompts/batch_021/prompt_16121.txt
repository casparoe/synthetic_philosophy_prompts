I've been teaching a graduate seminar on experimental philosophy this term, and last week's session on the "restrictionist" challenge turned into an argument that never really got resolved, so now I want to see how you'd handle it. You probably know the basic setup: Weinberg, Nichols and Stich ran surveys back in the early 2000s finding that ordinary people's intuitions about Gettier-style knowledge cases (and, in a related study by Machery and colleauges, about reference in Kripke's Gödel case) split noticeably along cultural and socioeconomic lines, and they used this to argue that philosophers have no principled basis for treating "what seems obviously true to me and my colleagues" as evidence for a theory of knowledge or meaning -- hence "restrictionism" about which intuitions get to count as data. About half my students think this basically sinks the traditional armchair method in epistemology and philosophy of language. The other half kept bringing up two complications: first, the "expertise defense," the idea that a trained philosopher's intuition is less like a random layperson's gut reaction and more like a chess master's snap judgment, so maybe it deserves special evidential weight; and second, the fact that several later, larger replication attempts (including a big multi-country one on the Gettier cases specifically) turned up a lot less cross-cultural variation than the original studies did, which muddies the whole empirical basis of the challenge.

What I'd like from you is an essay that actually commits to a position instead of just cataloguing the back-and-forth. Given all of the above -- the original restrictionist argument, the expertise defense, and the messy replication record -- should philosophers change how they use intuitions as evidence, and if so how (throw them out entirely, restrict the evidential pool to trained experts, only trust intuitions that turn out to be cross-culturally convergent)? Or does the whole challenge basically collapse once you take the failed replications and the deeper methodological worries about what these surveys are even measuring seriously? Don't hedge into a "reasonable people disagree" non-answer -- tell me where you actually land and defend it against the strongest version of the view you're rejecting.
