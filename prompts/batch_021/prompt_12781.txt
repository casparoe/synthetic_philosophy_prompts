Last night an old friend from graduate school and I spent close to three hours arguing, over coffee that went cold an hour in, about Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein on rule-following. The specific point we couldn't settle was how much weight the "sceptical solution" really puts on the existence of a linguistic community, as against resources that would, in principle, be available to an isolated individual: she wanted to treat the communitarian element as a separable add-on that could in principle be replaced by facts about a single speaker's own consistency over time, while I kept insisting that the entire point of tying the sceptical solution to the private language material was to foreclose exactly that possibility. Neither of us conceded anything, and I'm now a little wary that the argument has left me primed to see that particular mistake everywhere, including in places where it may not actually be one.

This is a live concern because I TA a graduate seminar on philosophy of language, and one of the take-home midterm answers on my desk this week addresses precisely this material, so I'd like a read on it from somewhere other than my own, possibly compromised, judgment before I commit to a mark. The question set to the students was: "Explain the sceptical paradox about meaning that Kripke presents in Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language, and the 'sceptical solution' he attributes to Wittgenstein. Your answer should address (a) why standard candidate meaning-constituting facts — dispositional, introspective, Platonistic — are argued to fail, (b) how the sceptical solution differs from a 'straight' solution to the paradox, and (c) how the sceptical solution bears on the possibility of a private language." Here is the student's answer in full:

"In Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language, Kripke reconstructs an argument he finds in Wittgenstein's later work and presents it as a novel sceptical paradox about meaning. Suppose I have only ever computed sums with addends smaller than 57, and I am now asked to evaluate '68 + 57.' I unhesitatingly answer '125,' confident that this is what I mean by '+'. A sceptic can point out that my finite past use is equally compatible with my having meant a deviant function Kripke calls 'quus,' defined so that it agrees with addition on every pair of numbers I have actually added but returns 5 whenever the pair includes a number 57 or greater — so that on the quus hypothesis the 'correct' answer is 5, not 125. Since nothing about my past use, taken by itself, rules out that I meant quus rather than plus, the sceptic asks what fact about me makes it the case that I meant plus, such that 125 is the answer I am now rationally required to give.

Kripke considers and rejects several candidate facts. Dispositionalism holds that meaning plus consists in a disposition to answer in certain ways; Kripke argues that once this disposition is properly idealized to remove the effects of fatigue, finite memory, and the like, it does succeed in picking out a unique determinate function, and it is this idealized disposition, rather than any occurrent mental episode, that Wittgenstein ultimately treats as constitutive of meaning. Appeals to introspectible mental content fare no better, since no finite, introspectible qualitative state could encode an infinite function. A Platonist appeal to grasping an abstract rule directly is rejected on the grounds that grasping a rule still requires interpretation to fix its application, threatening a regress reminiscent of Lewis Carroll's tortoise. Simplicity considerations fail as well, since quus only looks more complex than plus relative to a prior, question-begging choice of which functions count as primitive.

Having found no fact to ground meaning-ascriptions, the sceptical solution — modelled on Hume's treatment of induction and causation — does not try to beat the sceptic at his own game. It asks instead what role meaning-talk plays in our lives, and locates the assertability conditions for a claim like 'I mean plus by +' in the fact that the speaker's answer coheres with the pattern the speaker has consistently exhibited, so that what makes the claim assertible is fundamentally the individual's own longitudinal consistency rather than agreement with any further, independent standard. Kripke draws out the connection to the private language argument directly: since the diarist of Philosophical Investigations §258, who introduces a sign 'S' for a recurring sensation, has exactly this kind of internal consistency available to him, the sceptical solution vindicates the diarist's private use of 'S' against the sceptic, provided he is consistent in applying it. This is, on the reading offered here, the real payoff of connecting the two discussions: the sceptical solution does for private sensation-language what it does for arithmetic, showing that a suitably consistent individual practice is all that meaning ever required in the first place."
