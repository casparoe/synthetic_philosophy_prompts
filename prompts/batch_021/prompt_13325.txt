I write about science and philosophy for a general-interest publication, and I'm reporting a feature on how effective altruists and other long-termist thinkers lean on expected-value reasoning to justify acting on tiny probabilities of enormous payoffs -- the sort of reasoning critics dismiss with the "Pascal's mugging" label. While interviewing a decision theorist for background, I brought up the St. Petersburg paradox as the obvious cautionary tale about expected value, and she waved it off as "the easy case," steering me instead toward a much more obscure thought experiment called the Pasadena paradox, credited to Alan Hájek and Harris Nover, which she said breaks expected-value reasoning in a stranger way than merely sending it to infinity. She didn't have time to walk me through it, and what I've turned up on my own since either presumes a comfort with infinite series I don't have, or asserts the paradox's importance without actually explaining it.

Could you compare the Pasadena paradox with the classical St. Petersburg paradox in terms I could put in front of an intelligent general-interest readership? Both, as I understand it, involve waiting for a fair coin to land heads and being paid according to how long that takes, but the Pasadena game's payoffs are apparently constructed so that the resulting series is only conditionally, rather than absolutely, convergent -- which is where the Riemann rearrangement theorem comes in, and why some decision theorists insist the game's expected value isn't merely infinite, as in St. Petersburg, but strictly undefined. I want to understand exactly what work that distinction is doing philosophically: is "undefined" really a deeper or more damaging verdict for expected-value theory than "infinite," or was my source dramatizing a technical curiosity? And if there are serious published attempts to tame or reinterpret the Pasadena game that don't equally rescue St. Petersburg, I'd like my piece to represent that debate honestly rather than just repeating her framing -- so please don't paper over any genuine disagreement among decision theorists about how much damage the paradox actually does.
