In my philosophy elective, our teacher introduced Leibniz's Law as a basic law of logic, comparable to the law of non-contradiction: if two things share every property in common, they must actually be one and the same thing. But in some outside reading on metaphysics, I came across Max Black's thought experiment about a universe containing nothing but two perfectly identical iron spheres, which is treated as a serious, still-unresolved challenge to that very principle. I'm struggling to see how a purely hypothetical scenario could threaten something as fundamental as a law of logic. Does Black's argument expose a genuine crack in classical logic itself, or does it just show that identity is a far less settled notion than I had assumed? And either way, why do fields like mathematics and computer science still lean so heavily on "Leibniz's Law" without seeming to worry about this?
