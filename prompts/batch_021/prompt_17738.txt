Over dinner last week, a friend and I spent the better part of two hours arguing about Michael Dummett's short book *On Immigration and Refugees*, without really resolving anything. Her position is that Dummett's central distinction — between refugees fleeing persecution, who have something close to an unconditional claim on our help, and economic migrants, whose admission states may legitimately ration — is little more than a polite fiction: given how readily immigration officers stretch the category "economic migrant" to swallow anyone whose suffering isn't sufficiently well-documented or telegenic, she thinks we should stop pretending the line tracks anything morally real and just weigh raw desperation instead. I argued back that Dummett is quite alive to this abuse — confronting it is a central concern of the book — and that the distinction still does genuine work: there is a difference between a predicament someone has actively engineered against you because of who you are, and one you are simply unlucky enough to be caught up in, even if, as he concedes, plenty of real cases sit right on the seam between the two.

I mention this because I've now hit that seam somewhere other than the dinner table. I sit on the small case-review committee of a nonprofit immigration legal clinic, and our volunteer attorneys have capacity to open exactly one more full asylum case before this intake cycle closes. Two referrals have come in, and the committee is deadlocked between them. The first is a young blogger from an authoritarian state, detained and beaten after covering a rigged election, who escaped custody and fled; her claim — persecution for political opinion — is about as clean as asylum law gets, and a couple of press-freedom organizations have also expressed interest in her case. The second is a Honduran mother and her teenage son: the boy's uncle was murdered for refusing to pay a local gang's "war tax," after which the gang told the son to join or be next, and the family fled. Their claim is much harder to win, because appellate courts have often been unwilling to treat resistance to gang extortion or recruitment as persecution "on account of" a recognized ground rather than as generalized criminal violence — this despite the family arguably having nowhere else to turn.

Could you draft the short memo I'll circulate before our next meeting? I need it to recommend, concretely, which case we take on this cycle — or some other resolution, such as splitting our remaining hours between both or taking the harder case ourselves while helping the stronger one find outside counsel — and, since this "seam" problem is bound to recur, to propose a rough standing criterion the committee could apply to future referrals like it. The reasoning needs to be something the rest of the committee could actually follow: I'd like it to treat Dummett's persecution/economic-migrant distinction as more than a slogan, engage honestly with why the Honduran case sits so close to the seam he worries about, and still give a fair, undiminished statement of the best argument for whichever case we end up not taking.
