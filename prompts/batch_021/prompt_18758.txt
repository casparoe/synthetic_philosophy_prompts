I'm a novelist partway through a book whose protagonist is a nineteenth-century physicist who spends the back half of her career defending a theory of the luminiferous ether even as the observational evidence keeps shifting under her, and I've been struggling to articulate, for the reader, what it would even mean for her to have been 'closer to the truth' than her predecessors despite her own theory's eventually turning out to be false. Digging into this, I learned that 'verisimilitude' is not only a word novelists borrow for their own craft but a genuine technical problem in philosophy of science, one that begins with Popper's attempt to define truthlikeness for false theories in terms of their true and false logical consequences, and that famously ran into trouble when Miller and Tichý independently proved that his own definition could never rank one false theory as closer to the truth than another. I've since been invited to moderate the audience Q&A after a colloquium talk at my university by a philosopher of science known for defending the 'similarity' (or 'likeness') successor to Popper's project -- the line of work running through Tichý and Oddie to Niiniluoto's min-sum measure of truthlikeness -- as the correct way to rescue the idea Popper was groping toward.

I'd like eight to ten questions for this Q&A that don't just invite him to restate the position for a general audience. At minimum, I want one question that makes him explain, in terms my audience can actually follow, exactly how a similarity-based measure is meant to escape the fate of Popper's own definition; one that presses the charge that these similarity rankings are language-dependent -- that redescribing two rival theories in a different but logically equivalent vocabulary can reverse which one comes out more verisimilar -- and asks whether his preferred fix, such as relativizing truthlikeness to a fixed 'cognitive problem' or restricting comparisons to natural predicates, is a principled solution or a retreat from the language-independent notion Popper actually wanted; and one that goes after the more basic worry that measuring a theory's distance from the truth seems to require already knowing the complete truth, which threatens to make verisimilitude useless as a forward-looking guide for scientists -- like my physicist -- who must choose between live rival theories without knowing which, if any, is correct. For each of these, please add a short follow-up question keyed to the answer he's most likely to give, so that I have something ready if he reaches for the standard reply.
