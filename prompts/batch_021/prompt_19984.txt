During my career as a systems neuroscientist I worked on reward-related dopamine signaling, in the years when Wolfram Schultz's prediction-error findings were first giving experimental substance to the idea that the brain implements something like a reinforcement-learning algorithm. Since retiring I have been filling in the philosophical education I never had time for, and a former postdoc of mine, now working in AI safety, pointed me toward what is in some sense the mirror image of the problem I used to study: inverse reinforcement learning, which tries to infer an agent's reward function from its observed choices rather than predict choices from a known reward function. What caught my attention was a result I have seen attributed to Stuart Armstrong and Sören Mindermann, to the effect that behavior alone, absent some independently justified assumption about the agent's planning process or degree of rationality, is compatible with indefinitely many different reward functions, and that appealing to a simplicity prior over reward-planner pairs does not straightforwardly rescue the inference, since there is no guarantee that the simplest pairing consistent with the data is the one that tracks what we actually want to know.

I would like your help thinking through what is really at stake in this result, rather than a survey of the technical literature around it. Could you set out what you take to be the most important implications of this underdetermination for the broader project of inferring what people want from what they do -- as pursued both in AI alignment, where the hope is to learn human values from behavioral or preference data, and in economics' revealed-preference tradition, which has always rested on background rationality assumptions that this result seems to put pressure on? In particular, I would find it useful to know whether you think this should be read as a decisive objection to any purely behavior-based theory of preference, as a new formal face on an old problem I recognize from disputes over behaviorism and the interpretation of animal cognition, or as a limitation that practitioners can reasonably work around -- and if the latter, what would actually entitle them to the further assumptions doing the work.
