Got into it with my friend last night over Frederick Douglass and the Constitution and neither of us backed down. She thinks his big switch -- from the Garrisonian line that the document is pro-slavery, don't vote, don't hold office, no union with slaveholders, to his later view that the Constitution is actually an anti-slavery document once you read it right -- was basically him softening up for the political abolitionists. I think the later position was actually the harder, more serious one, because it's a real textual argument (three-fifths clause, fugitive slave clause, the fact that the thing never once says "slave," only "persons") and not just a vibe about the founders being awful.

Write the dialogue we should've had instead: Douglass, right after breaking with Garrison around 1851, having to defend the switch to Garrison himself, argument for arguement, with Garrison not letting him off easy. Keep both of them sharp -- Garrison's not just there to lose, "no union with slaveholders" was a real position with real reasons behind it. And don't wrap it up with them shaking hands, because on this particular question, they never really did.
