I am a novelist assembling a small collection of short fiction, each story built around a classic philosophical thought experiment, and I would like your help with the entry inspired by Max Black's 1952 dialogue on the identity of indiscernibles. Black's piece imagines a universe containing nothing but two perfectly similar iron spheres, offered as a challenge to Leibniz's claim that no two distinct things can be exactly alike in every property. Rather than simply restaging that scenario, please write a short story of around a thousand words, set wherever and whenever you judge best, in which two people, objects, or places turn out to match each other in every respect that could be named, including their relations to everything else around them, and yet remain, undeniably, two rather than one. Let a character genuinely sit with the difficulty this creates, rather than quietly resolving it through some difference the premise was meant to exclude, such as a private memory, a separate history, or a spatial "here" versus "there" treated as though it settled the matter by itself. I would rather receive a story that takes Black's puzzle seriously on its own terms than one that merely borrows "twins" or "clones" as set dressing.
