Within philosophy of mind, there is an ongoing debate about the "cognitive penetrability" of perception -- the question of whether our background beliefs, expectations, desires, and emotions can genuinely alter the content of what we perceptually experience, rather than merely biasing the judgments we form on the basis of a perceptual experience that is itself left unaffected. Defenders of a more modular picture of the mind, following Jerry Fodor and Zenon Pylyshyn, hold that early and mid-level visual processing is "informationally encapsulated" from what we believe and want: cognition may direct our attention and shape how we interpret what we see, but on this view it cannot reach down and change what is perceptually presented to us in the first place. Critics such as Susanna Siegel argue, to the contrary, that there are genuine cases of cognitive penetration -- one of her best-known examples involves a perceiver whose antecedent suspicion that a colleague is angry with them causes them not merely to judge but to perceptually experience the colleague's entirely neutral expression as hostile.

This debate came up recently when a friend of mine, who sits on hiring panels at work, described a pattern she had noticed over several rounds of interviews: once a panel has read a strong résumé, a candidate's halting, nervous delivery tends to be experienced as "thoughtful" and "deliberate," whereas the very same halting delivery from a candidate with a weaker résumé on paper is experienced as "unfocused" or "evasive" -- and, by her account, this difference seems to register immediately, well before anyone would say they have consciously reached a judgment about the candidate. I would like to understand what the most important implications of the cognitive penetration debate are for a case like this. In particular, does it matter, practically or ethically, whether résumé-driven effects like these operate at the level of perception itself rather than at the level of a later, separable judgment -- for instance, for how much epistemic responsibility we should assign to interviewers who are acting in good faith on what seems to them like a simple, direct read of the person in front of them, or for whether standard procedural fixes such as blind or structured interviews could really be expected to solve a problem that runs this deep?
