In the course of reporting on how AI-safety researchers think about bargaining between advanced, mutually predicting agents, I encountered a proposed defense against so-called acausal threats -- threats whose force comes not from any direct causal contact between threatener and target, but from each side's capacity to model the other's dispositions. The idea, associated with researchers at the Center on Long-Term Risk, is that an agent can protect itself by adopting a "surrogate goal": an extra objective it doesn't actually value, which it credibly commits to defending exactly as it would defend something it truly cares about, so that a rational threatener targets the surrogate instead of the real thing. Write an essay assessing whether this strategy can really work. Focus especially on the credibility problem it seems to face: since the surrogate is, by design, something the agent doesn't intrinsically value, it's unclear how the agent can be credibly disposed to bear real costs protecting it without that very disposition becoming a genuine vulnerability in its own right -- at which point the decoy has simply become a second real target. Weigh the main proposed fixes (for instance, grounding the commitment in policy-level or updateless reasoning fixed before any specific threat arises, or denying that credibility requires actually feeling the loss) against the objections, and defend a definite verdict: do surrogate goals genuinely reduce an agent's exposure to acausal threats, or do they just relocate the vulnerability?
