I spent thirty-four years as a field ecologist, most of it tracking seabird colonies along the northern coastlines, and it is only since retiring two years ago that I have had the leisure to take up philosophy properly — working through things slowly on my own, and lately with a small discussion group that meets fortnightly at the local library. Something that came up at a family dinner last weekend has stayed with me ever since, and since nobody at the table could settle it to anyone's satisfaction, I would value a more careful outside opinion.

My granddaughter has an important regional swim meet tomorrow morning, and on the evening before it she had more or less decided to skip her final scheduled practice session in favour of going out with friends. When I made some mild remark about the wisdom of that, my nephew, who is finishing an engineering degree and has a weakness for tidy formal arguments, came to her defence with a line of reasoning that brought the table to a standstill. Whether she wins tomorrow, he said, is already, at this very moment, either a true statement or a false one, even though nobody yet knows which; and if it is already true tonight that she will win, then no amount of extra practice can make that any truer, whereas if it is already true that she will lose, no amount of practice can make that any less true — so either way, he concluded, this evening's session cannot be what actually decides the result, and treating it as though it could is really just a confusion about how facts and time relate to one another. Everyone laughed and let her off the hook, but the argument has bothered me ever since: it feels obviously wrong, since effort surely does bear on outcomes, and yet I have not been able to locate the flaw in it.

Having spent a career thinking about prediction and uncertainty in natural populations, my first instinct was to file this under the old dispute between determinism and free will, but the longer I sit with it, the less that seems to be what is really going on: my nephew's argument never once appeals to prior causes, physical laws, or the state of the world before the race, only to the bare idea that a statement about tomorrow is already true or false today. I would be grateful, then, for a careful diagnosis rather than a general treatment of free will, since I suspect that debate is something of a red herring here. Is there a genuine flaw in his reasoning, and if so, precisely where does it lie? And if you think he is on to something real, I would like to understand just how much of ordinary practical deliberation — training for a race, or indeed anything else we bother to prepare for — it actually threatens.
