I'm a PhD student in philosophy working mainly on philosophy of mind, but a few of us in the department run a small podcast where we interview visiting speakers before their work-in-progress talks, and epistemology guests come up often enough that I end up prepping outside my own area more than I'd like. Next month's guest is an epistemologist who has built a good part of her reputation arguing that epistemic circularity comes in malignant and benign varieties, and that what's often called the bootstrapping problem for basic knowledge structures is really just an unglamorous instance of the benign kind, no more suspect than any other self-supporting use of a faculty we'd never think to fault elsewhere. The problem, briefly: if a source like perception or memory -- or, in the standard toy example, a car's fuel gauge -- can give you knowledge outright, without your needing independent, antecedent reason to trust it, then simply checking that source against itself, again and again, seems to let you "bootstrap" your way to knowing it's reliable, which feels like getting a substantive conclusion for free. Every time I've heard her present the material, the natural objection that this feels like cheating gets the same answer: it only has force if we've already smuggled in a demand for source-independent verification that no finite believer's foundational faculties could ever satisfy, so it's the demand, not her view, that ought to go.

I'd like your help drafting eight to ten interview questions that don't just give her room to repeat that answer. Please include at least one question that presses on whether the fuel-gauge bootstrapping case is genuinely analogous to lottery-proposition cases -- where most people are confident you can't come to know "this ticket will lose" from the base rate alone, however high that rate is -- and, if it isn't analogous, what the relevant disanalogy is supposed to be; one that asks her to say precisely where the malignant/benign line falls, and whether drawing it anywhere commits her to giving up a fairly plausible closure principle; and one that asks how her benign-circularity story is meant to relate to Crispin Wright's entitlement proposal -- whether she thinks entitlement solves the same problem her story solves, solves a different problem wearing the same clothes, or just is her own view under another name. For each of the eight to ten questions, please add a short, pointed follow-up tailored to whichever reply she's most likely to give, since I'd rather not be caught flat-footed with nothing beyond the deflection she always falls back on.
