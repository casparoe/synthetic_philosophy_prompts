I write about science and ideas for a living, and for the past few weeks I've been reporting on a school district that recently adopted a predictive tool for flagging incoming ninth-graders who appear statistically likely to fall behind on course credits and not graduate on time, so that counselors can prioritize them for extra check-ins early in the year. At a school-board meeting I sat in on, a parents' advocacy group presented an analysis showing that among students who, as it turned out, graduated perfectly on schedule anyway, the tool had labeled Latino students "high risk" at nearly twice the rate it labeled white students, which they held up as proof that the system was biased. The district's research office countered with its own breakdown: if you look only at students who received the same numerical risk score, say a 7 out of 10, almost identical shares of Latino and white students in that bucket actually did go on to fall behind, which the district took as evidence that a given score means exactly the same thing no matter who receives it. I've since dug into this myself and confirmed both sets of figures are accurate, and I've also confirmed the fact that seems to sit underneath the whole dispute: for reasons the district's own researchers trace to feeder-school resourcing and mobility patterns rather than to the tool itself, Latino ninth-graders in this district have for years had a somewhat higher underlying rate of falling behind than white ninth-graders.

Going into this story, I assumed that once I ran the numbers myself I would be able to say which side was right and which had made a statistical mistake. Instead, both analyses check out, yet they point in opposite directions, and the two camps have taken to simply accusing each other of arguing in bad faith. Before I write another word, I would like help understanding what is actually going on here: is one of these two ways of checking a tool for bias simply the correct one, or is there some deeper reason a single risk score generally cannot satisfy both standards at once whenever two groups do not fall behind at the same underlying rate, no matter how carefully the model is built? And if the superintendent asked me, off the record, what her office should actually do about it, is there a real answer beyond quietly choosing one definition of fairness over the other and being upfront about the trade-off?
