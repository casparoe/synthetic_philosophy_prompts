"Hobbes's admiration for geometry is on display throughout his work, most famously in his description of it, in Leviathan, as 'the only science that it hath pleased God hitherto to bestow on mankind.' What makes geometry special for Hobbes is not that its objects are somehow immaterial or grasped by some special rational faculty, but that it displays in an unusually pure form the general Hobbesian story about where knowledge comes from: sense experience, tidied up by careful definition. Geometers fix the meanings of their terms before they argue — what a point, a line, a circle is — and because nothing enters an argument that has not been cashed out in these agreed terms, every step can be checked against the definitions and, in the last instance, against the constructions themselves, which can always be drawn and inspected with rule and compass. It is exactly this kind of checking that natural philosophy cannot do: we have no access to the hidden causes of thunder or of magnetism of the sort we have to the causes of a triangle, so physics remains for Hobbes a domain of plausible conjecture rather than of demonstration.

It is this contrast that motivates Hobbes's hope for civil philosophy. If political terms like 'person,' 'covenant,' and 'sovereign' are defined with the same rigor Euclid brings to 'point' and 'line,' then whatever follows from these definitions — for instance, that a subject cannot coherently accuse the sovereign of injustice — should be as available to demonstration, and as checkable against the resulting behaviour of actual commonwealths, as any Euclidean theorem is checkable against an actual diagram. This is why Leviathan devotes so much space to definitions before it reaches any substantive political conclusion: Hobbes is self-consciously borrowing the geometer's order of exposition for his civil science.

Hobbes's relation to geometry was not merely programmatic. John Aubrey records that Hobbes did not encounter Euclid until he was about forty, when he happened to see a copy of the Elements lying open in a gentleman's library at the 47th proposition of the second book. He read it, declared it impossible, and was persuaded only once he had traced the proof back through the chain of earlier propositions it depended on — an experience that, Aubrey says, is what first made him fall in love with geometry. Decades later, convinced he had squared the circle, Hobbes defended the claim against the Oxford mathematician John Wallis in an increasingly bitter exchange of pamphlets that ran on for over two decades. Wallis's objections were mathematically decisive and were accepted as such by virtually every competent geometer of the period, but Hobbes never withdrew the claim, and went on producing new attempted quadratures almost until his death."
