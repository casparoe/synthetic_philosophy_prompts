I'm preparing a graduate seminar that pairs the history of ethics with the history of economic thought, and I want to push students past the potted version of Keynes they arrive with — "he predicted a fifteen-hour week and got it wrong" — toward the picture of the good life that prediction was designed to serve. In "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren" (1930), Keynes distinguishes "absolute" needs, which he expected growth to satisfy within a century, from "relative" needs tied to feeling superior to one's neighbors, which he granted might be insatiable; on this basis he predicted that the economic problem would be all but solved by 2030, freeing humanity to treat the love of money once more as the vice, even the "disgusting" pathology, earlier moralists had always taken it to be, rather than as the useful evil he thought at least another century of accumulation still required. The good life this abundance was meant to make room for was not Keynes's own invention: it was the creed he had absorbed as a young man from G. E. Moore, which he recalls, half admiringly and half mockingly, in the later memoir "My Early Beliefs" (1938), as a matter of cultivating "timeless, passionate states of contemplation and communion" centered on love, beauty, and knowledge, largely indifferent to achievement, consequence, or rule-following. Yet in that same memoir, written just as fascism was on the march, Keynes confesses that he and his Bloomsbury friends had badly underrated "the insane and irrational springs of wickedness in most men," and had taken from Moore only the vision of the ultimate good, discarding his willingness to bind that vision with ordinary moral rules.

Write an essay that puts these two texts to work on each other. Briefly address why growth of roughly the magnitude Keynes foresaw has not delivered anything like the leisure he predicted, and say whether that shortfall is better explained by the economics of his earlier essay (insatiable relative needs, distributional questions he underplayed) or by the psychology of his later one. Then settle the harder question, and defend a definite answer to it: is the Moorean good life Keynes describes — private, contemplative, unconcerned with achievement or with general rules — an ideal we still have good reason to endorse on its own terms, whatever becomes of the leisure needed to pursue it at scale, or does Keynes's own later verdict on his circle's naivety give us reason to think the ideal itself, and not merely the forecast built around it, was a mistake? Defend your answer against the strongest version of the opposing view.
