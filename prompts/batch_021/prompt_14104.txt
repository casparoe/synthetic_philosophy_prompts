Question: State the Church-Fitch proof that the knowability principle (the thesis that every truth is possibly known) collapses into the claim that every truth is actually known. Be explicit about which epistemic/logical principles are doing the work at each step, note one historical detail about the proof's origin, and briefly describe one strategy available to a verificationist who wants to resist the argument.

Student's answer:

Fitch's paradox targets the verificationist idea that truth is epistemically constrained, formalized as the knowability principle (KP): ∀p(p → ◊Kp) -- every truth is possibly known by someone at some time. The proof also needs the (very plausible) non-omniscience assumption: ∃p(p ∧ ¬Kp) -- some truth is never actually known.

Fix a witness for non-omniscience and call it a, so a ∧ ¬Ka is true. Instantiating KP on this very conjunction gives (a ∧ ¬Ka) → ◊K(a ∧ ¬Ka); since the antecedent is true, ◊K(a ∧ ¬Ka) follows.

Now a sub-proof shows this is actually impossible. Suppose, for contradiction, K(a ∧ ¬Ka). Knowledge distributes over conjunction (if you know a conjunction you know each conjunct), so Ka ∧ K¬Ka. Knowledge is factive (Kp → p, for any p), so instantiating this on ¬Ka gives K¬Ka → ¬Ka, hence ¬Ka. But we already have Ka on the same line, so Ka ∧ ¬Ka -- contradiction. So K(a ∧ ¬Ka) can't be true in any possible circumstance: ¬◊K(a ∧ ¬Ka).

That flatly contradicts ◊K(a ∧ ¬Ka) from before. So by reductio, one of the two starting assumptions has to go. Since the whole derivation only used this one instance of KP, the natural reading is that non-omniscience is what fails: granting KP even for this single case is already enough to guarantee that a gets known after all, and generalizing over every possible witness, KP simply forces omniscience outright. That's a strange result, but strictly speaking it's a discovery about what the knowability principle commits you to rather than a straightforward refutation of it -- if you're already committed to KP, omniscience is a surprising bonus, not a reductio you're forced to accept.

Historically, Fitch didn't claim credit for the core theorem himself: in his 1963 paper he attributes it to an anonymous referee report on an earlier, unpublished paper of his. The result is usually read as bad news for verificationist theories of meaning/truth like the one Michael Dummett defends, and one popular way to try to save such a view is the strategy, due to Dummett, of restricting KP so it doesn't range over "Cartesian" propositions like a ∧ ¬Ka in the first place, on the grounds that these are guaranteed to become false the moment anyone comes to know them, so they were never plausible candidates for the knowability thesis to begin with.
