I've been reading Lon Fuller's The Morality of Law for a jurisprudence seminar, and we just got through the Rex parable -- the king who tries and fails, eight different times, to make law (deciding cases ad hoc with no rules, then keeping the rules secret, then making them retroactive, then unintelligible, then contradictory, then demanding the impossible, then changing them constantly, then finally just not enforcing them the way they're actually written). That's supposed to distill the "eight desiderata" of the inner morality of law. Fuller's big claim, which he leans on pretty hard against Hart, is that a lawgiver who genuinely satisfies all eight tends to get nudged toward some baseline of substantive decency just by virtue of following the procedure properly -- its hard to be a monster while also being fully general, public, clear, non-contradictory, prospective, stable, possible to comply with, and congruently enforced.

This turned into a whole argument with a classmate who's a hardcore positivist, and now I can't let it go. Can you construct a genuinely detailed example of a single law or a whole legal regime that satisfies all eight desiderata -- like, we could go through them one by one and check off every box, no sneaking in a hidden violation of clarity or congruence -- and yet is still clearly, substantively unjust? Please don't just reach for Nazi Germany or apartheid South Africa, since those are the examples everybody always brings up, and Fuller would (and did) just reply that those regimes actually flunked several of the eight pretty badly, so it's kind of begging the question against him. I want something airtight enough on the procedural side that it actually puts pressure on Fuller's claim, rather than something that only looks like a counterexample until you inspect it closely.
