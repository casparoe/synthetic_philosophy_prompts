So I'm a philosophy PhD student (formal epistemology, plus a game theory minor that's basically borrowed wholesale from the econ department), and this week's seminar was on the epistemic foundations of backward induction — specifically Aumann's 1995 result that common knowledge of rationality (CKR) forces the backward-induction outcome in any generic finite game of perfect information. We spent basically the whole session on the centipede game (pot grows every round, you can "take" or "pass," and backward induction says take on move one even though both players do way better if they just keep passing for a while), and honestly I left more confused than when I walked in. Half the room kept re-running the Binmore/Reny-style complaint: if CKR is supposed to hold throughout the game, doesnt the very first "irrational" pass already falsify it? And if its falsified, how can "CKR implies backward induction" tell us anything about the rest of the tree, since the antecedent just went false? Our professor's line was something like: knowledge is factive, so this literally can't happen if CKR truly held at the root, and the objection is quietly smuggling in mere belief where it should be talking about knowledge — but a couple of us weren't totally sold that this dissolves the unease rather than just relabeling it.

Could you write me a dialogue — a page or so — between two epistemic game theorists hashing this out, say at a conference bar after somebody's talk on exactly this? One of them should run the Aumann-style defense as strongly as possible: the theorem is correct, the "paradox" trades on sliding between CKR-at-the-root and some stronger (and never actually stated) requirement that rationality-beliefs survive every counterfactual node, and the intuition that "surely they'd both pass for a while" just shows people don't really believe CKR holds, not that CKR fails to entail the result. The other should push the objection as hard as it can go — that insisting CKR survive off the equilibrium path is either vacuous, or that it quietly bakes in a specific and contestable belief-revision policy about what a deviation licenses you to infer about the deviator (and about what they'll do at later nodes). I don't want either of them just restating their opening position paragraph after paragraph — they should actually respond to whatever the other one just said. And they don't need to reach a tidy resolution — I'd honestly be a little suspicious if they did — I just want the strongest version of both sides actually grappling with each other, including drifting into adjacent stuff like common vs. mutual knowledge, or knowledge vs. common belief, if that's genuinely where the exchange leads.
