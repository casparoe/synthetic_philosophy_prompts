I teach a seminar on the ethics of war, and this semester it's kind of come back to bite me. One of my students, a senior in the university's ROTC program, wrote his term paper defending McMahan-style revisionist just war theory over Walzer's "moral equality of combatants," and took it a lot more to heart than I expected, because he's now filed for a conscientious objector discharge — not as a pacifist (he's said flat out he'd have fought in WWII), but because he's become convinced the specific deployment he's been assigned to is unjust, and that killing enemy combatants there would be wrongful killing even though it's legally sanctioned and even though he'd probably only count as "excused" rather than justified if he did it anyway. His command has no idea what to do with an objection this philosophically sophisticated, so they asked me to sit in on the informal review panel as a civilian advisor, since apparently my syllabus shows up as Exhibit A in his packet. Which is a little horrifying, honestly.

The trouble is the actual policy (DoD Instruction 1300.06, or whatever number it is now) only recognizes objection to "participation in war in any form" — selective objection to one particular war doesn't qualify, on the old logic that you can't have individual soldiers relitigating jus ad bellum on their way to the front. So on paper this is a fast denial. But it nags at me that the policy just helps itself to Walzer's side of the argument without ever making the case for it, when plenty of serious people, following McMahan, would say that forcing this kid to deploy anyway is forcing him to do something he has good reason to believe is seriously wrong.

The panel meets a week from Monday and wants a short memo from me recommending what to do — deny, grant, or some middle option like reassignment to a noncombat role — with real reasons behind it rather then just "policy says no." Could you draft that memo? I want it to actually engage the kid's argument on its own terms, but also be honest about the strongest reason the policy might be right to hold the line where it does even if Walzer's underlying picture turns out to be false.
