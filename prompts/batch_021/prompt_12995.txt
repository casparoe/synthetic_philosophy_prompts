Over dinner last week, a friend of mine who is finishing a PhD in philosophy of science and I got into a long, unresolved argument about the DSM-5-TR's 2022 addition of Prolonged Grief Disorder (PGD) as a standalone diagnosis. She kept coming back to Ian Hacking's idea of the looping effect: his claim that psychiatric categories are "interactive kinds" rather than "indifferent kinds" like isotopes or asteroids, so that once people learn how they have been classified, the classification feeds back into how they understand and perform their own condition, shifting the very behavior the criteria were meant to capture and eventually prompting experts to revise the criteria again. Her standing example was Hacking's own case study of multiple personality disorder in Rewriting the Soul, which she thinks proliferated partly because it existed as a recognized, well-publicized category; her worry is that PGD — which requires, among other things, that intense yearning or preoccupation with the deceased persist beyond twelve months in adults (six in children) alongside at least three of eight further symptoms such as identity disruption or a pervasive sense that life is meaningless — could do something similar to grief, turning it into a script bereaved people learn to monitor themselves against rather than something they simply undergo. I countered that this kind of worry, taken too far, ends up denying real people real treatment: something like one in ten bereaved adults reportedly meets criteria for clinically significant prolonged grief, and without a formal diagnosis they are left with vague, non-billable "bereavement support" instead of protocol-based grief therapy. Neither of us gave ground, and the argument has become unavoidably relevant, because I sit on my hospital system's bereavement care clinical policy committee, which meets in three weeks to decide whether our grief program should formally adopt PGD screening and diagnostic coding at intake.

Could you draft the memo I will bring to that meeting? I need a clear recommendation — adopt, reject, or adopt with specific conditions — defended in a way that would persuade a committee member who has never heard of Hacking, while still engaging the looping-effects worry on its merits rather than acknowledging it and moving on. If my friend has a point, I want to know what follows operationally: how we would screen, how counselors would be trained to introduce the diagnosis to a grieving patient, and whether we should build in a review period or some way of tracking for the very self-fulfilling effects she is worried about. Please give the committee a document it could actually vote on, with reasoning specific enough to survive next week's follow-up argument with my friend, not a general survey of the debate over psychiatric classification.
