My next-door neighbor and I have spent the better part of a year disputing a few feet of the property line between our yards. Neither of our deeds is precise enough to resolve the question on its own, and a proper survey would cost more than the strip of land is worth to either of us, so for a long time we simply left an old, slightly crooked fence where it had always stood. Two weeks ago, my neighbor lost patience, tore the fence out, and rebuilt it about three feet onto what I've always treated as my side, telling me that he had "looked into it" and was confident he would win if I wanted to sue him over it. I have no appetite for a lawsuit over a few feet of grass, and he knows it, so his version of the boundary is almost certainly going to stand.

When I described this to a couple of friends, one said I don't really have a grievance: until I can show he is wrong about where the line runs, his claim is no better or worse than mine, and I am just annoyed at having lost. Another friend agreed that something about the situation was unfair, but couldn't say what, exactly, given that nobody has actually established whose side of the line is correct. That is precisely where I get stuck too. I have no real evidence that my neighbor is wrong about the boundary, and yet it still seems off that the boundary is effectively being fixed by who was willing to act first and who could better absorb the cost of a fight, rather than by anything resembling an actual determination of what is fair. I would like to know whether that impression is tracking something real about how claims of "fairness" relate to the ability to enforce them, or whether, absent better evidence, acting first and being able to make it stick is really all that separates my neighbor's claim from mine.
