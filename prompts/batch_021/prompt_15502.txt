Our department's philosophy of logic colloquium has finally managed to bring a speaker to campus in person rather than by video link: a philosopher who has spent much of her career defending a dialetheic solution to the Liar paradox, working in the tradition of paraconsistent logic associated with Graham Priest. On her view, the Liar sentence is a genuine dialetheia, both true and false, and classical logic is simply mistaken to insist that no contradiction could ever be true. I will be chairing the Q&A immediately following her talk, and since audiences always spend the first several minutes on the same reflex -- flat incredulity that anyone could think a contradiction is true -- I would like the questions to move past that quickly and go straight for the parts of the position I think are least settled.

Could you draft nine or ten questions for that session? I want at least one that presses on how her paraconsistent logic is supposed to block explosion without giving up so much ordinary reasoning (disjunctive syllogism, in particular) that a critic could fairly say she has changed the subject rather than solved the paradox; one asking for the positive case for treating the Liar as a truth-value glut rather than a gap, given that gap theorists agree the sentence resists an ordinary bivalent verdict and simply draw the opposite conclusion from that fact; one on how her solution is meant to survive a strengthened Liar built to say of itself that it is false and not also true, since handling that sentence seems to require a negation strong enough to exclude a glut and thereby to reopen the very paradox she thought she had closed; and one on Curry's paradox, which is generated without using negation at all and so looks like it calls for restricting the conditional, or the structural rule of contraction, quite independently of anything dialetheism buys her -- I'd like to know whether she thinks her solution to the Liar actually extends there, or whether she needs a second, unrelated fix. For each of those four, please add a short follow-up that anticipates the reply she is most likely to give and doesn't let the exchange stop there, and close the list with one question asking what would have to be true for her to give up dialetheism rather than simply accept one more contradiction.
