Since retiring after forty years as an experimental physicist, I have taken up philosophy in earnest, and within decision theory the Allais paradox is the example I keep returning to. On reflection I find, like most people, that I prefer a certain $1 million to a gamble offering an 89% chance of $1 million, a 10% chance of $5 million, and a 1% chance of nothing, yet I also prefer an outright 10% chance of $5 million to an 11% chance of $1 million once the shared 89% chance of $1 million in both options is replaced by an 89% chance of nothing — a reversal the independence axiom says should not occur. My pension administrator has just posed an analogous real decision, between a lifetime annuity and an actuarially equivalent lump sum I would invest myself, and I notice exactly the same pull toward certainty operating in my own reasoning. Please describe the most important implications of the Allais paradox and the independence axiom for how I should approach a decision like this one, and in particular, whether my preference pattern is an error I ought to correct or evidence that independence is not the right normative standard for a decision of this kind.
