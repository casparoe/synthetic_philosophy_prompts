A friend who sits on the investment committee of a small venture fund described a practice that has been nagging at me. The committee reviews one deal at a time: each of the eight members reads the due-diligence memo independently, then, in a fixed seating order, states simply "invest" or "pass," without giving reasons. She's noticed that once the first two say "invest," almost everyone else falls in line — including members who told her privately beforehand that their own read of the memo was lukewarm.

I mentioned this at dinner, and two friends of mine, Marcus and Elena, argued about it for a while without either one backing down:

(1) Marcus: This is a textbook information cascade, and the committee is fooling itself: once the first two say "invest," everyone downstream is just parroting, so the last six votes carry no independent information at all. They should collect votes simultaneously and in writing — right now "eight votes of confidence" might really be two.

(2) Elena: That's unfair to the later voters. Setting aside your own lukewarm read because two people ahead of you already said "invest" isn't parroting — it's exactly what a rational Bayesian should do, since two independent positive signals outweigh one so-so private one. The real mistake would be clinging to a weak signal against stronger public evidence.

(3) Marcus: Fine, no individual member is being irrational. But that doesn't rescue the committee. The whole point of eight people in the room is eight independent reads of the evidence. If six of them defer in equilibrium, the fund has built an eight-person committee that behaves like a two-person one, and no single step was anyone's mistake.

(4) Elena: You're overstating both the disease and the cure. Cascades are fragile — one member with an unusually strong signal, or enough conviction to dissent loudly, can send the whole sequence the other way. And "eight independent reads" was always aspirational: everyone at that table has sat in the same firm for years and absorbed the same house priors, so less information is really lost than a clean textbook cascade would suggest.
