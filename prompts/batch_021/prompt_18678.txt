I've spent years reading LessWrong and its offshoots, where "shut up and multiply" and the torture-versus-dust-specks thought experiment are treated as near-common sense: of course the numbers count, and intuitions to the contrary are just scope insensitivity waiting to be corrected. I host a small philosophy podcast, and next month's guest is a moral philosopher who has built her career defending the opposite conclusion -- Taurek's argument, in "Should the Numbers Count?", that you do no wrong by saving one stranger instead of five others you could have saved, and that a coin flip, not simple addition, is the fair way to decide such cases. Draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having her simply restate the view: I want at least one question each on whether her case against aggregating strangers' claims also rules out any duty to prefer a million lives to one; on why fairness favors an even coin flip between the two groups rather than a lottery weighted toward the larger group, which giving each individual an equal chance would seem to require; on how she would resist Scanlon's suggestion that the "extra" person's claim can break a tie between one-to-one comparisons without any illegitimate summing of welfare; and on whether her position is really anything more than a philosophically dressed-up version of the scope insensitivity that effective altruists have spent years training themselves out of. For each question, add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give.
