I sit on my university's names and memorials review committee, which adjudicates petitions to rename or recontextualize campus buildings honoring historical figures. A residence hall named Truman Hall since the 1950s is now the subject of a formal renaming petition, and unusually, the petition doesn't lean on generic "war crimes" language: it reconstructs Elizabeth Anscombe's 1956 argument against Oxford's decision to award Harry Truman an honorary degree -- that the atomic bombings were not a foreseen-but-unintended side effect of striking legitimate military targets, but the very means by which mass civilian death was supposed to break Japan's will to fight, which makes "murder" the right description no matter how the resulting toll compares to the alternatives. Two committee members want to deny the petition, for different reasons: one holds that the civilian deaths really were an unintended side effect of a legitimate strategic aim, so double effect applies after all and Anscombe's charge never gets off the ground; the other grants that the deaths were intended but argues that refusing that trade against a land invasion projected to kill even more people, on both sides, would itself have been the greater wrong. I've followed enough double-effect and trolley-problem debates on rationalist blogs over the years to know this isn't going to dissolve neatly, but the committee still has to vote in three weeks. Please draft the memo I'll circulate beforehand recommending rename, keep, or keep-with-contextualization, with reasoning the other two members could actually respond to point by point, and with a fair statement of the strongest case for whichever option you don't recommend.
