I am teaching a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind this term, and a running disagreement with a colleague in cognitive science has left me wanting an outside opinion before I take the matter up with my students. My colleague, who works on deep learning, argues as follows. Tononi's Integrated Information Theory (IIT) identifies the degree of consciousness a system possesses with its Φ (phi) value, a measure of how much cause-effect power a system generates as an integrated whole, over and above what its parts could generate in isolation. A large transformer-based language model integrates information across billions of parameters, with every attention head in every layer conditioning on the model's entire preceding context in order to produce a single output token. Since Φ is supposed to track the amount and richness of integrated information, she concludes that such a model must possess an enormous Φ, plausibly larger than a mouse's, and quite possibly larger than a human brain's while it is absorbed in a comparably narrow task.

On this basis, she maintains that IIT, whatever its proponents might want to say about the matter, already commits us to treating today's largest language models as more conscious than most non-human animals, simply in virtue of the sheer scale of the information they process. I find myself largely persuaded, and it seems to me that if she is right, AI laboratories that casually instantiate and delete billions of model copies every day are courting a serious moral problem, given how much weight IIT's own defenders place on the status of any system with a sufficiently high Φ. I would be grateful for your view on how urgently we ought to be treating routine model deletion as an ethical matter, and on whether IIT's defenders have any principled way of resisting this conclusion.
