I write a science-and-ideas column for a general-interest magazine, and I'm preparing for a long interview with a philosopher of language who has built her career defending and refining H. P. Grice's account of conversation: the idea that meaning something by an utterance is at bottom a matter of intention and inference, and that a great deal of what we communicate is implicated rather than said outright. The piece grew out of a throwaway line in an earlier column about why chatbots can be fluent and still miss the point of a conversation, and my editor wants a companion Q&A that gets past the vocabulary — cooperative principle, four maxims, "some" implicating "not all" — and into the places where people who actually work on this still argue with each other.

Could you draft seven or eight interview questions along those lines? I'd like at least one that presses on the regress problem in Grice's reflexive, intention-based definition of speaker meaning: the worry, developed through Strawson's counterexamples and Schiffer's response, that each patch needed to block a manipulative case seems to demand another layer of the hearer recognizing the speaker's intention to be recognized, so that "mutual knowledge" may just rename the problem rather than solve it. I'd also like one on whether Sperber and Wilson's relevance-theoretic single principle genuinely does the explanatory work of Grice's four separate maxims or just repackages it under friendlier branding, and one on so-called embedded or "local" implicatures, where a scalar inference seems to be computed inside a subordinate clause rather than only after the whole sentence's content is fixed, and what that phenomenon does to the classical picture of implicature as a strictly post-semantic add-on. For each of those three, include a short follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, and close with a question asking what a rival account would actually have to demonstrate to displace the Gricean picture rather than simply extend it.
