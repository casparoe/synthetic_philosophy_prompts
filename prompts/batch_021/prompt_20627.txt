I'm several chapters into a novel that follows a law clerk on a three-judge state court of appeals, and I've reached a scene I'd rather build on something structurally sound than fudge for the sake of drama. The panel is reviewing a probate court's decision to admit a codicil in which an elderly widow, only weeks before her death, rewrote her will to leave the family's summer house to just one of her three grandchildren, cutting the other two out entirely. Under the doctrine the panel applies, such a codicil is void for undue influence only if two conditions both hold: first, that the favored grandchild occupied a position of trust over the widow and used it to press her toward the change, and second, that her own capacity to resist that pressure was actually overborne, rather than her acting from ordinary affection, grief, or stubbornness. At conference, the three judges reach these individual findings. Judge Alaoui is convinced pressure was exerted but thinks the widow's will remained her own, and so votes to uphold the codicil. Judge Berger finds no improper pressure at all, but is convinced the widow was by then too frail and confused to resist anything, and also votes to uphold. Judge Calloway finds both pressure and an overborne will, and votes to void it. My clerk-protagonist, tallying this for the presiding judge, notices something unsettling: taken question by question, a majority finds pressure was exerted (Alaoui and Calloway), and a separate majority finds the widow's resistance was overborne (Berger and Calloway) — results that together point toward voiding the codicil — even though a plain headcount of the three judges' own bottom-line votes gives two for upholding it and only one for voiding it. She has a nagging sense that this split between deciding issue-by-issue and deciding by outcome has a name somewhere in the legal or social-choice literature, but can't place it precisely, and I'd rather the memo earn its conclusion on the merits than lean on a citation she wouldn't really have on hand.

Could you write the memo she ends up producing for the presiding judge? I want something a real judge could act on before the panel finalizes its opinion: a clear recommendation, stated near the top, about how the case should come out and which of the two procedures — deciding the two questions separately and combining the results, or simply polling the judges' own bottom-line votes — the panel should use to get there. It should include the reasoning a judge would need in hand before signing an opinion built on it, and, since this is the kind of choice that will function as an unstated precedent for how the court handles split votes on multi-part legal tests generally, it should say plainly what that choice commits the court to going forward. I'd also like it to take seriously, rather than wave off, the strongest case a thoughtful judge could make for whichever procedure the memo ends up not recommending — the scene won't work dramatically if that side reads as an obvious loser. Please keep the whole thing tied to this case's specific facts rather than letting it drift into a general essay on voting procedures.
