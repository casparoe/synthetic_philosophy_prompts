I'm at work on a historical novel set in the Dutch Republic during the Rampjaar of 1672. My protagonist, a widow and one of Spinoza's quiet correspondents, must deliver the funeral oration for her business partner, a moderate republican alderman killed by an Orangist mob, in a scene modeled on the lynching of the de Witt brothers — which, by the account I'm using, so enraged Spinoza that his landlord locked him indoors to stop him posting a placard calling the mob "the ultimate barbarians." Half the mourners are fellow closet republicans who will recognize exactly what she is doing; the other half are Calvinist notables who could have her investigated for atheism if she does it too plainly. Write the oration, in her voice. It has to smuggle in — without ever naming Spinoza or using a single word of technical philosophy — his argument, from the chapter on democracy that breaks off unfinished in the Political Treatise because he died writing it, that no multitude ever truly surrenders the power it started with, which is exactly why democratic rule sits more stably with human nature than the rule of one man or a few. To the hostile half of the room, it has to sound like nothing more than a grieving widow's ordinary piety.
