I'm a PhD student in ancient philosophy, currently taking a graduate seminar that works through Aristotle's *Politics* and the account of exchange in *Nicomachean Ethics* V.5. Over dinner last week, a friend from the economics department and I ended up arguing about cryptocurrency, and I realized I wanted an answer grounded in Aristotle's own categories rather than in modern monetary theory, so I'd like to put the question to you properly.

Here is the puzzle. Aristotle distinguishes household exchange aimed at genuine need (chreia) — the "natural" acquisition of goods, bounded by the finite requirements of self-sufficiency — from chrematistike, the pursuit of money as an end in itself, which he treats as unlimited in principle. He reserves special condemnation for lending at interest, tokos, a word that also means "offspring": the charge is that interest makes money unnaturally give birth to more money, when money, as a mere conventional measure (nomisma, from nomos, "custom" or "law"), is supposed to be sterile. Two features of present-day monetary practice bear on this scheme and seem to pull in opposite directions. Central banks now create fiat currency in essentially unlimited quantities, most visibly through quantitative easing, fully severed from any commodity backing, which looks like it should be Aristotle's clearest case of the unnatural. Bitcoin, by contrast, has an algorithmically fixed supply that can never exceed 21 million units, which sounds more like the bounded scarcity of a natural good than like limitless self-multiplication, even though it is also purely virtual and arguably useless for satisfying any need apart from being exchanged.

I'd like you to work out, as carefully as you can from Aristotle's own arguments rather than from any later economic theory, how he would most plausibly rule on these two cases. Specifically: would he recognize either fiat currency or Bitcoin as nomisma in his own sense at all, given his reasons for thinking a currency needs some tie to genuine use? Does Bitcoin's hard supply cap count in its favor by his lights, as a mark of natural limitation, or is that the wrong kind of scarcity to matter to him, since nothing about the coins themselves satisfies any chreia? And would "mining," as computational work that produces new units, count for Aristotle as a form of production at all, or would he see it as an elaborate simulation of natural wealth-getting that does not actually supply anything useful? Please flag which parts of your answer you take to follow fairly directly from the texts and which parts are closer to informed speculation on your part.
