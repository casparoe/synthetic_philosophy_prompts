As a software engineer, I spend a fair amount of time deciding how far to trust claims I can't personally verify -- a colleague's assurance that a function is thread-safe, a third-party library's documentation, an unverified answer on Stack Overflow. That habit got me reading about the epistemology of testimony, and I ran into the long-running dispute between reductionists and anti-reductionists over why anyone is entitled to believe what they're told. Reductionists, following Hume, hold that our justification for accepting testimony ultimately reduces to our own inductive evidence that testimony tends to be reliable; anti-reductionists, following Reid, hold that accepting testimony is a basic, non-inferential source of belief, on a par with perception or memory.

Write a dialogue between a contemporary reductionist and a contemporary anti-reductionist about testimony, triggered by a mundane case: a traveler unhesitatingly believes a stranger's directions to the train station. Have the anti-reductionist press the regress (or circularity) problem Coady raised against Humean reductionism -- that establishing testimony's general reliability by induction would itself require relying on a great deal of testimony we have no independent way of checking, from history and geography to other people's honesty. Don't let the reductionist simply fold: have them retreat to something like Elizabeth Fricker's more modest 'local' reductionism, which trusts testimony case by case on cues to the speaker's competence and sincerity rather than demanding a track record for testimony in general -- and have the anti-reductionist argue that this retreat either quietly presupposes the very general reliability it was meant to avoid assuming, or just relabels the anti-reductionist's own picture. Give me several real rounds of back-and-forth, with neither side landing a clean knockout, and end with the two of them trying to pin down one concrete case where their positions would actually license different verdicts about whether to believe someone.
