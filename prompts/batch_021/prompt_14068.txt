I am writing a novel that follows a three-person climbing team on the morning of a summit push, when the least experienced member announces that, for good personal reasons, she wants to turn back. The other two feel wronged in a way that seems to outrun ordinary disappointment: in their eyes, she owes them her continued participation simply because of what the three of them jointly settled on doing, regardless of what she, as an individual, now wants. Before I write the scene, I want to get the underlying philosophy right. Please write an essay adjudicating the dispute between reductive accounts of shared intention, such as Michael Bratman's, which explain this sort of bindingness entirely in terms of meshing individual sub-plans, mutual responsiveness, and common knowledge among persisting individual intentions, and non-reductive accounts, such as Margaret Gilbert's plural-subject theory or Raimo Tuomela's account of the "we-mode," on which the obligation attaches to the joint commitment itself and cannot be dissolved by any one party's unilateral change of heart. Weigh each side's strongest arguments and most damaging objections, and close with a definite verdict on which kind of account better explains the climbers' situation, rather than a non-committal survey of the field.
