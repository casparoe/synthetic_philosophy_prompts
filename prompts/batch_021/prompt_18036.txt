I've been reading Overcoming Bias and then Less Wrong since not long after Eliezer started the Sequences, and of all the ideas that came out of that scene, Roko's basilisk is the one I feel has been most thoroughly flattened by the wider internet into a pure meme — "the AI that tortures you later for not helping build it now" — with none of the actual decision theory that made Eliezer take it seriously enough to delete the post and ban discussion of it for years. A friend from my old meetup group and I got into it recently over whether the argument has any real force once you take the underlying machinery on its own terms, and we couldn't settle it, so I want a considered outside take.

Write an essay addressing this question directly: does an ideally rational agent, reasoning today, have any genuine decision-theoretic reason to act on the basilisk's threat — for instance, by helping bring the relevant AI into existence or otherwise currying favor with it? Weigh the actual case for taking the threat seriously — roughly, that under something like timeless or updateless decision theory an agent's choices are correlated with the outputs of logically similar computations, including any simulations of that agent run by other parties, rather than only with what the agent causally brings about — against the standard lines of resistance: that a well-designed agent should precommit to ignoring threats of exactly this shape regardless of the consequences, on pain of being exploitable by any would-be extortionist; that ordinary causal decision theory gives an agent no reason to comply at all, since nothing done now can cause a future AI's past behavior; and that there is no principled way to privilege this particular hypothetical future agent over the indefinitely many incompatible ones that could just as easily be imagined threatening you for equally arbitrary reasons. Bring in whatever else seems relevant to you — Parfit's hitchhiker, Pascal's mugging, and the separate question of whether Eliezer's original decision to treat the whole thing as an information hazard was itself justified are all fair game.

I don't want a survey that ends by shrugging and concluding that reasonable people disagree or that it all depends how you define rationality. Reach an actual verdict and defend it: does the argument succeed against a sufficiently well-designed agent, and if it fails, at exactly which step does it first go wrong? A conditional verdict is fine as long as it is substantive — something like "the threat has no force against updateless agents but does bind naive evidential reasoners" — but I want a specific diagnosis, not just a catalogue of considerations.
