So this is a weirdly specific thing that happened to me, but bear with me. Back in September I made a bet with my younger sister to force myself to finish revising a dissertation chapter: I told her I'd Venmo her $600 if I hadn't sent my advisor the new draft by a certain Friday, and to make it "count" she made me say out loud, on a recorded voice memo, that I intended to pay up if I flaked. She's about to finish her own philosophy PhD and nearly fell over laughing, said I'd basically reinvented Kavka's toxin puzzle for myself, except with Venmo instead of a mind-reading billionaire.

Turns out her whole dissertation is built around that exact puzzle and its relatives, so I went down a rabbit hole and read enough to follow along. She defends what she calls "resolute choice": the view that you genuinely can, and rationally should, form an intention to do the costly thing that'll be totally pointless by the time you'd have to actually do it (drink the toxin, actually pay up once hiding is an option), and that this is a real third mode of practical rationality alongside the standard "sophisticated" and "naive" responses, not just a fancy label for willpower. Her go-to move whenever someone corners her on this at a party is "that's not irrational, that's just what it takes to be the kind of person your future self, and other people, can actually trust" -- and then she changes the subject, which worked fine on me for years because I had no idea what she was talking about.

Well, now I know what shes talking about, and I run a tiny philosophy podcast (like 200 listeners, recorded out of my closet) and I've finally talked her into coming on properly instead of just ranting at me over Thanksgiving. I don't want to hand her questions she can answer with the rehearsed version -- I want to actually push on the view. Could you draft me 8-10 interview questions, each with a short follow-up keyed to whatever answer she's most likely to give? I definitely want one that presses on whether "resolute choice" is doing any real work beyond Bratman's idea that stable intentions are just good policy for a planning agent (i.e. is she smuggling in the same answer under a fancier name), one that forces her to either bite or dodge the bullet on the nuclear-deterrence version of the puzzle (a country that's already been attacked gains nothing from actually retaliating -- does a resolute chooser launch anyway, since that's what made the deterrent credible in the first place?), and one that makes her diagnose my actual bet on the spot -- whether the recorded voice memo means I never really tested the puzzle at all, since I'd built in an external enforcement mechanism. End the list with a question about what would actually count as evidence that she's wrong and the sophisticated-choice people had it right all along.
