Me and my friend Dev got into it pretty deep last night after our philosophy seminar over Avicenna's flying man argument (the one from the Shifa, in the section on the soul) -- you know, where he has you imagine a fully grown adult created instantaneously in midair, blindfolded, with arms and legs spread out so they don't even touch each other or the rest of the body, no sound, no sight, basically zero sensory input of any kind. Avicenna's claim is that even like that, this person would still immediately be aware that "I exist," aware of their own self, without being aware of having a body at all -- and he uses that to argue the soul/self must be a separate, immaterial thing from the body, not just a bundle of sensations. Dev kept insisting its basically just a medieval cogito, and I said no, Descartes is doing something different (finding what survives radical doubt), while Avicenna's whole move is isolating self-awareness from body-awareness specifically to prove the soul isn't physical.

Anyway around 1am we somehow ended up arguing about whether the argument says anything about AI. Like, if you can supposedly be fully self-aware while totally cut off from any body or senses, would Avicenna look at something like a language model -- no body, no senses in any human sense, but perfectly capable of outputting "I think" or "I exist" -- and conclude that, by his own logic, such a thing could in principle have the same kind of immediate self-awareness as the flying man? Or would he say the comparison falls apart completely, and if so, exactly where, given how he actually built the argument? Neither of us could settle it and Dev still thinks I'm wrong, so I want a more careful outside take: what would Avicenna's actual verdict be here, and what specifically in his account of self-awareness and the soul would drive that verdict?
