An old friend from my graduate school days, who left academic philosophy years ago to train as a couples' therapist, mentioned a case to me last week that I have not been able to stop turning over in my mind since. I have spent most of my career teaching ethics at a fairly abstract level, so it was a useful jolt to be asked, in effect, to apply it to somebody's actual marriage.

The couple in question have been married for just over a decade, have two young children, and both describe themselves as genuinely devoted to one another; but the physical side of the marriage has cooled over the years, and each has separately admitted, to my friend at least, to being drawn to other people. They are now debating whether to loosen the terms of their marriage to allow for occasional outside involvements, and the disagreement between them seems to run deeper than the practical question of what to actually do. The wife resists the idea, not on religious or reputational grounds, but because she treats her own surge of jealousy at the very prospect as trustworthy evidence: if she really loved and trusted her husband as a wife should, she reasons, the thought would not bother her, so the fact that it does tells her something is wrong with the plan. Her husband takes the opposite view. He regards jealousy as nothing more than an inherited social reflex, no more authoritative than an old taboo, and thinks two sufficiently rational, secure adults ought to be able to reason their way past it entirely, much as one reasons oneself out of a superstition, once they see that it serves no real purpose.

My friend suspects both of them are being too quick, but could not quite say why when she raised it with me, and I found I could do little better on the spot, beyond a vague sense that each is treating jealousy as a simpler kind of thing than it probably is. I would like to think the matter through properly before I write back to her, and would rather do so from first principles than by consulting whatever the current popular literature on open relationships has to say. Is the wife right to treat her jealousy as a reliable signal of how much she loves him? Is the husband right to treat it as mere conditioning that reason alone ought to be able to dissolve? And if neither has quite got it right, what would a more defensible view actually say about how much weight their jealousy deserves, and about what, if anything, a couple in their position owes each other and their children while they work this out?
