I have no formal background in philosophy or the history of mathematics, but in my own reading I've become fascinated by Thomas Hobbes's long feud with John Wallis over Hobbes's supposed geometric proofs, including several attempts at squaring the circle that Wallis and other mathematicians repeatedly showed to be flawed — criticisms Hobbes never once accepted. What strikes me is how tightly that stubbornness is bound up with Hobbes's deeper conviction that geometry is the one science of which human beings can have perfect certainty, precisely because we invent its definitions and construct its figures ourselves, whereas natural philosophy only ever yields hypotheses about causes we did not make. Two later developments bear on that conviction, though Hobbes could not have known of either: in 1882 Ferdinand von Lindemann proved that pi is transcendental, finally settling, in Wallis's favor, that circle quadrature by compass and straightedge is impossible; and, decades before that, mathematicians had already shown that fully consistent non-Euclidean geometries exist, so Euclid's geometry cannot be the only logically possible one. Would either discovery actually threaten the argument that geometry's certainty rests on our authorship of its own definitions, or does that argument escape both essentially unscathed, even as Hobbes's personal reputation does not? And given what his conduct in the Wallis affair suggests about his temperament, would he ultimately have conceded the point about the circle, or would he have simply dug in, the way he did back then?
