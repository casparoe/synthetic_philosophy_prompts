So I went down a rabbit hole a while back reading old LessWrong threads about Roko's basilisk, specifically the decision-theory stuff that's supposed to be doing the actual work (timeless decision theory, then later updateless/functional decision theory). Whenever this topic comes up with my friends everyone just does the "lol Pascal's mugging for nerds" dismissal, which always felt kind of unsatisfying to me since it doesnt really engage with the specific TDT/UDT machinery that made people take the argument seriously in the first place — it just treats the whole thing as generically low-probability-high-stakes reasoning gone wrong, which feels like it's dodging the actual mechanism.

Here's the argument I want you to make instead: that the basilisk is undermined from the inside, by the very decision theory it's supposed to rely on, rather than by some outside appeal to small probabilities or general Pascal's-mugging suspicion. My understanding is that UDT/FDT-style agents are specifically supposed to precommit (in a "logical time" sense, prior to knowing the details of any particular threat) to not caving to blackmail or extortion, exactly because being the kind of agent who caves makes you a magnet for more of it. So I want you to argue that a future superintelligence reasoning this way would have no decision-theoretic incentive to actually carry out the "torture everyone who didn't help build me" policy against agents who are themselves appropriately updateless, and that this means the basilisk fails on its own theoretical premises rather than just being improbable. Get as into the weeds as you need to on acausal trade, logical time, or commitment races to make the argument work, and please flag anywhere you think a basilisk-sympathizer could push back on it.
