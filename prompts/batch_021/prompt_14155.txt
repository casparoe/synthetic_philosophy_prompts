A couple weeks ago a coworker said something like "if Sam doesn't get the promotion, she's quitting," and I made the mistake of saying "well whatever that sentence means, its probability just has to be the probability she quits given she doesn't get it" -- because I'm an actuary and that's genuinely how my brain parses conditional statements. She pushed back hard, and me being me, I couldn't let it go, so I went down a rabbit hole and landed on David Lewis's triviality results from the 70s, which afaik prove you basically can't have indicative conditionals be ordinary true-or-false propositions AND have their probability equal the conditional probability, except in degenerate cases where the probabilities collapse down to almost nothing distinct. Now it's genuinely messing with how I think about stuff at my actual job, not just some argument by the coffee machine anymore.

What I can't get a handle on from the survey articles is how anyone still holds onto conditionals being real propositions after that result (I gather the Stalnaker-ish camp tries to), versus people who just say fine, they're not truth-apt at all, and "the probability of a conditional" is really just assertability tracking conditional probability (the Adams/Edgington move) -- and each side clearly thinks the other is dodging something. Could you write me a dialogue between a truth-conditional theorist and a "conditionals aren't propositions" theorist actually hashing this out? I want the truth-conditional person to engage with why the triviality proof doesn't just sink their view rather than hand-waving past it, and I want the other person pushed on the classic embedding problem -- like how do you make sense of "it's not true that if Sam doesn't get the promotion, she's quitting" if there's no proposition there to negate. I'd like a genuinely close fight where it's not obvious by the end who's got the better of it, not one side just being a mouthpiece for the "clearly correct" view.
