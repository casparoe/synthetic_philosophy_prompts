Compare Larry Wright's etiological (selected-effects) account of biological function with Robert Cummins's causal-role account. Cummins's view is often charged with being too permissive: since a trait's function is just whatever it contributes to some capacity of a containing system relative to a chosen analysis, and the theory itself does not say which analysis is correct, even an intuitively accidental effect -- such as the sound a beating heart makes -- can qualify as a function relative to some system-level analysis (say, one built around a physician's diagnostic practice). Wright's view avoids this by grounding function in selection history, but faces a mirror-image problem with exaptation: a structure co-opted for a genuinely new use seems to acquire that function as soon as it starts helping, well before enough selection could have occurred to explain the structure's current presence in etiological terms. Even after long co-option, moreover, it is unclear whether Wright's original schema privileges the trait's new role over its ancestral one, since it never specifies how much selective history counts toward explaining why the trait is there. On balance, which account handles these two problems better, and can either be repaired without simply borrowing the resources of its rival? Answer by engaging with the actual mechanics of each theory, not merely the pretheoretic verdicts they were designed to capture.
