I write a column on ideas and arguments for a general but philosophically literate readership, and the next installment takes on Fitch's paradox of knowability. Before I file it, I would like your comments on the draft below — not a line edit, but a check on whether the argument actually holds together and whether I have treated the main rejoinder to it fairly.

Here is the draft:

Popular science writing loves a result that puts a hard boundary on what we can know — Gödel's theorem, Heisenberg's uncertainty principle, the interior of a black hole. Fitch's paradox of knowability, a much less famous piece of reasoning from 1963, works differently and is in some ways more unsettling: it doesn't identify any particular fact as unknowable. It shows that the thesis "every truth is in principle knowable" is nearly impossible to assert without absurdity, and it does so in a handful of steps of ordinary modal logic — no physics, no arithmetic, no self-reference of the Gödelian kind.

Start with a claim many verificationists and semantic anti-realists want to make: that truth and knowability never really come apart, so that for any true proposition p, it is at least possible for someone to know p. Call this KP. Add a premise almost nobody denies: that we are not collectively omniscient, so at least one truth is not actually known by anyone, ever (call this NO).

Fitch's trick is to apply KP not to some ordinary fact but to the specific truth that NO guarantees exists: "p is true, and nobody knows it," for whichever unknown p you like. KP says even this conjunction must be possibly known. But knowing a conjunction means knowing both conjuncts, so knowing "p, and nobody knows p" would require knowing p and knowing that nobody knows p; and since knowledge is factive, knowing that nobody knows p entails that nobody knows p — flatly contradicting the fact that you, in that moment, know p. So the conjunction "p and p is unknown" can never be known, for any p at all, purely as a matter of its logical form. Run that result back through KP, and the only way to avoid contradiction is to deny that any such conjunction is ever true — that is, to deny NO. Assume every truth is knowable, and you can prove every truth is actually known.

Since almost nobody wants to defend actual omniscience, the anti-realist has to give up KP in its unrestricted form. The standard move, associated with Neil Tennant and, on somewhat different grounds, Michael Dummett, is to restrict KP to what Tennant calls Cartesian propositions — roughly, propositions for which the very idea of knowing them isn't already self-defeating — and to concede that KP was never meant to cover instances like "p and p is unknown" in the first place. Critics reply that this restriction was custom-built to survive one proof and was never independently motivated, so it trades a paradox for an ad hoc patch.

This isn't only a dispute about theories of truth for its own sake. Any account that cashes out truth, or the right answer, as whatever some idealized process — verification, warranted assertibility, a sufficiently long chain of argument, even an ideal debate between AI systems in front of a judge — would eventually certify is making a KP-shaped commitment, and inherits a KP-shaped problem the moment "certifiable in principle" and "actually certified" come apart.

I'd welcome comments on three things in particular: whether the reconstruction of the proof in the third paragraph is actually valid, or whether I've smuggled in something beyond factivity and knowledge distributing over conjunction; whether the fourth paragraph is fair to Tennant and, especially, to Dummett, whose response I suspect I've compressed too far; and whether the closing analogy to AI debate schemes illuminates the point or just borrows Fitch's authority for an unrelated worry.
