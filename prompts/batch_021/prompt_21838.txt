I've spent the last few weeks reading my way into formal epistemology for a piece on how philosophers think about rational self-trust across time, and I keep circling back to Bas van Fraassen's Reflection Principle. As I understand it from his original 1984 paper "Belief and the Will," the idea is that your current credence in a proposition, conditional on the supposition that your credence in it at some later time will be a particular value r, should itself equal r — otherwise a sufficiently clever bookie can construct a diachronic Dutch book against you, extracting a guaranteed loss purely from the mismatch between your present and anticipated future opinions. Stated that way, it sounds like little more than a rational agent trusting her own future judgment, which seems almost too innocuous to reject.

What I can't shake, though, is the family of counterexamples that turn on anticipated non-evidential belief change: cases where you know today that your credence tomorrow will shift not because you'll have learned something, but because you'll have taken a memory-wiping drug, been hypnotized, or simply forgotten the evidence you currently have. In those cases, deferring now to your anticipated future credence feels less like epistemic humility and more like pre-emptively discounting your own better-informed judgment in favor of noise — yet the Dutch book argument, taken at face value, doesn't seem to care whether the future shift comes from learning or from tampering. I've even wondered whether something like the Sleeping Beauty problem, where credence changes upon waking without any apparent new evidence about the coin, might be a disguised version of the same difficulty.

Could you write me a dialogue between two formal epistemologists, talking over drinks after a workshop on self-locating belief, who disagree sharply about whether Reflection survives this family of cases? One should hold that the principle, suitably understood, is basically correct and that the counterexamples either fail on inspection or motivate only a narrow, principled restriction; the other should hold that any restriction strong enough to handle the counterexamples empties the Dutch book argument of the work it was supposed to do, since it now needs an independent notion of "legitimate" updating to tell genuine learning apart from mere tampering. I'd like them to really engage with each other — responding to specific moves rather than repeating their opening positions — and to get into the actual mechanics of the Dutch book argument and at least one concrete case, rather than resting on slogans about humility or self-respect. Neither should have an easy time of it; they should both clearly know the literature cold.
