And here's the student's answer:

(a) Wilson's worry is that positing Big-G Grounding on top of all the small-g relations we already need (realization, composition, determination, etc.) is uneconomical -- once determinates are fixing their determinables and parts are composing wholes, a further generic grounding relation over and above these doesn't buy any extra explanatory power, it's just extra ontology for no payoff. So by Occam's razor we should cut it and let the small-g relations do all the work themselves.

(b) No -- rejecting a single Big-G relation doesn't automatically force you to give up a univocal notion of fundamentality. You can still define "fundamental" disjunctively across the small-g relations: x is fundamental iff x isn't on the dependent side of realization, composition, determination, set-membership, or any of the others. Whether that disjunctive definition counts as genuinely "unified" in the sense grounding theorists usually want is a further question, but it isn't ruled out just because there's no single relation doing the work.

(c) Fine treats grounding as a relation that can hold between facts or entities generally, similar to how causation relates events, which is why he's comfortable saying one object can ground another. Schaffer, by contrast, builds grounding out of a primitive sentential operator, "because," which combines with whole sentences the way "and" or "if" does rather than with singular terms. Since an operator can only take sentences as inputs, this is part of why Schaffer's framework gets read as fact-only, while Fine's relational approach leaves room for grounding to relate objects and properties too.
