I sit on the three-person adjudication panel of a small private foundation that funds independent researchers through multi-year fellowships — the sort of small, EA-adjacent grantmaking body that has proliferated around the rationalist world in recent years — and something that happened at our most recent meeting has been nagging at me ever since.

We were deciding whether to claw back the remaining funds from a grantee whose project had gone badly off track. Under our internal rules, we may only revoke funding if two separate conditions both hold: first, that her actual work departed substantially from the research plan she originally submitted, and second, that the departure was avoidable rather than attributable to circumstances genuinely outside her control (a key collaborator withdrawing, an irreplaceable dataset becoming unavailable, and so on). When we worked through the case question by question, a majority of the panel — two of the three of us — concluded that the work had indeed departed substantially from the plan, and a majority — a different pair, as it turned out — concluded separately that the departure had been avoidable. Since both conditions were satisfied by majority vote, it seemed we were bound by our own rule to revoke the funding. But then our chair, almost as an afterthought, asked each of us simply to declare yes or no on revocation itself, and it turned out that only one of us actually favored revoking it; the other two, despite each having sided with the majority on one of the two questions, privately believed the other question came out the opposite way, and so both voted no. The straight up-or-down tally came out two to one against revocation — flatly contradicting what the issue-by-issue tally had implied.

We ended up going with the up-or-down vote, but it has stayed with me that we could apparently have reached the opposite, equally "democratic" outcome just by asking the questions in a different order. It has the flavor of the impossibility-result posts I've come across over the years on LessWrong and Overcoming Bias, but I can't place exactly which one, if any. I described the episode to a friend who is deep into voting theory, and his first reaction was that it sounded like some relative of Arrow's impossibility theorem, though that never quite felt right to me, since none of us were ranking alternatives — we were just voting yes or no on a couple of separate questions and then on a bottom line that was supposed to follow from them. Is there an actual, recognized phenomenon at work here? And if so, does the literature on it suggest any principled way either to decide which of the two tallies should have governed, or to design the vote in advance so that this kind of split can't arise — or is it simply confused to think one of the two outcomes was more "correct" than the other?
