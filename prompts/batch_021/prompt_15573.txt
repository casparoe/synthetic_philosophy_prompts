I completed an MA in philosophy of mind before moving, only a few months ago, into a research engineering role on an interpretability team, and a disagreement from this week's reading group is still bothering me. We were discussing recent probing work that tries to identify a direction or subspace in a language model's internal activations that tracks something like "what the model actually takes to be true," independently of whatever it has been prompted, fine-tuned, or instructed to output. One of the senior researchers dismissed the whole project, invoking Dennett's intentional stance and his "Real Patterns" paper in particular: since Dennett treats belief-talk as being on a par with talk of an object's center of gravity — a calculational device we are licensed to use because it earns its keep predictively, not a discrete thing that is "really" located anywhere inside the system — she argued that hunting for a computational correlate of "the model's actual belief" is a category mistake. On her reading, once the intentional stance is doing its predictive work there is simply no further fact about what a system really believes for a probe to discover, so treating a probe's output as revealing the model's real, hidden belief just mistakes a stance-relative shorthand for a natural kind.

Everyone else in the room seemed to find this decisive, and for a while I did too, but it has kept nagging at me, partly because "Real Patterns" is a paper I remember as arguing for the reality of exactly this kind of higher-level pattern rather than against it. I would like to work a paragraph addressing this into an internal note I am drafting on the philosophical assumptions behind our probing work, so I want to get the underlying philosophy right rather than just recycle a rebuttal that merely sounds plausible. Could you help me think through whether this Dennettian objection to belief-probing is actually sound — whether the intentional stance really does rule out looking for an internal correlate of belief in the way my colleague suggested — and, if the argument does not hold up, where exactly it goes wrong?
