I'm researching a piece on how early-twentieth-century thinkers pictured life after scarcity, and I keep circling back to an unresolved tension inside Keynes's own thinking about what makes a life good. As a young Cambridge Apostle, Keynes was devoted to the final chapter of G. E. Moore's *Principia Ethica*, where Moore argues that personal affection and the contemplation of beautiful things are, by a wide margin, the most valuable things life has to offer; in "My Early Beliefs" Keynes recalls how he and his friends adopted this as something close to a private religion, while quietly setting aside Moore's separate, far more cautious chapter on why ordinary conduct should still be governed by rule and convention rather than by direct appeal to the ideal. Yet in "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren," the older, more economically-minded Keynes sounds much less confident that anyone besides a gifted few could actually live by that ideal even once material want had been abolished: he worries about a bred-in human "purposiveness," predicts that most people will handle real leisure badly, and even suggests that avarice, usury, and the love of money will have to go on being treated as virtues for a while yet, precisely because they are not good in Moore's sense but are still doing necessary psychological work.

I'd like to see this tension turned into a dialogue rather than left as description. Could you write an exchange, maybe ten to fourteen turns, between Moore and this older Keynes — imagine them meeting not long after Keynes wrote "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren" — in which each presses on the specific thing that actually troubles him about the other's position, rather than trading generic lines about pleasure versus work? I'd want Keynes to turn Moore's own neglected chapter on conduct back against him, asking whether Moore's book had already conceded that most people need rules and useful fictions rather than unmediated pursuit of the ideal, and if so, whether that concession quietly undercuts the confidence his students once placed in the youthful religion; and I'd want Moore to have a real answer ready, for instance by insisting on a sharp line between what is intrinsically good and what human psychology merely happens to tolerate, rather than simply folding under the objection. Neither should give in too easily — I'm genuinely curious whether their disagreement resolves into a real philosophical impasse, or turns out to rest on a confusion that a bit more precision could dissolve.
