I served on a jury for the first time last month, in a burglary trial, and something that happened during deliberations has stayed with me longer than the verdict itself. About two hours in, one juror who had decided on the defendant's guilt from the opening statements argued that we shouldn't bother re-examining a forensic report the defense had introduced late in the trial, one that seemed to complicate the timeline the prosecution had presented. His reasoning was that he already knew, from the witness testimony and the defendant's shifting alibi, that the defendant was guilty, and that if he genuinely knew this, then any report suggesting otherwise had to reflect a mishandled sample or a lab error, so there was no real point in poring over it again. Several of us objected that this was exactly the sort of thing a jury is supposed to weigh with an open mind, but he wasn't moved, and observed, not unreasonably, that "keep an open mind" is an instruction, not an argument against his.

When I brought this up with friends over dinner a few nights later, one of them said he was obviously just being overconfident and left it at that, but this doesn't quite capture what has kept me turning his argument over in my head since. On reflection, his reasoning isn't simply a failure of open-mindedness: if he genuinely did know the defendant was guilty, and knowledge requires truth, then it does seem to follow that any evidence pointing the other way would have to be misleading in some way, which would make discounting it in advance a matter of taking his own knowledge seriously rather than mere stubbornness. Yet that can hardly be the end of the matter, since the same move would let anyone sufficiently confident about anything dismiss all future evidence against it without a glance, which seems like a poor way to run a jury, or for that matter, to hold any belief responsibly. I would like help getting clear on whether there is a genuine flaw in this pattern of reasoning, and, if so, exactly where it breaks down.
