A friend of mine, who is finishing a doctorate in evolutionary biology, and I spent most of a long dinner last week arguing about the units-of-selection debate, and neither of us walked away satisfied. Her position is essentially the hard Dawkins line: once you have inclusive fitness theory and Hamilton's rule to explain apparently altruistic or group-beneficial traits, talk of group selection is at best a redundant vocabulary and at worst a relic of the Wynne-Edwards-style thinking that Williams demolished in the 1960s. I pushed back with what I remembered of the Sober-and-Wilson-style revival of multilevel selection theory, and in particular with the claim — which I associate with the Price-equation literature, and which I understand Hamilton himself half-conceded already in the 1970s — that kin selection and multilevel selection are, at least in the additive case, formally equivalent ways of partitioning one and the same evolutionary change, rather than rival empirical hypotheses about what is really happening in nature. Neither of us could actually reconstruct why that equivalence is supposed to hold, or say anything precise about what happens to it once social interactions stop being additive and become synergistic, which I gather is where things get genuinely messy again.

What I would like from you is the explanation we couldn't give each other at the table. Concretely: given that both sides already grant that individual organisms are, in some banal sense, the obvious locus of selection, what exactly does the multilevel Price equation partition into within-group and between-group components, and why does recovering Hamilton's rule from that partition count as evidence that gene-level and group-level selection talk are describing one causal process rather than two competing ones? Then, what precisely breaks, or has to be patched, once fitness effects stop being additive — does the equivalence just get harder to compute, or does it fail in some more principled way that would let a committed group selectionist claim genuine, non-notational ground? And finally, once all of that is on the table, is there anything substantive left for my friend and me to actually disagree about, or does the whole dispute dissolve into a preference between equally adequate formalisms?
