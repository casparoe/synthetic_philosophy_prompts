I am drafting a scene for a historical novel set in Copenhagen not long after Denmark's 1849 constitution, in which two minor characters fall into an argument at a café: a young theology graduate steeped in Kierkegaard's polemic in Two Ages (specifically its closing section, usually excerpted on its own as "The Present Age"), and a newspaper editor who regards the new freedom of the press as the very engine of the era's political maturity. Please write their exchange as a philosophical dialogue of around 600–800 words.

The graduate's case should track the actual shape of Kierkegaard's argument rather than the vague charge that he "distrusted the media": the distinction between a public and an actual people, generation, or congregation; the claim that the press manufactures and sustains this abstraction while no one within it can be held responsible for what it says; and "leveling" as a diagnosis of a reflective, passionless age rather than a simple complaint about democracy. The editor should meet this with an equally serious, independent case for why a large modern polity needs exactly the kind of impersonal public Kierkegaard distrusts, and should also press the graduate on a genuine tension: Kierkegaard's own later resort to a self-published broadsheet, The Moment, to reach that very public. I want the graduate to offer a substantive answer to this rather than a dodge, and I want both characters to keep responding to what the other has just said rather than simply restating their opening positions.
