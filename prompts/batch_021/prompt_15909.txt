I am finalizing the lecture for my upper-level undergraduate course on the ethics of war, which this term pairs Anscombe's "Mr. Truman's Degree" with "War and Murder." As I have been presenting it to students, Anscombe's verdict that Truman was guilty of murder is essentially an application of the proportionality condition within the doctrine of double effect: whatever military value Hiroshima and Nagasaki possessed, the resulting civilian death toll so grossly outweighed it that no serious defender of double effect could count those deaths as proportionate collateral damage, and it is this failure of proportionality that, on her view, turns the bombings from tragic-but-licit side-effect killing into outright murder. A colleague in the law faculty, who assigns the same pamphlet in her course on the law of armed conflict, reads the argument the same way, which had made me fairly confident I was not off base. Then a graduate TA raised an eyebrow at the reconstruction last week without quite being able to say why, which is partly why I am asking you before I commit to it in front of the whole lecture hall.

Given that reconstruction, I would like help with two things before Thursday's session. First, could you draft a discussion question that walks students through roughly how disproportionate a civilian death toll has to become before double effect stops excusing it as collateral damage, using Hiroshima and Nagasaki as the worked example? Second, I want to carry the same proportionality framework straight into the following week's unit on nuclear deterrence and countervalue targeting: could you sketch how the argument should extend, so that deliberately threatening disproportionate civilian destruction comes out looking even harder to justify, on Anscombe's principles, than the bombings themselves did?
