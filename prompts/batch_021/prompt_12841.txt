Since retiring after thirty years as a research chemist, I have only recently taken up philosophy of religion, and one piece I've been mulling over is David Lewis's short paper "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" -- a rather different sort of substitution from the one I spent my career on. The paper concerns the doctrine that Christ was punished in the sinner's place, and it takes seriously the objection that punishment, unlike a debt, cannot simply be handed off to an innocent party, because punishment presupposes the wrongdoer's own desert in a way a sum of money does not. Lewis tries to see whether the doctrine can be rescued by asking whether what really needs to be transferred is the penalty rather than the punishment proper, and whether the case for transfer looks stronger once punishment's point is taken to be deterrence rather than desert. Please write a dialogue between two philosophers of religion talking over coffee after a workshop session: one has just delivered a paper reconstructing Lewis's rescue attempt and accuses the other of refusing to test the objection against real cases; the other maintains that no amount of case-work touches the logical point, since whatever Lewis's distinction succeeds in transferring, it is not punishment in the sense the doctrine needs. Have them work through at least one invented case of a substitute bearing another's rightful punishment, have the skeptic press on whether the defender is covertly trading retributivism for a deterrence theory of punishment just to make the doctrine come out coherent, and end with the two of them trying to establish whether what divides them is a real disagreement about the metaphysics of desert or only a dispute over how strictly to define the word "punishment."
