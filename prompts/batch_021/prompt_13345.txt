So this happened after workshop last week and it's been rattling around in my head alot since. Someone in my writing group read a scene where an elderly woman keeps calling her dead son's old cell number just to hear his voicemail greeting one more time -- and yeah, half the table got choked up, me included, before anyone even got to feedback. Afterwards at the bar, two people from the group, Marcus and Dana, ended up in a genuine argument about what had actually just happened to us, and I liked it enough that I wrote down the gist of it because I wanted a second opinion from someone who thinks about this stuff for a living (that's not me, I just write the stuff, I don't think about why it works).

(1) Marcus opened by saying whatever we felt in there wasn't real grief, more like a fire drill -- his evidence being that nobody did anything, nobody reached for a phone or even really moved, and if a woman had told us this actually happened to her that same night we'd have reacted somehow, so whatever that lump-in-the-throat thing was, it must've been a rehearsal, not the real article. (2) Dana said that test proves too much, since we don't do anything when we read a strangers obituary either, and nobody thinks that grief is fake just because we stay in our chairs. (3) Marcus said the two cases aren't parallel: with the obituary you believe the person existed and is now dead, full stop; with the woman in the story you know the entire time there's no son, no voicemail, nobody -- so there's no object there for real grief to even attach itself to, and belief in the object was exactly the ingredient missing. (4) Dana shot back that belief can't be the necessary ingredient, because you can produce the exact same reaction just by vividly imagining, purely as a hypothetical, your own kid's voice on an old answering machine -- no claim that it's true, not even a little -- and you'll still feel your throat tighten; if merely entertaining a thought is enough to get the physical reaction going, belief was never doing the work to begin with. (5) Marcus said that concession actually hands him the whole argument: if the real-belief case and the merely-imagined case get you to the same feeling by two different routes, that's precisely the distinction he's been drawing -- a state produced with no belief that anything exists isn't the same kind of state as full-blown grief, no matter how alike it feels from the inside, same way a stage gun isn't a real gun just because it's the same shape and weight in your hand.

Neither of them gave an inch and I went home more confused than when I'd arrived, plus a little worried about how much of my own job apparently runs on tricking people's nervous systems.
