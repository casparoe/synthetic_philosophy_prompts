My gran (we called her Gogo, she was Xhosa, from a small town near Mthatha) passed away on Sunday, and because apparently I'm "good with words" for a software engineer, I've been asked to give the eulogy at her funeral this weekend. Here's what's freaking me out: Gogo was the person who actually taught us grandkids about ubuntu — not the watered-down "be nice to people" version, but the real thing, and she'd get geniunely annoyed if you got it wrong. I remember my aunt once told her "you built this whole family up yourself, Ma" as a compliment, and Gogo pushed back hard, said the sentence didn't even make sense on her terms, because there's no separate "herself" sitting behind all those relationships that could've built anything alone — she was adamant that the self doesn't come first and then enter into relationships, the relationships are what there is. So I know for a fact the eulogy can't be the standard "she was such a strong independent woman who did it all" thing, even though that's honestly the default template for eulogies about tough old ladies, and several people are absolutely going to expect that version.

The audience is split in a way that's stressing me out. Half the room grew up with this stuff and live it every day, so if I stand up and explain "umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu, a person is a person through other persons" (or however that's actually spelled, I've seen it a couple different ways) like it's a fun fact I googled, I'm going to sound like an idiot lecturing my own family about their own culture at their own mother's funeral. But my partner is flying in from London and has never heard any of this, plus a couple of my work friends are coming, so I definately can't just assume everyone's already on the same page either. And then there's a deeper wrinkle I fell into during a 1am google spiral: apparently there's real disagreement among African philosophers about how far to push this idea — I think it was a guy named Menkiti arguing personhood is basically earned over a lifetime through your relationships and community standing, so a newborn isn't fully a person yet, versus another philosopher, Gyekye maybe, who thought that goes too far and there's still something there in a person from the start, before community gets to work on them. I have no idea how you'd even gesture at that tension in front of a grieving family without it sounding like you're saying my dead grandmother "achieved" personhood, like some kind of unlockable trophy, which she would have found hilarious but which would also get me quietly disowned by my mom.

Could you actually write the eulogy for me? It needs to comfort a room of grieving people, land for both halves of that audience without either lecturing the family or losing my partner, and it needs to be built around this relational idea of personhood structurally — shaping what the eulogy even is, not just dropping the proverb in as a nice quote — without turning into a philosophy lecture or saying anything Gogo would've rolled her eyes at.
