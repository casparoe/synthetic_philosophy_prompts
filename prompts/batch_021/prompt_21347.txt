So I've been teaching the Judy Benjamin case in my formal epistemology seminar this week -- the soldier lost somewhere in a region split into Red and Blue territory, which is also cut the other way into a "Headquarters Company" half and a "Second Company" half, so there's four quadrants total. She starts out with a flat prior over the four quadrants, then gets a garbled radio message that only says: if she's in Red territory, its 3-to-1 that she's in the HQ half rather than the Second Company half. Most of my students shrugged and said "fine, just update within Red and leave Blue alone," but that's not actually what you get if you update by minimizing cross-entropy (relative information) against the flat prior subject to that one conditional constraint -- the HQ/Second split within Blue moves too, even though the message never says a single word about Blue. That's van Fraassen's original point, and it's the reason I don't think "just be a good Bayesian about it" is the free win my students assume it is.

I'd like you to write me a dialogue between two formal epistemologists hashing this out at a whiteboard after a colloquium talk, maybe 700-1000 words. One of them defends minimizing cross-entropy as the uniquely correct generalization of conditionalization to this kind of evidence, and has to actually answer the "why does Blue move at all" objection rather than dodge it. The other is convinced the Blue-side shift is a symptom of something arbitrary baked into the method (e.g. a hidden dependence on how you carve the space into "atoms") and wants a more surgical update rule that leaves anything the message doesn't touch alone -- but then has to say what that rule actually is without it collapsing into ad-hocery of its own. Don't let either of them be a pushover: I want real back-and-forth where each move gets a real counter-move, and I want them actually arguing about the structure of this specific four-way partition rather than trading generic slogans about conservatism or minimal change.
