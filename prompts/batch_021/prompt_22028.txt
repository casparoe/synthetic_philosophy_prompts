I've spent some time working through Hume's essays on economics on my own, especially "Of Money" and "Of the Balance of Trade," and one argument keeps nagging at me: his price-specie-flow mechanism. Under a gold and silver standard, he argued, a trade surplus draws in specie, which raises domestic prices, which then erodes the very competitiveness that produced the surplus in the first place, so the imbalance corrects itself without anyone needing to intervene. On this basis he thought mercantilist attempts to permanently hoard gold through trade policy were self-defeating.

No country has used specie as money for decades now, exchange rates float freely, and the United States has run large, persistent trade deficits almost continuously since the mid-1970s without anything resembling the correction Hume describes. What do you think he would make of this? Would he conclude that his mechanism simply stopped applying once money was detached from a physical commodity, or would he insist some equivalent adjustment is still happening, just relocated to exchange rates or capital flows rather than specie and prices? I'm also curious whether he'd have anything to say about how the dollar's status as the world's reserve currency seems to let the US dodge that adjustment altogether.
