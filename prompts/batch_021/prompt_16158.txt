Ok so I had this whole argument with my friend last night about higher-order theories of consciousness -- her point was that if a higher-order thought can misrepresent its target, you could get a HOT with literally no target at all, and the theory still has to call that phenomenally conscious, which seems nuts. Turns out its not just a bar argument: I'm on my hospital's clinical ethics committee, and this week's case turns on almost exactly that. Patient is months out from a hypoxic injury, meets behavioral criteria for unresponsive wakefulness syndrome (vegetative state), but on an Owen-style imagery fMRI task (imagine playing tennis vs. imagine walking through your house) she shows healthy-control-like activity in motor and parahippocampal areas -- with basically nothing in prefrontal cortex. Half the team reads that as good evidence she's conscious and maybe able to suffer, so we should increase analgesia and slow down the family's push to scale back care; the other half -- including a neurologist who's committed to higher-order theories -- says following instructions on an imagery task doesn't establish there's an actual higher-order thought about that state, so no prefrontal activity means we shouldn't infer consciousness at all. Can you draft the short memo for Thursday's meeting: one clear recommendation with a timeframe, that seriously engages the strongest objection from whichever side you don't land on, instead of just shrugging that 'theories disagree'?
