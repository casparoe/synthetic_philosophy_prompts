I'm finalizing the reading-group session for my graduate seminar on classical Indian epistemology, and one sūtra from the pramāṇa section of Gautama's Nyāya-sūtra has been giving me more trouble than I expected, so I'd like a second opinion before I stand in front of the room. Sūtra 1.1.4 gives the school's canonical definition of perception (pratyakṣa): "indriyārthasannikarṣotpannam jñānam avyapadeśyam avyabhicāri vyavasāyātmakaṃ pratyakṣam" — word by word, a cognition (jñānam) produced by the contact of a sense organ with its object (indriyārthasannikarṣotpannam) counts as perception (pratyakṣam) just in case it is avyapadeśya (not associated with, or not expressible by, a name), avyabhicāri (non-deviating, i.e., unerring), and vyavasāyātmaka (determinate, having the character of a settled judgment, as in "this is a cow").

What I can't get to sit comfortably together are the first and third of those qualifiers. A determinate cognition of the "this is a cow" variety seems, on the face of it, to already involve the universal cow-ness, and hence some implicit tie to the word "cow" — exactly the sort of thing avyapadeśya looks designed to rule out. Vātsyāyana's Bhāṣya tries to head this off mainly by using avyapadeśya to separate perception from testimony (śabda) rather than to strip perception of all conceptual content, but that has always struck me as more of a stipulation than a solution, and I know the apparent tension is exactly what later Naiyāyikas — and Buddhist critics like Dignāga and Dharmakīrti, arguing from outside the school — made a great deal of once the nirvikalpaka/savikalpaka (indeterminate/determinate) distinction got worked out in more detail.

Could you help me think this through with enough rigor that I can turn it into a one-page handout for the seminar? Specifically: (1) What is the most textually and philosophically defensible way of reading avyapadeśya and vyavasāyātmaka as jointly satisfiable in a single cognitive episode — is Gautama compressing two stages into one sūtra, is avyapadeśya doing far more modest work than "free of all conceptual content," or is there a better resolution somewhere in the commentarial tradition (Vātsyāyana, Jayanta Bhaṭṭa, Udayana, Gaṅgeśa)? And (2) why did this particular seam in the definition end up mattering so much to the wider Nyāya–Buddhist dispute over whether universals (sāmānya) can be objects of direct perception at all, rather than products of conceptual construction (kalpanā)? I would rather have your own reconstruction of the argument than another general summary of "the two-tiered theory of perception in Indian philosophy" — I can get that much from any handbook.
