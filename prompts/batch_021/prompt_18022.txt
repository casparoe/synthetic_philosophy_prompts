I spent four decades as an experimental physicist, and only in retirement have I turned to philosophy in earnest, beginning, fittingly, with a fellow scientist who made the same crossing: Alfred North Whitehead. Having worked through *The Aims of Education and Other Essays*, I keep returning to three of his commitments: his warning against "inert ideas" — facts and formulas received into the mind but never used, tested, or set into fresh combination; his account of the "rhythm of education," which moves cyclically through the stages of romance, precision, and generalization; and his claim, in "Universities and Their Function," that a university earns its name only by uniting the young and the old in the imaginative pursuit of knowledge still being made, not by transmitting settled facts, which could be done as well by correspondence. I would like your considered judgment of what Whitehead would have made of today's self-paced, algorithmically adaptive online degree programs, in which a student can complete a course of study through pre-recorded lectures and AI tutoring with little or no sustained contact with a researcher actively working in the field. Please ground your answer in the specific commitments above, rather than a generic verdict of mixed feelings, and state what changes, if any, he would have insisted on before such a program could count as genuine university education by his own standards.
