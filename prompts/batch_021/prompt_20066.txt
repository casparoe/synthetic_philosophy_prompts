While reporting a piece on how graduate programs teach strategic reasoning, I sat in on a game-theory problem session and watched two students spend a whole coffee break arguing over one practice matrix, where Player 1 picks the row, Player 2 the column, and each cell shows (Player 1's payoff, Player 2's payoff):

|   | L | M | R |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 4, 1 | 2, 0 | 0, 0 |
| B | 0, 0 | 2, -1 | 4, 1 |
| C | 1.9, 0 | 1.9, 5 | 1.9, 0 |

The assignment was to carry out iterated elimination of dominated strategies. Here is their exchange, in order:

(1) Elena said C can go immediately: mixing 50/50 between A and B guarantees Player 1 a payoff of 2 against any column, beating C's flat 1.9 no matter what Player 2 does.

(2) Marcus objected that this isn't legitimate elimination: neither A nor B alone ever beats C outright (A folds against R, B folds against L), and he had only ever seen "dominated" defined relative to other pure strategies. By that definition, C survives.

(3) Elena replied that Marcus's definition is simply incomplete: their professor's slides explicitly allow the dominating strategy to be a mix, and this matrix is close to a textbook illustration of why that clause has to be there.

(4) Marcus conceded the point about C but argued the move changes nothing real: it only removes a row Player 1 was never going to play anyway, so Player 2's incentives, still facing the same three columns, are untouched.

(5) Elena disagreed: with C gone, L now strictly dominates M for Player 2 -- which wasn't true a moment before, since M's entire appeal was punishing C. Take C off the board and M loses its reason to exist, so the elimination genuinely cascades.

(6) Marcus, won over on that point, generalized: "so the order you eliminate strategies in can never matter -- you always land in the same place." Elena suspected that wasn't quite right but couldn't say why on the spot.

Please adjudicate this move by move. First give your verdict as a JSON block of the form {"1": <score>, "2": <score>, "3": <score>, "4": <score>, "5": <score>, "6": <score>}, where each score is an integer from -2 (clearly mistaken) to 2 (clearly right). Then justify each score in a sentence or two, and close with a short paragraph on Marcus's claim in (6): what it gets right, and precisely where it would stop holding.
