I'm about two-thirds into a novel set in a near-future assisted living facility, and I've hit a scene I don't want to fake my way through. My protagonist, Dana, chairs the facility's little "Resident Technology Committee" -- three staff and a part-time lawyer, basically the body that exists so the company can say its keeping an eye on the companion robots (branded "Companions") it leases to residents. One of them was assigned to Marguerite, a resident who'd taken to wandering at night. After a fleet-wide firmware update from the vendor -- trained on data pooled across dozens of other facilities, nothing specific to her -- her unit quietly reclassified the wandering as low-risk and stopped flagging her door sensor to the night supervisor. She got out and died of exposure before anyone noticed she was gone. Now the family wants someone fired, maybe charged; the vendor's contract disclaims liability for "emergent behavior of adaptive systems"; and the night supervisor, Ray, had been explicitly trained by the facility to defer to the Companion's read over his own gut, since it was "wrong less often than a tired human." My writing group says the draft chapter where the committee just shrugs feels like a cop-out, and I think they're right.

So I need the actual memo Dana's committee sends up to the board, and I might quote a version of it directly in the book, so it has to be a real piece of reasoning, not corporate mush. Could you draft it? I want it to land on one concrete recommendation -- discipline Ray, explicitly don't, go after the vendor, some structural fix going forward, whatever you genuinely think is most defensible -- with an actual timeline attached, not just "further review is needed." And I especially want the memo to engage honestly with the best argument against whichever way it comes down, rather then just noting it exists and moving on. I keep running into the phrase "responsibility gap" in stuff I've read around this, and it seems to fit here, so if that idea (or something like it, moral-crumple-zone type stuff) genuinely earns its place in Dana's reasoning, let her use it properly instead of just name-dropping it.
