I'm a few years into a philosophy PhD (metaphysics side of things, causation and grounding), and something that happened at a dinner with some friends last weekend has been stuck in my head, mostly because it's basically a folk-version of a debate I keep running into in my own reading, except nobody at that table has ever heard of the literature. My friend Dev asked another friend, Mara, to water his enormous fig tree while he was out of town for three weeks; she forgot, and it was basically dead by the time he got back. When he told the story at dinner, half-joking, he said "yeah, she killed my tree," and Mara pushed back way harder then the joke really deserved.

Here's roughly how the exchange went. (1) Mara said she didn't kill anything — the tree died of thirst, and there's a real difference between causing a thing to happen and merely failing to prevent it. (2) Dev said that's a distinction without a difference: not watering it is exactly what killed it, same as not giving someone their insulin kills them. (3) Mara said okay, but then by that logic every other person on Earth who also didn't water that tree that week is equally a killer, since none of them watered it either, and that's absurd, so "not watering" can't be what's doing the causal work. (4) Dev said the difference is obvious: she'd specifically agreed to do it, she had the job, nobody else did. (5) Mara said that just shows she was the one responsible for the tree, not that she caused its death — you don't get to turn an obligation into a causal fact just because you want somebody to pin it on. (6) Dev brought up that courts convict people of negligent homicide for pure omissions all the time (a lifeguard who doesn't jump in, a parent who doesn't feed their kid), which is the law treating not-doing-something as a genuine cause of death, not a figure of speech. Mara's comeback was that legal liability is a bad guide to metaphysics, since courts assign blame for a bunch of practical reasons that have nothing to do with what actually caused what. It ended with Dev accusing her of hiding behind semantics because she felt guilty, and Mara saying the semantics is the whole issue.

Neither of them is a philosopher, and honestly I don't think either one is just being dense — I'd rather get an outside read than referee it myself, since I've read enough on this that I don't trust my own neutrality anymore. Could you go through moves (1) through (6) and say who comes out ahead at each one, not just who wins overall? Give me your verdict first as a JSON block like {"1": score, "2": score, ..., "6": score}, with each score an integer from -2 (badly wrong) to 2 (clearly right) for whoever made that move, then a couple sentences justifying each score. And if you think there's some option Mara and Dev are both overlooking that would handle the case better than either of their positions, I'd like to hear that too.
