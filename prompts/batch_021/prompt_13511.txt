Random one from grading medieval pol phil papers today. I keep telling students that Aquinas's line on tyrannicide is basically 'never take matters into your own hands -- deposing a ruler is for the public authority, and a private citizen who kills a tyrant is just committing sedition dressed up as justice' (mostly drawing on On Kingship). But it occured to me while marking: if legitimate authority for Aquinas is grounded in ruling for the common good, doesn't his own logic undercut that blanket ban -- a tyrant who rules purely for himself has already forfeited the very authority that would make killing him 'sedition' in the first place, no? Is that an actual tension in his view, or am I oversimplifying something he's more careful about?
