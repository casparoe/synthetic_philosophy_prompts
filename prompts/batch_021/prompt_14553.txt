"Williamson's knowledge norm says that you may assert p only if you know p. Since knowledge is just justified true belief, this means you're permitted to assert p exactly when you have a true, sufficiently justified belief that p. Williamson motivates the norm with lottery cases: even if I'm 99.9999% sure my ticket lost, it still sounds off for me to flatly assert 'your ticket lost,' because I don't know it, I only have very strong evidence.

The main rival is the justified (or 'reasonable') belief norm, which says you may assert p as long as your justified in believing p, whether or not you know it. Sutton and Turri defend this view, since it handles Gettier cases better: if I have a justified true belief that fails to be knowledge only because of some Gettier-style luck, the knowledge norm says I'm not permitted to assert it, which seems too strict, because from the inside my epistemic position is exactly as good as it would be if I did know.

Lackey's selfless assertion cases show that knowledge can't be the norm of assertion. Her example is a devoutly religious science teacher who personally disbelieves evolution but knows the evidence supports it, and asserts it to her class anyway. Since she doesn't believe what she asserts, but the assertion still seems totally proper, this shows that knowledge cannot be the norm of assertion, because she fails to satisfy it and yet asserts properly regardless."
