Ok, this has been bugging me for ages. Bertrand's paradox always gets trotted out on rationalist blogs as the classic case where perfectly reasonable symmetry/indifference reasoning, applied to one single well-posed question (probability that a random chord of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle), gives you three genuinely contradictory answers -- 1/3, 1/2, 1/4 -- depending on which symmetry you pick, with no principled way to say one is more correct then the others. Doesn't that basically sink the whole Jaynes-style project of grounding "objective" priors in invariance arguments? Like, if the method contradicts itself on a toy problem this clean, why do rationalist-sphere Bayesians still treat symmetry/invariance reasoning as a legit way to pin down priors for way messier real-world questions? Am I missing something here, or is this just a known sore spot that people quietly ignore?
