In a reading group my advisor co-runs — it mixes people working on mechanism design, program evaluation, and AI alignment, so terminology from all three fields comes up in the same conversation — we recently discussed the cardiac-surgery "report cards" introduced in the US in the 1990s: states began publishing risk-adjusted, surgeon-level mortality rates so that patients and hospitals could identify underperforming surgeons. Follow-up work suggested measured mortality fell partly because surgeons grew reluctant to operate on the sickest, highest-risk patients, who then struggled to find anyone willing to treat them. Two people in the group disagreed sharply about how to describe this.

Farah called it a clean case of Goodhart's Law: mortality was a decent proxy for surgical quality until it became a target, at which point it stopped being one.

Leo objected that this is really Campbell's Law, not Goodhart's. Goodhart, he said, was originally describing a statistical regularity — like the relationship between a monetary aggregate and inflation — breaking down under pressure, whereas here self-interested agents deliberately changed their behavior once they knew they were being scored. That is a different mechanism, closer to what's sometimes labeled "adversarial" gaming as opposed to the merely statistical, "regressional" kind, and collapsing the two erases a real distinction.

Farah answered that the division is too tidy: Goodhart's own 1975 point was precisely that an observed regularity collapses once pressure for control is placed on it, and that is exactly what happened here, via an optimizing agent changing its behavior in response to being measured. On her view that is not a rival phenomenon but a species of Goodhart's own, and Campbell simply redescribed it in a social-science idiom the following year.

Leo granted that her reading of the 1975 text is defensible, but said the lumping still costs something: a regressional problem calls for a statistical fix — better risk adjustment, more data — while an agent actively routing around the metric requires changing incentives or discretion instead, and calling both "Goodhart" obscures which situation you are actually in.
