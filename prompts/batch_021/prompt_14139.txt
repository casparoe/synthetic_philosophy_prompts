Compare Simon Blackburn's and Allan Gibbard's respective strategies for solving the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism. Blackburn's quasi-realism handles embedded moral claims through a "logic of attitudes": accepting a conditional such as "If lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong" is analyzed as a higher-order attitude of disapproval directed at certain combinations of first-order attitudes, with logical inconsistency explained as the practical impossibility of jointly satisfying the attitudes in question. Gibbard's norm-expressivism instead models normative thought via a possible-worlds-style semantics defined over pairs of possible worlds and maximally specific "hyperplans," so that the standard logical connectives apply to normative sentences in essentially the same way they apply to descriptive ones. Which approach offers the more general, non-ad-hoc treatment of embedding across the full range of connectives — not just modus ponens, but negation, disjunction, and iterated embeddings — and which better explains what it is for two speakers to have a logically inconsistent moral disagreement, rather than merely expressing different feelings side by side? Defend your answer, and consider whether the weaker of the two proposals could be repaired by borrowing resources from the stronger one.
