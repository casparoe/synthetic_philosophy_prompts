"The fitting-attitude (or 'buck-passing') analysis holds that for something to be good just is for it to be fitting -- warranted, correct -- to have some pro-attitude toward it, like desiring it or admiring it. Value itself doesn't give us reasons; instead the object's natural properties give us reasons to desire or admire it, and its being valuable just consists in there being such reasons. This lets us explain value without positing some extra non-natural property.

The wrong-kind-of-reasons (WKR) problem is that not every reason to have a pro-attitude toward something is relevant to whether it's good, so the analysis as stated proves too much. Rabinowicz and Rønnow-Rasmussen's case: a demon says he'll torture you forever unless you desire a saucer of mud. You now have an overwhelming reason to desire the mud, but the mud hasn't become good, and nobody would treat the threat as evidence that it is. So the buck-passer has to separate these 'wrong-kind' reasons from the 'right-kind' ones -- the ones grounded in the object's actual good-making features -- without just defining right-kind reasons as 'the ones that track value,' since that would smuggle value straight back into the analysis it's supposed to explain.

The most influential response is Scanlon's distinction between object-given and state-given reasons: object-given reasons are grounded in features of the object itself, state-given reasons in facts about what follows from being in the attitudinal state. The demon case is state-given -- the badness of torture is a fact about the state of desiring, not about the mud -- so we get a tidy criterion: only object-given reasons are fittingness-relevant.

The trouble is that this is really just a label for the distinction we already wanted, not an explanation of it: it doesn't say why facts about the state of desiring can never be fittingness-relevant, it just stipulates that they aren't, which starts to look ad hoc once you notice that Rabinowicz and Rønnow-Rasmussen themselves flag possible counterexamples on both sides. Since no clean, non-stipulative line has been drawn, I think the WKR problem shows that fitting-attitude analyses of value are false."
