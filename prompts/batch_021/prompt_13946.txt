In a graduate reading group on medieval political thought that I teach, two students, Elena and Marcus, got into a disagreement about how to read Aquinas on tyrannicide that the rest of us couldn't settle. Here is roughly how the exchange went, and I would value an outside judgment on it.

(1) Elena argued that Aquinas does hold a real tyrannicide doctrine. In his commentary on the Sentences, he distinguishes a ruler who lacks legitimate title from one who holds it but exercises it unjustly, and for the first he denies that subjects owe any obedience at all -- precisely because a usurper never had genuine authority to begin with, so forcibly removing him is not rebellion against authority, just resistance to a private aggressor.

(2) Marcus replied that this shows less than Elena thinks: denying a duty to obey is not the same as licensing killing. Aquinas's one sustained discussion of what to actually do about a tyrant, in De Regimine Principum, is strikingly cautious -- he warns that private attempts at removal usually fail or install a worse tyrant in the old one's place, and assigns the remedy instead to whichever public authority originally set the ruler up.

(3) Elena answered that Marcus was collapsing two cases Aquinas keeps separate, and shortchanging the usurpation text besides: Aquinas does not merely withhold a duty of obedience there, he treats the usurper as having no standing to command at all, which is exactly the situation in which meeting force with force needs no further license. The caution Marcus was quoting concerns a different problem -- a ruler who still holds legitimate title but governs badly. So the real picture, she said, is a coherent two-track view: usurpers may be forcibly resisted as any aggressor could be, while title-holders must be dealt with institutionally. That is a tyrannicide doctrine, just a conditional one.

(4) Marcus responded that having no standing to command still does not get you to a general right of private citizens to hunt a usurper down after the fact, rather than merely resist him in the moment -- and that even on Elena's own two-track reading, "dealt with institutionally" is thinner than it sounds for the harder case, since many of the regimes Aquinas has in mind have no body actually capable of deposing a king, so his remedy there collapses into endurance and prayer. He added that, next to John of Salisbury, who a century earlier had commended killing a reigning tyrant outright, Aquinas's carefulness looks like a considered narrowing of the tradition rather than a doctrine with fine print.
