For a novel I'm drafting, one of my characters is a logician who has quietly abandoned her early enthusiasm for Kripke's fixed-point theory of truth, and I need her to explain to a student, without hand-waving, exactly why the fix never fully held up. I have to understand the technical crux myself before I can write her honestly. Kripke's construction lets the Liar sentence come out neither true nor false -- a genuine truth-value gap -- rather than forcing the contradiction Tarski regarded as inevitable in any sufficiently expressive language containing its own truth predicate. Critics object, though, that this is undone the moment we're entitled to say, from outside the construction, that the Liar sentence is 'not true,' which looks like exactly what the sentence itself asserts, reinstating the paradox one level up. Could you explain precisely why Kripke's own resources fail to block that move, and then tell me whether Gupta and Belnap's revision theory -- which diagnoses the Liar as perpetually unstable under revision rather than as a gap -- genuinely escapes a revenge problem of the same shape, or only relocates it?
