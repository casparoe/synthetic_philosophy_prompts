Two of my graduate students got into a spirited argument after a session of my seminar on paradoxes of infinity, and I would like a neutral outside adjudication of how it went.

The occasion was Thomson's lamp: a lamp, initially off, is switched on at the half-minute mark, off at the three-quarter-minute mark, on again at the seven-eighths-minute mark, and so on, so that infinitely many switchings are completed within a single minute. The question is whether the lamp is on or off once the minute has elapsed.

(1) Alina opened by arguing that this is a genuine proof that completing an infinite series of tasks -- a "supertask" -- is metaphysically impossible: the lamp can't be on at the end, since every time it was switched on it was subsequently switched off again, and it can't be off, for the mirror-image reason. Since neither option is available, the description of the process is self-contradictory, and no incoherent process can actually be carried out.

(2) Marcus objected that this shows less than Alina thinks. Nothing about the switchings before the one-minute mark fixes what happens at the one-minute mark itself: there is no last switch, since the switching rate grows without bound as the minute closes in, so there is no final state for a later state to inherit from. The lamp could simply be on at the one-minute mark, full stop, without contradicting anything true at any earlier instant. So the puzzle shows only that specifying a process at every instant before a limit doesn't thereby fix its state at the limit -- not that anything is contradictory.

(3) Alina replied that this "solution" just relocates the problem: if the state at the one-minute mark isn't fixed by anything before it, then it is simply uncaused, and anyone willing to accept that has no principled grounds to object to a lamp that spontaneously turns purple at the one-minute mark, or a coin that settles on a face for no physical reason at all.

(4) Marcus answered that "not fixed by the pattern" is not the same as "uncaused": plenty of otherwise deterministic setups need a boundary condition to be complete -- initial conditions in mechanics, for instance -- and stipulating the state at the limit is exactly that kind of addition, not a causal gap. He added that even granting Alina's worry, it would show at most that this particular, discontinuously oscillating supertask needs such a stipulation; other supertasks, such as Achilles's infinitely many strides in closing the gap on the tortoise, have their limiting state fixed automatically by continuity, so her argument doesn't touch supertasks in general.

(5) Alina's rejoinder was that this concession costs Marcus more than he thinks: the supertasks anyone would actually want to put to use -- ones that could, say, settle an otherwise undecidable question by running through infinitely many cases and reporting a verdict at the limit -- are exactly the discontinuous, boundary-sensitive kind. If those always need an extra, arbitrary stipulation just to yield a determinate outcome, supertasks can't do any philosophical or computational work that wasn't already put in by hand.

Neither of them would concede, and I'm not convinced either was simply wrong. Could you go through (1) through (5) and say, for each, whether the claim is correct, mistaken, or right about one thing while wrong about another -- and then say, on balance, who has the stronger case overall, and whether there's a consideration neither of them raised that ought to bear on it?
