Below are six arguments that Douglass, along with the political abolitionists who shaped his thinking (particularly Lysander Spooner and Gerrit Smith), used to read the Constitution as anti-slavery rather than pro-slavery. - "silent-text": the document never once uses the words "slave" or "slavery," relying instead on euphemisms ("other persons," "such persons," "person held to service or labor"), which Douglass took as proof that the framers refused to sanction property in human beings at the level of fundamental law.
- "preamble-purposes": the Constitution's own stated ends, establishing justice and securing the blessings of liberty among them, are incompatible with a legal order that tolerates slavery, and should govern how ambiguous clauses are construed.
- "plain-meaning-over-intent": the document should be read according to its public, written meaning, not according to the private pro-slavery motives that some individual framers happened to hold.
- "natural-law-declaration": the Constitution must be interpreted in harmony with the natural-rights principles of the Declaration of Independence, which limit what any legitimate legal text following it can permit.
- "narrow-clause-construction": each of the three clauses usually cited as pro-slavery (the three-fifths, migration-or-importation, and fugitive-from-labor clauses) is narrow or ambiguous enough that none of them affirmatively establishes slavery.
- "political-engagement": whatever the document's compromises, enslaved people are better served by abolitionists contesting elections and wielding constitutional politics than by Garrisonian withdrawal from the Union.
