I work as a machine learning engineer, not a philosopher, but something that keeps happening on the job has started to bother me on a more conceptual level. When I train several different models on the same dataset, same features and target, just varying the architecture, hyperparameters, or random seed, I routinely end up with models that score essentially identically on held-out accuracy, yet disagree substantially on individual predictions, on which features they treat as most important, and on the qualitative "story" they seem to encode about the underlying process generating the data. People in the ML world sometimes call this the "Rashomon effect" or "predictive multiplicity": one and the same body of evidence, the training set, turns out to be compatible with a whole family of models that behave quite differently from one another once you look beyond the aggregate accuracy number.

I mentioned this over drinks to a friend from university who went on to do a philosophy PhD, half expecting her to find it a boring engineering footnote, and instead she got quite animated: she says this is essentially a modern, empirical rediscovery of the underdetermination of theory by evidence, specifically the Duhem-Quine point that no finite body of evidence, taken on its own, ever picks out a single theory, since any hypothesis is only ever tested jointly with a raft of auxiliary assumptions that can absorb the blame whenever a prediction fails. I don't know the philosophy of science literature well enough to judge whether she's onto something real or just getting carried away by a surface-level analogy.

So I would like your independent assessment. Please compare the classical Duhem-Quine underdetermination thesis with the machine-learning phenomenon of predictive multiplicity: in what precise respects are they instances of the same underlying problem, and in what respects might the ML case be importantly different, for instance because it concerns a fixed finite sample rather than a theory's fit with all of its possible empirical consequences, or because it isn't obvious what, if anything, plays the role of an "auxiliary hypothesis" when the object under test is a trained model rather than a scientific theory? I'd especially like your honest verdict on whether the classic Quinean move of protecting a favored hypothesis by revising something else in the web of belief has any genuine counterpart in how practitioners handle predictive multiplicity, or whether that is exactly the point at which the analogy breaks down.
