I'm working on a feature about the stranger results to emerge from early twentieth-century logic, and Skolem's paradox is the one that seems hardest to square with ordinary mathematical practice. The downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem shows that ZFC, if it has a model at all, has one whose entire domain is countable, yet that same theory proves Cantor's theorem, that the real numbers cannot be put into one-to-one correspondence with the natural numbers. Since every subset of a countable set is itself countable, the model must contain a set that is countable when viewed from the outside, yet provably uncountable within the model itself—a flat contradiction, not merely a paradox. Given that, why do set theorists still trust ZFC's account of the infinite rather than concluding that the entire hierarchy of cardinalities above countable is simply an artifact of whichever model one happens to be reasoning in?
