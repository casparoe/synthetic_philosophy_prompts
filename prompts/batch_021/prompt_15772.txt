I'm outlining a novel whose secondary plot follows a retired classics tutor named Marcus, who spends his evenings arguing with his godson, a cryptocurrency entrepreneur, about the ethics of the young man's business. I want their exchanges to feel intellectually earned rather than a straw-man debate, so Marcus's objections need to be built out of Aristotle's actual economic theory rather than generic hand-wringing about greed. I've been rereading the relevant stretches of Politics Book I and the discussion of reciprocal justice in Nicomachean Ethics Book V, and I want to make sure I'm putting the real argument in Marcus's mouth: the distinction between oikonomia (household management aimed at acquiring only what a good life requires) and chrematistike (the unlimited, self-perpetuating art of wealth-getting); Aristotle's claim that money (nomisma) is a measure of value that exists by convention (nomos) rather than by nature; and his notorious argument that lending at interest is the most unnatural way of making money, since it treats currency itself as something that can give birth to more currency, rather than as a mere instrument of exchange.

What I'd like from you is a careful, textually grounded reconstruction of how Aristotle would likely assess cryptocurrency specifically — not electronic payments in general, but something like Bitcoin, where units are "mined" through computation rather than issued by any state, where most holders are in it for speculative appreciation rather than the exchange of goods, and where the asset has no conceivable use apart from serving as money. Would Aristotle regard mining as an even purer, more literal case of currency "breeding" currency than the interest-bearing loans he condemned — since here there is not even a lender and borrower trading something real, only computation converted directly into a claim on value? Or does the complete absence of any state or communal backing pose a genuine difficulty for his account of money as resting on convention, one his framework might not straightforwardly survive? I'd rather you work through the actual argumentative structure than default to a generic verdict about greed or moderation — Marcus's dialogue needs real philosophical teeth, and I want to know where, if anywhere, the analogy strains.
