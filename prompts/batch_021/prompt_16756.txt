I've spent a fair amount of time over the years working through old Overcoming Bias and LessWrong threads on decision theory, and I keep coming back to Hofstadter's notion of superrationality: the claim that in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma between two agents who reason identically, the rational move isn't the dominant-strategy defection that classical game theory recommends, but mutual cooperation, since whatever conclusion one player reaches, the other — being a kind of psychological twin — will reach as well. Part of what makes that argument work, as far as I can tell, is precisely that it leans on the two players being close to perfect copies of each other in a perfectly symmetric situation.

What I've never seen properly argued out is what happens once people try to stretch this reasoning to license so-called "acausal trade": the idea that agents with no causal contact whatsoever — say, civilizations tucked away in causally disconnected regions of an inflating universe, or agents whose choices will simply never be compared — can still benefit by acting as though they'd struck a bargain, purely because each can reason that a similar enough agent elsewhere would follow the same policy. That feels like a much bigger leap than the twin case: the agents in question are no longer identical, there is no way to check that anyone has "held up their end," and it isn't obvious that "trade" is even the right word for an arrangement with no communication and no enforcement.

Could you write a dialogue between two people who both fully accept Hofstadter's original superrationality argument for the symmetric twin case, but who disagree sharply about whether it generalizes to acausal trade? One should defend the extension as the natural next step once you replace the requirement of literal identity with mere correlation between decision procedures; the other should accept the narrow twin-PD case while resisting the further step, pressing on exactly where the symmetry breaks down, what "keeping your end of the bargain" could even mean without causal contact, and whether the whole apparatus is doing anything beyond restating the hope that other rational agents will happen to converge on your preferred policy. I'd like both participants to be given real, substantive objections and rejoinders, with neither one simply folding or missing the other's point.
