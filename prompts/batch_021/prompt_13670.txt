I'm a software engineer at a small company, and a few months ago I ended up handling the fallout when a fairly minor UI change my team had shipped got picked up by a cluster of anonymous accounts on a link-aggregator site where our product occasionally comes up. Within about half a day there were several hundred comments, a couple of meme screenshots, and a few "someone should tell them how bad this is" posts elsewhere. None of these people were ever in the same room, or even particularly aware of one another; they were just scattered individuals in different time zones, each independently adding a sentence or an upvote to the same growing verdict. Read on its own, almost none of the comments were especially sharp or damning: most were a single line, and plenty were just an upvote on someone else's one-liner. Even so, the cumulative effect on me and my two teammates was closer to dread than anything a single critic had ever produced.

That struck me as strange, because about a year earlier, a reasonably well-known blogger in our space had written a long, scathing, fully signed post arguing that an entire category of feature we had built was actively bad for users. Read line by line, that post was far more damaging than any individual comment in the pile-on, and I remember mentally arguing back with it, sentence by sentence, the way you would with a specific person you disagreed with. The anonymous swarm was nothing like that: there was no one in particular to argue with, and no position, exactly, that anyone had committed to, just a kind of verdict that seemed to have settled over us from nowhere and everywhere at once.

I've tried to explain to a few friends why these two experiences felt different in kind rather than just in degree, and the explanations on offer (it's just a matter of scale, it's just anonymity removing accountability, it's just algorithms making things loom larger than they really are) each capture something without accounting for the whole of it. What I would like to know is whether there is a real, defensible distinction to be drawn between being judged by an identifiable person (or a small group of identifiable people) and being judged by this kind of diffuse, anonymous crowd that never actually assembles anywhere, even holding the actual content and harshness of the criticism fixed, or whether the sense that these are different in kind rather than just in scale is really just an illusion produced by scale and anonymity that doesn't track anything real. And if the distinction does hold up, I would be curious what follows from it in practice: whether it should change how much weight this kind of verdict deserves relative to a signed one, and how someone might go about holding their ground against it in a way that goes beyond advice like "don't feed the trolls" or "focus on the substance, not the source."
