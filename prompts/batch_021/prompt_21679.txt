I'm writing a feature on the philosophical foundations of AI reasoning, and I've been reading about what epistemic logicians call "the problem of logical omniscience": the standard idealization, built into both epistemic logic and Bayesian formal epistemology, that a perfectly rational agent assigns full certainty to every logical and mathematical truth the moment it accepts the relevant axioms. It seems to me that Gödel's incompleteness theorems settled this question decades before anyone tried to build such a system: since no consistent formal system rich enough to do arithmetic can prove every truth expressible in its own language, doesn't that mean logical omniscience is impossible not merely as a practical limitation but as a matter of principle, for any mind or machine whatsoever? If so, Bayesian rationality would seem to rest on a strictly incoherent ideal, with a hard Gödelian ceiling on how rational any AI system could become no matter how far its reasoning is scaled up. Before this goes into print, I want to make sure I have the logic right — does the inference from Gödel's theorem to those conclusions actually hold?
