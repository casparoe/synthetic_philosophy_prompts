I have never studied economics or philosophy formally, but for a few years now game theory has been something of a spare-time obsession, and two results I keep returning to seem to pull against each other. Aumann's agreement theorem holds that two people who share the same prior can never agree to disagree: once their posteriors on some question are common knowledge between them, those posteriors must coincide. Rubinstein's email game, which grew out of that same broad tradition, seems to show that common knowledge is almost unreachable in practice: two generals who only stand to gain by attacking together keep sending confirmations back and forth, each message carrying some small independent chance of being lost, and however many hundreds of confirmations get through, the plan never becomes common knowledge between them, so in equilibrium neither one ever attacks. Please compare the role that common knowledge plays in these two results. Does the fragility exposed by Rubinstein's game mean that Aumann's theorem has little to tell us about disagreement between real people, or are the two results really answering different questions that only look like they are in tension? Please justify your answer.
