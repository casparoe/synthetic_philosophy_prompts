I sit on the town council of Amity Hollow, a planned community founded twelve years ago around an ideal of direct, participatory civic life: weekly open town meetings at which any resident may speak and vote, a volunteer-run fire watch and school co-op, and a homemade harvest festival that serves as the town's only real "entertainment." A resident who sold a startup has offered to fund a permanent theater and underwrite a resident troupe for its first five seasons, and most of the council is inclined to accept. One member, explicitly invoking Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert, argues that we would be converting citizens who currently make their own civic and social life into paying spectators of professionals' counterfeited passions, and that an institution tolerable in a large, already-corrupt city is not a harmless import for a small town that has not yet contracted whatever disease theater is supposed to remedy. In the spirit of Chesterton's Fence, I do not want to dismiss this as merely quaint, but neither do I want to lose a generous, time-limited offer to an eighteenth-century pamphlet without a real argument on the merits. Please draft the memo I will circulate before next month's vote: a concrete recommendation -- accept, decline, or accept with specific conditions -- that engages the strongest form of the participation-versus-spectatorship worry rather than dismissing it, and states honestly what the town gives up under whichever option you recommend.
