I'm a novelist putting together a small collection of stories that each use a genuine result from mathematical logic as their hidden architecture rather than their explicit subject. The idea is that a reader with no technical background should still feel the shape of the underlying idea pressing on the characters, the way you can feel a plot's logic before you could ever state it as a rule. The story I'm currently stuck on is meant to be built around Skolem's paradox. I first ran into it years ago in a philosophy-of-mathematics reading group, tucked inside a much longer argument about set-theoretic realism, and it's the one idea from that whole year that never quite worked itself loose: if ZFC is consistent, the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem guarantees that it has a model that is merely countable, and yet that very model satisfies the sentence asserting that uncountable sets exist. I understand the resolution well enough to explain it badly at a dinner party -- the bijection that would enumerate the supposedly uncountable set exists in the wider universe but not inside the model itself, so "uncountable" turns out to name a relation between a set and the model it is being judged from, rather than a property the set has outright -- and I know that Skolem himself drew from this a real skepticism about whether any first-order theory can pin down a notion like cardinality absolutely, rather than only relative to whichever model one happens to be standing in. What I don't have is a human situation with that same shape.

Could you write that story? I would like it kept small -- two or three people, a single evening or one long conversation, rather than a seminar room with mathematicians arguing at a blackboard -- and I would like you to resist the obvious move of cashing this out as a simulation-reveal or an AI narrative; the vertigo should land on people who are otherwise perfectly ordinary, not on placeholders for an idea. The logic needs to be load-bearing rather than decorative: whatever turns in the plot should trade specifically on the difference between a bijection failing to exist inside a system and failing to exist at all, not on some vaguer gesture at hidden immensity, though none of it has to be named in those terms on the page. I would rather the story end on that vertigo than resolve it into a tidy epiphany, and something in the neighborhood of two thousand words would suit the collection best.
