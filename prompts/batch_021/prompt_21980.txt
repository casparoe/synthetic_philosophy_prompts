My father died three weeks ago after almost forty years as a public defender, and I've been asked to give the eulogy at his memorial next weekend. He never went to university and wouldn't have called himself a philosopher, but he read constantly, and there was one passage he came back to for as long as I can remember, from Pascal: the idea that justice without force is powerless, force without justice is tyrannical, and that since we could never manage to make what is just strong, we ended up making what is strong count as just instead. He used to recite some version of it at the dinner table after a bad week in court, half as a joke and half, I always suspected, as the truest thing he actually believed. I only found out a few years ago that he was quoting Pascal and hadn't worked the line out himself.

The trouble is that the idea is genuinely bleak, and I don't want to soften it into something he would have found dishonest, because he was clear-eyed about how often the state's force simply gets called justice while the people he represented had almost none of their own behind them. But I also can't let the eulogy suggest he thought the whole effort was pointless, because it plainly wasn't to him — he went back to that courtroom for four decades. About half the room will be colleagues from the public defender's office who will recognize the passage immediately and will notice if I flatten it into something comforting he'd have rolled his eyes at; the rest are family and old friends who've heard him quote "the Pascal thing" for years without knowing where it came from or quite what it meant. Could you write the eulogy for me, something around six or seven minutes long? I'd like it to actually put the idea to work — maybe even the thought that becoming a lawyer was his own answer to that passage, a way of lending justice a little force since he couldn't make force just on his own — rather than mentioning Pascal once and then moving on to safer material.
