So I'm taking this dual-enrollment "Intro to Formal Epistemology" class at the community college near me (trying to rack up some credits before senior year), and this week we covered van Fraassen's Reflection Principle. From what I got, it basically says your credence right now in some claim, given that you already know for a fact what your credence in it will be later, should just equal that later number — like if I'm 100% sure that tomorrow-me will be at 70% confidence that it's gonna rain, then today-me should also already be at 70%. My professor's one-line summary of the whole point was "treat your future self like an expert whose opinion you should defer to," which honestly seemed like solid life advice too, not just some probability rule.

But then I started poking at it and it kind of falls apart in my head? Like say I know for a fact that after I pull an all nighter finishing my English essay, I'll be so sleep-deprived and loopy that I'll be sitting there 95% confident I just wrote the best essay of my life, even though its probably gonna be pretty mediocre. Doesn't Reflection mean I'm supposed to already believe, right now, before I've even started writing, that theres a 95% chance it'll be amazing — just because I know that's what future zombie-brain me is gonna think?? That seems obviously wrong, so I'm stuck on whether I'm just misapplying the principle somehow, or whether this is an actual known issue with it that my professor conveniently skipped over. Which is it, and if the answer is "it only applies sometimes," how do you actually tell in general when you're supposed to trust future-you versus when you're not?
