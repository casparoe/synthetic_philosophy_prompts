I'm plotting a novel in which one of my two central characters, a woman in her fifties named Ilse, underwent a callosotomy some fifteen years before the story opens -- a last-resort treatment for catastrophic epilepsy -- and although the surgery itself will never be explained on the page, I want the philosophical scaffolding behind her interior life to be sound. Reading around the split-brain literature, I kept running into Thomas Nagel's well-known observation that once you look closely at what these patients can and cannot do, neither "one mind" nor "two minds" comfortably describes the case; our ordinary concept of a single subject of experience simply doesn't carve it cleanly. What I need help sorting out is how two later attempts to get past that impasse actually compare: on one side, the view associated with Roland Puccetti that we should just accept that two distinct persons have been sharing a skull all along, the surgery merely revealing a duality that was already there; on the other, the kind of position that runs through Tim Bayne's work on the unity of consciousness, on which a single subject persists throughout, and what looks like two simultaneous streams is better understood as radically disunified access, or a rapid alternation in which hemisphere's contents count as conscious at any given moment.

Could you set out these two positions in enough depth to show where they genuinely disagree, rather than trading slogans, and then tell me which one handles two things better: the fact that split-brain patients are behaviorally almost indistinguishable from anyone else outside tightly controlled, lateralized-presentation testing, and the more recent findings -- I have in mind the Pinto-and-colleagues studies from a few years back -- suggesting that some information crosses the disconnection even under experimental conditions, in ways the classic two-streams story doesn't predict. I'd like an actual verdict, not an even-handed summary, and if there's some feature of the phenomenon that neither camp accommodates comfortably, I'd like to know about that too, since it may be exactly the seam along which I want Ilse's own halting self-descriptions to run.
