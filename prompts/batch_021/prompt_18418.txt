I've spent the past few months reporting on the odd little renaissance that old-school decision theory is enjoying inside AI-safety and "rationalist" circles, where people fret about superintelligent agents being predicted, simulated, and blackmailed by other agents, and where this has revived interest in exotic decision problems that most academic philosophers had filed away as a settled backwater. While researching the piece, I kept running into Gibbard and Harper's 1978 case "Death in Damascus" — their decision-theoretic recasting of the old "Appointment in Samarra" fable, in which fleeing to safety only guarantees keeping the appointment with Death after all — which keeps getting cited as the crux example for whether causal decision theory (CDT) can even give a coherent recommendation once a sufficiently reliable predictor is in the picture. I understand the bare mechanics: that no pure act comes out "ratifiable" once Death is stipulated to predict the man's movements reliably, and that a mixed strategy is usually wheeled in to rescue the theory. What I can't tell from blog-post-level summaries is how much weight this objection is actually given once you factor in Andy Egan's "psychopath button" case, which is often read as showing that patching CDT with ratifiability doesn't work in general, and the newer functional decision theory proposals coming out of that same rationalist/AI-safety world (including a paper that more or less announces its ambitions in its title, "Cheating Death in Damascus"), which claim to dissolve the puzzle without any randomizing at all.

For a sidebar accompanying the piece, I'd like to get the actual state of this argument right rather than hand-wave at it. Could you write me an essay, pitched at an intelligent general-interest reader but not dumbed down, that lays out the strongest form of each position — the ratifiability/mixed-strategy defense of CDT, the Egan-style case for thinking ratifiability can't save it, and the functional-decision-theory claim to resolve the case cleanly — and then defends one specific verdict on whether Death in Damascus shows that causal decision theory, in anything like its standard form, has to be abandoned? Please also say a word about how evidential decision theory handles the very same case, since I don't want to imply by omission that it gets off easy. I need an actual position I can put to sources and ask them to argue with, not a "reasonable people disagree" shrug.
