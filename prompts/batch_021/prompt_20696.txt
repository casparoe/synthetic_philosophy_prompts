So in my Just War Theory seminar we just spent two weeks on Walzer vs. McMahan on the moral equality of combatants, and ngl it's messed with my head more than anything else this semester. Walzer's whole move in Just and Unjust Wars is that jus in bello and jus ad bellum are independent - a soldier fighting on the unjust side who still plays by the rules of engagement hasn't done anything wrong, he's just as much a "moral equal" as the guy on the just side. Then McMahan comes along and says that's basically incoherent: if your side's cause is unjust you don't actually have a right to kill anyone, full stop, you're just excused sometimes because of duress or nonculpable ignorance. Our TA kept hammering on the idea that a lot of traditionalists are quietly conflating permission with excuse, which felt kind of devastating in the moment, but then I also don't really see how an army is supposed to function if every private has to personally adjudicate jus ad bellum before returning fire.

Can you write me a dialogue to help me get both positions straight? Have one guy channel Walzer - moral equality, the independence thesis, the epistemic argument that soldiers can't be expected to know which side is just, maybe the point about the whole war convention collapsing if you drop equality - and have the other channel McMahan's revisionism - liability tracking moral responsibility rather than uniform rules, the justified-vs-excused distinction, the idea that being confused or coerced doesn't make you innocent, just less blameworthy. Neither one should fold or be an obvious strawman, they should actually engage with whatever the other guy just said instead of restating thier own opening spiel, and its fine if they're still going, unresolved, when the sun comes up.
