Since retiring from a career in epidemiology, I've taken up philosophy of science on the side, which is probably why a case before my state's Environmental Health Compensation Board has started to bother me more than a regulatory question should. Two chemical plants, discharging entirely independently into a shared aquifer, each released enough solvent on its own to have caused the illnesses we are now asked to compensate; under our charter's strict "but-for" rule -- a firm owes damages only if its discharge was necessary for the harm -- neither plant owes anything, since removing either one alone would not have prevented the harm. Some board members find that conclusion absurd; others consider it the only principled way to avoid holding a firm liable for harm it made no difference to. Please draft the short opinion I need to circulate before our next meeting: recommend how to resolve this specific claim and whether our causation standard should change, and give the members defending strict but-for reasoning a real argument to answer rather than a caricature of their view.
