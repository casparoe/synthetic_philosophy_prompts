I'm a few years into a philosophy PhD (mostly decision theory / formal epistemology) and once a month a handful of us from different departments run an informal reading group on population ethics and longtermism-adjacent stuff. Last session was on infinite aggregative ethics and it turned into a real argument between two regulars, Kasper (philosophy) and Lior (physics), that nobody managed to resolve, so I'd rather get an actual outside opinion than just let it die at "agree to disagree."

The case we were using: a countably infinite population, one person per positive integer, whose welfare levels relative to a neutral zero are exactly 1, -1/2, 1/3, -1/4, 1/5, -1/6, ... forever. Summed in that order it's just the alternating harmonic series, so total welfare comes out to ln 2, a mildly good world. (1) Kasper's opening move was that since that series only converges conditionally and not absolutely, the Riemann rearrangement theorem means you can take that exact same set of welfare levels, hand it out to that exact same population in a different order, and get literally any real number as the "total," or +infinity, or -infinity, or no limit at all - and since nothing about who has what has actually changed, only the bookkeeping order, "total welfare" can't be what makes an infinite world better or worse than another; sometimes the comparison just has to be undefined. (2) Lior said the rearrangement isn't a genuine alternative world, just a redescription: real populations come with a built-in order already (temporal, causal, spatial), so once outcomes are individuated properly - this welfare, to this person, at this time - theres no actual ambiguity, only an artificial one you get from pretending order isn't part of what the outcome even is. (3) Kasper replied that this just relocates the arbitrariness onto whatever feature gets crowned "the" privileged order - why time rather than, say, distance from some reference point, or ID number - and that it gives no verdict at all in cases with no natural order, like the causally disconnected regions philosophers reach for in these examples all the time, so really it's a retreat to a smaller domain dressed up as a rebuttal. (4) Lior said a restricted domain isn't a defect in a theory, any more than Newtonian mechanics is "incoherent" just for going silent at relativistic speeds. (5) Kasper came back that the disanalogy is that this bites even a single, perfectly ordinary population in ordinary time, since you still owe a reason for privileging temporal order over other equally well-defined orderings, and that in relativistic spacetime there isn't even a well-defined global temporal order for spacelike-separated events, so "just use time" isn't the free, innocent fallback Lior's treating it as. (6) Lior conceded the relativity point but came back with swapping the strict linear sum for a limit over an expanding sequence of finite regions instead, apparently closer to how physicists handle total charge or energy in infinite systems - while admitting this might just relocate the arbitrariness into which expanding sequence you pick, rather than actually get rid of it, and left it there.
