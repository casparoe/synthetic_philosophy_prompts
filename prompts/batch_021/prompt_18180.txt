I'm the grad student rep on my department's stats-review panel -- we're the ones who sign off on a dissertation's analysis section before it goes to the full committee -- and we've hit a case I don't know how to think about. The dissertation has 14 seperately pre-registered hypotheses across three studies, each individually significant at p<.05 and defended rigorously on its own terms, with nobody on the panel disputing any single result. But with 14 independent tests at alpha=.05 you'd expect something like even odds that at least one is a false positive just by chance, so now the panel's arguing over whether to (a) require a Bonferroni-type correction that would knock a couple of findings below significance, (b) leave the findings alone but make the student add a paragraph admitting some subset is probably wrong, or (c) leave it alone entirely since every dissertation has this issue and nobody usually treats it as fatal. Half the room thinks option (b) is flatly incoherent -- you can't accept each of the 14 claims and also assert that at least one is false -- and I have a nagging feeling this is the preface paradox, or maybe the lottery paradox, or some mashup of the two, but I don't remember which, or whether that even matters for what we do. Can you draft the memo I'd bring back recommending one of (a)/(b)/(c) with actual reasons, that takes the 'incoherent' objection seriously instead of hand-waving it, and is honest about the best argument for the option we don't choose?
