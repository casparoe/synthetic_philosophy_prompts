A friend of mine who is finishing a philosophy PhD on animal ethics came over for dinner last week, and we ended up arguing for the better part of three hours about wild animal suffering. She holds, following writers like Oscar Horta, that the disease, starvation, and predation that fill wild animals' lives matter exactly as much, morally, as suffering we cause directly, and that our instinct to just let nature take its course reflects an unexamined preference for "the natural" over the good. I argued that ecosystems aren't ours to manage animal by animal, and that even well-intentioned intervention tends to produce effects nobody foresaw. Neither of us budged, and I came away more unsettled than I expected, because the argument turns out not to be merely academic for me at the moment.

I sit on the volunteer advisory board of a small land trust that manages a fenced reserve of about 2,000 acres, home to a resident herd of roughly 200 elk that have been unable to migrate to lower-elevation winter range since the reserve's boundary fence went up in the 1970s to keep them off the adjacent highway and ranches. This winter's snowpack is running well above average, and our staff ecologist estimates that without intervention we will lose 15 to 20 percent of the herd to starvation over the next two months, concentrated among calves and older cows. One board faction wants emergency feed stations in place before the next storm; another points out that concentrating elk at feed sites is a well-documented way to spread chronic wasting disease, that supplemental feeding will mask an overpopulation problem the fence itself created, and that feeding the herd through one bad winter will make it very hard to refuse every bad winter afterward. Our founding charter commits the trust only to "protect and preserve the reserve's wildlife and habitat," language both factions are now citing against each other.

Could you draft the short memo I need to bring to the board's meeting this Friday? It should recommend one specific course of action for this winter, state what would have to be different for that recommendation to change, and address directly whether the case for feeding this herd — given that the trust's own fence is what traps it — commits us to a much broader obligation to relieve wild animal suffering elsewhere on the property, or whether there is a principled way to act here without that further commitment. Whichever way you come down, engage honestly with the strongest version of the losing position rather than letting it lose by default.
