I've somehow ended up the token philosopher on my state's forensic science advisory commission -- long story involving my dean and a committee slot nobody else wanted. We're rewriting the accreditation standard for crime-lab pattern-comparison units (fingerprints, tool marks, that sort of thing), and its turned into a real fight. The current standard just says examiners have to maintain 'adequate independence from case-irrelevant information,' which nobody has ever actually cashed out, and now there's a concrete proposal on the table: require a sequential-unmasking protocol, where the examiner doing the actual print-to-print comparison is shielded from context -- a confession, which side ordered the test, another examiner's conclusion -- until after she's logged her initial call.

Half the commission thinks this is just the forensic version of a double-blind drug trial: if knowing 'he confessed' can shift whether a print looks like a match, an examiner's confidence that she 'just sees' the match isn't independent evidence of anything. The veteran examiners pushing back are making a point I don't think is just sour grapes -- they say matching prints is a trained perceptual skill, and that skilled perception legitimately draws on background knowledge rather than being contaminated by it, the same way a birder actually sees a Cooper's hawk where the rest of us see 'a bird, I guess.' It's basically the cognitive-penetration-of-perception debate we chew over in phil of mind, except here it decides whether someone stays convicted. Can you draft the two-page memo I'll circulate before next month's vote? I need an actual recommendation -- adopt it, adopt some narrower version, pilot it first, or reject it outright -- and reasoning that seriously engages the 'expertise changes what you see, not just what you infer' objection instead of just repeating the double-blind analogy back at it.
