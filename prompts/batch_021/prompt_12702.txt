Over the past couple of years I have spent a good deal of my free time thinking about puzzles involving infinity, without any formal background in mathematics or philosophy, and Thomson's lamp is the one I keep returning to. The lamp starts off; you switch it on, then off, then on again, each switch coming after half the interval since the last, so that infinitely many switches occur within a single minute. Whether the lamp is then on or off seems to have no answer at all, which I have always taken as showing that the whole procedure is illegitimate rather than merely strange.

More recently I came across what seems to be a relative of this puzzle, sometimes called the Ross–Littlewood paradox: a vase that starts empty, into which several balls are placed and out of which one is removed at every stage, the stages likewise compressed so that infinitely many of them occur within that same minute. Here, unlike with the lamp, there does seem to be a definite fact about how many balls remain once every stage is finished, and that fact depends, unsettlingly, on exactly which ball is removed each time rather than on how many go in and out: if it is always the lowest-numbered ball still present, the vase ends up completely empty, even though it holds strictly more balls at every stage than it did the stage before.

I would like help thinking through how these two cases relate. Why does the lamp's process apparently end in a state with no answer at all, while the vase's process ends in a definite, if wildly counterintuitive, one? Does that contrast reveal something genuinely incoherent about completing infinitely many steps in one of the two cases, or does it just show that our intuitions about infinite processes fail in different ways depending on the details? I would value your own reasoned judgment on which puzzle poses the deeper problem for the coherence of supertasks, and why.
