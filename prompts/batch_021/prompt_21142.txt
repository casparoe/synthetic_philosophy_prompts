Ok so this has been living in my head since an ACX-adjacent meetup a few weeks back where somebody brought up the bit in the Meno where Socrates argues about the road to Larissa -- the guy who's actually travelled the road and the guy who just correctly guessed which fork to take are equally good guides in practice, but Socrates still wants to say the traveller's state is worth more, something about true opinions being like Daedalus's statues that run off unless you tie them down with a reason (I might be butchering that image, its been years since I read it properly). Nobody at the meetup could actually cash out *why* the tied-down version is worth more once you already have the true belief, beyond "well obviously it is," so I went home annoyed and wrote up my own attempted dissolution, and I'd like you to actually poke at it before I embarrass myself with a shortform post.

Here's the core of it: I think what people call the swamping problem (why does slapping "reliably produced" onto a true belief add value, when the truth was already doing all the work) only looks unanswerable if you insist on evaluating the belief at the single instance where it happens to be true. But we don't score beliefs one query at a time, we score the policy that generated them, same as you wouldn't rate a weather forecaster on whether today's forecast happened to land. A stopped clock and a working clock can say the exact same true thing at the exact same moment, but they have wildly different expected value across the neighboring moments and situations where you'd go on to consult them -- and I want to say knowledge just is true belief produced by something clock-like rather than stopped-clock-like, and the "extra value" over mere true belief is nothing but that expected value across the reference class, finally cashed out honestly instead of gestured at.

So, comments please: (1) did I mangle the Larissa/Daedalus bit, (2) does the "score the policy, not the instance" move actually dissolve the swamping worry, or does it just push the same question up one level (isn't reliability itself only good because it correlates with future truths, which puts truth right back in the driver's seat), and (3) I have a strong suspicion I've badly reinvented some named position from the epistemology literature without knowing it, so if you recognize the parents of this idea please just tell me who they are so I can stop pretending I thought of it from scratch.
