I've been prepping my decision theory seminar this week and fell down a rabbit hole on the centipede game that I can't quite climb out of. Quick recap for my own sanity: two players alternate turns for up to six rounds, passing a shared pot back and forth that grows a little each round. On your turn you can either "take" — pocketing the lion's share of the current pot and leaving your opponent a much smaller cut, ending the game right there — or "pass," growing the pot and handing the decision over. If neither of you has taken by round six, the game just ends automatically and you split what's there evenly. Standard backward induction says the player at round six should obviously take, since passing just gets them an even split instead of the bigger cut; knowing that, the player at round five should take too, rather than pass into a worse position; and so on all the way back, so the "rational" outcome is the very first player taking immediately, right out of the gate. My partner and I actually tried a version of this with poker chips over dinner last week just to see what happened, and neither of us took until round four, which is what got me stuck on this again.

Here's the bit that's bugging me. That backward-induction argument seems to lean on both players having common knowledge of each other's rationality, all the way down the tree. But say you're the player deciding at round three, and your opponent already passed twice — something they were never supposed to do if the theory's premises held. Has the very assumption that was supposed to justify "just take it" already been falsified by the time you're asked to act on it? I think this is basically what Pettit and Sugden dubbed the "backward induction paradox" back in the 80s (or maybe I'm misremembering who gets the credit), and I recall it kicking off a whole back-and-forth in the literature — Aumann, Binmore, and others — about whether reaching a node that "shouldn't" have been reached changes what's rational to do from there, or whether that's beside the point.

Could you write me a dialogue between two equally sharp decision theorists who find themselves actually sitting across from each other at round three of exactly this game, real cash on the table between them? One of them wants to take right now and can defend backward induction against the charge that it's self-undermining; the other thinks the fact that they've already gotten this far changes the rational calculus, and that passing is at least as defensible. I'd like them to genuinely grapple with each others strongest moves — especially whether just being at an "off-path" moment like this gives you new information you're rationally obligated to act on, or whether that's a confusion.
