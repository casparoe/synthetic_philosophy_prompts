I've recently started taking my no-limit hold'em game much more seriously, largely because I'm about to move up to stakes where sloppy play would actually cost me real money, and as part of that I've begun running my post-flop decisions through a solver instead of trusting my gut. One recurring pattern in the output has been bothering me, and I haven't found anyone who can explain it in a way that actually satisfies me. In river spots where my range is polarized — mostly strong value hands and bluffs, with very little in between — I'll often give the solver several bet sizes to pick from: something small, one or two medium sizes, a pot-sized bet, and an overbet. Almost every time, it ignores everything in the middle and splits its action only between checking and the very largest size on offer.

What I can't get past is that the abandoned medium sizes don't look like losers when I examine them directly. Compared head-to-head against checking, there are plenty of opponent holdings and responses where the medium bet does better. Compared head-to-head against the overbet, the same is true. So taken one at a time, neither alternative beats the medium bet outright. A friend of mine who's read more of the underlying theory said this doesn't matter, because the medium bet is still beaten once you allow a blend of the other two options, even though it isn't beaten by committing fully to either one on its own. To me that sounds almost self-contradictory: how can a single option lose to a combination of two alternatives if it doesn't lose to either alternative by itself?

I'd like to actually understand what's going on rather than just deferring to the solver as an oracle, particularly since I can't exactly pull one up mid-hand at the table. Is my friend's claim actually coherent, and if so, what's the real logic that allows a fixed option to be beaten this way? And assuming it is coherent, does that logic give me anything I can use to build my own betting strategy by hand in these spots, or is it really just an artifact of how these tools compute their answers, with no practical lesson for how I should be playing?
