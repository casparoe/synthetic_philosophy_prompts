For my grad seminar on democratic theory we spent two whole sessions stuck on "the boundary problem" -- the puzzle (I think the term goes back to a guy named Whelan in the early 80s) that any democratic procedure for deciding who counts as part of "the people" already needs a bounded people in place to run that procedure, so you either get an infinite regress or a founding moment that was never itself democratic. The readings gave us two rival fixes: Goodin's all-affected-interests principle, which says whoever's interests are affected by a decision should get a say in it, and Abizadeh's argument that any bounded, self-determining demos is illegitimate because border coercion itself has to be justified to everyone it coerces, insiders and outsiders alike. My study partner is convinced the coercion view is obviously narrower and more sensible; I think it actually explodes just as wide as the affected-interests view once you take border enforcement seriously, and neither of us could talk the other down.

Could you write me a dialogue between two political theorists who hold these two positions, arguing it out over a concrete case rather then in the abstract -- say, a country's legislature is about to approve an upstream dam or mine that will pollute a river a neighboring country's farmers depend on downstream, and that neighboring population has no vote in the deciding legislature. One theorist should defend a version of the all-affected-interests principle and push for enfranchising (or at least formally consulting) the downstream population; the other should defend a version of the all-subjected-to-coercion principle and resist that, at least in its literal voting form. I want real engagement, not talking past each other: the coercion theorist should press the affected-interests side on how it avoids requiring something like a global electorate for basically every decision of consequence, and the affected-interests theorist should press back on whether "coercion" can be drawn narrowly enough to exclude the downstream farmers without also, on pain of inconsistency, excluding people the coercion theorist clearly does want included, like resident non-citizens. And somewhere in there they both have to own up to the fact that whichever principle they pick still needs some already-bounded body to adopt it in the first place, which just is the regress they were supposed to solve in the first place. Please don't let either one concede the whole argument -- I'd rather it end with them narrowing down exactly what they still disagree about.
