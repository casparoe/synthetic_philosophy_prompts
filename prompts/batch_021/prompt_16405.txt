I've spent the last few weeks working through the classical arguments for the existence of God with a small reading group that grew out of some LessWrong-adjacent blogs and Discord servers I've followed for years, and this week's text was Aquinas's Third Way from the Summa Theologica. I already had a decent feel for cosmological arguments from contingency in their modern dress — Leibniz's version via the Principle of Sufficient Reason, or Alexander Pruss's contemporary defenses of it — but Aquinas's own formulation is terser and more thoroughly Aristotelian, and I keep getting stuck on one specific inferential move. Here is the relevant passage, in the standard Dominican Fathers translation:

"The third way is taken from possibility and necessity, and runs thus. We find in nature things that are possible to be and not to be, since they are found to be generated, and to corrupt, and consequently they are possible to be and not to be. But it is impossible for these always to exist, for that which is possible not to be at some time is not. Therefore, if everything is possible not to be, then at one time there could have been nothing in existence. Now if this were true, even now there would be nothing in existence, because that which does not exist only begins to exist by something already existing. Therefore, if at one time nothing was in existence, it would have been impossible for anything to have begun to exist; and thus even now nothing would be in existence — which is absurd."

(Aquinas then goes on to conclude that there must therefore be something whose existence is necessary in itself, which he identifies with God, but that final stretch isn't what's tripping me up.)

The move that bothers me is the slide from "every contingent thing is, at some time or other, not in existence" to "there was some one time at which nothing at all was in existence." One person in the reading group, who did graduate work in philosophy of religion, said this looks like the same complaint Anthony Kenny made against the Third Way: that it's a quantifier-shift fallacy, structurally identical to inferring "there was a moment when every seat in the theater was empty" from the much weaker "every seat was, at some point during the run, empty." I'd like help thinking through whether that charge really sticks. Is there a charitable reading of Aquinas — maybe turning on the per se/per accidens distinction, or on background Aristotelian commitments about matter, corruption, and an eternally old universe — that blocks the fallacious inference and lets the argument go through in something like its original form? And if the move can't be salvaged, does the broader contingency-style cosmological argument survive in some other guise, such as Leibniz's or a contemporary modal formulation, that doesn't rely on this particular piece of temporal-frequency reasoning at all?
