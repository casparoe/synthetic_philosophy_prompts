For my philosophy of cognitive science seminar this term, we've spent the last two weeks on classical debates in symbolic AI, and one thing from the readings hasn't left me alone. We started with McCarthy and Hayes's original 1969 paper, where they coin "the frame problem" for the narrow logical puzzle of specifying which facts a robot's world-model should leave untouched after an action, without hand-writing an explosive number of "frame axioms." But then we read Dennett's essay "Cognitive Wheels," and his parable of the robots R1, R1D1, and R2D1 — each redesigned to fetch a spare battery from a room that also contains a ticking bomb, and each failing for a different reason (missing the side effects of its own plan, drowning in a flood of true-but-irrelevant implications, or stalling out forever while sorting relevant implications from irrelevant ones) — convinced me the narrow logical puzzle is really just the visible tip of something much less tractable: the question of how any resource-bounded reasoner decides what's even worth thinking about before time runs out.

A problem set that week also walked us through the Yale shooting problem, which made it clear that even something as mundane as "a loaded gun stays loaded while nothing touches it" is surprisingly hard to get a nonmonotonic logic to represent correctly. Afterward, a classmate and I ended up arguing at office hours about whether this whole cluster of issues is really just a historical artifact of GOFAI-style, logic-based planning, or whether it's still quietly lurking inside modern systems that don't reason over explicit frame axioms at all — we didn't reach a conclusion.

For the creative component of this course's final project, I'd like to submit an original short story that dramatizes this predicament, but without simply retelling Dennett's bomb-in-the-room scenario, since I suspect at least a couple of classmates will do exactly that. Could you write a short story (roughly 1000–1500 words) centered on a bounded reasoning agent — it needn't be a robot; it could be a person, an institution, or something stranger — that must act under genuine time pressure, where the dramatic tension comes specifically from the frame problem in its broad sense: the impossibility of tractably bounding which of the countless consequences and non-consequences of an action are worth representing before it's too late. I'd like the story to make the distinction between the narrow "which facts persist" problem and the broader epistemic problem of bounding relevance felt through plot and incident rather than spelled out as exposition, and I'd like it to close on an unresolved or ambiguous note rather than a tidy fix, since nobody working on this, Dennett included, seems to think it admits of one.
