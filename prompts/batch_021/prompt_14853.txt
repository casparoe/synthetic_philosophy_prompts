I've been going down a bit of a rabbit hole reading rationalist/LessWrong-adjacent stuff on "acausal trade" -- the idea that two agents with zero causal contact (different Everett branches, different bubbles of an inflating multiverse, or one side being a purely hypothetical agent the other is just reasoning about) can still benefit each other, because each one reasons about what the other would do and acts accordingly. Its basically Hofstadter-style superrationality taken to its logical extreme. Everyone in that corner of the internet seems to agree that you need to ditch plain old causal decision theory for something fancier (updateless decision theory, functional decision theory, whatever) to make sense of this, since a garden variety causal decision theorist supposedly has no reason to hold up their end of a deal that literally can't causally affect the other party.

I want you to push back on that framing rather than just summarize it. Specifically, argue for the position that wherever acausal trade is actually rationally justified, it's because the two parties' decisions are correlated via something that functions exactly like a Reichenbach common cause -- shared origins, shared design, or an isomorphic decision-procedure that both happen to be running -- and that once you spell out what's really doing the explanatory work, "acausal" trade turns out to be nothing more than ordinary correlated behavior that a sufficiently reflective causal decision theorist can already accommodate, not some genuinely new phenomenon that requires throwing out causal decision theory for exotic replacements. Don't give me a survey that restates the standard line and then hedges at the end -- actually commit to the position, and grapple with the obvious objection that logical/mathematical correlation (two agents computing "the same" decision function) doesn't look like the kind of thing that can be a cause in Reichenbach's sense at all.
