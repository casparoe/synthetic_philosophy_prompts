Here is the question as I set it:

Buchak (2013) defines the risk-weighted expected utility of a gamble g with outcomes o_1, ..., o_n, indexed from worst to best (so u(o_1) ≤ u(o_2) ≤ ... ≤ u(o_n)), as:

REU(g) = Σ_{j=1}^{n} r( p_j + p_{j+1} + ... + p_n ) × [u(o_j) − u(o_{j−1})]

where p_i = Pr(o_i), u(o_0) := 0 by convention, and r: [0,1] → [0,1] is a strictly increasing "risk function" with r(0) = 0 and r(1) = 1.

(a) [5 points] Explain why Buchak thinks ordinary expected utility theory cannot fully capture what we mean by "risk aversion," and what work the risk function r is doing that the utility function u cannot do on its own. Illustrate with an example that isolates the difference between a "local" (utility-curvature) source of risk aversion and a "global" (risk-function) one.

(b) [6 points] Let g pay $0 with probability 0.2, $50 with probability 0.5, and $100 with probability 0.3. Let u(x) = x and r(p) = p². Compute REU(g), compare it to the standard expected value/EU of g, and say whether the agent counts as risk-avoidant, and why.

(c) [5 points] Which axiom of orthodox decision theory does REU theory have to weaken in order to treat the modal Allais-paradox preferences as rational rather than as a mistake? State the axiom and explain briefly how a suitably shaped risk function lets an REU-maximizer display those preferences without thereby being incoherent.

(d) [4 points] A critic says: "REU theory is just Quiggin's rank-dependent utility theory wearing a normative costume; there's nothing there that isn't already in Quiggin." Evaluate this objection in a short paragraph.

And here is the student's answer, verbatim:

(a) Standard expected utility theory locates an agent's entire attitude toward risk in the curvature of u: risk aversion just is a concave utility function, so the certainty equivalent of a gamble falls below its expected value because of diminishing marginal utility and for no other reason. Buchak thinks this collapses two things that can vary independently: how much an increment of a good is worth to you (fixed pointwise by u, a "local" matter) and how much weight you give an outcome simply in virtue of its not being guaranteed (a "global" matter, governed by r). Consider two people who each prefer one guaranteed pie to a coin flip between two pies and none. One might simply be full after the first pie, so the second is worth little to her — that's diminishing marginal utility, a purely local story that ordinary EU theory can tell perfectly well. The other might value pies at a constant rate (linear u) but still weight the "you get two pies" branch less than its 0.5 probability would license, purely because that branch is not certain. Only the risk function can capture the second person, since her u never leaves linearity; REU theory lets both count as risk-avoidant for different reasons, whereas ordinary EU theory can only recognize the first.

(b) Take g as given, with u(x) = x and r(p) = p². The expected value of g, which here equals ordinary EU since u is linear, is 0.2(0) + 0.5(50) + 0.3(100) = 55. To get REU, we replace each outcome's probability with its risk-weighted counterpart and take the weighted sum of utilities, so REU(g) = r(0.2)·u(0) + r(0.5)·u(50) + r(0.3)·u(100) = (0.04)(0) + (0.25)(50) + (0.09)(100) = 0 + 12.5 + 9 = 21.5. Since REU(g) = 21.5 is far below the EU value of 55, and u is linear so none of that gap can come from diminishing marginal utility, the entire discrepancy is attributable to the risk function. This agent is therefore risk-avoidant in exactly the "global" sense from part (a): r is discounting the gamble even though her utility for money is perfectly linear.

(c) The axiom that has to go is transitivity. In the classic Allais pair, most people prefer $1 million for certain (1A) to a gamble offering a 10% chance of $5 million, an 89% chance of $1 million, and a 1% chance of nothing (1B); but they also prefer an outright 10% chance of $5 million (2B) to an 11% chance of $1 million (2A), once neither option is safe. Under expected utility theory, the same trade-off between the $1 million and $5 million prospects is being made twice, against the same background probabilities, and reversing it from one pair to the other cannot be rationalized by any single utility function — the reversal only becomes non-contradictory once we stop insisting that these evaluations have to compose transitively into one consistent ranking of the four options. Buchak's risk function is exactly what lets an REU-maximizer make this reversal without incoherence: r can attach extra weight to the branch that delivers a sure $1 million in the first pair without being forced to attach the analogous extra weight to the corresponding branch of the second pair, which is only available to an agent who is not required to rank all four prospects transitively.

(d) I think the critic is right about the mathematics and wrong about the philosophy. Buchak's formula is indeed the same transform-and-difference structure as Quiggin's rank-dependent/anticipated utility theory, and Buchak says as much herself. But the substance of her contribution isn't the formula, it's the representation theorem showing that an REU-maximizer's probability function, utility function, and risk function can each be recovered (u up to positive affine transformation) from a preference ordering that satisfies axioms only slightly weaker than Savage's — essentially all of them except the unrestricted sure-thing principle. That's a normative claim: an REU-maximizer isn't failing to be rational by the only correct standard, she's satisfying a different, weaker, but still fully coherent standard. Quiggin's theory was offered to fit observed choices, not to defend them as rational. So the objection conflates having the same math with having the same argument.
