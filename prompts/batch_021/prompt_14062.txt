So I'm in this small epistemology reading group that meets every other week (mostly people wrapping up MA's or prepping PhD applications), and after we covered the peer disagreement stuff -- Elga, Christensen, Kelly, that whole cluster on the equal-weight view versus just sticking to your guns -- a few of us went for food and ended up in a live version of the debate that I can't stop turning over. Maya and Josh are both fast at mental math, and they independently worked out everyone's share of the bill with tip and got different totals. Roughly in order, here's what happened:

(1) Maya said this was a textbook case for conciliationism: since neither of them has any special reason to think she's better at arithmetic than the other, finding out they disagree should push each toward the other's number rather than each just trusting her own math.

(2) Josh said that can't be the whole policy, because it would also commit you to giving equal weight to anyone who disagrees with you, including people who are obviously worse reasoners -- he brought up a mutual friend who trusts his fantasy football gut over the stats every single week. Nobody thinks you owe that guy 50% of your credence.

(3) Maya said that's exactly why the view only kicks in for genuine peers, and you're allowed to downgrade someone's peer status using considerations that don't just presuppose they're wrong about the very question currently in dispute -- track record, relevant skill, whether their method is independantly known to be reliable on similar questions. The fantasy football friend fails that test on independent grounds; Josh, who's never been shown to be worse at mental math than her, doesn't.

(4) Josh said fine, but conciliationism is itself exactly the kind of thing professional epistemologists -- who are obviously each other's peers -- disagree about all the time, and plenty of them land on something more like steadfastness. So by her own logic, shouldn't Maya conciliate her way down to a lot less confidence in conciliationism itself, maybe down to the point where she can't really assert it as flatly as she just did?

(5) Maya said maybe your policy for handling disagreement has to work as a kind of fixed point you reason from, not something you yourself conciliate about -- sort of like how you can still use logic to evaluate an argument that concludes logic is unreliable, without that being viciously circular. But she admitted this might just be a dressed-up way of exempting her own view from her own rule, and wasn't sure how you'd even tell the difference.

Neither of them budged and it kind of derailed the rest of dinner. Could you go through moves (1) through (5) and say who's got the better of it at each step, not just who wins overall? I'm especially curious whether move (5) is a legitimate response to (4) or just special pleading dressed up in an analogy, and whether there's something like a standard answer to this self-undermining problem that both of them are missing.
