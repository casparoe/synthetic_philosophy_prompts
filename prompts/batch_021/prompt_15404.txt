I'm a software engineer who builds perception pipelines for a small robotics project, and a few colleagues and I started an informal lunchtime reading group that wandered from predictive-coding models of vision into the philosophy-of-mind literature on "cognitive penetration" -- the question of whether what a perceiver believes, wants, or fears can directly alter what they perceptually experience, rather than merely altering what they later judge, remember, or attend to. I have no formal training in either philosophy or vision science, so I've mostly been relying on secondary summaries, and I keep running into the same pattern: almost every famous example I come across turns out to have a serious skeptic arguing that it is really just a shift in attention, a memory contamination, a response bias, or a post-perceptual judgment call dressed up as a change in seeing. I gather the hard-line position, associated with Fodor, is that perception is "informationally encapsulated" and essentially untouchable by cognition, but the live disagreements in the field seem to be fought case by case rather than at that level of generality.

Since I can't adjudicate the primary literature myself, I'd like your help sorting the following purported cases by how strong the case is that each one reflects genuine cognitive penetration of perceptual experience itself, rather than being better explained by attention, memory, response bias, or ordinary post-perceptual judgment:

1. Children from poorer families judging physical coins to be larger than children from wealthier families judge the same coins to be.
2. Hungry participants rating pictures of food as bigger or more vivid than satiated participants rate the same pictures.
3. Fatigued participants, or participants wearing a heavy backpack, judging a hill or staircase to be steeper than rested participants judge it to be.
4. A gray, banana-shaped cutout looking faintly yellow to viewers who know that ripe bananas are yellow.
5. Speakers whose language has a dedicated word for a particular color category discriminating two shades that straddle that category's boundary faster than speakers whose language lacks the word.
6. Trained wine tasters describing a white wine that has secretly been dyed red using typical red-wine vocabulary rather than white-wine vocabulary.
7. Thirsty participants judging a bottle of water to be physically closer to them than an equally distant, thirst-irrelevant object.
8. Radiologists perceiving an abnormality "pop out" of a scan within a fraction of a second, in a way untrained viewers cannot at the same fixation point and exposure duration.
9. Participants who stand to win money if an ambiguous figure is read as a letter rather than a number reporting seeing the letter more often than participants who face the opposite incentive.

Please give me your answer as a JSON object with four keys -- "genuine_penetration", "probably_penetration", "probably_not_penetration", and "not_penetration" -- each mapping to a list of the item numbers above, ordered from strongest to weakest case within each list, formatted as follows:
