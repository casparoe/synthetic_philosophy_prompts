"Mill's stance on socialism moved a long way over his lifetime. In the 1848 first edition of the Principles of Political Economy he was openly hostile to the socialist and communist writers of his day, treating private property, and the wage relationship that goes with it, as basically permanent features of any advanced economy. By the time he drafted the (unfinished) Chapters on Socialism at the end of his life he had moved a long way toward the socialists: he argues the wage system is probably only a transitional arrangement, and that some form of collective control of capital may eventually prove both fairer and, once habits adjust, more productive. Tellingly, though, Mill stays much keener on cooperative schemes -- worker-owned, profit-sharing firms of the kind already being tried in France and England -- than on full communism, since cooperatives keep reward tied to individual effort while communism's equal shares regardless of contribution risk killing off the incentive to work at all. He never gives up the Malthusian worry, already present in the Principles, that no redistributive scheme can survive unchecked population growth. And true to his liberal instincts, he insists any move toward socialism has to come gradually and by voluntary experiment rather than revolutionary imposition, a conclusion he explicitly draws from his reading of Marx's account of factory conditions in the first volume of Capital. So although Mill keeps paying lip service to individuality and liberty of thought, by the end of the Chapters he has essentially landed in the same place as Marx, just by a slower and more cautious route."
