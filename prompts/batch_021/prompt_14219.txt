I'm reporting a feature on the strange afterlife of Gottlob Frege's reputation, prompted by how unsettled historians of philosophy still seem by the private diary he kept in 1924, the year before he died: in it, the founder of modern quantificational logic writes approvingly of authoritarian rule and argues that Jews should be deprived of political rights, in striking tension with the detached, rigorous persona that scholars like Michael Dummett spent decades establishing for him in English-language philosophy. Before I start interviewing sources, I want to pressure-test the two camps I keep running into so that neither gets flattened into a caricature in print. Please write a dialogue between two Frege scholars: one who holds that the diary is morally damning but philosophically inert, with no more bearing on the soundness of his logicism or the sense/reference distinction than Newton's biblical chronology has on his mechanics, and one who thinks the same cast of mind that made Frege so contemptuous of psychologism, and of grounding logical truth in convention or consensus, is not so easily walled off from the cast of mind behind the diary's politics. Have them engage each other's strongest point directly rather than talk past one another, and don't let either position come out looking like the easier one to hold.
