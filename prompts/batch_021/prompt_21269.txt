I'm about two-thirds through a novel where a big emotional question hanging over the back half is whether the reader's still supposed to be angry at my narrator's dad -- a guy who walked out on the family after years of drinking that everyone in the book agrees started because of his own pretty brutal childhood. I brought the relevant chapter to my biweekly writing group (we meet at this diner near me, nothing fancy) and it kicked off a side-argument between two other people there, Ines and Callum, thats been rattling around in my head ever since. I can't referee it myself, so I'm hoping you can.

Ines opened (1) by saying that once you trace the causal chain back far enough -- his dad, his dad's drinking, the whole inherited mess -- resenting the guy personally stops making sense; it's like being mad at weather. Callum pushed back (2) with something he half-remembered from a philosophy class, some old P.F. Strawson essay about what Strawson calls "reactive attitudes" -- resentment, gratitude, all the stuff that makes up ordinary relationships. Callum's version was that fairness doesn't require the dad to be some uncaused first-mover of his own personality -- it just requires normal adult capacities to register and respond to reasons, minus whatever specific excusing or exempting stuff actually applies to him -- and a bad childhood isn't automatically exempting, or literally no one would ever be responsible for anything, since everyone's got *some* causal history behind them. He also said you can't actually keep treating someone you're still in a relationship with as a pure weather-system to be managed rather than someone to be resented or forgiven -- that's not a more clear-eyed version of the relationship, it's a different relationship, or none at all. Ines wasn't done (3): fine, she said, maybe that's an accurate description of how our attitudes work, but it doesn't tell her whether they're *fair* -- maybe people are just built to be unfair to other people with sufficiently sad backstories, and the fact that she can't help still resenting the dad doesn't prove the resentment's deserved. Callum's comeback (4) was that fairness attaches to whether the guy had those ordinary reason-responsive capacities *at the time* -- he held down jobs for years, had long lucid stretches, and chose to keep drinking through some of them -- which is exactly the ordinary weak-willed case the whole framework's built to handle, not some special new exemption. Ines's last word, which we never resolved because it was somebody else's turn to get workshopped, was that addiction is supposed to mess with exactly the reason-responsive wiring Callum's now leaning on, so who's to say those "lucid" stretches were as free as they looked.

Could you go through this move by move -- (1) through (4) plus Ines's dangling addiction point -- and say who's got the better of it at each individual step, not just who wins overall? I'd also love to know what you think both of them are missing, and, since Callum was working from half-memory, whether he's even representing Strawson's actual argument fairly or fudging it in his own favor. (And no need to tell me how to handle the dad in revision -- I just want the argument itself sorted out.)
