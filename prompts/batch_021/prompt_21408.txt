A friend and I recently had a long argument about how Leibniz himself -- not a modern defender of his views -- would respond to Max Black's 1952 thought experiment describing a universe containing nothing but two qualitatively identical spheres. My friend insisted that Leibniz's only real recourse is the move he already made against Newtonian absolute space in his correspondence with Clarke: deny that a "shifted" or otherwise indistinguishable arrangement of the universe is a genuinely distinct possibility in the first place, so that Black's two-sphere scenario never arises as a coherent alternative to a one-sphere universe. I argued that this maneuver secures the identity of indiscernibles only across possible worlds, and leaves open what Leibniz would say once we grant, for the sake of argument, that such a symmetric universe is actual, and ask whether its two occupants count as genuinely distinct substances given his own commitments about relational space and about bodies as mere phenomena bene fundata ("well-founded phenomena") grounded in monads. We never resolved it. Working strictly from Leibniz's considered metaphysics, rather than the standard contemporary gloss on the identity of indiscernibles, how do you think he would actually respond if confronted with Black's scenario: would he abandon the principle, block the scenario by some route other than the shift argument, or dissolve the puzzle entirely by denying that spheres could ever count as substances in his sense? Defend your answer with reference to specific commitments from his correspondence with Clarke and his mature monadology.
