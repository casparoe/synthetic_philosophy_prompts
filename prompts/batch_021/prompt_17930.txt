This is my first term TAing a course on my own — my advisor, who usually runs it, is on research leave this year — and I don't yet have a confident sense of how strict to be about certain kinds of error, so before I commit to a grade I'd like an independent read on one answer from the take-home final. The course is an advanced undergraduate seminar on the ethics of war, taken by a mix of philosophy majors and a few history and political science students fulfilling a requirement, and one question asked students to reconstruct G. E. M. Anscombe's argument, in "Mr. Truman's Degree" (1956), that Truman was guilty of murder for his role in the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, and that Oxford was wrong to award him an honorary degree for it.

Here is the question as it appeared on the exam:

"Reconstruct Anscombe's argument, in 'Mr. Truman's Degree,' for the claim that Truman was guilty of murder. Be sure to explain (a) the distinction she relies on between intending a death and merely foreseeing it, and (b) why she thinks the number of lives that might have been saved by ending the war quickly cannot settle the moral question. Then state, in your own words, the strongest objection to her argument, and say whether you find it persuasive."

And here is the student's answer, in full:

"In 1956, Oxford's governing body voted to award former U.S. President Harry Truman an honorary doctorate. Anscombe, then an Oxford philosopher, opposed the award and had a pamphlet, 'Mr. Truman's Degree,' privately printed and distributed to make her case. Her central claim is that Truman is guilty of murder, and that a university willing to honor him shares in denying it.

The argument turns on the distinction, familiar from the doctrine of double effect, between intending a bad outcome and merely foreseeing it as a side effect of doing something else. Anscombe would allow that a state can permissibly kill civilians as an unintended side effect of, say, bombing a munitions factory that happens to sit near houses. What she denies is that this is what happened at Hiroshima and Nagasaki. Since it was obvious in advance, given the size of the bombs and the fact that they were aimed at whole cities rather than isolated installations, that huge numbers of civilians would certainly die, Truman must be said to have intended their deaths rather than merely foreseen them: a death you know for certain will occur is a death you intend, whatever you tell yourself about your purposes. This is also why she thinks the lives-saved calculation is beside the point. Even granting that the bombs shortened the war and saved more lives overall than an invasion would have cost, this could at most show that Truman brought about a good result by an act of murder, not that no murder was committed, since on her view the numbers never bear on whether an act falls into a category that may not be done at all, whatever the consequences. It is worth noting that this is the same 1956 pamphlet in which Anscombe first coins the term 'consequentialism' for the view she is attacking here — the view that no description of an act, including 'killing the innocent,' can rule it out independently of its consequences.

The strongest objection, I think, is that the intending/foreseeing distinction is doing more work than it can bear, because it depends on how the act gets described. Truman's defenders could just as easily redescribe his intention as 'ending the war as quickly as possible,' with the civilian deaths falling under mere foresight — exactly as Anscombe redescribes the factory case to make the workers' deaths foreseen rather than intended. If intentions are this redescribable, it isn't clear the distinction can do the moral work she needs it to do. I find the objection serious but not decisive: Anscombe's likely reply is that not every redescription is equally available, because the intention has to fit the actual plan of action. Destroying the population of the city was the mechanism by which the war was supposed to end, not an unfortunate by-product of pursuing that end some other way, so 'ending the war quickly' is not a rival description of the same plan — it is a description of the further end the plan served."
