A few months ago a shipment error at the small distribution company where I work turned into a two-week argument about whose fault it was, and it left me quietly obsessed with something I later learned has an actual name in philosophy. The dispute kept circling around sentences like "if the manifest wasn't double-checked, that's why the pallet went to the wrong dock" and "if the manifest had been double-checked, the pallet wouldn't have gone to the wrong dock." Those two sentences feel almost interchangeable in ordinary speech, but the more I turned them over the more they seemed to be doing different jobs: one is a claim about what, given what we now know, must have happened, and the other is a claim about some alternative version of that afternoon that never actually occurred.

Curious, I went looking and found that this maps onto a real distinction that logicians and philosophers of language draw between indicative and counterfactual (or subjunctive) conditionals. I even ran across the famous pair of sentences about the Kennedy assassination that apparently gets used to show the two aren't just stylistic variants of each other: "if Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, someone else did" reads as true, while "if Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have" reads as false, even though on the surface it looks like the same conditional with the verb tense shifted. From what I could gather, without being able to follow all the technical detail, people like Robert Stalnaker and David Lewis analyze the counterfactual one in terms of some "closest possible world" where the antecedent holds, while the indicative one gets treated quite differently by people like Frank Ramsey and Ernest Adams, tied instead to how probable the second half is given the first half rather than to truth in some other imagined scenario at all.

I don't have any formal training in logic or philosophy, so I can't judge how much weight this distinction can really bear, but it seems to me like it should matter a great deal for something very mundane: how ordinary people assign blame and reconstruct "what really caused" a bad outcome once it has already happened, whether that's a workplace incident like mine, a car accident, or just private regret over a decision. Could you describe the most important implications of the indicative/counterfactual distinction for exactly that kind of after-the-fact causal and moral reasoning? I'd especially like to understand whether the two kinds of conditional can genuinely diverge in truth value or warrant in a way that lets two people, each using "if" correctly by their own lights, end up in a dispute that no amount of arguing can resolve until someone notices they're using two different tools, and whether there is any principled way, in the heat of a disagreement like the one at my company, to tell which of the two kinds of "if" is actually the right one for settling who or what was responsible.
