I'm a retired physicist, and since I left the lab a few years back I've been spending a lot of my free time finally reading through classic papers I always meant to get to but never had time for. Last week I sat down with Turing's actual 1950 paper, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," instead of just the pop-science version of the Turing test everybody knows, and objection nine (the very last one, right before the "learning machines" section) genuinely stopped me in my tracks. I knew he dealt with the theological objection and Lady Lovelace's, but I had no idea that near the end he brings up telepathy, clairvoyance, precognition and psychokinesis, and flatly says the statistical evidence for telepathy is "overwhelming," calling the ESP objection "quite a strong one" in his own words -- stronger, it seems, than most of the objections he spends rest of the section demolishing. My wife thinks I've lost the plot getting this worked up over a 70 year old computer science paper, but here we are.

What's been rattling around in my head ever since is the actual mechanics of the fix Turing proposes. He doesn't just wave the problem away: he works through a scenario where a telepathic human contestant could score suspiciously well guessing the suits of cards (something a machine limited to chance obviously can't match), then adds this odd extra wrinkle where the machine's own random-number generator, if it's a physical one, might get skewed by the interrogator's own psychokinesis, so that, in his words, "with E.S.P. anything may happen." His fix for all this is the famous "telepathy-proof room" -- basically a psychic Faraday cage. But a couple sentences earlier in that same passage he'd already admitted that thinking might be exactly the kind of phenomenon where ESP is "especially relevant," which sounds to me like he's half-conceding that ESP isn't just noise leaking in from outside the test, but is tangled up with the very thing the test is trying to measure.

So could you help me actually pick this apart, rather then just summarising the section back to me (I've read it closely, I don't need the highlights)? Specifically: what are the genuinely distinct ways ESP threatens the imitation game here -- I count at least three, the human receiver's advantage, the machine's RNG getting hijacked, and the interrogator possibly bypassing the whole question-and-answer setup via his own clairvoyance -- and does sealing everyone in a "telepathy-proof room" actually neutralize all of them, or only the easy ones? And is there a real tension between treating ESP as a shieldable side-channel versus Turing's own hint that it might be constitutive of thinking itself, or am I just over-reading a throwaway line in a paper he dashed off fairly informally?
