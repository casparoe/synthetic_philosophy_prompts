I teach moral philosophy and serve as the independent civilian member of my country's Conscientious Objector Review Board. Over dinner last week, a friend who studies international law and I spent nearly three hours arguing about whether an ordinary soldier can be held personally at fault for fighting competently in a war his own government started unjustly. His position was that a conscript or a junior officer is essentially never well placed to adjudicate the justice of the war he has been sent to fight, so that fighting within the laws of war cannot itself be a personal moral failing, whichever side he is on. I kept objecting that once a soldier can see plainly enough that his country is waging a war of outright conquest, "I only followed the judgment of people better placed to decide" starts to look like an evasion rather than an excuse. Neither of us gave ground, but the argument turned out to be usefully timed, because on Monday I have to bring a live case to the board.

The applicant is a reserve officer, five years into his service, currently mobilized for an active deployment. He is explicit that he is not a pacifist: he says he would deploy without hesitation for a clearly defensive war or a UN-mandated mission, and that his objection is only to this particular campaign, which he describes, citing his own government's stated justifications, as a war of annexation dressed up as self-defense. Our governing regulation grants conscientious objector status only to those opposed to "participation in war in any form," and board practice has always been to deny this kind of selective objection, on the grounds that letting soldiers pick and choose their wars would make military discipline unworkable and would hand political judgments to conscripts who have no special standing to make them. Of the board's five members, two want to deny the claim squarely on those grounds, one wants to grant it outright on the ground that ordering someone to fight in a war he sincerely and reasonably believes to be a war of conquest is already hard to justify, and the rest haven't committed. I need to circulate a short written recommendation before Monday's session. It should reach a definite recommendation -- grant, deny, or some third option such as reassignment to non-combat duties -- give reasoning the other members could actually respond to, and take the strongest version of whichever side it doesn't adopt seriously, rather than dispatching it with a restatement of the regulation.
