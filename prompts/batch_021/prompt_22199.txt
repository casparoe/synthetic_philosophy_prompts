Helen Marsh, my philosophy professor, died three weeks ago, and her family has asked me to give the eulogy at her memorial this coming Saturday. She never married and had no children, and over the last several years I think I ended up closer to her than almost anyone except two old friends from her graduate school days. I should say up front that I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher -- I only took her seminar because a friend said it would be an easy elective, and I ended up staying on for two years afterward as her research assistant, writing the randomization and blinding code for a couple of her experiments on people who claimed psychic abilities, because she insisted that if you were going to test these claims at all, you had to test them properly. By reputation, and by her published record, she was one of the most exacting debunkers in her corner of the field, and she caught at least one fairly well-known medium in an embarrassing fraud. What almost nobody outside her closest circle knew, and what she only explained to me on the long nights we spent running trials, was that she'd spent something like thirty years on this work not out of simple skepticism but because of a problem she traced back to Sidgwick and called, only half-jokingly, "the dualism of practical reason": the idea that she couldn't make morality fully binding on a rational person without some further fact about the universe -- a "moral order" guaranteeing that duty and self-interest ultimately coincide -- and that she refused to just assume that fact on faith the way her own churchgoing relatives did. So instead she went looking for evidence. She never found anything she was willing to call conclusive, and she was scrupulously public about that failure, even though it must have cost her something.

Here's the complication I'm wrestling with. Back in graduate school, she and those two friends -- both elderly now, and both coming on Saturday -- apparently made a pact, deliberately modeled on one Sidgwick himself made with his own circle, that whichever of them died first would try to find some way of signaling the other two. As far as I know, in the three weeks since she died, nothing has happened that she herself would have accepted as evidence of anything. Her wider family, who loved her but never really understood the research, will also be in the room, and I suspect a few of them are hoping I'll say, in some form, that she's "found her answer now" or "is watching over us." I don't want to stand up in her name and say something she would have considered intellectually dishonest, especially about the one question she spent her whole career refusing to answer cheaply -- but I also don't want to deliver eight minutes of methodological caveats at her funeral. Could you write the eulogy I should actually give? It should run to about seven or eight minutes read aloud, be honest about how the search really went, make an actual decision about whether and how to mention the pact, and still send her off in a way that feels like a goodbye rather than a seminar.
