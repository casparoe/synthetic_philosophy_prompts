I've been falling down a bit of a Gödel rabbit hole for a piece on the odd private theologies of famous 20th-century logicians, and the thing that's really hooked me is his correspondence with his mother Marianne, plus the "Max Phil" notebooks that Hao Wang draws on in A Logical Journey. Gödel apparently worked out this quasi-argument for an afterlife that isn't religious in the usual sense at all — something like: either the world is rationally ordered and everything in it, including a human mind, has a meaning, in which case it would be absurd for that mind to spend decades slowly developing itself only to be switched off right as its getting somewhere, so some kind of continuation must exist; or the world just isn't rational in that way, in which case the whole question (and honestly a lot of scientific reasoning too) loses its footing anyway. That's obviously a different animal from the famous ontological proof of God he wrote down late in life, and different again from the "mind vs. machine" disjunction from his Gibbs lecture, but all three seem to come out of the same rationalist, Leibniz-flavored place, and I keep getting them tangled up in my head.

Next month I'm taping an interview for the podcast with a Gödel scholar who's spent real time in the Nachlass, and I don't want the episode to just be forty minutes of "wow, isn't it fun that a genius secretly believed in ghosts." Can you draft me 8-10 interview questions that actually dig into the substance? I'd like at least one that forces her to spell out the logical structure of the afterlife argument rather than just gesture at it, one that presses on whether his mathematical Platonism is doing real argumentative work there or is more like vibes, one that gets her to sort out how this argument relates to the ontological proof and the Gibbs lecture disjunction, one on why this "rationalistic theism" apparently led him to dismiss most institutional religion and even academic philosophy of religion as kind of sloppy, and one — gently, but not skippable — on the irony that a man whose whole optimism rested on "the world is rational" ended up dying of self-starvation because he'd become too paranoid to trust anyone preparing his food. For each question, add a quick note on the answer she's most likely to reach for first, plus a sharp follow-up so it doesn't just land as a rehearsed soundbite.
