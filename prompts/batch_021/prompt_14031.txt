I'm a novelist several drafts into a book set in 1951 in Kessenau, a small Central European country I've invented that spent the previous decade under a one-party regime modeled loosely on the Third Reich. The chapter I'm currently stuck on turns on a scenario that will be familiar in outline to anyone who has read the Hart-Fuller exchange: during the war, my protagonist's aunt denounced her husband to the secret police for a bitter joke he made about the regime while home on leave; he was tried and sentenced to death under statutes that were, by every formal test then in force, validly enacted law, though his sentence was commuted to a penal battalion and he survived. It is now 1951, and a small civilian tribunal -- on which my protagonist's mentor, a meticulous and rather severe judge named Anton Reiff, sits as the junior member -- must decide whether the aunt can be prosecuted for what she did, given that her act was lawful under the very statutes the new republic has since repudiated as monstrous.

I'd like the novel to get the jurisprudence right rather than gesture at it -- my editor keeps saying the chapter reads like a seminar rather than fiction, but I'd rather nail the reasoning first and worry about trimming it for drama later. So I'm hoping you can draft the internal memorandum Reiff would circulate to the other two judges before they rule: the kind of document a careful, slightly self-doubting judge writes to talk himself into a position he will then have to defend out loud. It should settle on one concrete disposition of the case -- dismissal, conviction under a newly enacted and openly retroactive statute, conviction on the theory that the wartime statute was never valid law to begin with, or some other option you think Reiff would genuinely consider -- and it should lay out a real procedure and timetable for the panel to reach a verdict before the case draws public attention. I don't want a memo that labels the two sides "positivism" and "natural law" and stops there; I want it to do the work Hart and Fuller actually did, including something like Fuller's claim that a legal order which betrays the inner morality of law -- publicity, generality, non-retroactivity, and the rest -- forfeits its claim to be treated as law at all, set against Hart's worry that this move lets the tribunal dodge the fact that it is choosing, right now, to punish an act that was lawful when it was performed. Please write the memo itself, in Reiff's voice, addressed to "the panel," and long enough to actually argue the point -- something like 500 to 700 words.
