I'm putting together a new unit on the ethics of influence for a seminar I'm teaching this term, and a story a former student told me over coffee last week has stuck with me as a possible case study. I couldn't give her as crisp an answer as I would have liked in the moment, despite having read a fair amount over the years about advertising, nudges, and similar topics.

She's now a mid-level manager at a software company, and her division recently needed to switch to a new project-management tool that most people were, at best, indifferent about. Rather than making the case for the switch himself at the team meeting, the division head privately asked two of the most respected senior engineers, whose judgment everyone in the room deferred to, to mention during the meeting, as though the thought had simply occurred to them on the spot, that they had already tried the tool and liked it. Neither the division head nor the two engineers ever disclosed that this had been arranged beforehand. He also had the sign-up sheet for switching over projected on the shared screen for the rest of the meeting, so that everyone could watch the list of names slowly fill in. By the end of the week, nearly the whole division had signed up. Nobody was lied to about the tool itself, which genuinely was an improvement, and no one who held out was threatened or penalized in any way.

My former student couldn't shake the feeling that something underhanded had happened, though she struggled to say exactly what, given that no false claims were made at any point. I'd like your help thinking through this with more precision than I managed at the time. Did the division head do anything worse than what any competent person does when trying to win over a skeptical room, and if so, what exactly makes it worse? I'm especially interested in whether the answer turns on his concealing his role in prompting the engineers' remarks, on his use of the visible sign-up sheet, on both, or on neither, and whether you can distill from this some general principle that would let my student sort future cases like it without having to re-litigate each one from scratch.
