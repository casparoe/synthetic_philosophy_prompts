I'm working on a novel whose central section takes place almost entirely inside a truck cab, on a two-hour night drive across a stretch of desert highway, and I want to use that confinement to stage a real philosophical argument rather than atmospheric small talk. Here is the setup: my protagonist, Vera, has had her car die forty miles short of town and is picked up by a stranger, Nadia, who mentions almost immediately that she's done this before and been stiffed every time — covered a tow, handed over gas money, never saw a cent of it back once the other person was safe in town with a working bank card. Nadia teaches decision theory at a community college and has since decided, half as a joke and half not, that she won't finish driving a stranded stranger into town unless she becomes genuinely persuaded, before they arrive, that the stranger will pay her back even though nothing will be able to force it. Vera, who left a philosophy PhD without finishing it, recognizes the shape of the situation at once — she's effectively been handed a live version of what decision theorists call Parfit's hitchhiker — and can't resist saying so.

Write the dialogue between them for the rest of the drive, in prose, with narration and dialogue tags as it would actually appear on the page, not as a labelled transcript, running to something like a page or two. Nadia should argue the hard-nosed line — pure causal decision theory, or what Gauthier calls straightforward maximization — that once they're in town, with the ride already given and nothing left to enforce, paying up would be a pure loss with no return, so a rational passenger, however sincerely she talks in the cab now, simply won't do it once she's safe, and Nadia has no business believing otherwise. Vera should answer with something in the spirit of Gauthier's constrained maximization or McClennen's resolute choice: that the rational move was made back at the roadside, in deciding to become someone who follows through, and that reopening the question in town isn't clear thinking but evidence of never having really made that decision. Push them past assertion and counter-assertion: I want Nadia to land the obvious objection — that "I should keep it because I decided to" starts to sound like rationalized stubbornness rather than rationality — and I want Vera to have an actual answer, not a restatement, perhaps distinguishing her view from the more recent move of simply calling the rational act "whatever the best policy would output," the way updateless or functional decision theorists do, though she shouldn't be allowed to drop that label in place of an argument. Somewhere in the exchange I'd also like them to confront the wider paradox head-on — that Nadia's own theory of rationality seems to want everyone to become exactly the kind of person that theory calls irrational once the money's actually due — and I'd rather the drive end in a genuine standoff than have either woman win.
