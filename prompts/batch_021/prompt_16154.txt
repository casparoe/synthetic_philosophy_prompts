I'm a PhD student in philosophy specializing in mathematical logic, and this term I'm TAing a graduate seminar on Gödel's incompleteness theorems while also sitting in on an "agent foundations" reading group run by some computer scientists down the hall — a combination that has been more distracting from my dissertation than I'd like to admit. In the seminar we treat Löb's theorem as a slightly exotic strengthening of the second incompleteness theorem: for a theory T with enough arithmetic, if T can prove the conditional "if T proves P, then P" for some sentence P, then T already proves P outright, and the unprovability of T's own consistency falls out as the special case where P is taken to be falsum.

In the reading group, the very same theorem resurfaces under the name "the Löbian obstacle." Yudkowsky and Herreshoff's old MIRI report on tiling agents uses it to argue that an agent reasoning in a fixed formal system can never fully trust a successor — or even its own future self — that reasons in that same system, because doing so would require the agent to prove a soundness schema for the very system it relies on, and Löb's theorem shows that this is impossible unless it already has access to a strictly stronger logic than the one being certified. Their proposed workarounds all involve some kind of escalating or otherwise restructured hierarchy of proof systems across successive versions of the agent, and by the authors' own admission none of them is fully satisfying.

What I can't stop wondering is what Gödel himself would have made of this specific obstacle, given how carefully he policed the philosophical morals he was willing to draw from his own theorems. In the 1951 Gibbs lecture he was explicit that incompleteness supports only a disjunction — either the human mind outstrips the deductive power of every finite machine, or there are absolutely unsolvable mathematical problems of a certain kind — and while he says he finds the first disjunct the more plausible one, he is careful never to claim that incompleteness by itself refutes mechanism outright. On Hao Wang's report, he also seems to have thought of the mind as something that develops over time rather than as a fixed, static system, which is part of why he resisted identifying it with any single machine. I'd like your reconstruction of how he would have reacted to the Löbian obstacle, grounded in what he actually wrote and is reliably reported to have said rather than his general reputation as an incompleteness theorist. Would he have read it as a small vindication of the disjunction — evidence that a reasoner confined to one fixed formal system really cannot bootstrap full self-trust, so that anything like an "unlimited" artificial reasoner would need to be dynamically self-transcending in something like the way he speculated the mind might be — or would he instead have pushed back, on formal or philosophical grounds, against reading a theorem about proof systems as telling us anything substantive about agents at all?
