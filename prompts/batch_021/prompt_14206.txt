Aristotle has these two discussions of money that I feel like never really get put together. In Nicomachean Ethics V he says that for an exchange to be just, the goods involved have to somehow be made "commensurable," and money is basically a stand-in for need (chreia) that makes this possible -- but he also points out that money is called nomisma because its worth rests on nomos (convention) rather than nature, so its kind of arbitrary and changeable by fiat. Then in the Politics he says charging interest is the most unnatural way of making money, because money's whole job is to facilitate exchange, and interest (tokos, which literally means "offspring") is basically money breeding money against its own nature.

What I can't tell is whether these two bits actually cohere into one real theory of value/exchange (need-based, maybe? there's that famous Marx footnote reading Aristotle as groping toward a labor theory of value and failing because Greek society ran on slavery), or whether Aristotle's just being inconsistent -- treating money's value as mere convention whenever thats convenient for his argument, and as tracking some real underlying equality whenever thats convenient instead. Can you write an essay working through the main ways of resolving this and then actually pick one answer, rather than just listing the options? Don't hedge -- tell me which reading you think is right and why.
