Two people in a reading group I'm part of (we meet up to keep arguing about whatever our phil of language seminar didn't finish covering) spent a whole session on Bradley's regress and its still bugging me because I don't think either of them actually won. Quick setup in case it matters: the puzzle is what makes "Socrates is wise" a unified, truth-evaluable proposition rather than just the pair {Socrates, wisdom} sitting next to each other -- and Bradley's old worry is that any relation you introduce to tie the two together will itself need a further relation tying it to its terms, forever.

Roughly how it went:

(1) Deniz said Frege's fix -- concepts are "unsaturated," objects "saturate" them, so predication needs no extra glue -- is a non-answer. Calling the concept unsaturated just names the fact that it combines with objects, it doesn't explain how.

(2) Ana said that's uncharitable: the point of unsaturatedness is to deny predication is a relation between two free-standing items that could then need a further link. A concept is gappy by nature, so an object filling the gap isn't some further achievement that needs its own glue -- there's nothing for a regress to grab onto.

(3) Deniz said fine, but why believe there are two fundamentally different kinds of entity here rather than just objects all the way down? He brought up how even Frege gets tangled trying to say what a concept is -- try to refer to one ("the concept horse") and grammar instantly turns it back into an object -- and took that as proof "unsaturated" is a label for the mystery, not a fix for it, since Frege can't even state his own view without lapsing into the picture he's rejecting.

(4) Ana said the awkwardness cuts the other way: of course naming a concept turns it into an object, that's exactly what function/argument logic predicts, not a confession that the view is broken.

(5) Deniz brought up the newer move of treating propositions as predication-acts rather than abstract structures, like maybe that does better, then talked himself out of it: asking what makes "predicating wisdom of Socrates" one act instead of two acts performed side by side is the same question as what unifies concept and object, just moved from metaphysics into psychology. Stipulating predication is "inherently" combinatorial isn't better than stipulating concepts are "inherently" unsaturated.

(6) Ana said the two primitives aren't equally cheap: Frege invented unsaturatedness just to solve this one problem, whereas treating predication as a basic asymmetric act is backed up independently by how perception and judgment seem to work generally (seeing something as red looks like applying "red" to it, not entertaining red and the thing separately). Even if both views end in a primitive, she thinks one is earning its keep elsewhere and the other isn't.

They just went in circles after that. Can you go through this move by move and say who's got the better of it at each numbered step, plus flag anything both of them are missing? And give me your own take at the end: does either move actually defuse Bradley's regress, or has everyone here just found a fancier place to hide the same primitive?
