My wife Miriam died two weeks ago after a long illness, and I'm giving her eulogy this Saturday. She taught Latin American literature for over thirty years and had one long-running obsession: Borges's essay "A New Refutation of Time," the one where he builds a genuinely careful argument, leaning on Hume's bundle-of-perceptions self and Berkeley's denial of matter, that there's nothing for time's "succession" to happen to, so time in the strong sense isn't real. I'm a retired physicist -- spent my whole career on statistical mechanics and the thermodynamic arrow of time, and only got seriously into philosophy after I retired -- so for years I'd needle her that entropy settles the question, and she'd always turn to the same page: the essay's ending, where Borges admits the argument doesn't actually save him from anything, he's still stuck being himself, still "the tiger" and "the river," and the world is, in his own words, "unfortunately real." Miriam always said that reversal was the one truly honest paragraph Borges ever wrote. Saturday's room is half her old literature department, who know that essay cold, and half our kids, grandkids, and my physicist friends, who've never heard of it. I don't want to reach for the cheap version of the argument and tell everybody time isn't real so she isn't really gone -- she'd have seen through that in two seconds flat, its exactly the move that essay refuses to let you get away with. Can you write the eulogy, about 5 minutes read aloud, that takes the whole arc seriously, argument and collapse both, and still actually comforts a room full of non-philosophers?
