So the AI assistant our office started using for scheduling and travel stuff kicked off a whole argument between two coworkers, Greg and Mira, and it's been going for days now.

It'll check your calendar, compare flights, draft an email to your manager for sign-off, wait for the reply, then actually book the ticket, all on its own once you say go. Greg says watching it chain those steps together proves it's an agent now, it's got something like a goal and it's pursuing it. Mira says that's a category error: it's just predicting the next likely bit of text, even when that text happens to be an email or a tool command, so "having a goal" is really just it simulating a character who has one, no actual goal required. Greg comes back with: the effects are real though, money actually gets spent, emails actually get sent, and if you judge a thing by what it does rather than what's happening inside it, it's an agent, full stop -- same standard we already use for animals or other people whose heads we can't see into either. Mira says fine, but then a thermostat's an agent too, since it also acts to bring about a state it "wants," and that can't be right. Greg says the thermostat isn't modeling itself or planning several steps ahead, that's the real difference. Mira's comeback is that multi-step planning is still just next-token prediction stretched over more text, there's no seperate "planner" bolted in underneath, so its simulation all the way down no matter how many steps it fakes. Greg's last word was that by that logic humans don't really plan either, since underneath it's all just neurons firing, so her own argument proves too much.

Can you go through this move by move and say who's got the better of each point, not just who wins overall -- and is there some bigger thing they're both missing that would've ended this faster?
