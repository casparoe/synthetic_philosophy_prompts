I spent my working life as a population geneticist, and since retiring I have been sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group, which has left me trying to work out how much of my old training survives contact with the literature on the metaphysics of race. Below are notes toward a short piece I have been asked to present to the group, and I would welcome your comments before I take it further.

The result I was raised on is Lewontin's, from his 1972 survey of classical genetic markers: the great majority of human genetic variation, something on the order of 85 percent, occurs within any single population rather than between the populations traditionally sorted into races, with only a small residue attributable to the racial grouping itself. I took this, as most people in my field did, to settle the question of whether race has any genetic reality worth taking seriously. What I had not appreciated is how much weight the philosophical literature puts on a complication raised by Edwards, under the label "Lewontin's fallacy": Lewontin's statistic describes variation locus by locus, and a low value at every single locus is compatible with near-perfect classification once many loci are used jointly, because the classifying power lives in the correlations among loci rather than in any one locus's variance. Rosenberg and colleagues' clustering studies from the early 2000s are usually cited to make the point vivid: an ancestry-blind clustering algorithm, run over enough markers from a worldwide sample, tends to recover clusters that track the major continental groupings fairly well. So the clean inference I learned as a student was not exactly wrong, but it was not the whole story either.

The move I am less sure how to evaluate is what some philosophers of race do with this complication. They use it to argue that race can still be a "kind" in a fairly robust sense, by helping themselves to Richard Boyd's homeostatic-property-cluster account of natural kinds. That account was built originally to explain how biological species can be real without necessary and sufficient membership conditions: a species is held together by underlying mechanisms such as interbreeding and shared selective pressures that keep a cluster of properties co-occurring even though no single property is essential. The race version substitutes social mechanisms, such as endogamy, segregation, and the historical machinery of racial classification itself, for the biological ones, while letting the ancestry-correlated genetic clustering just described do the work of keeping the resulting pattern statistically real.

What bothers me is not the genetics, which I think I now understand, but the transplant. Boyd's machinery was built for clusters that hold together independently of what anyone believes about them: a species does not reorganize itself once biologists start classifying it. Race, on Hacking's and Haslanger's tellings, is not like that: the classifying is part of what produces the pattern, through self-identification, intermarriage, and the shifting boundaries of official categories. That sounds much closer to what Hacking calls a looping effect than to anything in Boyd's original cases. So it looks to me as though calling race a homeostatic-property-cluster kind buys back the respectability of natural-kind talk while quietly changing what is doing the clustering: from causal mechanism to socially mediated meaning.

I would be glad for your comments on three things in particular: first, whether I have the genetics and its history right, or am still oversimplifying either Lewontin or his critics; second, whether the worry in my fourth paragraph is a genuine problem for the homeostatic-cluster strategy, or whether I am missing a way to make the social mechanisms do the same work as the biological ones without equivocating; and third, whether bringing in a kind with no biological angle at all, such as money, would sharpen the argument, or would simply confirm that race was never comparable to a species-like kind to begin with.
