For a while now I've been reading about the replication crisis in my spare time, and I keep running into the claim that it traces back to a genuine disagreement between frequentist and Bayesian statistics, not just a difference in preferred formulas. Specifically, several things I've read assert that you can construct a data set where the p-value comfortably clears the conventional 0.05 threshold, while a careful Bayesian analysis of that same data ends up putting most of the posterior probability on the null hypothesis being true. I understand the basic definitions of p-value and posterior probability well enough, but I can't picture concretely how the two could pull in genuinely opposite directions on identical evidence rather than one side simply being mistaken. Could you construct a specific, realistic example — with an actual sample size, effect, and stated prior — where this happens, explain in plain terms what feature of the two frameworks makes such a split possible, and say a little about why this kind of case is relevant to arguments about the replication crisis?
