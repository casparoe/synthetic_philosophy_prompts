I spent thirty-seven years as a metallurgist, running failure-analysis and quality-control tests on structural alloys for a living — deciding whether a batch of steel or aluminium actually had the properties on the spec sheet: fatigue-resistant, non-brittle, corrosion-resistant under salt spray, and so on. Since retiring I've taken up reading philosophy for fun (don't ask why a metallurgist ends up in a philosophy reading group, it's a long story involving a grandson and a used bookstore), and a few weeks back the group somehow landed on the metaphysics of dispositions, which I'd never given a seconds thought to despite basically building a career on them. I definately thought I had this one figured out already: something has a disposition just in case the right conditional is true — a metal is brittle iff it would fracture if you struck or bent it past some threshold. That's more or less the whole philosophy behind every test rig I ever built. Then I read about "finkish" dispositions and so-called masks, and now its bugging me.

A fink is something like a dead wire hooked up to a gadget that senses the instant a conductor touches it and makes the wire live right then, so it conducts even though a second earlier it had no disposition to at all — or the mirror case, a genuinely live wire that gets killed the instant it's touched, so it never conducts despite really being disposed to. A mask is a different animal entirely: nothing about the object's makeup changes and the disposition stays fully intact, but something extra in the setup quietly blocks the manifestation anyway — a wine glass that's exactly as fragile as ever but happens to be sitting in a nest of styrofoam when you drop it, or a poison you swallow along with an antidote that stops it killing you even though it's just as toxic going down as it always was. Finks and masks break the simple "would-happen-if" test in opposite directions, and it needles me precisely because that test is what my entire professional life was organized around — I now can't shake the thought that a rig full of "passed" results might not actually track the properties I thought I was measuring.

So here's my ask: write me an essay that actually settles this for me rather than just touring the landscape. Can some more careful patch on the conditional analysis — holding the causal basis fixed for a bit after the trigger, tacking on "under normal conditions" clauses, or whatever the best move is — survive both finks and masks, or do these cases show that dispositions can't be defined away into conditionals at all, and have to be taken as primitive, unanalyzable causal powers instead? Go through the strongest arguments on each side, but don't leave me with "thoughtful people disagree" — tell me where you'd actually land, even if the honest answer is something qualified like "it survives finks but not masks."
