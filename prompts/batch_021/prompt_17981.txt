Over the winter break I went through a box of my late grandfather's wartime papers that my grandmother had kept for almost seventy years: flight logs from his service as a radar operator on B-29s in the Pacific, a handful of letters home, and, folded in among them, newspaper clippings about Hiroshima and Nagasaki that he had evidently saved but never once mentioned to anyone in the family. This term I am TAing an upper-level "Ethics of War" seminar for the first time, and coming across those clippings in the same month that I have been grading take-home exams on Anscombe's "Mr Truman's Degree" has made an assignment I designed as a fairly dry exercise feel considerably less abstract than it did back in September. Before I enter a final grade, I would like a second, disinterested reading of one student's answer, since I have now read it several times and keep changing my mind about how good it actually is.

The exam question read: "In 1956, Elizabeth Anscombe circulated a pamphlet, 'Mr Truman's Degree,' opposing Oxford's plan to award Harry Truman an honorary doctorate because of his decision to use the atomic bomb against Hiroshima and Nagasaki. Reconstruct the structure of her argument. Your answer should explain (a) why her objection is not simply a pacifist's objection to killing as such; (b) what work the doctrine of double effect does for her, and how she uses it to distinguish the bombings from a permissible act of war; and (c) how this episode connects to her later, more general treatment of 'consequentialism.'"

Here is the student's answer, in full: "Anscombe's pamphlet was a protest against Oxford's plan to award President Truman an honorary degree, on the grounds that he had ordered the atomic bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. Her basic charge was that this amounted to honoring a mass murderer: the bombs killed overwhelmingly civilian populations, and for Anscombe the intentional killing of innocent people is absolutely forbidden, no matter what good end it is supposed to serve. In this respect her position was essentially pacifist: she thought that once you are willing to count enough enemy lives as an acceptable price, or to kill noncombatants in order to shorten a war, you have adopted a philosophy with no real limits, one that will justify almost any atrocity provided the numbers come out right. This is why she was so unimpressed by the argument, common at the time, that the bombs saved lives overall by making a land invasion of Japan unnecessary: for Anscombe, this kind of trade-off reasoning is itself the problem.

Central to her case is the doctrine of double effect, which holds that an act with a bad effect, such as civilian deaths, can be permissible provided that effect is not the intended means to the good end but only a foreseen side effect, and provided the good achieved is proportionate to the harm done. Anscombe uses this principle to explain why she is not committed to condemning all killing in war: a soldier who fires on an enemy position and foresees that a nearby civilian may die has not thereby murdered anyone, so long as the civilian's death was not the point of the attack. She argues that Hiroshima and Nagasaki cannot be justified on these terms, because the two cities were themselves the targets: the deaths of their inhabitants were not a side effect of some other military objective but the very mechanism by which Japan's surrender was supposed to be secured. That, for her, is enough to make the bombing an act of murder rather than an act of war, regardless of how many lives it might have saved on net.

This same suspicion of weighing goods against evils resurfaces two years later in 'Modern Moral Philosophy' (1956), where Anscombe coins the term 'consequentialism' for any theory that makes the rightness of an act depend entirely on its consequences. She argues that consequentialism, taken seriously, cannot recognize any action as absolutely forbidden, not even the judicial execution of an innocent person, since a consequentialist can always in principle be shown a case where breaking the rule produces better results. The Truman pamphlet is really a preview of this complaint: Anscombe thought the men defending the bombing were reasoning exactly like consequentialists, and that this style of reasoning is itself a symptom of what she elsewhere calls a corrupt mind, whatever conclusion it happens to reach."

Could you grade this on a twenty-point scale and, separately from the number, tell me precisely what is and is not working in it? I would like the comments broken out by the three parts of the question, (a), (b), and (c), plus one line on overall accuracy, formatted as a short JSON rubric; I am trying to hold myself to citing the specific sentence that earns or loses credit rather than giving an overall impression, and it would help to see that done once by someone with no stake in whether this particular student did well.
