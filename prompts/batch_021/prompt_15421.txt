Last week a friend and I spent far too long arguing about how to split a dinner bill. We had each worked out our share, tip included, by mental arithmetic, arrived at different totals, and then found ourselves arguing not about the arithmetic itself but about how much each of us should defer to the other, given that we regard one another as roughly equally reliable at that kind of calculation. The disagreement quickly became a larger argument about what you owe someone, rationally, when a person you have every reason to regard as your epistemic peer disagrees with you. My friend defended the conciliationist line that peer disagreement obligates you to move your credence substantially toward theirs; I argued for something closer to the steadfast view, that it can be entirely rational to hold your ground if you trust your own reasoning. Neither of us budged, which struck me afterward as not without irony, since it left an obvious question hanging: if my friend's own view says that disagreement should pull you toward the middle, and she and I are, by her own lights, peers on this very question of how to handle disagreement, doesn't her position commit her to moving toward mine?

I'd like you to write a dialogue between a conciliationist and a defender of the steadfast view that works through a case built like ours: a mundane, repeatable calculation -- splitting a check, say -- carried out independently by two people with matched track records who arrive at different answers and must decide how much ground to give. Don't let it end in a tidy truce. Have the steadfaster press the conciliationist hard on the self-undermining worry that occurred to me afterward -- that applying conciliationism to the disagreement about conciliationism itself seems to require abandoning it -- and give the conciliationist a genuine rejoinder rather than a quiet concession.

I'd also like the two of them to work through how one would ever establish, without begging the question, that two people really are peers on a given issue rather than one quietly being better positioned, and whether the entire dispute secretly turns on taking a side on the uniqueness thesis -- whether a body of evidence rationally licenses only one doxastic response -- as against allowing that equally rational people can land in different places given the very same evidence. Keep both voices equally capable, and don't let either one land an unanswered knockout blow.
