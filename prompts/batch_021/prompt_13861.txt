I don't have any background in philosophy, but a minor incident during a home repair last year set off a line of thinking I haven't been able to shake since. An electrician we had never used before told us that our house's wiring had an unusual fault requiring an expensive same-day fix, and I realized, standing there deciding whether to pay him, that I had no independent way of checking whether any of this was true. All I had to go on was his word, a license number, and a few good reviews online, and even those only carried weight with me because I trust the licensing board and the review site to be honest — which is, again, just more people's word for it. Trying to work out why I paid him anyway, I went looking online and found that this is apparently a genuine, long-running dispute in epistemology, usually traced back to a disagreement between Hume and Reid, over whether believing what someone tells you is its own basic source of knowledge, on a par with perception or memory, or whether it always has to be backed up by your own independent evidence that people of that kind, on matters of that kind, tend to be reliable.

The more I have turned this over, the more it seems like both sides have a real problem rather than just an intuition against them. The view that testimony always needs independent backing (I gather this is called reductionism) seems to run into a regress: for most specialists, the only "evidence" I could ever gather for their reliability is itself more testimony — credentials, referrals, licensing boards — so the independent check never actually bottoms out in anything I verified myself. It also sits oddly with how small children take in enormous amounts of testimony, including their entire first language, long before they could possibly have assembled any such evidence. But the opposing view (anti-reductionism, if I have the label right), on which we are simply entitled by default to believe what we are told unless something specific tips us off, seems to leave the door wide open to any sufficiently confident and polished talker, and I cannot work out what is supposed to stop that from just collapsing into gullibility.

Could you write me a dialogue between a committed reductionist and a committed anti-reductionist about testimony, set in my kitchen right after the electrician has left, in which they argue over whether I was being reasonable? I would like both of them to be equally sharp and to actually engage with each other's strongest point rather than restating their own view past one another — and I would especially like to see the reductionist pressed on the regress problem and the child-testimony case, and the anti-reductionist pressed on what, if anything, keeps default trust from sliding into gullibility toward a confident charlatan.
