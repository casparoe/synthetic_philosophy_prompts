A friend and I spent much of a recent dinner arguing about whether it even makes sense to be angry at a machine. The dispute started when she admitted to feeling genuine resentment toward an airline's AI chatbot after it answered a simple request with a curt, unhelpful non-answer, and I objected that resentment is simply the wrong category of response to something like a chatbot -- you might as well resent a vending machine for swallowing your coins. She countered that a large language model isn't obviously like a vending machine, since it produces the kind of fluent, apparently purposive behavior we ordinarily treat as grounds for holding an agent responsible in the first place, so perhaps our reactive attitudes are tracking something real even there. Neither of us could reconstruct P. F. Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" in enough detail to settle the point, though we agreed it was the obvious place to look.

I'd like you to do what we couldn't. Please reconstruct Strawson's framework with some care: his distinction between the "participant" attitudes we take up toward fellow agents (resentment, gratitude, indignation) and the "objective" attitude we take toward things to be managed, treated, or simply avoided; and his further distinction between conditions that merely excuse a particular piece of conduct without changing our overall view of the agent ("he didn't realize what he was doing," "he was pushed") and conditions that exempt the agent more globally, tipping us into the objective stance across the board (extreme immaturity, psychological abnormality, and so on). Bear in mind, too, his central naturalistic claim: that our commitment to the participant stance is too deeply woven into human life to be rationally dislodged by any purely metaphysical consideration, such as the truth or falsity of determinism. Given all this, how do you think Strawson himself would have judged a sufficiently sophisticated conversational AI: is such a system, even in principle, a possible object of the participant attitude, or must our stance toward it remain permanently objective, however humanlike its outputs become? Please commit to one answer on Strawson's behalf, rather than simply listing considerations on each side, and justify it using his own concepts and arguments rather than more recent AI-ethics literature he never encountered.
