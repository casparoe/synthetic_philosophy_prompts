So in my AP Gov class we've been doing a unit on democratic theory and my teacher brought up this thing called the "boundary problem" that kind of broke my brain a little. The idea is that before you even get to HOW a democracy should decide stuff (majority rule, whatever), theres this earlier question of who actually counts as "the people" doing the deciding in the first place, and apparently that question can't really be settled by a vote because you'd need a demos to already exist to vote on who's in the demos, which is circular. She mentioned there's basically two rival answers to this: the "all affected interests" principle (if a decision affects your interests at all, you deserve some kind of say in it) and the "all subjected to coercion" principle (you only get a say if you're actually bound by the coercive power of the law being made). Apparently these two line up most of the time but pull apart hard in cases like cross-border pollution, where the people getting hurt (say, a country downwind of another country's coal plants) aren't subject to the polluting country's laws at all, so the coercion principle would seem to exclude them even though their interests are obviously affected a ton.

This whole thing got real to me bc our class had this huge argument about whether green card holders should be allowed to vote in local elections and nobody could actually explain WHY citizenship is the right cutoff, everyone just kept repeating their gut reaction. So could you write me a dialogue between two philosophers, one who's all in on the all-affected-interests principle and one whos all in on the all-subjected-to-coercion principle, arguing it out over a specific case (the cross-border pollution one, or a different one if you think it makes the contrast sharper) to see who should actually count as part of the demos? I want them to really engage with each other's strongest objections instead of just restating their own view over and over, and if its possible, id like it to end with at least some sense of whether one principle is more defensible than the other, or whether they're just stuck talking past each other.
