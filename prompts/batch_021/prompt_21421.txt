so i've been down a rabbit hole with decision theory thought experiments, and the one stuck in my head lately is "Death in Damascus" - the version where Death is basically a near-perfect predictor who's already picked, ahead of time, which of two cities (Damascus or Aleppo) he'll be in tomorrow, and you're in Damascus right now trying to decide whether to stay put or run to Aleppo, knowing that whatever you pick, Death has probably already guessed it right. the "official" answer a lot of decision theorists seem to land on is that neither pure choice is stable - picking one city makes the other look safer once you factor in what that pick itself tells you - so the only move that actually holds up is to flip a coin and let chance decide.

around the same time i've been reading about Chrysippus's "co-fated" comeback to the old Lazy Argument, the one that says its pointless to call a doctor since your recovery is fated either way. Chrysippus said some fated stuff is "co-fated" with your actions - your recovery is fated to happen together WITH the doctor visit, not around it, so the visit still matters. he used pretty much the same move to defend omens and divination, arguing that knowing what's coming doesn't make your reaction to it pointless.

i haven't seen anyone actually connect these two, so i want to try it on you: if you sat Chrysippus down and walked him through Death in Damascus, what do you think he'd actually tell you to do - flee, stay, or something else - and would his co-fated move genuinely get him out of the puzzle, or does the "near-perfect predictor" part break it in a way an ordinary doctor visit never has to face? i want your real reconstruction of the reasoning he'd use, not a "he'd probably say something about fate" cop-out.
