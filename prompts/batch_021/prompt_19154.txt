I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on the history of decision theory, currently writing a chapter on Daniel Bernoulli's 1738 resolution of the St. Petersburg paradox: replace monetary value with a concave "moral" utility — he used the logarithm of wealth — so that the game's expectation comes out finite. I want to test that move against a puzzle it was never built for. In Nover and Hájek's Pasadena paradox, a fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads on toss n, paying (-1)^(n+1)·2^n/n for that outcome; unlike St. Petersburg, whose expectation simply diverges to infinity, the Pasadena series is only conditionally convergent, so by Riemann's rearrangement theorem its "expected value" can be made to equal any real number, or none, just by resumming the same equiprobable outcomes in a different order. Suppose the Pasadena game were put to Bernoulli himself, reasoning solely with what was available to him in 1738 — over a century before Riemann proved that reordering a conditionally convergent series can change its sum, and two centuries before Menger showed that any unbounded utility function harbors its own Petersburg-style gamble. Would Bernoulli recognize Pasadena as posing a genuinely new problem for his diminishing-marginal-utility proposal, or would he apply the same fix and miss that something different, and worse, has gone wrong? Reach a definite verdict on what Bernoulli himself — not a modern decision theorist ventriloquizing him — would actually have concluded, and defend it by appeal to the specific resources and blind spots of his own argument.
