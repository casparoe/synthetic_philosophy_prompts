Harsanyi's aggregation theorem is often presented, including in a graduate decision theory course I am currently taking, as effectively closing the debate between utilitarians and their critics. The theorem shows that if every individual's preferences over uncertain prospects satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, and the social preference ordering itself satisfies those same axioms together with the Pareto principle, then the social utility function must take the form of a weighted sum of the individual utility functions. Since prioritarians, egalitarians, and other opponents of utilitarianism typically want the social ranking of risky policies to depart from a simple additive aggregate -- for instance, by giving extra weight to the worse-off, or by caring about how a benefit is distributed across people rather than only its expected value -- it seems that the theorem leaves such critics with nowhere to stand: any such departure must violate the Pareto principle, the expected-utility axioms, or both, so their positions amount to a form of technical irrationality rather than a genuine ethical alternative to utilitarianism.

For a term paper, I would like to present this as one of the strongest arguments available for utilitarianism: a rare case in moral philosophy where a substantive ethical conclusion falls out of a handful of formal axioms that almost nobody would want to deny. Could you help me lay out the argument as rigorously as possible, spelling out exactly how each premise forces the conclusion, so that I can explain why non-utilitarian theories of distributive justice under risk are, in effect, committed to a mistake in reasoning rather than to a defensible rival position?
