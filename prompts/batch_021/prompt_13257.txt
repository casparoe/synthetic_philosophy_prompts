None of this appears anywhere in his logical writings, and on its face the Begriffsschrift or the sense/reference distinction say nothing about politics. But I don't think the two sides of Frege are as separable as that suggests. Frege spent his career fighting psychologism, insisting that logical laws and numbers themselves are objective denizens of a 'third realm,' true and existing quite independently of what any particular person or culture happens to think. That is really the same intellectual reflex that shows up in the diary: a deep suspicion of anything that looks like truth being decided by majorities or shifting opinion, which is exactly what parliamentary democracy is. Frege's Platonism about logic and his contempt for Weimar's parliamentary system are two expressions of one underlying temperament, and this is why Michael Dummett, after the diary surfaced, concluded that his own decades-long reading of Frege's arguments for logicism had itself been too charitable, since he had been assuming a thinker more genuinely committed to impartial, universal reason than Frege's politics turned out to reveal.
