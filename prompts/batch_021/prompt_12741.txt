I'm a Classics and Philosophy double major, and I'm also the student member of an ad hoc committee our Faculty Senate convened, at the Provost's request, to decide whether the university should add a mandatory "late-career capacity review" to our existing post-tenure review policy. Right now every tenured professor is reviewed on the same age-neutral five-year cycle; the proposal on the table would additionally require anyone who turns 75 to undergo a targeted review of their continued fitness to teach and to supervise graduate students. The proposal exists because one professor's serious decline went unaddressed for years, since nothing in current policy would have flagged it before real harm was done to his students; but the committee keeps getting stuck on a second case, raised by the "no" side, of a professor of the very same age who is by any measure still at the top of her field, and who would obviously sail through such a review while feeling, not unreasonably, insulted at having been made to take it. We're split roughly down the middle: half the committee thinks an age trigger is just ordinary prudence, no different in kind from what airlines or several judiciaries already do; the other half thinks singling out faculty by birth year is exactly the sort of thing our own nondiscrimination policy exists to forbid, however reasonable it might sound in any single case.

This term I'm also taking a seminar on Cicero's philosophical dialogues that just spent two weeks on De Senectute, and it's turned out to be more useful for the committee's problem than I expected, though less tidily than I expected too. Cato spends most of the dialogue rebutting the standard complaints about old age, but he doesn't deny that bodies genuinely decline -- he concedes it outright and argues the decline is beside the point for the capacities his own late-life role actually requires. He also tells the story, almost in passing, of Sophocles' sons hauling him into court to have him declared unfit to manage his own property, a case that was apparently settled not by reference to his age at all but by Sophocles simply reading the jury the play he happened to be finishing at the time. And separately, Cato is unexpectedly insistent that the irritability and stinginess people blame on old age are really just the faults of particular men's characters, visible long before anyone went grey. Two of us argued after class about whether that adds up to any coherent view of when, if ever, age itself should be allowed to trigger scrutiny, or whether Cato is just an unusually persuasive old man making the best possible case for his own situation.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate to the committee before our next meeting, about a page long? It should reach a specific recommendation -- adopt the age-75 review, retain the current age-neutral cycle as the sole mechanism, or propose a concrete alternative, including what it should actually involve, how often, and starting when -- and the reasoning should put Cicero's arguments to real use rather than cite them for color. Because the committee is genuinely split, I'd also like it to give a fair and specific account of the strongest reason for whichever option it doesn't end up recommending, detailed enough that people on that side would recognize their actual position in it rather than a caricature of it.
