I'm the student head of my high school's Philosophy Club. Each week a different member writes a short "tutorial-style" question for the rest of us to answer in twenty minutes, closed-book, and an officer does a first pass at grading before our faculty sponsor checks it over. This week's question, written by one of our seniors, was:

"Explain the rule-following paradox that Kripke presents in *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language*. What, precisely, does the 'skeptical solution' concede to the skeptic, and what does it withhold from him?"

Here is the response I've been asked to grade, from one of our members:

"Kripke's puzzle starts from an apparently reasonable request: name the fact about me that made it true, in the past, that I meant addition by '+' rather than some other function. He dramatizes this with the 'quus' function, ⊕, which is defined to agree with plus for every pair of numbers small enough that I've actually added them before, but which returns 5 for any pair involving a number I've never summed, such as 68 and 57. Since I have only performed finitely many additions in my life, nothing in my past use of '+' seems able to rule out that I always meant quus, so that 5, not 125, was the 'correct' answer all along.

The obvious response is to say that meaning isn't fixed by past performance but by a disposition covering all cases, performed or not. Kripke has two replies. First, my actual dispositions are as finite as my performances: past some size of number, or once fatigue or death intervened, I would stop answering at all, so my dispositions underdetermine the infinite function 'plus' just as much as my history did. Second, and more importantly, even a disposition that matched plus perfectly on every input would only tell us how I am disposed to answer, not how I ought to answer; meaning is a normative notion, and a disposition to err is still just a disposition, not a mistake by its own lights. So no fact about my past behavior, dispositions, or introspectible mental states fixes the meaning of '+'.

Wittgenstein's skeptical solution, which Kripke presents as continuous with Hume's treatment of causation, does not try to beat the skeptic at his own game by finding some overlooked fact. It grants that the skeptical argument is correct: there is no such fact. What it denies is that this concession is any cause for alarm, because our practice of ascribing meaning was never really answerable to facts of that kind in the first place. Instead, what makes it correct for me to answer '125' is that this is the answer the rest of my linguistic community would also give, and would endorse me for giving; a deviant answer would eventually get me corrected, or excluded from being treated as a competent speaker at all. 'Jones means addition by plus' comes out true because Jones's use agrees with the community's, and it is this agreement, not any fact about Jones's own mind or history, that meaning-claims are really tracking. This is also why Kripke thinks a solitary speaker, with no community to agree or disagree with him, could never really mean anything by his words."

Our rubric splits 20 points as follows: 5 for correctly stating the paradox (including why no finite fact about past use settles which function was meant), 5 for explaining why an appeal to dispositions can't rescue a "straight" solution, 8 for correctly characterizing exactly what the skeptical solution does and doesn't concede to the skeptic, and 2 for clarity and organization.

Could you grade this against that rubric?
