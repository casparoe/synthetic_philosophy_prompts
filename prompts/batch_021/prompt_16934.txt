A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about Wittgenstein's *On Certainty* -- specifically, about whether "hinge propositions" amount to anything philosophically new, or whether they are just foundationalism wearing different clothes. She pointed out that hinges like "the earth has existed for a long time" or "here is a hand" seem to do exactly what Cartesian and Moorean foundations do: they are held immune from doubt so that everything else can be tested against them. I wanted to insist that the hinge and river-bed metaphors mark a genuine break from foundationalism, especially given Wittgenstein's insistence that such certainties are ultimately groundless rather than self-evident, but I couldn't make that difference precise enough to convince her. Please compare Wittgenstein's hinge propositions with the more general foundationalist strategy of halting the regress of justification via privileged basic beliefs. In particular, can something "groundless" still do the regress-stopping work that foundations are supposed to do, and if so, is "foundationalism" really the right label for what Wittgenstein is up to, or is he offering a genuine alternative to the justificatory picture that foundationalism presupposes?
