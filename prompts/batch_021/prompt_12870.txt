Quick background in case it's not fresh: the simple conditional analysis of dispositions says x is disposed to give response r to stimulus s iff x would give r if it underwent s (glass is fragile iff it would break if struck). Martin's fink example kills this - a live wire hooked to a gadget that cuts the current the instant something touches it, so the wire is live (has the disposition) but "if touched it would shock you" comes out false, since touching is exactly what trips the gadget. Lewis's fix was to require an intrinsic causal basis the object keeps through the stimulus: you have the disposition iff there's some basis B you have right now such that undergoing s while retaining B would cause r. Finks get excluded because they wipe out B before it can do its causal work.

Then there's the other classic problem case, usually called masking or antidotes depending which paper you read (Johnston's term vs. Bird's poison example) - swallow poison plus an antidote and you don't die, but you're still poisoned, your basis B never went anywhere, the antidote just jams the pathway downstream. This is supposed to be a genuinely different animal from finks: finks touch the basis, masks leave the basis alone and interfere later in the chain. Lewis's fix handles finks but famously struggles with masks, since B is retained the whole time.

My claim to Theo: the fink/mask distinction isn't a fact about the world, it's just a byproduct of where you draw the boundary around "the object." Take a self-sealing tire - puncture it and the sealant lining immediately flows into the hole and plugs it before any real air loss happens. Is the tire disposed to deflate when punctured? Draw the box around the bare rubber wall and this is a textbook antidote case: the wall keeps its would-deflate basis, the sealant intercepts downstream. Draw the box around "the tire as sold, sealant included" and there's no mask at all, because that object's basis was never the deflating kind to begin with - it's just not disposed to deflate, same as how an ordinary rock isn't secretly fragile-but-masked just because it never shatters.

Theo's comeback: borderline cases don't sink the distinction. In Martin's wire and the poison case, the intervener (a remote gadget, a separately-swallowed chemical) is obviously not part of the object by any sane standard, so the line holds up fine where it counts, vagueness at the edges notwithstanding. I said "obviously not part of the object" is exactly the judgment doing all the work, and it's an interest-relative call, not a metaphysical discovery about intrinsic properties - which matters a lot since Lewis needs "intrinsic" to track something mind-independent, not just our packaging conventions.
