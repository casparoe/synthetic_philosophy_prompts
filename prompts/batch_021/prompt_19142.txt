I spent about thirty years doing probabilistic risk assessment for nuclear plants before I retired, and these days I fill my time reading moral philosophy, which is a whole new game for me. Right now I'm working through Parfit (Reasons and Persons, and I think there's also a paper he wrote with the economist Tyler Cowen on the same topic) and some of the climate economics literature it points to, the whole Stern-vs-Nordhaus fight over what discount rate to use. Everyone in this stuff seems dead certain that "pure time preference" — discounting a person's future wellbeing just because it comes later, not because of risk or growth or anything else — is basically an indefensible prejudice, no better than caring less about someone because they happen to live far away.

Here's what's been bugging me though, and my old reading-group friend (a retired economist) couldn't quite talk me out of it. In PRA we always treat a complex system as having some roughly constant hazard rate of catastrophic failure per unit time, which gives you the exact same exponential survival curve as radioactive decay, the classic e^(-λt). It seems to me any civilization, or any person's continued existence, sits under exactly that kind of hazard: plague, asteroid, whatever, theres always some background rate of the whole enterprise just ending. So "the probability that a future good ever actually arrives" is already decaying exponentially in precisely the way a positive discount rate assumes, before you even get to growth or diminishing marginal utility. Doesn't that mean pure time preference isn't some irrational bias after all, just ordinary Poisson bookkeeping, the same law that tells me how much cesium-137 is left in a fuel rod after thirty years also tells me how much of the future's wellbeing "survives" to be worth counting now? So why do Parfit and this whole crowd write as if discounting is a moral failure of imagination rather than just decent risk accounting? Am I missing something, or did they just not think this through?
