My uncle Ron is retiring this spring after 38 years teaching economics, and I got roped into giving the toast at his retirement dinner. Thing is, Ron's whole career he's been the guy who explains the paradox of voting to anyone who'll sit still -- how the odds of your vote actually being the one that decides a real election are so close to zero that, by his own rational-choice math, the couple hours it costs to go vote almost never pays for itself. He's not being edgy about it, he genuinely believes the math. Except he's also personally worked as a precinct volunteer every single election since Reagan and has probably dragged a few hundred neighbors to the polls over the years by nagging them one by one. I'm a software engineer, not a decision theory person, but I've sat through enough of his dinner-table lectures to know I can't just get up there and say the cliché "every vote counts, it might be the deciding one" line, because thats literally the naive view his whole career was built on debunking -- and half the room, his old grad students and department colleagues, will wince if I say it anyway. The other half is just neighbors and cousins who only know him as the annoying guy with the clipboard every November. Can you write the toast, something like 4-5 minutes read out loud, that actually uses the paradox of voting as the backbone of the tribute instead of a one-line joke, and finds an honest way to make sense of a guy who's whole intellectual life said voting doesn't rationally pay off but who spent 40 years personally making sure everyone around him did it anyway?
