I'm a PhD candidate on my university's research-misconduct inquiry panel — seated there because policy requires the panel to include someone of the respondent's own rank — and the respondent is a postdoc accused by his supervising PI of fabricating results in a co-authored paper. Partway through deliberations, we learned that the PI herself was found, twelve years ago at another institution, to have quietly excluded inconvenient outliers without disclosure — classified there as a "detrimental research practice" rather than misconduct, in a finding never made public. The panel has split: some members want to treat her history as disqualifying, invoking the equitable rule that a party with "unclean hands" forfeits standing to seek relief, and would pause the inquiry pending a ruling on whether she may bring the charge at all; others insist fabrication is either in the data or it isn't, that decade-old conduct elsewhere is beside the point, and that dwelling on it is a tu quoque our filing deadline cannot absorb. Draft the short memo I'll bring to next week's meeting: it should recommend concretely how the panel should treat the standing objection and how the inquiry itself should proceed, and it should give whichever side turns out to be wrong real reasons to engage with, rather than a wave of the hand.
