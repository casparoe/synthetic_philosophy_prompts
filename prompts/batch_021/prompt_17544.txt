A friend of mine and I, both midway through a graduate seminar on social choice theory, spent most of dinner last week arguing about Harsanyi's social aggregation theorem, and neither of us left satisfied. She holds that once you grant that individual and social preferences over lotteries both satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, and you accept even a fairly weak Pareto condition, you are more or less rationally cornered into utilitarian aggregation: social welfare has to come out as some positively weighted sum of individual expected utilities, and anyone who resists that conclusion while still endorsing the premises owes an account of which one they're willing to give up. I kept bringing up cases in the spirit of Diamond's coin-flip example—an indivisible good, two equally deserving claimants, and a social planner whose commitment to the ex ante Pareto principle seems to leave it unable to explain why an actual fair lottery is worth anything over other arrangements that happen to generate the same expected utilities—and argued that this shows the aggregative framework is blind to something we obviously care about: whether people are treated fairly as a matter of procedure, not merely of expected payoff. She wasn't moved, and countered that my position amounts to rejecting an ex ante Pareto improvement that both claimants would rationally have agreed to in advance, which struck her as an odd thing for a fairness-based view to be saddled with. We went back and forth until the restaurant closed and neither of us had budged.

I'd like to see this argument conducted properly, by two people who are sharper about it than we were. Could you write a dialogue between Nadia, who defends the ex ante Pareto principle and accepts the utilitarian aggregation Harsanyi's theorem delivers, and Simon, who thinks considerations of ex post or procedural fairness can justify departing from it even at some ex ante Pareto cost? I want them to respond to each other's actual arguments rather than just restate their own positions: Nadia should have to give a real answer to the intuition that a fair coin flip carries some value that outstrips the expected utilities it distributes, and Simon should have to explain how his view avoids simply rejecting a prospect that every affected party would have consented to beforehand. Please don't let either of them concede the point or come out the obvious winner—I'd rather see exactly where the disagreement stops being tractable than watch one side tidily dispose of the other.
