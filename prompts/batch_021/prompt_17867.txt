Me and a friend got into a whole thing the other night (started because I bailed on the gym again, lol) about whether "weakness of will" is even a real phenomenon or just a fancy name for normal economic behavior. She's way more into the behavioral econ side of things and kept bringing up Ainslie's hyperbolic discounting stuff -- like, how $20 in a week beats $10 today when you're looking at it from a month out, but the moment the $10 becomes available right now, the discount curves cross and the $10 suddenly wins, no extra irrationality required, its just baked into the shape of the curve rather than the exponential one econ 101 assumes. Her whole point was that this basically dissolves akrasia as a philosophical puzzle: there's no weird case of doing what you judge worse while still judging it worse in that very moment, its just that your preference itself flips as the reward gets close, full stop.

I kept trying to say that's not the puzzle Davidson was actually pointing at in "How Is Weakness of the Will Possible?" -- his thing is specifically about a single agent, at a single moment, doing A while still holding, right then, that B was better all things considered (that's basically what his conditional/unconditional judgment distinction, and the whole "principle of continence" business, is for). If Ainslie's model is just describing your preferences changing across different time-slices of you, that seems like a totally different animal from "same time, same judgment, wrong action anyway" -- more like changing your mind than being weak-willed. I couldn't fully articulate why that distinction should even matter though, and she just said I was being pedantic and that philosophers love making simple things complicated for a living.

So can you build the argument I was reaching for? Specifically: argue that Ainslie's hyperbolic discounting model, whatever it explains in behavioral economics, does not actually explain (or explain away) weakness of will in Davidson's sense -- because it's a model of diachronic preference reversal across successive "selves," not of the synchronic conflict between judgment and action that's the actual philosophical puzzle. Give me the strongest version of that argument, not a wishy washy "well both sides have a point" answer -- I want actual ammo for round two of this argument.
