I've spent enough time on LessWrong and Astral Codex Ten that "that's just an ad hominem" has become an almost reflexive response whenever someone brings up a speaker's own conduct instead of engaging their argument, and I'd always filed "you're a hypocrite, so you don't get to say that" under the same heading: the tu quoque fallacy, textbook material from any critical-thinking course. Whether an action is wrong doesn't depend on who happens to be pointing it out, so noting that a critic doesn't practice what they preach looked to me like a pure non-sequitur, a way of dodging the actual question on the table. This came up concretely last week when a friend and I argued about a politician who preaches austerity while living lavishly at public expense: I maintained that the hypocrisy was strictly irrelevant to whether the austerity policy itself is sound, and that anyone tempted to think otherwise was just committing a basic logical error.

Then I went looking for how philosophers actually treat this and found an entire literature on "the standing to blame" — G.A. Cohen's essay on who has standing to condemn terrorists, R. Jay Wallace's work on hypocrisy and moral address, and a cluster of more recent papers building on both — that treats a hypocrite's loss of standing as a real and independent phenomenon rather than as a fallacy at all. These philosophers seem perfectly willing to grant that the hypocrite's underlying accusation is true, while still insisting that the hypocrite has no business being the one to level it. That looks to me like exactly the move the fallacy is supposed to rule out as illegitimate. So which side should give way here: are these philosophers dressing up a tu quoque in respectable jargon, or was the critical-thinking-class version of the fallacy too quick, and if the latter, what exactly does it get wrong?
