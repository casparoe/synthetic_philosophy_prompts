A friend and I got into a long argument last night about Aristotle's sea-battle passage and what it does or doesn't show about fatalism. She defended the standard supervaluationist treatment of future contingents — the Thomason-style semantics built on branching time — as the way to block the fatalist argument in De Interpretatione 9 while keeping classical logic fully intact. That struck me as sleight of hand: if "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is neither true nor false today, then the disjunction "there will be a sea battle tomorrow, or there will not be" can't be true either, so excluded middle has quietly been abandoned, whatever the supervaluationist chooses to call it. Is my friend just wrong to insist she's preserved classical logic here, or is she trading on some distinction I'm missing that would let the disjunction come out true even though neither disjunct is?
