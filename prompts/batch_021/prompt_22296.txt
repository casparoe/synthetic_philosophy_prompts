Three months ago I switched jobs and started commuting into the city on a regional rail line where the platform queue is treated almost sacredly: people arrive early specifically to stand in a snaking line along the platform rather than cluster near where the doors will open. Last week a man in a suit walked past roughly fifteen of us who had been waiting for ten minutes and stepped onto the train the moment the doors opened, and I was startled by how angry this made not just me but several strangers nearby, two of whom actually said something to him. I brought up the incident at dinner with two friends, Naomi, who is finishing an MA chapter on David Lewis's 1969 book Convention, and Felix, a management consultant with a soft spot for game theory who is otherwise allergic to what he calls "philosophy for its own sake." What started as a throwaway comment turned into a surprisingly sharp argument about whether something as mundane as queue order really is a "convention" in Lewis's specific technical sense, and a week later I still haven't been able to settle it in my own head.

(1) Naomi opened by arguing that the queue is about as clean an instance of a Lewis convention as you could ask for. It arose the way Lewis says conventions typically do -- not by explicit agreement, but by precedent, with each day's boarding order setting an expectation for the next -- and it persists as a coordination equilibrium: a settled boarding order beats no order at all, everyone expects everyone else to hold their place, and everyone prefers to hold their own place on condition that everyone else holds theirs. It also satisfies Lewis's arbitrariness condition, since other systems (numbered tickets, a lottery, a free-for-all) would have worked just as well had that been what people came to expect instead. (2) Felix objected that this can't be the whole story: if the norm were merely an arbitrary equilibrium propped up by mutual expectation, jumping it should produce mild confusion at most -- the reaction you'd have if cars unexpectedly merged in the "wrong" alternating pattern -- not the outrage-on-a-stranger's-behalf we actually saw, which looks like a response to unfairness, something Lewis's apparatus of self-interest and expectation was never built to capture. (3) Naomi replied that the intensity is still compatible with Lewis: once a regularity is common knowledge and people have built real plans around it -- how early to show up, when they can stop watching the platform -- conformity generates something that functions like an entitlement, and the anger is just the felt cost of a frustrated, common-knowledge-backed expectation, not evidence of some separate notion of desert. (4) Felix pushed back that this proves too much: we rely just as heavily, and just as publicly, on the convention of which side of an escalator to stand on, yet violating that earns a dirty look at most, never a rebuke -- so mutual reliance can't be what's driving the difference. Something about queueing specifically, that it allocates a scarce good according to time already invested, looks like an independent fairness rationale, exactly what Lewis's arbitrariness condition was supposed to rule out of a genuine convention. (5) Naomi's last word was that unequal moralization doesn't show unequal arbitrariness: the escalator rule and the queue rule can both be arbitrary at the point of selection, and all Felix has shown is that some arbitrary conventions later get annexed by an independent moral norm about allocating scarce goods fairly. That would mean Lewis was right about what makes the queue a convention -- he just never claimed his theory also explains why some conventions come to feel like matters of justice while others don't.

Could you adjudicate this move by move? First give me a verdict as a JSON block of the form {"1": <score>, "2": <score>, "3": <score>, "4": <score>, "5": <score>}, with each score an integer from -2 (clearly mistaken) to 2 (clearly right); then briefly justify each score, referencing Lewis's actual conditions for convention where it matters. Finally, close with a short verdict on the question the exchange never quite resolves: does first-come-first-served queueing satisfy Lewis's definition of a convention, and if the moral charge around queue-jumping outruns anything in that definition, does that expose a real gap in Lewis's account, or is it simply a phenomenon the account was never trying to explain?
