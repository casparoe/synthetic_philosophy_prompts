My mother, a scholar of Madhyamaka Buddhist philosophy who spent something like thirty years almost entirely on one Indian thinker, Nāgārjuna, died two weeks ago, and I have been asked to deliver her eulogy this coming Sunday. Within her department she was best known for her devotion to what she called, half-affectionately, the catuṣkoṭi, or tetralemma — the four-cornered pattern (it is, it is not, it is both, it is neither) that Nāgārjuna uses throughout his root text, the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, and that he turns most pointedly on exactly the question everybody at a memorial service wants answered: whether a person who has just died still exists somewhere, or doesn't, or both, or neither. Some years ago, at a dinner with her graduate students, half-joking but clearly not entirely, she dictated what she called "the four things nobody is allowed to say at my funeral," and made them repeat it back to her like a catechism: that she's in a better place, that she's simply gone, that she's somehow both here and at peace, or that it's beyond words and we shouldn't try to say anything at all. I wasn't at that dinner. One of those students emailed me the list, word for word, as a condolence, and I can no longer pretend I haven't seen it.

The service will be small, maybe seventy people, and split unevenly: the large majority are family and old friends who have never heard the word "emptiness" used in this particular technical sense and simply want to feel, for eight or ten minutes, that someone has said something true and tender about a woman they loved; a smaller group are former students and colleagues who wrote dissertations under her on exactly this material and will notice at once if I quietly smuggle in one of the four forbidden claims, or if I use her other favorite idea — that conventional truth and ultimate truth are two registers, not one real fact and one polite lie — as a loophole instead of honestly. She would also have been furious at a eulogy that turned her own emptiness doctrine into a clever hiding place, a smug fifth answer dressed up as humility; she used to say that anyone who treats emptiness itself as some special, exempt truth has missed the entire point of the book. My brother, who has even less patience for any of this than I do, has told me twice not to "turn Mom's funeral into a seminar."

I write novels for a living, not philosophy, so I have more or less the opposite problem from the one she spent her career training students to solve: I can generally make a room feel something, but I don't yet trust myself to make it feel something that is also true by her own exacting lights. Could you write the eulogy for me? It should run about eight or ten minutes read aloud, and it needs to actually comfort the people in the first group without giving anyone in the second group grounds to corner me at the reception afterward and explain, kindly but at length, exactly where I went wrong.
