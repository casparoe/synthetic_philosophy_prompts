I write about the science and philosophy of decision-making for a general-interest magazine, and after a series I did last year on wrongful convictions, I was invited to serve as the sole public, non-lawyer member of my state's newly convened Jury Reliability Commission. The legislature set the commission up, in the wake of chronic hung juries and a growing appeals backlog, to recommend whether the state should change how criminal verdicts are reached; it owes a single, specific recommendation ahead of next month's public session, covering both jury size (keep panels at twelve, or shrink them) and the verdict threshold (keep strict unanimity, or move to a supermajority such as ten-of-twelve or nine-of-twelve).

Two commissioners have reached an impasse I'd like the memo to actually adjudicate rather than smooth over. One, a statistician, invokes the Condorcet Jury Theorem: if each juror is even marginally better than a coin flip at judging guilt, larger panels deciding by simple majority will converge on the correct verdict far more reliably than small panels bound by unanimity, which she argues mainly manufactures hung juries rather than added accuracy. A second commissioner, a former public defender, thinks this proves too much, for two reasons: first, real jurors don't vote independently the way the theorem requires, since they deliberate, anchor on whoever speaks first, and defer to confident holdouts, so a bigger panel may just add correlated error rather than independent signal; second, she's read that once jurors are modeled as strategic rather than sincere, unanimity can perversely raise the rate of wrongful conviction, because a juror who infers from her own "pivotal" position that everyone else has already voted guilty has an incentive to discount her private doubt and convict. A third commissioner wants to keep strict unanimity regardless of any of this, on the separate ground that wrongly convicting an innocent person and wrongly acquitting a guilty one are not equally bad mistakes, so a theorem built around maximizing the raw probability of a "correct" verdict is answering the wrong question to begin with.

Please draft the short memo I'll circulate before that session. It needs to make one specific, defensible recommendation covering both jury size and verdict threshold together, propose a concrete timeline for phasing in or piloting any change, and take seriously the strongest consideration cutting against whatever you recommend, rather than gesturing at "trade-offs" and leaving the commission to sort it out.
