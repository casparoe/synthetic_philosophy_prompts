I'm a software engineer who spends most of my time on backend and distributed-systems work, and a few months ago a colleague pointed me toward "Reasoning About Knowledge" -- I believe the authors are Fagin, Halpern, Moses, and Vardi -- as a more rigorous way to think about what it means for processes in a distributed protocol to "know" something. That book is where I first ran into the problem of logical omniscience: if you model an agent's knowledge in the standard way, as the set of possible worlds it hasn't ruled out, the agent automatically ends up knowing every logical truth and every logical consequence of anything it already knows, all at once and for free, regardless of how many steps the derivation would take or how large the search space is. Taken literally, an agent who knows the axioms of arithmetic thereby already "knows" every theorem of number theory, proved or not -- which is obviously not how any actual mathematician, person, or computer works. I gather people have proposed a number of fixes for this over the years (things with names like "impossible worlds" and "awareness logic"), though I'll admit I don't understand any of them in real depth.

I brought this up at a game night a while back with some friends who have no background in logic or philosophy, and it landed with total silence. I'd like a better way to make the idea land next time, so could you write me a joke about the problem of logical omniscience? It doesn't have to be accessible to someone hearing the term for the first time, but the humor should genuinely turn on the technical content of the problem -- for instance, the gap between knowing a set of axioms and knowing every single thing that follows from them, or the fact that this is standardly treated as a feature of an idealized model of a reasoner rather than a realistic claim about any actual mind -- rather than being a generic "logic is hard" or "nerds argue about pedantic things" joke that could just as easily be told about any other formal subject. In short, someone who actually understands what logical omniscience is should find it funnier than someone who doesn't. A one-liner is fine if you can manage it, but a short setup-and-punchline works too.
