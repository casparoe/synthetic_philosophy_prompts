I've recently started attending a Unitarian congregation that spends a fair amount of its adult-education time reclaiming historical figures as forerunners of nontrinitarian Christianity, and at last month's session the discussion leader put Isaac Newton on the same shelf as Arius, describing Newton's private rejection of the Trinity as essentially "the same heresy, recurring." Until then I only knew Newton from physics class, so it came as a real surprise to learn that he spent decades on unpublished theology arguing that the doctrine of the Trinity was a fourth-century corruption of true Christianity engineered above all by Athanasius, and that he kept all of this hidden his whole life -- his own successor in the Lucasian chair, William Whiston, was stripped of the professorship and expelled from Cambridge in 1710 for saying much the same thing out loud. I pulled Rob Iliffe's Priest of Nature off the library shelf to prepare a response for the next meeting, and it's clear the "same heresy" framing is more contested among historians than my discussion leader let on.

Could you compare Newton's mature antitrinitarian theology with Arius's original fourth-century Christology, focusing on where the two substantively agree and where they diverge -- for instance, in how each grounds the Son's subordination to the Father, whether each treats the Son as created out of nothing versus "begotten" in some other sense, and how much weight each places on technical metaphysical vocabulary like ousia and hypostasis as against scriptural philology and church history? In which respects is Newton's heresy really its own idiosyncratic position rather than a simple revival of Arius's, and, in your judgment, does that difference matter for whether my discussion leader's "fellow traveler" framing does justice to either thinker?
