After forty years in evolutionary ecology, I retired three years ago and have spent much of my time since reading philosophy of science, Kuhn especially, which has left me warier than I used to be about a task now in front of me. I sit on the scientific advisory panel for a foundation that funds early-career researchers, and this cycle's most contested proposal asks whether niche construction and developmental plasticity, rather than selection on standing genetic variation, principally drove a particular cichlid radiation. Two reviewers scored it outstanding for its ambition and coherence; three scored it poor, since it barely engages the standard population-genetics literature and its preliminary data show none of the signatures -- a selective sweep, say -- that the field normally requires as evidence of adaptation, a standard that already presupposes the gene-first process the proposal questions. I keep thinking of Kuhn's point that rival paradigms can be judged only by standards internal to one of them, but I don't want that to become an excuse for the panel to dodge the decision. Please draft the memo I'll circulate before Friday's meeting: recommend fund, decline, or invite a revised resubmission, with concrete guidance on what would have to change and by when; give reasons the low scorers and the applicant could each take seriously; and say how much weight, if any, the panel should give reviewers whose real objection is that the proposal doesn't look like normal science in this field.
