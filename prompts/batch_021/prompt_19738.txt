I have no academic background in philosophy, but after a year of reading about free will and determinism on my own, I stumbled onto Aristotle's puzzle about the sea battle: whether a statement like "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is already true or false today, given that saying yes seems to threaten the openness of the future, while saying no seems to threaten the idea that every statement about the world has to be one or the other. I later learned that logicians built two rival, named responses to this, usually called the Peircean and Ockhamist views: the Peircean treats a statement about a genuinely contingent future event as true now only if it holds no matter how things go, so that "there will be a battle" and "there won't" can both end up false, while the Ockhamist keeps the statement true or false today, fixed by whichever course of events actually happens, and denies that this counts as the kind of necessity that threatens freedom.

Please compare the two positions in some depth. I'm especially interested in whether the Ockhamist's single "actual" future among the branches does genuine philosophical work or just stipulates the very thing it claims to leave open, and whether the Peircean's willingness to let "there will be a battle" and "there won't" both come out false is a cost worth paying. On balance, which view does more to actually defuse the fatalist threat rather than just relabel it? Please justify your answer.
