David Lewis's counterpart theory and the rival doctrine of genuine transworld identity offer competing analyses of what makes a claim like "Humphrey could have won the election" true. On the counterpart-theoretic picture, standardly paired with the view that possible worlds are concrete, causally and spatiotemporally isolated wholes, no individual is ever part of more than one world; the sentence about Humphrey is true just in case some counterpart of his — an individual in another world who resembles him closely enough to represent him there, without being strictly identical to him — wins an election there. On the rival picture, Humphrey himself, numerically one and the same across worlds, is the direct subject of the claim, and no resembling stand-in is needed, since possible worlds are simply stipulated as ways things might have gone for people and objects already picked out, rather than identified after the fact by matching qualitative descriptions.

Write a dialogue between two philosophers of modality, one a committed counterpart theorist and the other a defender of genuine transworld identity, set at the reception following a colloquium talk on modal metaphysics. Have the identity theorist open with Kripke's well-known "Humphrey objection": that counterpart theory seems to change the subject, since Humphrey presumably has no stake in whether some merely resembling individual wins an election in another world. The counterpart theorist should answer with more than a bare restatement of the theory, engaging for instance with whether ordinary speakers' failure to think in terms of counterparts is really good evidence against the analysis, and with whether the identity theorist's own Humphrey would have any better claim to care about a numerically identical but causally isolated self in a merely possible world than he has to care about a mere counterpart.

From there, have the counterpart theorist go on the offensive, pressing the identity theorist with either Chisholm's puzzle about identity across a chain of gradually varying worlds or a scenario in which two equally good candidates for being Humphrey turn up in a single other world, in order to ask whether transworld identity can be made determinate without covertly relying on something that is, in all but name, a counterpart relation. Give the identity theorist real resources to resist this challenge as well, such as an appeal to non-qualitative individual essences or a willingness to bite the bullet on vagueness, rather than a quiet concession, so that the two interlocutors remain equally sharp throughout and the disagreement can end still live rather than resolved in either side's favor.
