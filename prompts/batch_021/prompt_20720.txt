Q: State the Condorcet Jury Theorem, including its key assumptions. Then explain (a) what happens to the reliability of majority voting as group size grows when those assumptions hold, (b) at least two ways the assumptions tend to fail in real deliberative bodies and what that implies for the theorem's conclusion, and (c) one implication of all this for arguments for epistemic democracy. (15 pts)

STUDENT ANSWER:
The Condorcet Jury Theorem says that if a group is choosing between two options, one of which is objectively correct, and each voter independently has a better-than-even chance p > 0.5 of picking the correct one, then the majority vote beats any individual voter, and as the group gets larger the probability the majority is correct climbs toward 1. It's the mathematical backbone of "wisdom of crowds" arguments for democracy: even so-so voters converge on the truth in large enough numbers.

Two assumptions are carrying most of the weight. First, independence: nobody's vote can be too correlated with anyone else's. In real juries or committees, people anchor on the same early speaker, read the same news, or defer to whoever sounds most confident, so votes end up correlated rather than independent, which weakens or can erase the benefit of adding more people. Second, competence actually has to be above 0.5 for each voter. If the question is technical enough that jurors are basically guessing and competence dips under a half, piling on more such voters doesn't help -- the majority just gets somewhat less reliable as more of these voters are added in.

There's also a deeper wrinkle from Condorcet's own work: his paradox of cyclical majorities shows that pairwise majority voting can generate an intransitive group ranking (A beats B, B beats C, C beats A) even though every individual voter's preferences are perfectly transitive, which further undermines the idea that majority procedures reliably track a correct answer.

Put together, the epistemic case for democracy is shakier than the headline result suggests -- it leans on real deliberative bodies actually achieving independence and minimal competence, conditions that are easy to state and hard to guarantee in practice.
