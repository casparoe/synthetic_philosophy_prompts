I recently moved from a general analytics role into my bank's model risk management group, and one of my first assignments has been to help review a new credit-scoring model before it can be approved for use in loan underwriting. At a working session last week, two of the senior members of the team, Marcus and Priya, got into a fairly heated exchange about whether the model should be signed off on fairness grounds, and I've been asked to summarize the discussion for our next meeting. Having sat through it, I'm not sure I can say who actually had the better of it at each stage, rather than just at the end.

Marcus opened by arguing that the model is fair because it is well-calibrated: within every racial group, applicants who receive a given predicted default probability actually default at close to that rate, so a score of, say, 20% means the same thing regardless of who is being scored. Priya responded that this misses what actually matters, since the model's false-positive rate — creditworthy applicants who are wrongly denied — is noticeably higher for Black applicants than for white applicants, and that gap, not calibration, is the harm that regulators and applicants actually care about. Marcus came back by pointing out that it is a mathematical fact that a single score cannot be calibrated within every group and simultaneously produce equal false-positive and false-negative rates across those groups, unless the underlying default rates happen to be equal or the model predicts with perfect accuracy — so, he said, Priya was effectively asking for something that cannot exist. Priya conceded the math but said the fix was obvious: drop the single threshold and set a separate, group-specific cutoff for each racial group so that the error rates come out equal instead. Marcus objected that this means two applicants with the exact same predicted risk would get different decisions purely because of their race, which sounds like textbook disparate treatment and would likely be illegal under fair-lending law even before you get to whether it's fair. Priya's last word was that a single, race-blind threshold isn't neutral either — if the difference in default rates between groups is itself partly a residue of past discriminatory lending and credit-reporting practices, then applying one uniform cutoff just launders that history through a seemingly neutral number, and calling that "equal treatment" begs the question.

Could you go through this exchange move by move and say who has the stronger argument at each specific point, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I would also value your take on whether Marcus and Priya, between them, are missing some more fundamental issue that neither of their positions actually addresses.
