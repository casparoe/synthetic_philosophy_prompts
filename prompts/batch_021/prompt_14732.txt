I'm outlining a novel whose protagonist, owing to the peculiar mechanics of her situation, can never tell whether a given moment is the first time she's living through it or the second -- the two occasions are subjectively indistinguishable, and nothing available to her settles which. I want her in-the-moment reasoning to survive real scrutiny rather than just read as atmospheric, so I've been studying the absent-minded driver problem: Piccione and Rubinstein's puzzle about a driver who must take the second of two identical, unmarked highway exits (taking the first is worst, missing both merely mediocre, hitting the second best), yet can neither distinguish the exits nor recall whether he has already passed one. The problem spawned a real dispute over whether such a driver can even coherently assign, via ordinary updating, a probability to "which exit is this," and whether the strategy that's best to commit to in advance (the "planning-optimal" strategy) should simply override whatever seems best once you're actually standing at the fork (the "action-optimal" strategy) whenever the two diverge. Write an essay that weighs both sides of this dispute and defends a specific, unhedged verdict -- and spell out what your verdict implies my character should actually think when she catches herself unable to locate herself within her own timeline.
