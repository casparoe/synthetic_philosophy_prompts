The following passage comes from Book II, Chapter XXI ("Of Power") of John Locke's *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1690). Locke has just used the examples of a man falling from a collapsing bridge and a man seized by convulsions to argue that some actions are neither voluntary nor free. He then writes:

"Again: suppose a man be carried, whilst fast asleep, into a room where is a person he longs to see and speak with; and be there locked fast in, beyond his power to get out: he awakes, and is glad to find himself in so desirable company, which he stays willingly in, i.e. prefers his stay to going away. I ask, is not this stay voluntary? I think nobody will doubt it: and yet, being locked fast in, it is evident he is not at liberty not to stay, he has not freedom to be gone. So that liberty is not an idea belonging to volition, or preferring; but to the person having the power of doing, or forbearing to do, according as the mind shall choose or direct. Our idea of liberty reaches as far as that power, and no farther. For wherever restraint comes to check that power, or compulsion takes away that indifferency of ability to act, or to forbear acting, there liberty, and our notion of it, presently ceases."

This example is sometimes mentioned as a forerunner of Harry Frankfurt's later cases, in which someone who would have been made to act a certain way regardless nonetheless acts on their own initiative, cases Frankfurt uses to argue that moral responsibility does not require the ability to have done otherwise. But Locke does not conclude that his sleeper is responsible, or free, in staying; he concludes only that the man's staying, though voluntary, falls short of liberty. Spell out exactly what Locke's argument here does and does not establish, and then assess whether it genuinely anticipates Frankfurt's later point or is doing different work that only looks similar on the surface, taking into account how Locke elsewhere in the Essay ties moral good and evil to the voluntariness of our actions rather than to our liberty.
