And here's the student's answer, typed up exactly as they wrote it (exam conditions, so don't dock for prose style):

"Anekantavada is the Jain doctrine that reality is many-sided (ananta-dharmatmaka) -- no finite predicate can capture an object in its entirety, so any single assertion is at best partial. Nayavada is the theory of these partial standpoints: a naya is a specific angle from which we describe a thing (e.g. the substance-standpoint vs. the modal-standpoint), and each naya is legitimate as far as it goes but false if pushed as the whole story. Syadvada is the logical machinery that keeps this honest: every assertion gets prefixed with "syat," meaning "perhaps" or "maybe," flagging that the speaker isn't fully certain the predicate holds without qualification -- a kind of built in epistemic humility. This gets formalized in the saptabhangi, the seven-fold predication: syat asti (in some respect it is), syat nasti (in some respect it is not), syat asti-nasti (in some respect it both is and is not, taken successively), syat avaktavyam (in some respect its inexpressible, because you can't state the is and the is-not simultaneously in language), plus three more combinations of avaktavyam with the earlier options. On the non-contradiction worry: Jains would say "the pot exists" and "the pot does not exist" aren't really contradictory, because they're indexed to different respects -- the pot exists qua clay-thing but doesn't exist qua cloth-thing -- so there's no violation of the law of non-contradiction once the respects are made explicit. And this is really the heart of anekantavada as an answer to dogmatism: since literally any claim can be correctly asserted as true in some respect via the right syat-qualification, no standpoint gets to claim a monopoly on truth, and every naya turns out to be just as valid as every other once its properly qualified. So the relativism charge isn't really a problem for anekantavada -- it's basically just what anekantavada honestly admits to being, a principled relativism rather than an accidental one."
