In a reading group on formal epistemology, we recently worked through Aumann's theorem that two Bayesian agents who share a common prior cannot "agree to disagree": if their posterior probabilities for some event become common knowledge, those posteriors must coincide. This led us into the related Milgrom–Stokey no-trade theorem, which seems to sharpen the point into a claim about markets: starting from a Pareto-efficient allocation under a common prior, if it becomes common knowledge that a trade is on offer, no trade motivated purely by newly arrived private information can leave every party better off in expectation, because a rational counterparty's very willingness to accept the trade should tip you off that you are on the losing side of it.

One member of the group, who trades options professionally, objected that this seems flatly contradicted by his daily experience: sophisticated professionals take opposing positions against one another on the basis of private research all the time, and a great deal of that volume looks like pure speculation rather than hedging, liquidity management, or rebalancing. He concluded that the sheer scale of speculative trading on real exchanges must therefore count as strong empirical evidence against modeling market participants as rational, common-prior Bayesian agents -- that something like pervasive irrationality, rather than any more benign story, has to be doing the explanatory work. I'd like to know whether that is the right lesson to take from the no-trade theorem for how we should think about the rationality of real-world financial markets, or whether the inference from "high trading volume" to "the rational common-prior model must be empirically false" goes wrong somewhere.
