In a seminar on the metaphysics of science, we spent a week on the divide between Humean and anti-Humean accounts of laws of nature, and I keep coming back to a question our professor raised only in passing. On the Humean side, David Lewis's Best System Analysis holds that a law is just whichever regularity turns up as an axiom or theorem of the true deductive systematization of all the particular, non-modal facts that best balances simplicity against strength; there is no further metaphysical glue of necessitation binding a law to its instances. Lewis was explicit that he saw this as the natural extension of what's sometimes called Hume's Dictum -- that there are no necessary connections between distinct existences -- and he named his broader picture "Humean supervenience" accordingly. Ranged against this are anti-Humean views such as Armstrong's proposal that laws are relations of natural necessitation holding between universals, and Tim Maudlin's contention that lawhood is itself a fundamental, unanalyzable feature of reality, irreducible to patterns of events or to any further relation at all.

Here is the complication that's been nagging at me. Hume himself never talked about universals, possible worlds, or best systems: he was writing about causation and induction, not laws of nature in this modern technical sense, so his enlistment as patron saint of one side of the debate is already an anachronistic extension of his actual views. And it isn't even settled among Hume scholars what those views were. On the traditional "Old Hume" reading, he was a straightforward regularity theorist for whom causation in the objects just is constant conjunction, which would make him a natural ancestor of Lewis. But on the "New Hume," or skeptical-realist, reading associated especially with Galen Strawson, Hume actually held that there are real, mind-independent causal powers or connections in nature, and that his famous skepticism was only ever a claim about what we can know or conceive of them, never a denial that they exist.

Given that interpretive fork, I'd like your best-supported guess as to where Hume himself would actually land if the contemporary debate could somehow be explained to him. Would he sign on to Lewis's Humean supervenience as the fulfillment of his own project, or would he regard Lewis's flat denial of hidden necessitating powers as a piece of dogmatic metaphysics that outruns his own merely epistemic skepticism? And on the other side, would his objections to "necessary connexion" talk bite just as hard against Armstrong's necessitation relation between universals, seeing as that too posits a further, imperceptible ingredient binding one existence to another, or is there something in Armstrong's or Maudlin's picture that Hume might actually have found more congenial than Lewis's?
