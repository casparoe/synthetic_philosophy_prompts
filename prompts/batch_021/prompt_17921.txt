Random tangent from actual work but this has been living in my head for like a week now. We had a whole team argument about whether to finally switch our commit message format (we've had the same slightly weird home-grown convention for like 6 years and one of the newer folks wants us to move to Conventional Commits), and one of our more philosophy-brained senior engineers offhandedly said "it's just a Lewis-style convention, that's why nobody wants to be the first to switch." I had no idea what that meant so I went and read up on David Lewis's old book "Convention" from '69. His basic idea, if I'm getting it right, is that a convention is a solution to a recurring coordination problem (like which side of the road everyone drives on) that keeps reproducing itself because everyone conforms, everyone expects everyone else to conform, and crucially this is "common knowledge" — not just everyone knowing it but everyone knowing that everyone knows it, all the way up. Also which particular regularity you land on is basically arbitrary, any of a few options would've worked just as well, you just needed everyone to land on the same one. I also came across some stuff (evolutionary game theory? might be botching the name) suggesting later people showed you don't even need all that common-knowledge-and-rationality machinery — convention-like patterns can apparently just fall out of blind imitation or reinforcement over time, no one has to be reasoning about anyone elses reasoning at all.

What I'd love from you is the most important implications of all this — Lewis's account plus that pushback — for how conventions actually behave inside a codebase, a team, or an open source project. Concretely: does this framework explain why some conventions (tabs vs spaces, naming schemes, commit formats, whatever) are so absurdly sticky even once basically everyone privately agrees the alternative is better? Does it explain why different companies converge on totally different but equally workable standards instead of one global one? And is "it's just a Lewis convention" actually a good reason for me to stop pushing for the change, or is it more of an excuse people use to defend inertia? You don't need to go to bat for Lewis specifically, I'm more interested in whatever the real upshots are, even if the honest answer is basically "this 60s philosophy book doesn't map onto git commit conventions as cleanly as your coworker made it sound."
