Ok so I'm a philosophy PhD student working on Hume's epistemology, and completely unrelatedly (or so I thought) I've been reading through the old MIRI "embedded agency" material in my spare time -- the sequence by Garrabrant and Demski, I think. One of the open problems they raise is "naturalized induction": something like Solomonoff induction or AIXI works by having the agent hold a huge hypothesis space of possible environments and update on evidence, but this quietly assumes the agent is seperate from, and bigger than, the environment it's predicting, so that it can always fully simulate whatever hypothesis it's currently entertaining. A genuinely embedded agent, though, is just a part of the world it's trying to model, so the "true hypothesis" would have to contain a full description of the agent itself sitting inside a world at least as big as the agent -- basically a map that has to contain itself at full resolution, which runs into exactly the kind of self-reference trouble you'd expect. Halfway through a chapter it hit me that this is basically a sharpened, formalized descendant of Hume's problem of induction, except Hume never had to worry about the reasoner being a proper part of the very system it's reasoning about.

What I can't figure out is what Hume himself would actually say if you explained this to him. Would he read "naturalized induction" as just his own skeptical argument dressed up in computer science clothing -- "I told you there's no rational grounding for induction, here's yet another reason why" -- and shrug, pointing back to custom and habit as the only answer there was ever going to be? Or is the embeddedness twist doing real philosophical work that his account genuinely doesn't cover? I'm also curious about the connection to his bundle theory of personal identity -- he famously couldn't find a unified self on introspection, and was reportedly still unhappy with that account by the time of the Appendix to the Treatise. Would he see an embedded agent's inability to hold a complete, self-containing model of itself as basically the same puzzle he'd already flagged as unsolved, just formalized two centuries later? Don't just give me the "we can't really know" cop-out -- I want your best reconstruction of the view given what he actually committed to in the Treatise and the Enquiry.
