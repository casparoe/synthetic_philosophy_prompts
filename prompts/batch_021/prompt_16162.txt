A few of us who used to work together at my last job still get dinner every couple of months, and last time the conversation drifted into brain-teasers, as it tends to with this group. Someone brought up the classic wager in which a fair coin is flipped repeatedly until it lands tails: you win $2 if it's tails on the very first flip, and every additional head before that doubles the prize, so one head then tails pays $4, two heads then tails pays $8, and so on, meaning a run of nine heads before the first tail pays $1,024, a run of twenty pays over two million dollars, and nothing in the rules caps how long the streak, or the prize, can grow. The question on the table was whether you'd pay $150 for a single shot at this game, and two people, Marco and Elena, kept arguing about it long after everyone else had moved on to dessert. Neither of them has any formal background in decision theory or philosophy (we're all software engineers by trade), but the argument got surprisingly deep, and I honestly couldn't tell you who came out ahead.

(1) Marco said the expected value of the payout is, strictly speaking, infinite: each extra doubling of the prize is exactly canceled out by the halving of its probability, the sum never converges, and a rational agent maximizing expected value should therefore be willing to pay any finite amount to play, which makes turning down $150 a mistake. (2) Elena said that conclusion is absurd on its face, since nobody sane would pay $50,000 for one shot at this, and if "maximize expected value" really recommends that, it's a strike against expected value as a decision rule rather than a fact anyone is obligated to act on. (3) Marco granted the sticker shock but pointed to a standard fix: the real error is maximizing expected dollars instead of expected utility. Money has diminishing returns (going from $0 to $1,000 matters far more than going from $10,000,000 to $10,001,000), and once the same infinite sum is run through a utility curve that flattens out that way, it converges to something modest; a low willingness to pay, he said, is exactly what expected-value reasoning recommends once it's applied to the right currency. (4) Elena said that fix is too narrow. You can keep the identical coin-flipping structure but redefine the prize at each stage directly in terms of Marco's own utility curve, so that stage n is worth exactly twice as much, on his scale, as stage n-1, however many dollars that takes; as long as the curve never actually stops climbing, however slowly, the same non-converging sum comes right back. So the only way to close off the problem for good, she said, is to insist there's a hard ceiling on how good an outcome could ever be for you, a cap on utility itself and not merely on money, which is a far stranger and stronger commitment than "money has diminishing returns." (5) Marco answered that a ceiling isn't so strange once you notice that whoever actually offers a bet like this, a casino, a friend, anyone, has finite net worth, so every real version of the wager already has finite expected value once you price in even a tiny chance the payer can't cover an astronomical prize. The truly unbounded version, he said, is a pure idealization nobody ever actually faces, so nothing about capped utility needs to be settled just to decide what to do about an actual $150 offer.

Elena wasn't fully convinced, but the check arrived before she could respond and the conversation moved on. I'd like your take on this move by move rather than just an overall winner: for each of the five numbered points, tell me whether it's basically correct, overstated, or mistaken, and why, and then tell me whether there's a consideration neither of them raised that actually matters for deciding whether $150 would have been a reasonable price to pay.
