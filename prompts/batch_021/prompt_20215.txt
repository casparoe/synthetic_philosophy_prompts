Over the past couple of months I've taken up regular sessions in a flotation tank: total darkness and silence, water at skin temperature, nothing to feel or see. A friend finishing a philosophy MA remarked, not entirely in jest, that I had essentially volunteered for Avicenna's flying man. It is the figure he imagines, in slightly different versions across several of his works, created whole in mid-air, limbs held apart so that none touches another or anything else, senses cut off from body and world, who would still affirm his own existence while affirming nothing about his organs or his body at all. That comment sent me looking into what the argument is actually supposed to establish, and I keep running into the same fork in the literature. One reading takes it to do real metaphysical work: since the self is affirmed while the body is not, the argument is meant to show that the self just is an immaterial substance distinct from the body — a miniature dualist proof sometimes described as anticipating Descartes' cogito by some six centuries. A more deflationary reading, associated with Marmura and those who follow him, takes the argument to establish only an epistemic point — that self-awareness is immediate and non-inferential, independent of any awareness of one's own body — without yet telling us what kind of thing the self is, leaving the case for the soul's immateriality to Avicenna's other, separate arguments.

Write me an essay that lays out this dispute with the textual and philosophical considerations that favor each side, and then actually settles it rather than leaving it open: does the flying man argument, by itself, establish anything about the metaphysics of the soul, or only about the character of self-awareness? Reach a verdict and defend it.
