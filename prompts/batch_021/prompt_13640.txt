I'm researching a feature on the history of scientific efforts to investigate paranormal claims, and Henry Sidgwick keeps coming up: he was a founding member of the Society for Psychical Research in 1882, served as its first president, and spent decades investigating mediums and apparent telepathy — rigorously enough that the Society itself later cast doubt on some of its own promising early results, including the well-known Creery sisters thought-transference trials, once signs of covert signaling emerged. Less often discussed is why he bothered in the first place. In the concluding chapter of The Methods of Ethics, Sidgwick argues that practical reason harbors an unresolved "dualism" between the claims of self-interest and of impartial duty, a conflict he thought could only be dissolved by grounds for believing that the universe has a moral order of its own — precisely what successful psychical research might have supplied. Write an essay assessing whether this backstory should lower our estimate of Sidgwick's psychical research as disinterested science, or whether his sustained skepticism toward the evidence he actually gathered shows that motive and method can be kept apart even under real pressure to believe. Defend a definite verdict, and say what it implies for whether the dualism of practical reason remains a live problem for ethics now that Sidgwick's hoped-for empirical rescue never arrived.
