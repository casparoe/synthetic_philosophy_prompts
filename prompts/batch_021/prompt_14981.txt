I'm about two-thirds through a novel set at Trinity College, Cambridge, in the years around 1675–1690, with a fictionalized young Newton as one of its central figures, and I've been struggling with a chapter that turns on his religious secrecy. To pressure-test it, I sent the draft to two people whose judgment I trust: Helen, an old friend who teaches the history of early modern science, and Marcus, a philosopher I met in graduate school who now works mostly on ethics. They ended up in an argument that had less to do with my prose than with how to judge the real Newton, and I've been unable to settle it myself.

(1) Helen said I'd made Newton too guilty-seeming: historically, he secured a royal dispensation in 1675 specifically excusing the Lucasian Professor from the ordination Trinity fellows otherwise required, which let him avoid ever publicly swearing to doctrines, chiefly the Trinity, that his private study of scripture and church history had already convinced him were later corruptions. That, she said, is closer to what historians call Nicodemism -- prudent outward conformity by a covert dissenter -- than to hypocrisy. (2) Marcus answered that the dispensation only shows Newton avoided one oath; it does nothing to excuse the further forty-plus years in which he let colleagues, students, and readers of his published work go on believing what he privately considered idolatry, without ever saying so publicly, even once he had become the most celebrated natural philosopher in Europe. (3) Helen replied that the danger was not abstract: Newton's own successor in the Lucasian chair, William Whiston, said openly what Newton had only ever written in private, and was stripped of his professorship and expelled from the University for it around 1710 -- exactly the fate Newton's discretion had spared him. (4) Marcus said that only sharpens his point: Newton had already drafted a manuscript arguing that two of the central prooftexts for the Trinity were scribal corruptions, arranged in 1690 to have it published anonymously in Holland through Locke and a contact there, and then pulled it back at the last moment -- a far lower-risk route than Whiston's, which Newton passed up not once but for the rest of a long life, including the years after Whiston's expulsion had already shown him exactly what the worst case looked like. (5) Helen said "lower-risk" is easy to say in hindsight: an anonymous manuscript on biblical philology, written in Newton's unmistakable style and passed through people who knew whose it was, was not obviously safe to a man who had every reason, after Whiston, to suspect that style and rumor could out him as thoroughly as a signature -- and that treating his caution as mere self-interest assumes a confidence about the odds that neither Newton nor Marcus actually had.

Neither of them gave ground, and I would like to understand the disagreement properly before deciding how the chapter should treat him. Could you go through moves (1) through (5) and say, for each, who has the stronger of the specific point being made there -- not just an overall verdict -- and flag anything relevant that both Helen and Marcus seem to be missing?
