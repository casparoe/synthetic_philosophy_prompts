In 1924, the last full year of his life, Gottlob Frege kept a short political diary that was not published until decades after his death, when it turned up among his papers. Its contents sit oddly against his reputation as the founder of modern quantificational logic and of the sense/reference distinction: in it he expresses contempt for parliamentary democracy and the Weimar constitution, enthusiasm for an authoritarian nationalist alternative, and explicit antisemitism, including the view that Jews should be stripped of political rights or driven out of Germany. Michael Dummett, the philosopher most responsible for establishing Frege's canonical status in twentieth-century English-language philosophy, has written of being deeply shaken on learning of the diary, and evidently felt compelled to address it directly in his later work on Frege rather than pass over it in silence. Unlike Heidegger, however, Frege was never a public political actor, and nothing in the diary is echoed in his technical papers on logic and mathematics; so far as anyone has shown, it is causally disconnected from the concept-script, the definition of number, and the theory of sense and reference.

This came up in a reading group on the history of analytic philosophy, where opinions split sharply. Some in the group thought the diary simply irrelevant to Frege's philosophy properly understood -- a biographical fact about a flawed man with no bearing on whether his arguments are sound. Others suspected a deeper resonance between the diary and specific philosophical commitments of his: his militant anti-psychologism, his insistence that logical laws and "thoughts" (Gedanken) inhabit a timeless, objective "third realm" wholly independent of any thinker's psychology, and his broader impatience with anything smacking of relativism or pluralism about truth. Write an essay that takes a stand on whether, and how, the diary bears on Frege's philosophy. Treat separately whether it bears on (a) the soundness of his logical and semantic work, (b) the interpretation of his wider philosophical outlook, and (c) the way the discipline should tell the story of its own founding -- engage seriously with the strongest version of the "no bearing" view before you do, but do not settle for a non-committal verdict on any of the three.
