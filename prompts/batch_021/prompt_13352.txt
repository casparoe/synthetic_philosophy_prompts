After forty years running an experimental condensed-matter lab, I retired three years ago and have finally had time to read philosophy properly rather than raid it for stray quotations, which is what scientists usually do. My reading group has been working through Leibniz, and I have a personal way in: I already knew him as a fellow physicist, from the vis viva dispute over how to measure force and from his correspondence with Clarke, where he wields the Principle of Sufficient Reason against Newton's absolute space. That same principle, pushed to its limit, is what has me stuck. Leibniz holds that each individual substance has a "complete concept" containing every predicate true of it for all time, fixed once and for all the moment God selects it, together with its compossible fellows, as part of the best of all possible worlds. That looks to me like a determinism at least as thoroughgoing as anything in classical mechanics -- and yet Leibniz wants ordinary moral responsibility, praise, blame, and desert to survive it intact.

What I can't settle for myself is whether his machinery for pulling this off actually works as moral philosophy, rather than as a face-saving metaphysical patch. He distinguishes "absolute" or "metaphysical" necessity, where the opposite is contradictory, from mere "moral necessity," where a wise agent is certain, but not logically compelled, to choose the best; he says that reasons and motives "incline without necessitating" the will; and against the old worry that a fixed outcome makes effort pointless -- the "lazy sophism" he inherits from the Stoics and takes up at length in the Theodicy against Bayle -- he replies that the means are exactly as fated as the ends, so that acting well is how the good outcome comes about, not something rendered pointless by it. I've come across readings, in something like the spirit of the old Russell-Couturat logicist interpretations of Leibniz, on which this whole apparatus just renames necessity without escaping it, leaving praise and blame as useful fictions for creatures too confused to see the necessity behind their own choices; and I've come across the opposite view, that the moral/absolute distinction is doing genuine philosophical work, no worse off than the distinctions compatibilists rely on today.

I'd like an essay that takes a side on this rather than surveying it neutrally. Lay out the strongest version of the charge that Leibniz's "moral necessity" is necessity in disguise and cannot underwrite genuine responsibility; then lay out the strongest Leibnizian rejoinder, drawing on the resources above (moral versus absolute necessity, inclining without necessitating, the reply to the lazy sophism, and the complete-concept/best-of-all-possible-worlds doctrine behind it); and close by defending your own considered verdict on whether Leibniz's ethics survives contact with his metaphysics, rather than leaving the question open.
