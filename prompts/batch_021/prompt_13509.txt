so quick context: I'm a 2nd-year econ PhD student, and this semester I'm TAing the microeconomic theory sequence that covers expected utility theory and the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms. Last week I taught the St. Petersburg paradox as a fun historical example — the coin-flipping game where you double your winnings every round and the expected payout comes out infinite, but obviously no one would pay more than like $20 to play it. I told my students that Daniel Bernoulli basically closed the book on this back in 1738 by pointing out that people maximize expected utility, not expected value, and once your utility function is concave (like log wealth), the infinite sum of payoffs collapses down into a small, finite expected utility. So the "paradox" was really just an artifact of naively assuming linear utility, nothing more.
