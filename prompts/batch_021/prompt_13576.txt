I've never studied philosophy or economics formally, but ever since getting hooked on game theory through a couple of pop-science books a few years back, I've been running an informal monthly "game night" with friends where we actually play out some of the classic thought-experiment games with real poker chips instead of just reading about them. Last month we tried a simplified version of the "centipede game": there's a pot that starts small, and on each turn a player can either "grab," which ends things immediately and gives the grabber a noticeably bigger slice than the other player gets, or "pass," which hands the turn over and grows the pot a bit more, up to a cap of six rounds, after which, if nobody has grabbed, the now-much-bigger pot is simply split evenly. Almost everyone at the table passed several times before eventually grabbing, which isn't what the book I'd read said "rational" players are supposed to do, and it set off a running argument between two of the regulars, Owen and Renata, that carried on by email for the rest of the week. I found their exchange genuinely hard to referee on my own and would like a more careful outside opinion.

(1) Owen opened by arguing that the truly rational play, according to backward induction, is to grab on the very first turn no matter what: at the last possible round, whoever's turn it is should obviously grab rather than let things end in an even split; knowing this, the player at the second-to-last round should grab immediately rather than pass a turn that, they can already predict, just ends with the other player grabbing anyway; and this reasoning unravels all the way back to round one, regardless of how many rounds are on the table or how big the pot would eventually grow. (2) Renata responded that this can't be the whole story about rationality, because it implies that two players who are both perfectly logical, and who both know the other is perfectly logical, and know that the other knows it, and so on -- what she'd seen called "common knowledge of rationality" -- end up walking away with less money than two trusting amateurs who simply keep passing for a while, and that a theory of rationality which makes mutual rationality actively costly, compared to naivety, has gotten something backwards. (3) Owen came back that there's nothing paradoxical there: being rational just means responding correctly to the situation you're actually in, not being guaranteed a good outcome, and plenty of familiar games -- he brought up the ordinary one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma -- already have the feature that mutual rationality leaves both sides worse off than mutual naivety would. (4) Renata's follow-up, which actually stalled the thread for a couple of days, was that Owen's backward induction argument only makes sense by having each player reason about what a rational opponent would do if the game somehow reached, say, round five -- but if it's truly common knowledge that both players are rational, then by Owen's own logic round five is never actually reached, since the whole game is supposed to unravel at round one. So what are those beliefs about round five even beliefs about? Either the argument treats "what happens at round five" as a live possibility, which quietly admits a rational player might sometimes pass after all, or it's reasoning about a scenario its own premises rule out -- and maybe the real lesson of the game isn't that rational players grab immediately, but that "everyone is rational, and everyone knows everyone else is too" simply isn't a coherent thing to assume about a game shaped like this one. Owen didn't have a ready reply, and the thread died down soon after.
