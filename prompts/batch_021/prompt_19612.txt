Had a long, kind of frustrating argument with my friend Priya over dinner last night about this "commitment races" idea that shows up a lot in AI safety and rationalist-adjacent writing (I think the term's originally from a Kokotajlo post, maybe 2019?). Priya's position was basically that commitment is just good game theory and more of it is always better: Schelling showed decades ago that the driver who visibly rips out his own steering wheel wins the game of chicken, so any sufficiently rational agent -- a person, a state, a future AI -- should want to lock in the hardest bargaining stance it can, as early and as irrevocably as possible, because that's what wins. She's doing a postdoc in mechanism design, so she had zero patience for my hand-wavy objection.

My objection was that this can't be the whole picture, because if every agent reasons this way your going to get agents committing before they've actually had time to think, purely to beat the other side to the punch, and if two agents both lock in incompatible demands (or incompatible threats) you get the mutually destructive outcome that neither of them wanted. That feels like a genuinely different animal from an ordinary arms race -- more like a race to destroy your own future options as fast as possible -- but I couldn't make that precise on the spot. We got even more stuck on updatelessness: I have a vague memory that being "updateless" is supposed to help here, but I couldn't explain why it wouldn't just relocate the exact same race one level up, into a fight over which prior, or which policy, you commit to being updateless with respect to. And neither of us could really say why "logical time" -- the order in which agents supposedly commit, even with no actual temporal sequence involved -- is doing any real work for agents that are, in some idealized sense, reasoning about each other all at once.

So could you actually walk me through the guts of the commitment races problem properly? I want to understand why commitment ends up being treated as scarce and risky rather than just uniformly good and more-is-better, what work "logical time" is doing in that story, why naive updatelessness doesn't just dissolve the problem instead of relocating it, and whether something like "safe Pareto improvements" (another half-remembered term from that night) is an actual way out or just a patch. I'd like enough depth that I can go back to Priya with something better than "trust me, it's more complicated than that."
