So I fell down a rabbit hole on the 'absent-minded driver' problem last week (found it via an old rationalist blog post, then tracked down the actual Piccione & Rubinstein and Aumann-Hart-Perry papers). Quick setup in case its new to you: a driver has to take the second of two highway exits, X and Y, but the intersections are indistinguishable to him -- basically state aliasing, like two different states of a program producing the exact same observation, so he's got no key to tell them apart. Exiting at X pays 0, continuing then exiting at Y pays 4, continuing through both pays 1. Worked out in advance ('planning-optimal'), the best policy turns out to be continuing with probability 2/3.

Here's the bit that messed with my head: apparently theres a real fight over whether 2/3 is still correct once you're actually standing at one of the intersections, not knowing if it's X or Y, trying to put a credence on which one it is. Piccione & Rubinstein say naive updating on that credence drags you toward always continuing (which is worse). Aumann, Hart and Perry say there's no paradox once you define the 'action-optimal' choice properly.

Write me a dialogue between two decision theorists hashing this out live, standing right at the intersection. One thinks talk of your credence about *where you are* is basically a red herring -- you just execute the pre-committed 2/3 policy, full stop. The other thinks the driver genuinely has (or needs) a credence about it, and has to explain why taking it seriously doesn't collapse into that self-defeating 'always continue' spiral. Give me real back and forth, each one engaging with the other's actual strongest point, not two people just talking past each other -- and don't let either side win too cleanly.
