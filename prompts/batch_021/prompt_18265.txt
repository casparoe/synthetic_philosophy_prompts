I don't have any formal background in philosophy or decision theory, but I spend a lot of my spare time reading independent AI-safety and rationalist writing, and one idea that has stuck with me is the "surrogate goal" as a defense against acausal threats: an agent that fears being threatened by some other agent it cannot causally interact with — one that could, say, simulate and harm something the agent cares about unless it complies — deliberately commits to treating some cheap, otherwise unimportant goal as though it mattered enormously. Since a rational threatener only cares about extracting concessions, and is indifferent to what exactly gets threatened, the idea is that it will target the decoy rather than the agent's real values, because doing so is just as effective for the threatener's purposes.

The more I sit with this, the less sure I am that it survives contact with a sufficiently sophisticated threatener, one who realizes the surrogate goal was only ever adopted for strategic reasons and was never truly terminal. Write an essay assessing whether committing to a surrogate goal is a genuinely stable defense against acausal threats. Address, in particular, the possibility of meta-threats aimed at the commitment itself, and whether the strategy offers anything beyond a blanket policy of never responding to threats at all. Weigh the strongest considerations on each side, but don't hedge: reach and defend a specific verdict, including any conditions under which that verdict would flip.
