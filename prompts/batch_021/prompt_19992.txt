Ok so this is kind of a random combo but I can't stop thinking about it and my friends are tired of hearing about it lol. In my school's EA (effective altruism) club we spent like three meetings on longtermism -- the argument, I think its originally from Bostrom and then Toby Ord's book The Precipice runs with it, that because the future could contain literal trillions of people if humanity doesn't wipe itself out, the sheer astronomical scale of that potential future means preventing existential risks (asteroids, engineered pandemics, misaligned AI, whatever) is basically the most important moral priority there is. Like the entire case leans hard on "the potential future is unbelievably vast, so the stakes are unbelievably vast, so this should dominate our ethics over basically everything else."

Then totally seperately, for the philosophy elective I take, we got handed this short essay called "Epilogue" by Frank Ramsey -- he's the guy Ramsey theory in math is named after, a genius who used to go back and forth with Wittgenstein, died at like 26. And in it he says pretty much the opposite thing: he flat out refuses to feel humbled by the vastness of the universe, says his "picture of the world" is basically drawn with the stuff he actually cares about (people, his friends, conversations, that kind of thing) big in the foreground and the stars shrunk down tiny in the background, and kind of makes fun of the idea that sheer size or vastness should tell you how much something matters. He even jokes that him personally being a big, heavy guy doesn't make him more important than anyone else, so why would a star being enormous make it more important than a person.

So here's my actual question, and I want a real answer not just "he'd probably disagree": if Ramsey got dropped into 2024 and someone explained the longtermist argument to him, would he accept it, reject it, or land somewhere weirder than either? Can you reconstruct the specific reasons he'd give based on what he actually believed, and then tell me whether you think that objection (assuming he has one) actually holds up against people like Bostrom and Ord, or whether longtermism has a good comeback to it?
