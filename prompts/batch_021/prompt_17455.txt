On LessWrong and its offshoots, Kavka's toxin puzzle is often treated as Newcomb's problem's plainer cousin: no predictor, no backward causation, just the question of whether you can rationally intend tonight to do something you already know you will have no reason to do once the reward has landed. Write a dialogue between a resolute-choice theorist and a sophisticated-choice theorist -- to use McClennen's labels for the two classic stances on cases like this -- set the morning after the billionaire's money has already been wired, with the vial sitting open on the table between them. The resolute theorist holds that a rational agent both can and should drink at this point, since follow-through is simply what it was to have really held the intention rather than faked it. The sophisticated theorist holds that drinking now is a needless cost with no offsetting benefit, whatever seemed true the night before. Have each answer the other's hardest question: what separates genuine plan-stability from self-deception, and why a sufficiently reliable billionaire would not already have seen through the sophisticated agent's foreknowledge that it would renege. Let neither side land a clean knockout.
