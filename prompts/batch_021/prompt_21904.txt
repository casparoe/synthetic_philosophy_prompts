So I'm a phil of language PhD student, and this came up in my department's semantics/pragmatics reading group last week in a way that never got fully resolved and has been bugging me since (I'm supposed to be doing quals reading instead of thinking about this, but here we are). We were going through the slurs literature and there's this basic fork in the road: some people, like Hom with his "combinatorial externalism," think the derogatory content of a slur is just ordinary at-issue, truth-conditional content — the word denotes some complex, socially-constructed property, and because that property is never actually instantiated (there's no real "ought to be treated as lesser for being F" property out there), every sentence predicating the slur of someone comes out false. Other people, more in the Potts conventional-implicature tradition, think the derogatory kick lives on a totally separate, non-at-issue expressive dimension that projects out of basically any embedding you throw at it, independent of whatever is happening to the at-issue content.

Could you write me a dialogue between two people holding these two views, arguing it out over a couple of specific test sentences instead of just restating the general positions? I want the crux to be something like "Sam is not a chink" and "if Sam were a chink, we still shouldn't fire him" — the truth-conditional person should want the negation/conditional to actually be operating on the offensive property the way it operates on any other predicate (so the sentence could, weirdly, even be used to deny racism), while the expressive-content person thinks the slur's punch survives no matter how you embed it, and leans on that as their main evidence. If there's room, get them to also deal with reclaimed, in-group uses, since thats the obvious curveball for both sides. Neither one should be a pushover — I want them actually engaging with the other's specific move each time rather then just restating their own view, and honestly it's fine, even better, if it doesn't resolve cleanly by the end, since I don't think this is actually settled in the literature either.
