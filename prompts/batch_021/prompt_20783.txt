I'm about eighteen months into a novel set in the uneasy years after the collapse of a small, fictional authoritarian state. One of my two leads presides over a tribunal charged with dealing with the old regime's judges, informers, and mid-level bureaucrats, nearly all of whom insist, with some justice, that they only ever did what the law of the day required of them. I want her private reasoning, not just her rulings, to carry real philosophical weight, so over the past few months I've been working through Hart and Fuller's actual exchange — Hart's 1958 Harvard Law Review essay on the separation of law and morals, Fuller's reply to it, then The Concept of Law and The Morality of Law — rather than leaning on the flattened, one-paragraph version of "positivism versus natural law" I still remembered from an undergraduate elective.

What's stopping me now is that both men turn out to be doing something more careful than that flattened version suggests, and I can't quite get the difference straight in my own head. Hart, despite being the positivist, still argues for a "minimum content of natural law": certain rules a legal system must contain, given brute facts about human vulnerability, scarcity, and limited altruism, if it is to serve the point of having law at all. Fuller, despite being the natural lawyer, locates law's morality not in any substantive content but in procedural virtues — generality, publicity, non-retroactivity, and the rest of his eight principles — that a system of rules must largely honor simply to function as law, and which he thought a sufficiently wicked regime, the Nazi one included, would tend to abandon wholesale. Hart was not impressed: he answered that those procedural virtues are really just conditions for efficiently pursuing any purpose through rules, good or evil — his own analogy was to the skill involved in administering poison effectively.

Could you lay out, on each man's own terms, what he is actually grounding the law-morality connection in, and then tell me which bridge — if either — does real work that a strict separability thesis couldn't already absorb? Defend your answer against the obvious comeback from the other side. I don't want a summary of the standoff; I want a verdict I can put real pressure on, because I'm about to draft the scene in which my judge decides the case of a woman who denounced her husband under a statute that was, by every positivist test then available, perfectly valid law.
