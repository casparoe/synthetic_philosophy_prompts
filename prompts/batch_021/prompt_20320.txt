So I've been down a bit of a rabbit hole this week and want to run something by you. Im a philosophy PhD student working on the history of utilitarianism (right now my dissertation chapter is on Bentham's hedonic calculus and how later critics went after it), and a footnote in something I was reading mentioned that Bentham had written a whole unpublished manuscript defending the decriminalization of homosexual acts — decades before anyone else in England said anything like that in print, and at a time when sodomy was still a capital offense there. It's called "Offences Against One's Self," written around 1785, and it just sat in his papers until Louis Crompton transcribed and published big chunks of it in 1978 (Oxford eventually put out a full scholarly edition, "Of Sexual Irregularities," in 2014). None of this comes up in the standard "greatest happiness principle / panopticon" version of Bentham you get in an intro ethics class, so I've been reading through the essay out of curiosity, but some of the actual argumentation is way more tangled than I expected, and I could use a second pair of eyes on one bit especially.

Here's the passage that's got me stuck. He's trying to work out whether paederasty (his term) could even coherently be called "unnatural":

"The nature of the question admits of great latitude of opinion: for my own part I must confess I can not bring myself to entertain so high a notion of the alluringness of this preposterous propensity as some men appear to entertain. I can not suppose it to [be] possible it should ever get to such a heighth as that the interests of the female part of the species should be materially affected by it: or that it could ever happen that were they to contend upon equal ground the eccentric and unnatural propensity should ever get the better of the regular and natural one. Could we for a moment suppose this to be the case, I would wish it to be considered what meaning a man would have to annex to the expression, when he bestows on the propensity under consideration the epithet of unnatural. If contrary to all appearance the case really were that if all men were left perfectly free to choose, as many men would make choice of their own sex as of the opposite one, I see not what reason there would be for applying the word natural to the one rather than to the other. All the difference would be that the one was both natural and necessary whereas the other was natural but not necessary. If the mere circumstance of its not being necessary were sufficient to warrant the terming it unnatural it might as well be said that the taste a man has for music is unnatural."

What I can't quite pin down is the logical shape of the move he's making here. Is he running the counterfactual (what if as many men would freely choose their own sex as the opposite one) to show that "unnatural" just collapses into "not strictly necessary for keeping up the population," in which case the word is doing no real moral work and his point is that the whole natural/unnatural vocabulary is basically empty? Or is he actually conceding that there's a real, if thin, sense in which the "regular" inclination is natural-and-necessary while the other is merely natural-and-not-necessary, and just denying that this difference can carry the enormous moral and legal weight people load onto it? Those feel like two different conclusions to me — one dissolves the concept, the other grants it a small but real content — and I genuinely can't tell which one he thinks he's earned by the end of the paragraph. It also seems oddly in tension with the Bentham I know from everywhere else, who's usually allergic to "according to nature" talk and treats appeals to nature more or less the way he treats natural rights (the "nonsense upon stilts" line) — here he seems to be doing real constructive work with the concept of "natural" instead of just torching it. Am I overreading that tension? Could you help me reconstruct the argument a bit more rigorously, more or less sentence by sentence?
