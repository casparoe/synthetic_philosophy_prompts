A friend of mine just went through splitting up her late mother's estate with her brother, and something about how they ended up doing it has been bugging me since she told me about it over dinner. There was a house, some savings, and a small stake in a stationery shop their mum had run for like twenty years, and by default, if the two of them couldn't agree on anything, state law would've just split the whole thing evenly between them. Neither wanted to go that route, since lawyers would eat into the estate for a year or two, so they hired a mediator instead. Rather than propose a flat 50/50 split, or have them haggle openly over who needed the money more (which felt gross to both of them, for a family thing), the mediator had each sibling work out, separately, the absolute best deal they could hypothetically get if the whole estate somehow bent entirely their way, on condition the other one still recieved at least what they'd have gotten under the automatic even split. Then she set the real settlement so each of them ended up with exactly the same percentage of their own hypothetical best-case gain above that floor — not the same number of dollars above the floor, but the same share of whatever each of them could, in theory, have hoped for.

My friend can't decide whether that's a genuinely smart way to divide things up, or just a mediator's trick for making an arbitrary number sound scientific. It feels fair in one sense, since neither of them ends up worse off relative to their own ceiling than the other is relative to theirs, but it's also strange, because those "best case" numbers were never actually going to happen and seem to depend a lot on how you frame what's on the table in the first place. I've got a vague memory, from a piece I did once on how economists model negotiations, that there's a whole family of formal answers to "what counts as a fair split," including the famous one associated with Nash, and that this proportional-gains idea might be a distinct, well-studied alternative rather than something the mediator invented on the spot. But I don't know that literature well enough to actually explain it to her. Could you help me understand what a rule like this is really doing — what notion of fairness is underneath it, how someone would defend it against a plain even split or against the Nash-style approach, and where its own soft spots are?
