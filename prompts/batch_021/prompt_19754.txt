"Slurs seem to keep their offensive force even when they're embedded -- under negation, in polar questions, in the antecedent of a conditional, etc. (e.g., 'Is Klaus a boche?' or 'If Klaus is a boche, he should still be treated decently' are both bad to say, even though no one involved is asserting that Klaus is a boche.) Taking this projection data as your anchor, compare Christopher Hom's Combinatorial Externalism to a broadly expressivist/use-conditional treatment of a slur's derogatory content. Which view predicts the projection facts more naturally, and why? Then say a bit about how each view might handle in-group reclamation."

And here's the answer, anonymized:

"On Hom's Combinatorial Externalism (CE), a slur like 'boche' is a genuine descriptive predicate, not just an insult tacked onto 'German.' It expresses a complex, socially constructed property built out of the norms of a discriminatory practice -- something like 'ought to be treated with contempt on account of being German, because Germans are contemptible for such-and-such reason.' Since that property is grounded in a false, unjust ideology, nobody actually satisfies it, so on Hom and May's later 'moral and semantic innocence' refinement the predicate has a null extension and every simple sentence built from it comes out false rather than meaningless -- 'Klaus is a boche' is false in just the way 'Klaus is a witch' is false, even though 'Klaus is German' is true. That's also why the slur can't be synonymous with its neutral counterpart: the two predicates don't even share an extension.

The rival picture, due to Kaplan, treats the derogatory content as a conventional implicature -- a separate, non-at-issue dimension of meaning that registers the speaker's contempt without contributing to what's said. Because it isn't at-issue, this view is well-placed to handle reclamation: an in-group speaker can use the word without triggering the usual implicature, since the relevant convention of use is keyed to the speech community, a bit like how a nickname can be affectionate in one circle and insulting in another.

Because CE treats the slur's contribution as ordinary asserted content, it actually predicts the projection facts quite naturally: the property is compositionally embedded in the sentence just like any other predicate, so negating, questioning, or conditionalizing carries that content along for the ride, the same way 'is cruel' stays part of the content of 'Klaus is not cruel.' So of course the offense survives embedding -- that's just compositionality doing its job. The expressivist has a harder time here: if the contempt is only an implicature riding on an assertion, and no assertion is being made in a question or a conditional's antecedent, it's unclear why the implicature should show up at all.

Both views have something to say about reclamation: Hom needs the in-group use to somehow not trigger the same complex property, maybe because the relevant norms are being cited rather than enacted; the expressivist can just say the convention differs by community. On balance the expressivist story seems more convincing to me, mainly because it doesn't force us to say every use of a slur is simply false."
