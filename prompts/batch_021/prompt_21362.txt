Here's the question as it appeared on the exam:

"Aquinas discusses the killing of tyrants in several places: his commentary on the Sentences, De Regimine Principum, and the Summa's treatment of sedition (IIaIIae, q.42). Explain what Aquinas actually thinks about the permissibility of tyrannicide. In particular: does he think a private citizen may licitly kill a tyrant, and does this depend on the distinction between a tyrant who lacks legitimate title to rule (a usurper) versus one who possesses legitimate title but governs tyrannically? How does his most extended discussion of the issue, in De Regimine Principum, handle the question, and how (if at all) does his overall position compare to more full-throated medieval defenses of tyrannicide, e.g. John of Salisbury's?" [10 points]

And here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"Aquinas's view is more careful than he's usually given credit for. He distinguishes two kinds of tyrant: one who is a tyrant 'in title' — someone who's seized power with no legitimate claim at all, a usurper — and one who is a tyrant only 'in the exercise' of otherwise legitimate rule, i.e. a king who was properly instituted but now governs for his own benefit rather than the common good. For the first kind, Aquinas thinks any private person may act against him, because a usurper never had authority to begin with and is really just a species of public enemy — there's no legitimate authority being violated by resisting or killing him, so no one needs to wait around for a public judgment. This is the position of the Sentences commentary.

For the second kind, the legitimately-instituted ruler who degenerates into tyranny, Aquinas's fullest treatment is in De Regimine Principum, and there he basically extends the same permission: since the whole point of instituting a ruler is the common good, a king who consistently rules against it has, in effect, forfeited the title he once had, so he collapses into the first category and can be dealt with the same way, including by private citizens. Aquinas even appeals to the killing of Eglon by Ehud in Judges as a scriptural precedent showing that God can use a private individual as an instrument of tyrannicide. The Summa's discussion of sedition backs this up: Aquinas says that disturbing a tyrannical regime isn't really sedition at all, since the tyranny itself, and not its overthrow, is the truly disordered condition — sedition is only committed if the tyrant's rule is disturbed in a way so inordinate that the community ends up worse off than it was under the tyrant.

So compared to John of Salisbury, who's usually cited as the harsher, more 'tyrannicide-friendly' medieval author, Aquinas basically ends up in the same place on the substance, just with more careful conceptual machinery to get there."
