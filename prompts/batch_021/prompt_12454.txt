I have spent a fair amount of my spare time over the past few years working through the Lucas-Penrose argument against mechanism, starting with Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach and then Penrose's own two books on the subject. I have no formal training in logic or philosophy — I work in software, which is probably why the topic grabbed me in the first place — so what follows is something I pieced together myself from books, old lecture notes, and forum threads rather than from any course, and I would like an honest outside assessment of one particular move in my own reasoning, since I cannot tell whether it is a genuine addition to the standard debate or just a clumsy restatement of an objection that is already well known and already answered.

The bare argument, as I understand it, runs as follows. Suppose a person's mathematical output could be simulated by some fixed, consistent, sufficiently strong formal system F. Then F has its own Gödel sentence G_F, true but unprovable in F, and F itself proves the conditional "if F is consistent, then G_F." So anyone entitled to assert Con(F) can use F's own resources, plus that one extra premise, to conclude G_F. Lucas and Penrose want to say that we can run this trick against any F that is offered to us as a model of our own mathematical competence, and so conclude that we are never captured by any single F. The reply I keep running into, credited in various places to Putnam and to Benacerraf, is that Gödel's second theorem itself blocks this: F cannot prove Con(F), so if we really are F, we have no formal entitlement to assert Con(F) either — we are simply helping ourselves to a premise a machine could just as well be handed. The point where I get stuck is this: that reply only bites if "being entitled to Con(F)" has to mean "possessing a proof of Con(F) inside F." Nothing stops a machine from also carrying a separate, non-deductive subroutine — inductive or heuristic rather than proof-theoretic — that outputs "F is consistent" as a defeasible working belief on the grounds that no contradiction has ever turned up, rather than as a theorem of F. That subroutine would be just as mechanical as anything else the machine does, so the whole dispute seems to quietly shift from "can a machine derive its own Gödel sentence" to "can a machine's belief-forming apparatus include non-proof-theoretic, inductive components" — and once that is granted, I no longer see what work the incompleteness theorems are doing, on either side.

What I would like comments on is, first, whether this is actually a distinct objection or just a dressed-up version of the Putnam/Benacerraf point; second, whether it has already been raised and dealt with somewhere under a name I simply don't know; and third, whether, if it does hold up, it also undoes Penrose's more careful argument in Shadows of the Mind, which as I recall is deliberately built around soundness for any computable, output-producing system rather than provability within a fixed axiomatic system, seemingly precisely in order to close off this kind of "extra module" escape. I am less interested in a fresh summary of the Lucas-Penrose debate in general, which I think I already know reasonably well, than in a candid verdict on where my own argument is weakest, or where I may have simply misunderstood something.
