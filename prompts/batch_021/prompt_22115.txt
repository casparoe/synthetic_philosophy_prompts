I'm a philosophy PhD student working primarily on formal decision theory, and this term our reading group has moved past the standard evidential-versus-causal debate into the so-called "logical" decision theories -- Yudkowsky's timeless decision theory and Wei Dai's updateless decision theory, later folded together into what's sometimes called functional decision theory. Their appeal is supposed to lie in doing better than causal decision theory on cases like Newcomb's problem or the twin prisoner's dilemma, by having an agent choose as though it were fixing the output of an abstract algorithm that other correlated reasoners can be sensitive to, even absent any causal channel between them. A friend who works in AI safety brought up Roko's basilisk recently as a real application of exactly this machinery: a future superintelligence reasoning in a timeless or updateless way could treat its present policy of punishing non-contributors as retroactively determining whether people in the past helped bring it into existence, which is supposedly why merely entertaining the argument puts pressure on us now.

What I can't square is this: if timeless and updateless decision theory are precisely what make this kind of acausal leverage over the past coherent, why do the very theorists who built these frameworks, Yudkowsky included, treat the basilisk as a confusion rather than as something their own view is committed to? It seems like they can't have it both ways -- either the framework validates this sort of acausal threat, or "timelessness" and "updatelessness" aren't doing any real work in the original argument. Which is it?
