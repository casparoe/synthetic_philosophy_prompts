I'm drafting a historical novel built around a conspiracy to assassinate a tyrannical prince, and two beta readers -- one a lapsed seminarian, the other a lawyer with a weakness for medieval history -- got into a running argument in the manuscript's margin comments over whether my conspirators could plausibly invoke Aquinas the way I have them do in the scene. I'm passing the exchange along because I honestly can't settle it myself, and neither of them will let it drop.

She argued that Aquinas gives them cover: in the Summa he says that disturbing a tyrant's rule is not sedition, since a regime aimed at the ruler's private advantage rather than the common good was never a rightly ordered government to begin with, so unmaking it cannot be a sin against order. He answered that this is true but beside the point for my scene, since that passage is usually read as licensing collective or public removal of a tyrant, not one man's knife -- and that in On Kingship, discussing exactly the case of a legitimately established ruler who curdles into cruelty, Aquinas is conspicuously wary of private initiative, warning that letting individuals judge for themselves who counts as a tyrant only invites ambitious men to murder decent rulers under that pretext. She replied that the line between a usurper and a legitimate ruler gone bad cannot be doing much work, since the subjects suffer identically either way, and a doctrine more concerned with a ruler's paperwork than his subjects' welfare is not one Aquinas could seriously have intended. He came back that Aquinas's worry was never welfare versus paperwork but who gets to act as judge in his own case, and that worry does not dissolve just because, in a given instance, the abuse happens to be real.

Could you go through their exchange move by move, say who has the better of it at each step -- not just who wins overall -- and note anything about Aquinas's actual position that both of them seem to be missing?
