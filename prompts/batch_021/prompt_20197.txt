A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about determinism in quantum mechanics and never quite settled the disagreement. We agreed that both Bohmian mechanics and the Everett, or many-worlds, interpretation are fully deterministic at the fundamental level, and that each must therefore explain why observers nonetheless seem to encounter genuinely random, Born-rule-distributed outcomes upon measurement. My friend insisted that the two interpretations solve this puzzle in essentially the same way, by converting determinism into a form of principled ignorance. I argued that the two moves are not really analogous: the Bohmian appeals to ordinary ignorance of an objectively existing but unmeasured initial configuration, much as in classical statistical mechanics, whereas the Everettian, following Deutsch and Wallace, has to invoke something stranger — a rational agent's self-locating uncertainty about which branch they will end up in, even though no fact about the outcome is hidden from anyone, since every branch is realized. Please compare how each interpretation actually accounts for the appearance of randomness in measurement outcomes, then settle our disagreement: are these two treatments of "apparent randomness" structurally the same move in different formal clothing, or is there a genuine difference in kind between them? And if there is a real difference, does it make one interpretation's account of probability more defensible than the other's?
