Last weekend a friend of mine, who's finishing a physics PhD, kept me up much later than I'd planned arguing about something she called the "Boltzmann brain problem." The basic idea, as she explained it, goes back to Boltzmann himself, who suggested that a universe as improbably low-entropy as ours might just be a rare statistical fluctuation inside an otherwise eternal, high-entropy equilibrium. The modern version is stranger: in cosmological models where accelerating expansion continues forever and the universe settles into a near-empty vacuum state, random fluctuations will, over sufficiently long stretches of time, occasionally assemble an entire functioning brain out of the surrounding chaos — complete with a full set of memories of a life it never lived — before it dissolves again a moment later. Because it takes a far smaller, far more probable fluctuation to produce one such isolated brain than to produce an entire orderly galaxy full of observers who arrived at consciousness the ordinary evolutionary way, these models seem to predict that almost every "observer" who ever has an experience like ours is one of these brief, spontaneous brains rather than someone with a genuine past.

My friend thinks this is a real problem and not just a curiosity: a theory that predicts your own memories and reasoning are almost certainly meaningless noise pulls the rug out from under any grounds you had for believing the theory in the first place, so on her view it counts as strong evidence against those cosmological models. I argued that the whole thing is too self-undermining to function as evidence about anything — if you can't trust your reasoning either way, you can't use it to rule theories in or out — and that in practice we just have to bracket the possibility and get on with things. Neither of us budged, and I've kept turning the idea over since.

What I keep getting stuck on is what it would actually be like from the inside, if "inside" even means anything for something that might exist for a fraction of a second. I'd like you to write a short story that takes this seriously as a lived experience rather than just a thought experiment. You could stay entirely within the instant of the fluctuation, or follow someone going through what seems like an ordinary day whose situation is not quite what it appears to be — I'll leave the setting to you. I'm less interested in a story about scientists debating the idea than in one that puts a reader inside it, with whatever vertigo that involves for the character's sense of memory, continuity, and whether even a dawning realization of what might be happening to them could mean anything at all.
