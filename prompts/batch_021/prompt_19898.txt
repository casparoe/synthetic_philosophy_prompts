As a software engineer, I frequently find myself asserting things like "this function is thread-safe" in a code review, or "root cause: a race condition in the cache layer" in an incident postmortem, without always pausing to ask whether I actually know these claims to be true or merely have strong grounds for suspecting them. I recently discovered that epistemologists debate this exact question under the heading of "norms of assertion": whether a proper assertion requires that the speaker know what they are asserting, merely that the assertion be true, or merely that they sincerely (or justifiably) believe it. Please explain this debate in some depth, in particular how considerations like Moore's paradox ("it's raining, but I don't believe it is") and the lottery case (where being 99.9999% statistically justified that a given ticket lost still seems too weak a basis for flatly asserting it) are used to argue for and against each account. I'd also like to understand whether the knowledge account can survive Gettier-style cases, in which an assertion still seems entirely proper even though the speaker's justified true belief falls short of knowledge purely by luck.
