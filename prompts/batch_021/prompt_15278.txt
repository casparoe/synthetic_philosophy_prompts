At my job I've been prototyping a pipeline that decides when an automated system should pause and pay for a costly human review before committing to an action, and while I was looking for background reading, a teammate pointed me to some classic work in decision theory. That's how I first ran across a result usually attributed to the statistician I. J. Good: for an idealized Bayesian agent who updates on new evidence and then picks whichever action maximizes expected utility given that evidence, the expected value of a free observation is never negative. The intuition is simple enough: if the observation wouldn't change what you'd do, it costs you nothing; if it would change what you'd do, you only switch when the switch is an improvement, so on average, gathering more information before acting can only help you, or at worst leave you exactly where you started.

What I can't quite square this with is something I've separately come across in medical ethics: the idea of a "right not to know," and the fact that plenty of people decline free, decision-relevant information when it is offered to them. People at risk for Huntington's disease or a hereditary cancer mutation sometimes choose not to take an available genetic test; people avoid opening a medical result, a bank statement, or a message they suspect contains bad news. These don't strike me as merely thoughtless choices — some of the people who make them have clearly reasoned their way there. Yet on its face, this looks like exactly the situation Good's theorem says a rational agent should never be in: turning down free information that bears on real decisions — family planning, insurance, how to spend the next few years — that they will have to make either way.

I'd like you to compare Good's theorem, as a formal result in Bayesian decision theory, with this real-world practice of deliberately declining free, decision-relevant information. Is the "right not to know" actually a counterexample to Good's theorem, or does it just expose an assumption baked into the theorem — for instance, that knowing something affects your well-being only indirectly, through the action it leads you to take, and never directly — that quietly fails to hold in these cases? And if the theorem can be rescued by folding the psychological cost of knowledge itself into the agent's utility function, does "the expected value of information is never negative" still tell us anything substantive about when we should want to learn more, or does it collapse into something true merely by construction, unfalsifiable by any pattern of real behavior? Please work through this and defend a specific verdict rather than simply cataloguing considerations on both sides.
