A friend and I spent most of a dinner arguing about Confucian role ethics after a seminar session in which we'd read the Analects passage where the Duke of She tells Confucius that in his own community there is a son so upright that he testified against his father for stealing a sheep. Confucius's reply is that uprightness (zhi) looks different where he comes from: fathers conceal their sons' wrongdoing and sons conceal their fathers', and it is in that mutual concealment, not in impartial testimony, that uprightness actually consists. My friend, drawing on Ames and Rosemont's account of Confucian ethics as genuinely role-constituted rather than a dressed-up variant of virtue ethics, took the passage as showing exactly what's right about grounding moral life in one's constitutive roles and relations rather than in some role-independent standpoint: there is no "self" behind the father-son relation capable of betraying it in the name of a higher impartiality, so testifying against one's father would be a kind of self-betrayal rather than integrity. I argued that this can't be the whole story: without some standard that isn't simply "whatever this role calls for," role ethics seems to have no principled way to distinguish this concealment from ordinary nepotism except by covertly borrowing something like ren as a role-independent yardstick, and no way to say what a person should do when their obligations as son and, say, as citizen, or as neighbor to the man who lost his sheep, actually conflict. We went around in circles for the better part of an hour and never got anywhere.

I'd like you to write the dialogue we should have had. Please write a conversation between two philosophers who are both genuinely fluent in Confucian role ethics but land on opposite sides of this passage: one who defends it as a paradigm case of what role ethics gets right, and one who presses the nepotism/particularism worry as a real structural problem rather than a cheap shot, perhaps drawing on the older Confucian-Mohist debate over graded versus impartial love. Make sure each side directly answers the other's strongest point rather than simply restating their own past it, so that if the defender appeals to graded love or to the relational self, the critic gets a genuine rejoinder, and vice versa, and don't let either character concede or arrive at a tidy synthesis by the end. I'd rather the dialogue close with the real crux of the disagreement sharpened than resolved.
