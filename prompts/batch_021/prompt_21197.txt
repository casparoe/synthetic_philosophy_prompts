The principle on the table (our professor called it the "enkratic principle" but don't quote me on the terminology) says its irrational to believe P outright while also believing that your evidence doesn't support P, or that you rationally shouldn't be so confident in P. Sounds obviously right the first time you hear it. But then in section last week we went through a case like this: a doctor near the end of a brutal 30-hour shift carefully works out a drug dosage, double checks the arithmetic, and feels totally confident in the number. It seems like learning this should make the doctor less confident in her dosage number, even though re-checking it (yet again) turns up nothing wrong. But if she ends up thinking "the number is still 40mg" while also thinking "my evidence says I shouldnt be this sure it's 40mg," isn't that just akrasia -- exactly the state the enkratic principle is supposed to rule out as irrational?

End with an actual verdict, defended with real reasons, on whether epistemic akrasia is ever rational -- not a wishy-washy "both sides have a point" non-answer.
