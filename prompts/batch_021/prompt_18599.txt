I'm in the middle of a graduate seminar on Kant's ethics this term, and the timing has turned out to be strange. For the past several months I've also been a "hospice foster" for a dog rescue near me, which means taking in senior dogs nobody expects to get adopted so they can spend whatever time is left somewhere other than a shelter kennel. My current foster, a fourteen-year-old shepherd mix named Hazel, has started declining quickly, and it's made the week we spent on the Metaphysics of Morals's discussion of duties "regarding" animals feel considerably less academic than it did on the syllabus. I've drafted the core of a short essay working through why Kant's position bothers me more than I expected, and I'd like an outside read before I bring it to my seminar's writing group. Here's the section I want feedback on:

Kant is usually summarized as holding that we have no duties to animals because they aren't rational, self-conscious beings capable of setting their own ends, and that's accurate as far as it goes. What the summary usually drops is where he actually puts the discussion. In the Doctrine of Virtue, duties "regarding" non-rational nature (animals, plants, even inanimate things worth preserving for their beauty) aren't discussed under duties to others at all; they show up in the Episodic Section on the amphiboly of moral concepts of reflection, appended to the duties we have to ourselves. Structurally, that's the whole argument in miniature: mistreating an animal isn't wrong because of what it does to the animal, but because of what it does to the agent who does the mistreating. The old-dog passage in the Lectures on Ethics makes the same point with an example: a dog that has served faithfully and can no longer serve should still be kept and cared for, not as a matter of justice owed to the dog, which cannot press a claim on its own behalf, but because failing to do so would blunt the very fellow-feeling that disposes us to treat other humans decently. My problem is that this makes the wrongness of cruelty toward an animal entirely hostage to a contingent empirical claim about moral psychology, namely that mistreating animals erodes our capacity for human-directed sympathy, and I don't think that claim can do the work Kant needs it to do. It seems too weak, since there's no obvious reason that a sufficiently compartmentalized cruelty, say, industrialized and physically removed from the agent, couldn't leave the relevant disposition untouched, in which case Kant's own machinery would have nothing left to say against it. And it seems to get the phenomenology backwards: when I think about what I owe Hazel in whatever time she has left, it doesn't present itself to me as a worry about my own character; it presents itself as being about her. I believe this is roughly the objection Broadie and Pybus raised back in the 1970s, though I'm working from memory and could have the names or the decade wrong. I also don't think Korsgaard's move in Fellow Creatures, relocating end-in-itself status in having a good of one's own rather than in rational autonomy as such, actually rescues Kant's view so much as it replaces it with a different one wearing Kant's vocabulary. My tentative closing thesis is that the amphiboly placement isn't a fixable oversight; it's forced by tying all moral standing to autonomy from the outset, which is exactly why every attempt to patch the view from the inside ends up smuggling in a non-Kantian premise.

I'd appreciate comments on a few specific things: whether I've got the textual architecture right (the amphiboly placement, the "to" versus "regarding" distinction, the old-dog passage); whether the "contingent empirical claim" objection is a fair rendering of the standard worry here or whether I'm flattening something more subtle; whether the Broadie-and-Pybus attribution and the Korsgaard reading are close enough to accurate to leave in a seminar paper without further checking; and, most importantly, whether the closing thesis actually holds up or just sounds good, i.e., is there a way of making the amphiboly placement look contingent rather than forced that I'm not seeing? I'd rather find the weak point now than in front of my seminar.
