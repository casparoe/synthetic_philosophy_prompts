And here's the student's answer, verbatum:

"For the Naiyayikas, anumana is inference from a mark (hetu) to what it invariably accompanies. The classic example: I see smoke on a hill and infer fire, because smoke and fire are invariably related (vyapti), as we've observed together in the kitchen. The syllogism has five members: pratijna (the hill has fire), hetu (because it has smoke), udaharana (as in the kitchen), upanaya (the hill has smoke, which is invariably accompanied by fire), and nigamana (therefore the hill has fire). Vyapti itself is just this citing of the kitchen as a shared example -- without an example both parties accept, there's no inference, which is why Nyaya insists the hetu must actually be present in similar cases (sapaksa) and absent from dissimilar ones (vipaksa). On the question of validity: Nyaya, like the Bhatta Mimamsakas, holds that a cognition is intrinsically valid (svatah pramanya) unless something defeats it, and inference counts as a pramana in its own right because its deliverances, unlike perception's, don't need this default presumption of truth to be overridden by further inquiry -- they're self-certifying. That's also why inference can range over unperceived objects, like fire behind a hill, extending our knowledge beyond present sense-contact."
