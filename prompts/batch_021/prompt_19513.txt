Our school's Honor Council is a student-run body that hears cheating and plagiarism cases and decides on sanctions, and every year there's a "turnover" banquet where the outgoing chair formally hands things over to whoever is replacing them. I was just elected to take over from Naomi, who has chaired the council for the last two years and is, by any reasonable measure, the most dedicated and effective chair we've had: she sat through as many as fifteen hearings, rewrote most of the procedures we still use, and was tough enough that even people she sanctioned generally admit afterward that she was fair. The complication is that two weeks ago, through an old group chat that resurfaced, it came out that Naomi let a friend copy her chemistry lab write-up freshman year, before she'd even joined the council. She hasn't tried to deny it or explain it away, and she's asked whoever speaks at the banquet "not to put me on trial, and not to pretend it didn't happen" — and since I'm the one giving the handover remarks, that request is now my problem to solve.

The room is going to be split in a way that makes this harder. The council members and our faculty advisor all know exactly what happened and have been arguing about it nonstop, while most of the parents, teachers, and younger students in the audience know Naomi only as the strict, fair chair and have no idea about the lab report. One of my friends on the council, who is really into philosophy, keeps insisting that Naomi forfeited what he calls her "standing to blame" the moment she let her own violation slide — that she was never entitled to sit in judgment of anyone after that, whatever the rules said. Another friend thinks that's a non sequitur: he says pointing out someone's hypocrisy like this (he called it "tu quoque") might be a fair complaint about her personally, but it does nothing to show that the people she sanctioned were actually innocent, or that the code itself was wrong to enforce. I don't know which of them has the better view, and I don't think the speech should try to settle it, but I do think whoever writes it needs to genuinely track the difference between "Naomi wasn't entitled to judge us" and "Naomi's judgments were wrong" — because otherwise I'll end up either pretending the lab report doesn't matter or accidentally implying that everyone she ever sanctioned got a bum deal, and neither of those is true.

Could you write the handover speech for me? It should run about three to four minutes, it has to genuinely honor what Naomi did for the council over two years, it can't say anything false about the lab report or soften it into nothing, and it needs to leave room for both halves of the audience — the ones who know the full story and the ones who don't — to hear it as sincere rather than as either a whitewash or a quiet trial.
