So I've been reporting a piece on "information avoidance" — the research area on why people skip the genetic test for Huntington's, don't check the banking app when they're overdrawn, or ask the surgeon to tell their partner the biopsy result first instead of them. Theres a whole psych/behavioral econ literature on this by now. But when I ran a draft past a decision theorist friend, he said I was dancing around the actual theoretical backdrop: there's a classic result called Good's theorem (I.J. Good, late 60s) that supposedly proves a rational Bayesian agent should never expect free evidence to leave them worse off — the expected value of information is always non-negative, as long as they go on to update and act optimally on whatever they learn. He said most of the "look how irrational humans are" framing in the avoidance literature just skips over what the theorem actually licenses you to conclude, and now I cant stop turning it over.

My editor hates wishy-washy conclusions, so here's what I need from you: an essay that actually gets into the guts of the theorem — why the proof goes through (it's basically a Jensen's-inequality trick: choosing your best action after you've seen the evidence beats committing to one before you've seen it, on average), and what exactly "non-negative in expectation" is and isn't promising you — and then adjudicates whether cases like Huntington's testing, spoiler-avoidance, or the classic finding that making information public can destroy an insurance arrangement that private uncertainty had made possible, are real counterexamples to the theorem, or just cases it was never making a claim about in the first place. Don't hedge and list considerations on both sides and shrug at the end — I want an actual verdict I can quote in the piece (a qualified one is fine, something like "the theorem is airtight but almost never the right thing to say to an information-avoider," if that's where you land), plus the argument that gets you there.
