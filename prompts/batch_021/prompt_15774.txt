I am at work on a novel whose two central characters are a father and his adult son, both professional philosophers, whose long-standing theoretical disagreement is thrown into painful relief when the son, driving distracted, badly injures a cyclist. I have loosely modelled the pair on the real Peter Strawson and his son Galen Strawson, largely because it strikes me as wonderful material for fiction that two people so close to one another — sharing a discipline, and presumably decades of dinner-table argument — should have ended up on opposite sides of one of philosophy's oldest disputes. Before I let my characters speak, though, I want to understand the actual philosophical terrain well enough that I am not simply putting a caricature of each position into their mouths.

Could you lay out Galen Strawson's "Basic Argument" against the possibility of true, desert-entailing moral responsibility, and set it properly against his father's account in "Freedom and Resentment," which grounds our practices of praise and blame in reactive attitudes such as resentment and gratitude rather than in any metaphysical fact about self-authorship? What I would really like to know is whether the father's naturalistic strategy genuinely meets the regress at the heart of the son's argument, or whether it only appears to, by quietly substituting a thinner, practice-based notion of responsibility for the deep, ultimate kind the Basic Argument was actually targeting. Please give the elder Strawson's position the strongest version of its case before you answer, and then tell me plainly which side comes out ahead, and why: I want the son's view to read as the harder one to dismiss in my novel, but only if that is something I can honestly earn on the page rather than simply assert by authorial fiat.
