I am a philosophy PhD student working on social epistemology, and for the chapter of my dissertation on prediction markets as truth-aggregating institutions I have had to teach myself the economics literature on no-trade theorems, since it keeps getting invoked on both sides of debates about whether markets are good at tracking truth. I understand the basic Aumann (1976) and Milgrom–Stokey (1982) story well enough to state it informally: if it is common knowledge between two Bayesian agents with the same prior that one of them is willing to buy an asset from the other at a given price, that willingness is itself evidence about the seller's private information, so the buyer should update on it — and once both parties fully account for what their counterpart's offer reveals, any apparent gains from trade wash out, leaving no reason to trade at all. My problem is that popular write-ups, and one very confident postdoc in my department, treat "common prior" and "common knowledge of rationality" as basically interchangeable ingredients in this result, and I do not think that is right, but I cannot reconstruct the argument precisely enough to say why.

Concretely, I would like you to walk through three things. First, what work the common-prior assumption is doing in Aumann's original agreement theorem and in the Milgrom–Stokey extension to actual trade, as distinct from what common knowledge of rationality (or of the offer to trade itself) is doing: can one get any interesting no-trade or no-bet conclusion out of common knowledge of rationality alone, without a common prior, or does the whole argument collapse the moment priors are allowed to differ? Second, how the Milgrom–Stokey result differs from a bare restatement of "agreeing to disagree is impossible," given that trade involves incentives and Pareto optimality of the pre-trade allocation in a way that mere belief-reporting does not. Third, and this is the part that matters most for my dissertation: prediction-market advocates seem to want it both ways, insisting that prices aggregate dispersed private information (which requires informed traders to actually act on what they know) while also leaning on efficient-markets-style reasoning to argue that prices are trustworthy precisely because they already reflect all available information. That second move sounds to me like it is importing the Grossman–Stiglitz worry about informationally efficient markets destroying the incentive to gather information in the first place, rather than the no-trade theorem proper — but I have seen both results cited for the same conclusion often enough that I suspect I may be drawing a distinction that does not hold up. Please sort out whether these are genuinely different arguments and, if so, what a defender of prediction markets owes in response to each; I would rather you flag places where I am conflating distinct results, or where the literature itself is unsettled, than smooth the gaps over for a tidy answer.
