Over a forty-year career I worked as a condensed-matter physicist, and it is only in retirement that I have begun reading philosophy with any seriousness. My current preoccupation is panpsychism -- the view, taken seriously and in some form defended by contemporary philosophers such as Philip Goff and David Chalmers, that consciousness, or some primitive precursor of it, is a fundamental and pervasive feature of matter rather than a late arrival that emerges only in brains. As I understand it, the chief technical obstacle facing the view is the so-called combination problem: the difficulty of explaining how the faint proto-experiences supposedly possessed by individual particles could combine into the single, unified experience of a whole subject, such as a person.

The more I turn this over, however, the less I am persuaded that it deserves to be treated as a distinctive philosophical difficulty rather than an ordinary unsolved problem in physics that further theorizing will presumably dissolve, much as has happened with other composition problems historically. Physics already contains precise rules describing how micro-level quantities combine into macro-level ones: electric charge adds in a perfectly straightforward way, and mass does too, at least to an excellent approximation once nuclear binding effects are set aside; and even an apparently holistic bulk property like temperature turns out to be nothing more than a redescription of the statistical spread of kinetic energies among a huge number of particles. Chemical bonding looked similarly mysterious before quantum mechanics supplied the missing law. Given this track record, why should we treat the combination problem as anything more than the temporary absence of an analogous formula for how proto-experiences sum -- a gap that a future science of consciousness might simply close -- rather than as evidence that panpsychism suffers from some special defect that ordinary physical theories, faced with their own open questions, do not share?

I raised this at my reading group, where a friend with a philosophy background insisted that modeling subjects of experience on additive quantities such as charge or mass already begs the question at issue, though she was unable to spell out the disanalogy to my satisfaction, and the rest of the group was divided. I would be grateful if you could clarify whether the combination problem really is a different kind of difficulty from ordinary cases of physical composition, and, if so, exactly where my proposed deflationary reading of it breaks down.
