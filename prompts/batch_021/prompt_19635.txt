Six weeks ago, a direct-to-consumer DNA test revealed that the man who raised me is not my biological father. Working through the practical fallout has been difficult enough, but I've also gotten stuck on a conceptual question I can't let go of. On Kripke's account, once we fix on the actual man involved, his name is a rigid designator that picks out that same individual in every possible world in which he exists, and Kripke's discussion of the necessity of origin suggests that the identity statement "X is my biological father" is therefore a necessary a posteriori truth, exactly like "water is H2O" — necessary, yet something I could only discover empirically, in my case through a laboratory test rather than through chemistry. Locke's account of personal identity, by contrast, locates identity in the continuity of consciousness rather than in facts about biological origin, which would seem to make my discovery close to irrelevant to who I "really" am. Compare these two frameworks in enough depth that I can see precisely where and why they diverge on a case like mine, and then tell me which one, in your judgment, gives the more defensible account of personal identity.
