I'm the deputy IT director for our regional hospital network, and I sit on the incident command committee that has to decide, by tomorrow evening's emergency session, how we respond to a ransomware attack that has been degrading our systems for three days now. I never studied philosophy formally, but about a year ago a friend sent me a philosophy blog post about something called Parfit's Hitchhiker, and it has stuck with me ever since: you're dying of thirst in the desert, and a driver will only give you a lift into town if he can somehow tell, before he picks you up, that you'll actually pay him once you arrive safely; but once you're standing in town with the ride already behind you, the "rational" thing to do, strictly by the lights of what benefits you from that moment on, is to keep your money, since paying gets you nothing further — except that a driver who can see this coming will never pick you up in the first place, and you'll die in the desert. The lesson I took from it was that it can be right, ahead of time, to make yourself into the kind of agent who will do something "irrational" later, and I've always assumed this was the real logic behind our hospital's long-standing policy of never paying ransomware demands, adopted five years ago on a previous board's watch partly on federal cybersecurity guidance and partly, I'm told, because one of the physicians on that board had read the same argument I had.

That policy is no longer academic. The attackers are asking for a sum well within what our cyber-insurance would reimburse, our forensics vendor says a clean recovery without paying could take another one to two weeks, two clinical units are back on paper charts, and our chief medical officer reported yesterday two medication near-misses she attributes directly to the outage. Four of our eleven committee members now want a one-time exception, arguing that whatever deterrent value our reputation for never paying might have is speculative, while the harm to patients this week is not. I don't think they're foolish for saying so, but it also seems to me that "just this once, given how bad it's gotten" is exactly the reasoning a policy like this exists to withstand, and that a policy which bends whenever the stakes are high enough was never really a commitment at all. I'd like you to draft the memo I'll bring to that session: it should recommend one specific course of action — holding firm, paying, or something more conditional — and it needs to take the patient-safety argument for paying fully seriously, on its own terms, rather than treating it as a temptation to be resisted on principle alone.
