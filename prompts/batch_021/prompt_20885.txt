I write literary fiction, and for the past two years I've sat on the five-member board that administers the Kestrel Prize, a small annual award for a debut novel published by a British independent press. Our funding comes from a regional arts council that is now pushing us, as a condition of renewal, to add a "readers' choice" strand this year: alongside the jury's winner, an online public vote conducted through our website and social media would decide a second, equally publicised award, on the theory that this will raise our profile and give a sales boost to shortlisted authors who badly need it. Two of my fellow trustees are enthusiastic; two are appalled, worried about vote-brigading by publicists with large followings, about a pile-on against any shortlisted writer who happens to be the target of unrelated online controversy, and more generally about handing literary judgment to whoever can mobilise the most attention — which leaves me the deciding vote. I'm currently drafting a novel narrated by a nineteenth-century newspaper editor, which sent me back to Kierkegaard's Two Ages (1846) — published just as the satirical paper The Corsair was turning him into a public joke — and in particular to the closing section usually excerpted as "The Present Age." His account of "the public" as an abstraction manufactured by the press, and of the leveling it works on genuine judgment, keeps striking me as bearing directly on our situation, even though he had newspaper readers in 1840s Copenhagen in mind rather than online polls.

We meet in three weeks to fix this year's rules before the submission window opens, and I need to circulate a short memo beforehand, partly to convince myself as much as the others. Could you draft it for me? I want a clear recommendation — adopt the readers' vote, reject it, or adopt some hybrid with specific safeguards — reached through real philosophical work rather than an invocation of Kierkegaard's name for atmosphere. Please engage specifically with his distinction between the public and more concrete forms of association, such as a people, a congregation, or an assembly, in which members are actually answerable to one another, and with his claims about how the press manufactures an opinion for which no one in particular is responsible. I'd also like the memo to confront a complication rather than smooth it over: Kierkegaard's own remedy for leveling was the single individual's inward turn, not better collective arrangements, and if anything he was hostile to the idea that any institution could legislate its way out of the problem he diagnosed — so say plainly what, if anything, that leaves a board like ours to do. And whichever way you come down, make sure the trustees on the losing side would recognise their strongest argument in how you represent it, rather than a caricature of it.
