I've spent a long time lurking on LessWrong, Overcoming Bias, and the Alignment Forum, and one thing that keeps nagging at me is how the phrase "money pump" gets used in what feel like two different registers there. In the classic register, it's the diachronic cousin of the Dutch book: an agent with a cyclical preference over some options A, B, and C (or, on some versions, merely incomplete preferences plus a willingness to accept a "free" sweetener) can be walked through a sequence of trades that leaves it strictly poorer while ending up back where it started, and this is supposed to show that transitivity and completeness aren't just tidy modeling assumptions but genuine requirements of rationality. In the other register — the one that shows up around discussions of updateless decision theory and Parfit's hitchhiker — the moral seems to run the opposite way: an agent that reoptimizes at every choice node (what Edward McClennen called a "sophisticated" chooser, as opposed to a "resolute" one who sticks to a plan even where deviating would look locally better) is exactly the kind of agent that ends up money-pumped, or stranded in the desert, because it can never credibly commit itself to anything that its later self would not independently endorse at the time.

Given that, could you lay out the most important implications of the money-pump literature — including the usual resolute-choice and sophisticated-choice replies, plus the more deflationary view that exploitability of this kind is simply a cost worth paying to keep otherwise-attractive incomplete preferences — for a fairly concrete question about my own life: how far I should actually go in cultivating resolute dispositions, such as an irrevocable multi-year donation pledge or a flat policy against ever renegotiating a deal with my past self, rather than treating every future decision point as a fresh occasion to reoptimize given whatever preferences I happen to have by then. My reading group has been stuck for weeks on whether "being resolute" names a substantive rationality constraint that the money-pump arguments actually license, or whether it's just a polite label for privileging your past self's preferences over a future self who never signed up for them — and I'd like to see the strongest version of both sides before we go another round.
