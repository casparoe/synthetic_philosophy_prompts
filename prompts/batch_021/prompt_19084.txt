My grandfather taught philosophy for thirty years before he retired, and somewhere in his seventies he did something none of his former colleagues quite expected: he became a genuinely observant Jew, keeping Shabbat and praying daily, while insisting to anyone who asked that none of his actual philosophical commitments had changed in the process. He used to say, citing the later Hilary Putnam -- the Putnam who wrote at length on Rosenzweig, Buber, Levinas, and Wittgenstein -- that the real mistake had always been to picture God as a being whose existence could be settled from some detached, God's-eye vantage point, the way one settles a scientific hypothesis.

What he took from that quartet was a different picture entirely: revelation as a standing relation rather than a message delivered once and closed, from Rosenzweig; the difference between addressing God as a Thou and describing him as an object like any other, from Buber; the thought that the ethical claim made on us by another person's face is the nearest thing we get to a trace of the divine, from Levinas; and the sense that religious practice is a form of life rather than a rival to physics, from Wittgenstein. Taken together, these gave him what he wanted: a way of being fully and unapologetically religious without ever again needing a supernatural agent who arranges events for reasons, or a soul that detaches from the body to watch over us from somewhere. Before he died, he told me plainly that he did not want his eulogy to smuggle that picture back in simply because it is the customary comfort at a funeral.

I am the one giving the eulogy, and the room will be split in a way that makes this genuinely difficult. Half of it is his old department and his grandchildren, who will hear any lapse into "he's in a better place, watching over us" as either an embarrassing concession or proof that I never understood him. The other half is his wife's observant family and congregation, for whom some version of exactly that thought is the main comfort a funeral is supposed to offer, and who loved him deeply and should not come away feeling corrected at his own service. I need a short eulogy, five minutes or so, that stays fully honest to how he actually thought death, God, and survival worked -- built from his own positive resources rather than a list of things he didn't believe -- and that still lands as consolation for both halves of the room rather than as a lecture on the philosophy of religion. Could you write it?
