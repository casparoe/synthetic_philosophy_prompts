Philosophers writing on moral blame sometimes distinguish a blamer's standing to blame from the truth of what they say: even when an accusation is completely accurate and the target is exactly as guilty as charged, a sufficiently hypocritical accuser can nonetheless lack the standing to voice it. Please argue for this claim, explaining why the truth of an accusation is not on its own sufficient to license the act of making it. Address directly the natural objection that any residual complaint against a hypocritical-but-correct blamer must really concern something else entirely — their manners, motives, or general character — rather than a genuine bar on the speech act of blaming itself, and explain why such a bar, if real, need not excuse the wrongdoer or lessen their culpability.
