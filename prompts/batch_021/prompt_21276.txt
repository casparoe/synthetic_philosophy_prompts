I spent about thirty-five years as a comparative physiologist, poking around fish gills, amphibian kidneys, that sort of thing, and now that I'm retired I've fallen down a philosophy of biology rabbit hole for fun (my daughter says its a mid-life crisis, except I'm about twenty years too late for that). A retired colleague roped me into a little reading group at the university library nearby, and this month we're on the old argument about what it even means to say a trait or organ "has a function." Turns out I used that word constantly for my entire career without once worrying about what I actually meant by it, which is a little humbling in hindsight.

As far as I can tell there are two rival camps. One (I believe it goes back to a paper by a philosopher named Wright sometime in the 70s, later sharpened up quite a bit by Millikan and Neander) ties a trait's function to its selective history — the function of a trait just is whatever it was selected to do by natural selection in the past, full stop. The other camp (Cummins, arguing against Wright, I think) says forget the history entirely — a trait's function is just whatever causal contribution it presently makes to some capacity of the larger system you happen to be interested in, regardless of how it got there.

Could you compare these two accounts for me in real depth, not just the one-line slogan version? Specifically I'd love it if you worked through two cases that keep nagging me. First: a brand new mutant trait that happens to do something useful the very first generation it shows up, before selection has had a single chance to act on it — does it already have a function on the historical view, or are we stuck saying it doesn't yet, even though it's doing something good for the organism right now? Second: something like a heart in arrythmia — can the "current causal contribution" side actually make sense of calling that a malfunction, given that malfunctioning seems to presuppose some standard the organ is failing to meet, which sounds suspiciously like it's smuggling history back in through the back door. Once you've worked through both cases, I'd like you to actually commit to a verdict on which account holds up better overall as a theory of biological function — please don't just tell me "both have their merits," take a side and defend it.
