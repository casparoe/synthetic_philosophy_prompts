For most of my working life I was an experimental physicist, and one habit I have never managed to shake off in retirement is a suspicion of any argument whose conclusion arrives too neatly. I have lately begun reading Plato for the first time, currently Jowett's translation of the Euthyphro, and I find that I am not stuck on the celebrated dilemma itself — every introductory account explains that well enough — but on the argument Socrates offers immediately afterward to show that "loved by the gods" cannot name the essence of piety. The exchange runs as follows (lightly condensed):

SOCRATES: Tell me then: is not a thing that is carried in a state of being carried because someone carries it, and not the reverse — that someone carries it because it is in a state of being carried? And is it not the same with a thing that is led, or a thing that is seen?

EUTHYPHRO: I think I understand you.

SOCRATES: And is it not the same with a thing that is loved? A thing is not loved by those who love it because it is in a state of being loved; rather, it is in that state because they love it.

EUTHYPHRO: Certainly.

SOCRATES: Then that which is dear to the gods is dear to them because it is loved by them, and not loved by them because it is already dear to them?

EUTHYPHRO: True.

SOCRATES: But if the dear-to-the-gods and the pious were one and the same, then, just as the pious would be loved because it is pious, so the dear-to-the-gods would be loved because it is dear to the gods; and just as the dear-to-the-gods is dear because it is loved, the pious would be pious because it is loved. Since these two accounts pull in opposite directions, the dear-to-the-gods and the pious cannot be the same thing: the one is loved because it possesses some further nature of its own that makes it worth loving, while the other has no nature beyond being loved. So it appears, Euthyphro, that in telling me the pious is whatever all the gods love, you have named an attribute of the pious, but you have not yet told me its essence.

EUTHYPHRO: I do not know how to say what I mean, Socrates. Somehow, whatever statements we put forward keep turning round and walking away from us.

I can follow the individual steps well enough, and the parallel with distinguishing a cause from a mere correlate is not lost on me, but I would appreciate help reconstructing the whole argument, premise by premise, so that it is clear exactly why Socrates thinks that being loved by the gods is an attribute of piety rather than its essence. I would also welcome your judgment on how far the same piece of reasoning travels: if it is sound, does it defeat only Euthyphro's particular proposal, or does it equally undermine any account that tries to define a value in terms of some attitude taken toward it, whether divine approval, the verdict of an ideal observer, or ordinary human sentiment? Please do not spend time restating the famous dilemma itself; I have that well in hand, and it is the machinery of this follow-up argument, and its reach, that presently escapes me.
