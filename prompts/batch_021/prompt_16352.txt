Here is the answer as submitted (lightly paraphrased to remove identifying details):

"Harsanyi's Social Aggregation Theorem assumes a society of individuals 1, ..., n together with a 'society' preference relation, all defined over the same set of lotteries over social outcomes and all satisfying the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms of completeness, transitivity, continuity, and independence; this guarantees that each individual, and society itself, can be represented by a cardinal utility function unique up to a positive affine transformation. Harsanyi then imposes a Pareto condition: whenever every individual is indifferent between two lotteries, so is society. From only these assumptions he derives the striking conclusion that society's utility function must be a weighted sum of the individual utility functions, U_society = a_1U_1 + ... + a_nU_n, where the a_i are fixed non-negative weights that the axioms require to be equal across individuals -- in other words, the only social welfare function consistent with the Pareto condition and expected-utility rationality is the classical utilitarian sum, in which everybody's utility counts the same. This is why the theorem is usually presented as a genuine proof of utilitarianism rather than a restatement of it, and why it is often said to reach this conclusion without needing to assume interpersonal comparisons of utility at the outset: the equal weighting simply falls out of the axioms. The intuitive engine behind the result is the same veil-of-ignorance idea Harsanyi uses elsewhere: a rational chooser who does not yet know which of the n individuals she will turn out to be assigns herself an equal 1/n chance of being each one, and maximizing her own expected utility under this equiprobability is exactly equivalent to maximizing the unweighted sum of everyone's utility, which is what fixes the weights in the aggregation theorem.

The most serious objection to this argument comes from Rawls, who denies that a party behind the veil of ignorance should reason by expected-utility maximization at all. Given the gravity and irreversibility of not knowing which position in society one will occupy, Rawls argues in A Theory of Justice that the rational choice behind the veil is to apply the maximin rule rather than to maximize expected utility, since no gain to the better-off can compensate for the risk of ending up in the worst position. Because this objection attacks the expected-utility axiom on which Harsanyi's derivation of equal weights depends, rather than the Pareto condition, it is generally regarded as the decisive challenge to Harsanyi's proof of utilitarianism from the original position."
