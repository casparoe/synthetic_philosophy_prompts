In your view, is Hart's position fully consistent given that he himself accepts some legal paternalism?"

And here's Jamie's answer:

"Patrick Devlin's argument, sometimes called the disintegration thesis, is that every society is held together by a shared morality in the same way it's held together by a shared government, and that if the state doesn't protect that shared morality it isn't protecting anything at all — society will just fall apart over time. Devlin developed this in reaction to the 1957 Wolfenden Report, which had recommended that private homosexual acts between consenting adults should be decriminalised since it was purely a private matter and none of the law's business. Devlin thought this was wrong in principle: the community, acting through the "reasonable man on the Clapham omnibus," has a right to punish conduct that provokes a genuine, widespread feeling of intolerance, indignation and disgust, because a society can't survive if it just tolerates whatever anyone wants to do behind closed doors. Devlin was careful to say this reaction has to be a properly considered moral verdict rather than blind prejudice, so his reasonable man test builds in the requirement that the community's revulsion is a legitimate, reasoned moral judgement and not just squeamishness.

Hart's response, in Law, Liberty and Morality, revives Bentham's harm principle: that the only legitimate reason to restrict someone's liberty is to prevent harm to other people, not just because other people are offended by what you do in private. On this basis Hart makes two main criticisms of Devlin. First, he says Devlin gives no actual evidence that tolerating homosexual acts, or any other unpopular private practice, would cause society to "disintegrate" — it's just an assertion with nothing backing it up. Second, he argues Devlin's theory proves too much, because on Devlin's logic literally any change to the moral status quo that produces strong enough public feeling could be criminalised, which would justify loads of laws we'd now see as obviously unjust.

That said, I don't think Hart's position is fully consistent, because Hart himself accepts that the law can sometimes stop people from harming only themselves, which is a form of paternalism that Mill's original harm principle wouldn't allow. So Hart has already given up the strict version of the principle he's using against Devlin, which makes it harder for him to say Devlin is wrong to restrict private conduct for a reason other than preventing harm."
