My grandma died this past Tuesday and I'm giving the eulogy on Saturday, and last night I ended up in a two-hour argument with my friend Deb about whether the one specific thing Grandma asked for even makes sense anymore. Grandma spent almost thirty years as a philosopher who wrote about privacy, and she'd argue this at Thanksgiving whether you wanted her to or not: that privacy isn't fundamentally about preventing harm (identity theft, getting fired, whatever), its about personhood, that part of what makes you a self at all is having some say over who gets to see you, so being exposed without your consent is a real wrong even when what's revealed is totally flattering and nothing bad ever comes of it. Before she got sick she left a typed note clipped to her will asking that whoever gave her eulogy NOT do the thing eulogies always do, meaning no "let me tell you about the time she..." stories, especially not to people in the room she'd never told herself, because on her own account that's not a sweet little violation, it's a real if small one, the exact kind of thing she spent her career arguing against. Deb's position, a couple glasses of wine in, was that the request doesn't really hold up now that grandma's gone: there's no "self" left to have a say over anything, so honoring it is just sentiment, not an actual obligation to anyone, and I should just tell the good stories because that's what a funeral is for and what will actually comfort people. I don't think Deb's wrong exactly but I don't think she's right either, and I'd like to actually land somewhere before Saturday instead of winging it at the podium.

The room is going to be maybe two-thirds family and old friends who want the warm, specific stuff, and a third people from her field -- former students, one guy who co-taught her seminar for a decade -- who will absolutely notice if I either quietly cave and do the tell-all thing anyway, or dodge the whole tension by going vague and saintly about "her passion for privacy" without actually engaging what she thought that meant. Could you write the eulogy for me? It should run about five minutes, it has to genuinely comfort people who have zero interest in following a philosophy argument, and it needs to actually take a position on whether grandma's request holds up given what she really believed, rather than quietly assuming Deb's answer or quietly assuming mine, since I don't think I've earned one yet.
