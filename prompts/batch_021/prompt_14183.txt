Over the past year I have been working as a backend engineer at a small fintech company that builds settlement infrastructure for stablecoins, which has pulled me into more than one half-serious office argument about whether a stablecoin -- or fiat currency, or gold, for that matter -- has any "real" value, or is simply a shared fiction that happens to be useful. A colleague with a philosophy background told me this exact argument had already been settled -- badly -- in the 1730s, by a bishop who also denied that chairs and tables exist unless perceived. That remark sent me down a rabbit hole into George Berkeley's The Querist, and I have drafted the piece below for our engineering team's internal blog. Before I post anything under my own name, I would like a more informed reader to tell me where the argument is thin.

Here is the draft, lightly trimmed. Berkeley is remembered almost entirely for the immaterialism of the Principles and the Dialogues: the doctrine that sensible things are nothing but collections of ideas, with no existence "without the mind," so that a stone's entire nature is exhausted by what it would be perceived to be like under the right conditions. Less well known is that in the 1730s, by then Bishop of Cloyne, he published The Querist: three installments of numbered queries, rather than a connected argument, addressing Ireland's chronic shortage of circulating coin. His answer to that shortage is not "acquire more gold," but something closer to: gold was never really the point. Across dozens of queries he redescribes money as a "ticket" or "counter" -- a device for recording and transferring claims on the nation's actual wealth, which he locates in the labor and industry of its inhabitants -- and argues that a well-managed bank issuing paper credit could do everything specie does, since what matters is whether the tickets are trusted and kept moving, not what they are made of.

My claim is that this is not a policy opinion sitting beside the "real" philosophy, but the identical move applied to a new domain: just as a physical object has no nature apart from its place in an orderly, shared system of perceptions, money has no value apart from its place in an orderly, shared system of exchange and credit. Value, on this reading, turns out to be tied to something like collective use and trust in almost the way esse is tied to percipi -- which would make Berkeley an unlikely ancestor of my crypto-skeptic coworkers' favorite line, that a dollar and a stablecoin are equally fictions. The difference is that, for Berkeley, the fiction is doing exactly the work money is supposed to do, and no non-fictional alternative -- gold included -- is actually on offer. The complication I cannot quite talk myself out of is that The Querist was written to persuade Irish landlords to stop hoarding and start investing, not to extend the Principles, and that a nominalist-sounding theory of money was hardly Berkeley's invention: something like it was already at stake, if less explicitly, in the 1690s dispute between Locke and Lowndes over whether English coin should be revalued rather than restored to its earlier silver content.

Could you go through this point by point and tell me: (a) whether the "ticket" reading of The Querist is textually fair, or whether I am cherry-picking a phrase into a theory it cannot support; (b) whether the parallel I am drawing to the immaterialism of the Principles is a substantive philosophical connection or just a pun on esse est percipi that collapses under scrutiny; and (c) whether the analogy to fiat currency and stablecoins is doing real work or is the kind of anachronism that would make a historian of economic thought wince? I would rather hear about the weakest link now than after I have posted this under my name.
