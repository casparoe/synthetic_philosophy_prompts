I'm a software engineer, and for the past couple of years I've served on our small Elections & Governance working group -- part of the nonprofit foundation behind an open-source project I've contributed to for a long time (a few hundred regular contributors, one elected "Project Lead" seat contested every year). We currently pick the Lead by instant-runoff voting: everyone ranks the candidates, and we eliminate whoever has the fewest first-choice votes, round by round, until someone has a majority. Last cycle there were three real candidates: one who wanted to keep development slow and conservative, one pushing a fairly disruptive rewrite, and one occupying a genuine middle position that people on both sides often listed as their second choice even when she wasn't their first. That middle candidate had the fewest first-choice votes and was eliminated in round one, even though the anonymized ballots later made clear she'd have beaten either of the other two head-to-head. The rewrite candidate ended up winning, and quite a few contributors were unhappy afterward; several admitted they'd ranked only their favorite and left the rest blank, out of a vague worry that ranking everyone sincerely could somehow backfire.

One working-group member, who has an economics background, now wants to replace instant-runoff with the Schulze method -- the pairwise, Condorcet-style method used for internal elections by Debian, Gentoo, and a few other free-software projects -- on the grounds that it would have picked the middle candidate and is generally much harder to game. Another member is dismissive, and keeps saying that Arrow's theorem already proved decades ago that no voting system can be fair, so arguing over which one we use is bikeshedding. That doesn't sound quite right to me; I'm fairly sure Arrow's theorem is about aggregating preferences into a full ranking rather than about picking a single winner. A friend of mine who works adjacent to decision theory, when I described the argument to her, said the theorem actually on point here is called Gibbard-Satterthwaite, and that as she recalled it, it applies to Schulze as well, just maybe not in quite the same way it applies to what we do now. I don't have the formal background to sort out how much of that is right, or what it should actually mean for our decision.

I've been asked to draft the memo our working group will send to the foundation's board recommending a course of action before we'd need to lock in a bylaws amendment ahead of next year's election. Could you write that memo for me? I'd like it to explain, for an audience of programmers rather than social-choice theorists, what Gibbard-Satterthwaite actually establishes and how, if at all, it bears on the "Arrow already settled this" objection; to compare instant-runoff and Schulze honestly, including whatever we'd be giving up by switching and not just the fiasco we'd be avoiding; and to close with one specific recommendation -- keep instant-runoff, adopt Schulze outright, adopt Schulze with some added safeguard, or something else entirely -- along with a realistic timeline for bringing it to the board. A page or two, pitched at people who can read code but have never heard of a social choice function, would be ideal.
