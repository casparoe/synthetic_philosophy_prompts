Here is the question as it was given to the class:

"The candlelit swatch (a variant of Jeffrey's cloth-by-candlelight example). A shopper is examining a swatch of cloth under weak candlelight. Before looking, she thinks it equally likely that the swatch is some shade of green (G) as that it is any other colour (¬G): P(G) = 1/3. Independently of colour, she also has settled views about the fabric: P(silk | G) = 0.6 and P(silk | ¬G) = 0.3.
(a) After a glance at the swatch, her credence in G shifts directly to 0.7, without her ever becoming certain of G, ¬G, or any other proposition that would license ordinary conditionalization. State the rigidity condition that licenses treating this as a Jeffrey shift, and explain briefly why no candidate proposition can rescue a strict-conditionalization treatment of this case. [4 pts]
(b) Compute her new unconditional probability that the swatch is silk after this shift. [4 pts]
(c) Suppose that, in addition to the glance, the shopper also runs her fingers over the swatch in the dark — an experience that shifts her credence in silk directly to 0.5 (up from the prior of 0.4), via a Jeffrey shift satisfying rigidity with respect to the colour partition. Compute her final joint distribution over {colour, fabric} under each of the two possible orderings of the two experiences — glance-then-touch, and touch-then-glance — and say whether they agree. Show your work for both orderings. [8 pts]
(d) Jeffrey conditionalization is sometimes criticized for being non-commutative in just this way. Explain why the non-commutativity arises here, and briefly assess whether it amounts to a genuine defect in Jeffrey's proposal as an account of rational belief revision. [4 pts]"

And here is the student's answer, reproduced in full:

"(a) Jeffrey's rule is needed exactly when the shopper's experience doesn't make her certain of any proposition, so there's nothing available for strict conditionalization to condition on. The condition that licenses treating her update as a Jeffrey shift is rigidity: for every other proposition H she has opinions about, her conditional credence in H given G, and given ¬G, has to stay fixed across the update — P_new(H|G) = P_old(H|G) and P_new(H|¬G) = P_old(H|¬G) — even though her unconditional credence in G itself moves. One might try to rescue strict conditionalization by saying she's really conditionalizing on some proposition like 'the swatch looked greenish,' but no fixed proposition of that kind will do the job: the very same visual impression could have left her with many different distributions over G and ¬G depending on the ambient light, her assumptions about the dye lot, and so on, so there's no single E with a stable P(·|E) whose conditionalization would reproduce her actual shift.

(b) P_new(silk) = P_new(G)·P(silk|G) + P_new(¬G)·P(silk|¬G) = (0.7)(0.6) + (0.3)(0.3) = 0.42 + 0.09 = 0.51.

(c) Glance, then touch. After the glance alone we're in the situation from part (b): P(G) = 0.7, P(silk) = 0.51, and the joint is P(G,silk) = 0.42, P(G,¬silk) = 0.28, P(¬G,silk) = 0.09, P(¬G,¬silk) = 0.21. The touch now moves P(silk) to 0.5, holding fixed P(G|silk) and P(G|¬silk) as computed from that joint (14/17 and 4/7, respectively). That gives a final P(silk) = 0.5 exactly, by construction, and a final P(G) = 0.5(14/17) + 0.5(4/7) ≈ 0.697 — learning about the fabric pulls her colour credence down a little from 0.7, since silk is evidence for green.

Touch, then glance. First the tactile impression moves P(silk) from the prior 0.4 up to 0.5. Then the glance moves P(G) to 0.7. Applying the same rigidity condition we used in part (b) — P(silk|G) = 0.6, P(silk|¬G) = 0.3 — gives P(silk) = (0.7)(0.6) + (0.3)(0.3) = 0.51, exactly the part (b) computation again.

So this ordering ends at P(G) = 0.7, P(silk) = 0.51, rather than P(silk) = 0.5, P(G) ≈ 0.697. The two orderings don't agree exactly, confirming that Jeffrey conditionalization isn't commutative in general — but the disagreement here is modest (0.50 versus 0.51 for the silk credence), so this particular example doesn't display a dramatic order effect.

(d) The reason the two orders disagree is that rigidity is a local condition: it pins down the conditional probabilities relevant to whichever shift is happening right now, but it says nothing about what happens to other conditional probabilities once you move on to the next shift, so information can leak between correlated partitions in an order-sensitive way. I think this counts as a genuine cost of the view. On the ordinary Bayesian picture, two agents who receive the same total evidence should reach the same posterior no matter the order in which the evidence arrived; a rule that makes the outcome depend on arrival order looks less like conditioning on evidence and more like a path-dependent heuristic that happens to coincide with conditioning in easy cases. Since both orderings in part (c) start and end with the shopper having had exactly the same two experiences, a satisfying theory of belief revision should tell her what to believe without requiring her to track which experience came first, and Jeffrey's rule doesn't manage that."
