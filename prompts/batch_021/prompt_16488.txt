At the fintech startup where I work, we build a model that scores small-business owners for an unsecured line of credit, and a disagreement broke out between two colleagues of mine, Priya (one of our data scientists) and Marcus (a product manager), during a fairness review we ran ahead of a routine fair-lending exam. The review covered two parts of the metro area we lend in: the Eastside district, which was subject to formal mortgage redlining decades ago and where our historical default rate runs around 24%, and the rest of the city, which I'll call Westside, where it's around 8%. The model turned out to be well-calibrated in both areas — applicants given, say, a 20% risk score defaulted about 20% of the time, whether they were from Eastside or Westside — but the rate at which applicants who would actually have repaid in full got denied anyway was roughly 35% in Eastside versus 11% in Westside. That gap is what set Priya and Marcus off. I'm a software engineer on the team, not a statistician or an ethicist, and while I could follow every move in their argument, I couldn't tell who was actually right.

The exchange went roughly like this. (1) Priya said the model is fair precisely because it's calibrated: a 20% score means the same one-in-five risk no matter whose application it's attached to, and forcing the denial rate for good applicants to match across areas would mean the score stops tracking real risk for at least one of them. (2) Marcus said that's beside the point: a creditworthy Eastside owner is over three times more likely to be wrongly turned down for money they'd have repaid than an equally creditworthy Westside owner, and "the score is technically accurate" doesn't make that unequal harm okay. (3) Priya answered that the unequal denial rate is just downstream of the unequal default rate — if the two areas' true default rates were the same, calibration and equal denial rates for good applicants would automatically coincide — so Marcus was blaming the model for a real difference the data simply reports. (4) Marcus replied that the "real difference" is itself the residue of decades of Eastside being cut off from credit, so treating today's default rate as neutral ground truth just re-launders that history through the model — and that holding calibration fixed while letting the harm fall where it falls is already a choice on Priya's part, not the neutral default she was treating it as. (5) Priya said that even granting the history, she didn't see a workable alternative on the table: closing that gap by lowering the bar for Eastside applicants would mean approving Eastside businesses that are statistically more likely to default than Westside businesses being turned down for credit, which sounds less like fairness than a different, equally contestable bias — and, more fundamentally, this isn't a modeling failure that better engineering fixes, because given how far apart the base rates are, there's a mathematical result showing you can't have a calibrated score that's simultaneously just as likely to wrongly deny a good applicant and just as likely to approve a future defaulter in both areas: close the gap on one kind of error, and, holding calibration fixed, you open a gap on the other. (6) Marcus said fine, granted — but that only proves some gap has to open somewhere, not which gap we should be willing to live with, so waving the math around doesn't get Priya out of the normative question she'd been dodging the whole time.

Could you go through this move by move and say who has the stronger case at each of the six steps, rather than just picking an overall winner? (Ties are fine if a step is a genuine wash, but please don't default to that just to avoid making a call.) I'd also like to know whether Priya is actually right in (5) that the two properties are mathematically incompatible here, and if so, what that fact does and doesn't settle about who wins the larger argument. Finally, please flag anything both of them seem to be missing that bears on the disagreement.
