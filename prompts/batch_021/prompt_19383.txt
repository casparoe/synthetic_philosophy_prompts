A friend of mine and I, both currently working through a graduate seminar on decision theory, spent most of a long car ride back from a conference debating the money-pump argument, and we never really got anywhere, which is why I'd like your help finishing the argument properly. The setup we kept returning to is the standard one: an agent with cyclic preferences—say, she prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A—can be walked through a sequence of trades starting from C: give up C for B plus a small fee, then B for A plus a small fee, then A for C plus a small fee, which returns her to C exactly where she began, only poorer, and which can in principle be repeated indefinitely. The usual moral drawn from this is that such an agent is a "money pump," and that this shows transitivity to be a genuine requirement of rationality rather than a mere convenience.

My friend thought this moral was too hasty. She pointed out that the argument only really damns a naive or myopic chooser, someone who evaluates each offer as it comes without looking ahead, and invoked Edward McClennen's distinction between naive, sophisticated, and resolute choosers to argue that a resolute chooser escapes the charge entirely: before any trading starts, such an agent settles on a plan for the whole sequence—take the first two trades, then refuse the third—and sticks to it, declining to swap A back for C even though, considered by itself at that later moment, she still prefers C to A. Since this agent is never actually pumped, and her cyclic preferences are left completely intact beneath the policy, my friend concluded that the argument exposes only a defective diachronic policy (myopia), not anything wrong with the preferences themselves considered synchronically. I objected that this doesn't dissolve the problem so much as relocate it: an agent who must repeatedly refuse a trade she still, by her own present lights, prefers is displaying exactly the rift between preference and choice that made the pump look irrational to begin with, and "assess the plan, not the isolated choice" is a substantive and question-begging stipulation about the proper unit of rational evaluation, not a neutral description of what resolute choosers do. She didn't concede the question-begging charge, and answered that ordinary practical reasoning already privileges plans over momentary preferences all the time—keeping a promise, or resisting a craving you know you'll regret feeding—so if anyone was smuggling in an assumption, it was me, treating momentary comparisons as automatically authoritative. Neither of us budged before we got back.

I'd like to see my side of this argued properly, since I don't think I managed it well live. Please construct the strongest case you can that money-pump reasoning keeps its force even against a resolute chooser—that being diachronically unexploitable doesn't vindicate cyclic preferences, and that privileging plans over momentary preferences needs independent motivation it doesn't automatically have. Engage directly with her promise-keeping and craving-resisting analogies rather than dismissing them out of hand, and tell me honestly, at the end, whether anything in her position survives your own argument.
