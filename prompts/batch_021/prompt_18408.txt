I'm a senior in high school currently working on my IB Philosophy extended essay, which I've decided to write on Schopenhauer's philosophy of music. I've played piano for years and I'm also taking my school's music theory elective this year, which is partly why this topic caught my attention when my teacher mentioned that Schopenhauer thought music was somehow more "metaphysical" than the other arts. Since then I've been working through Book Three of *The World as Will and Representation* on my own, using the R. B. Haldane and J. Kemp translation, which actually renders the title as *The World as Will and Idea*, since they translate "Vorstellung" as "Idea" rather than "representation." I think I have a decent grip on the big, famous claim — that music, unlike painting or poetry, doesn't copy any single Platonic Idea but instead directly expresses the Will itself. What's really tripping me up is the more specific argument in §52, where Schopenhauer tries to line up the different voices of a musical texture with his whole hierarchy of nature. Here is the passage:

"I recognise in the deepest tones of harmony, in the bass, the lowest grades of the objectification of will, unorganised nature, the mass of the planet. It is well known that all the high notes which are easily sounded, and die away more quickly, are produced by the vibration in their vicinity of the deep bass-notes. When, also, the low notes sound, the high notes always sound faintly, and it is a law of harmony that only those high notes may accompany a bass-note which actually already sound along with it of themselves (its sons harmoniques) on account of its vibration. This is analogous to the fact that the whole of the bodies and organisations of nature must be regarded as having come into existence through gradual development out of the mass of the planet; this is both their supporter and their source, and the same relation subsists between the high notes and the bass. […] Bass is thus, for us, in harmony what unorganised nature, the crudest mass, upon which all rests, and from which everything originates and develops, is in the world. Now, further, in the whole of the complemental parts which make up the harmony between the bass and the leading voice singing the melody, I recognise the whole gradation of the Ideas in which the will objectifies itself. Those nearer to the bass are the lower of these grades, the still unorganised, but yet manifold phenomenal things; the higher represent to me the world of plants and beasts. […] Lastly, in the melody, in the high, singing, principal voice leading the whole and progressing with unrestrained freedom, in the unbroken significant connection of one thought from beginning to end representing a whole, I recognise the highest grade of the objectification of will, the intellectual life and effort of man."

Could you help me actually work through the structure of this argument, rather than just restating the general "music is a copy of the Will" idea? My advisor wants to see that I understand the details, not just the headline claim. Specifically: (1) Is the appeal to the overtone series — the point that high notes depend on and are produced by the vibrations of low bass notes — meant as genuine physical evidence for the claim that the bass corresponds to unorganized nature, or is it more of a suggestive illustration dressed up in scientific-sounding language? (2) Since Schopenhauer thinks minerals, plants, and animals are already expressions of the will at various grades, what exactly earns melody specifically the right to represent distinctly human self-consciousness, rather than, say, the alto or tenor line? (3) Does this passage's language of "analogy" and "parallel" between music and nature actually sit well with the much stronger claim Schopenhauer makes elsewhere in this very same section, that music is "the direct copy of the will itself" — or is there a real tension between saying music merely resembles the structure of nature and saying it is essentially identical in kind to the thing nature is a manifestation of?
