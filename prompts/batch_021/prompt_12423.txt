I spent nearly forty years as an experimental physicist before retiring, and for the last couple of years I have been auditing philosophy courses at the university where my old lab used to be, mostly to indulge questions I never had time for while I was working. This term I am sitting in on a seminar cross-listed with economics, informally called "Rational Agency and Strategic Choice," which treats decision theory and game theory as philosophical subjects in their own right. Because I still remember enough probability and linear algebra to be useful, the instructor has taken to sending me a subset of the weekly problem sets to grade alongside her. I don't yet fully trust my own judgment on the more delicate points — a fact underlined for me by how surprised I was to learn that one of a player's own strategies can be strictly dominated by a *mixture* of that player's other strategies even when no single one of them dominates it outright — so before I return my marks on one particular submission, I would like an independent second opinion from you.

Here is the question exactly as it was set (worth 20 points), followed by the student's answer in full.

Question. Consider the following two-player game in normal form. Player 1 (Row) chooses among A, B, and C; Player 2 (Column) chooses between X and Y. In each cell, the first number is Player 1's payoff and the second is Player 2's payoff.

              X            Y
      A     4, 2         0, 1
      B     0, 2         4, 1
      C     1, 0         1, 3

(a) [10 points] Perform iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies on this game, where a strategy counts as dominated if it is strictly dominated by some mixed strategy available to that player, not only by a pure strategy. Show each round of elimination explicitly, and justify every elimination you make as well as every claim that a candidate strategy survives a given round. State the strategy profile(s), if any, that survive the full procedure.

(b) [5 points] Is the strategy profile you found in (a) a Nash equilibrium of the game? Is it a dominant-strategy equilibrium? Justify both answers by reference to the original, unreduced game.

(c) [5 points] A classmate objects as follows: "Player 1 should simply play C: it guarantees a payoff of at least 1 regardless of what Player 2 does, whereas A and B each risk a payoff of 0. So C ought to survive any reasonable elimination procedure." Evaluate this objection.

Student's answer.

(a) I first check Player 1's pure strategies against each other. A does not dominate B and B does not dominate A (A is ahead on X, 4 to 0, but behind on Y, 0 to 4). A does not dominate C either: although A beats C on X (4 to 1), C beats A on Y (1 to 0). By the same kind of reasoning, B does not dominate C, nor C dominate B. So no pure strategy of Player 1 is eliminated at the pure-strategy level.

However, dominance should be checked against mixed strategies as well, and here the picture changes. Take the mixed strategy that puts probability 1/2 on A and 1/2 on B. Its expected payoff against X is (1/2)(4)+(1/2)(0)=2, and against Y it is (1/2)(0)+(1/2)(4)=2. Since C's payoffs are only 1 against each of X and Y, this mixture strictly dominates C, even though neither A nor B dominates C individually. C is therefore eliminated in round 1, even though it survives if we only ever compare pure strategies to one another.

Turning to Player 2, and now restricting attention to Row's remaining strategies A and B: against A, X pays 2 and Y pays 1; against B, X again pays 2 and Y pays 1. So X strictly dominates Y once C is out of consideration, and Y is eliminated in round 2. It is worth noting that this dominance does not hold in the original game: with C still in play, Y actually pays Player 2 more than X does against C (3 versus 0), which is exactly why C had to be eliminated first.

With only column X remaining, Player 1 compares A and B on that column alone: A pays 4, B pays 0, so B is eliminated in round 3. The procedure terminates with the single surviving profile (A, X).

(b) Because (A,X) is the unique profile surviving iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies, it must be the game's unique Nash equilibrium — this is guaranteed whenever the procedure narrows the game down to one profile. Indeed, A is Row's best response to X (4 beats the 0 and 1 that B and C would respectively yield against X), and X is Column's best response to A (2 beats 1). Beyond this, since A ultimately beats out both of its rivals once the dominated options are cleared away, and X likewise beats out its rival, A and X are dominant strategies for their respective players, so (A,X) is in fact a dominant-strategy equilibrium, which as always coincides here with the unique Nash equilibrium.

(c) The classmate is invoking a maximin criterion: C guarantees Player 1 a payoff of 1 no matter what Column does, which is indeed better than the worst case of 0 that both A and B expose Player 1 to, so C really is Player 1's maximin strategy. But maximin and dominance are different standards, and part (a) shows they can disagree: dominance asks whether some other strategy or mixture beats a given strategy in every contingency, while maximin only asks how a strategy performs in its own single worst contingency, and a strategy can be optimal by the second standard while losing badly by the first, as C does here. More generally, this is really just a special case of the fact that maximin reasoning is only justified in strictly competitive, zero-sum games, where the minimax theorem guarantees it coincides with equilibrium play; since Player 1 and Player 2's interests are not simply opposed here, the classmate's safety-based argument has no real force, and the elimination of C in part (a) stands.

Could you grade this submission and, for each of the three parts, tell me specifically what is correct and what, if anything, is mistaken, rather than giving me an overall impression?
