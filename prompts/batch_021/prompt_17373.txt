So this argument happened a few weeks ago at a bar and I still haven't fully shaken it off. I was watching a soccer derby with two friends, Theo and Mara, who support the two rival clubs in town, and there's a handball in the box that the ref waves off. Normally that's just a five minute shouting match about the call itself, but this time it turned into something else, because Theo said he didn't think the other team's fans were lying when they swore they clearly saw a handball -- he thinks they actually did see it, like their brain served them a slightly different instant replay than the one we watched, not just a different opinion about the identical replay. Mara thought that was nuts. Her point was that everyone's eyes got hit by the exact same photons off the exact same screen, so there's only one "what happened," and whatever's different between the two sets of fans is how they're judging or labeling that one thing afterward, not what they're actually seeing. Loyalty warps your judgment, she said, not your eyeballs.

Theo's comeback was that the groaning and the cheering from both ends of the bar started before the ref even got the whistle to his lips, like peoples bodies were reacting as though they'd already seen a foul (or hadn't) faster than there'd have been time to consciously decide what to make of it -- and that speed is exactly why he doesn't buy that it's just an opinion stapled onto a shared experience after the fact. Mara's answer was that fast just means your expectations are steering what you attend to and how quickly you sort it into a bucket, which is still all downstream of the seeing itself, and that there's no experiment even in principle that could tell "he saw a different foul" apart from "he saw the exact same thing and instantly filed it differently because he wanted to" -- so it's not fair to go around confidently claiming the seeing changed. Theo shot back that if literally no possible evidence, however fast or automatic, would ever count for her, then she's just rigged her own position so it can't lose, and that's not the same thing as being right. That's roughly where it ended, mostly because the food showed up.

I'm not a philosophy person at all, just someone who's found this stuff weirdly fascinating since stumbling on a youtube video that used the phrase "cognitive penetration" for something totally unrelated, which is honestly the only reason this got framed the way it did instead of just "biased fans gonna be biased." I don't have a side here, I think they're each clearly onto something and clearly missing something too. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who actually had the stronger case at each specific point, not just who won overall? And then tell me if there's some angle on the whole question that you think both of them are missing entirely.
