I am several drafts into a novel that reconstructs a minor nineteenth-century uprising through the eyes of townspeople who have no idea it will matter to anyone; much of the narrative's tension depends on the gap between what they could have known at the time and what historians will later say the uprising led to. Researching the period has pulled me into the philosophy of history, where I have snagged on a problem I cannot resolve on my own. Historians routinely write sentences such as "the riot at the mill was the opening act of the rebellion" — descriptions that are true, and seemingly explanatory, only in light of events decades later. Hempel's covering-law model appears to rule this out as genuine explanation, since no general law linking antecedent conditions to the riot could have taken "opening act of a rebellion" as an available predicate at the time it occurred. Yet narrativist critics want to defend exactly this kind of retrospective structuring as a legitimate mode of explanation in its own right, rather than mere dramatic redescription: Danto calls sentences like this "narrative sentences" and treats their retrospective character as essential rather than disqualifying; Dray argues that historians are often doing something else entirely, reconstructing an agent's own reasoning rather than subsuming an act under a law; Walsh's notion of colligation has the historian explain an event by binding it into a larger whole — "the rebellion," "the agrarian crisis" — that confers significance on its parts. I would like a clear, intuitive account of exactly where the substantive disagreement lies between Hempelians and these narrativists: is there a real argument for why retrospective narrative structuring deserves to be called explanation, or does the whole dispute simply trade on an ambiguity in that word? Please reconstruct the strongest form of each side rather than resting on the standard textbook contrast.
