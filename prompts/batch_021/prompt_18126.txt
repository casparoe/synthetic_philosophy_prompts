I've been reading about "ergodicity economics," associated with the physicist Ole Peters, and Scott Garrabrant's writing on "geometric rationality," both of which push back on the idea that expected-value maximization is simply what rationality demands once wealth compounds over time. The central move is to distinguish the ensemble average of a gamble — what you would get by averaging across many parallel copies of yourself who each play once — from the time average — what a single bettor's wealth actually does when the same multiplicative gamble is played repeatedly — and to argue that for non-ergodic, compounding processes the two can come apart so badly that a bet with strictly positive expected value drives a real bettor to almost-sure ruin. On this view, Kelly betting is not merely a clever gambling heuristic but close to what rationality actually requires once wealth grows multiplicatively. Orthodox decision theorists tend to reply that this is old wine in new bottles: expected utility theory already handles compounding wealth perfectly well once a strictly concave utility function such as log wealth is plugged in, Kelly betting simply is expected-utility maximization for a log-utility agent, and the much-touted "almost sure" long-run superiority of Kelly strategies is exactly the kind of long-run argument that Paul Samuelson skewered decades ago — in a reply written, notoriously, entirely in one-syllable words — as irrelevant to any agent who does not, in fact, have all the time and repetitions in the world.

I would like to see this argument actually fought out rather than summarized. Please write a dialogue between two equally capable characters — a defender of geometric rationality and a defender of orthodox expected-utility theory — arguing about a specific case: a small research foundation has a one-time opportunity to accept a bet with an 80 percent chance of quadrupling its entire endowment and a 20 percent chance of losing it all, where only the quadrupled endowment would let the foundation fund a rare, non-repeatable research opportunity. The geometric-rationality side should have to explain why a one-off, institutional decision should be governed by reasoning designed for repeated multiplicative processes, and should confront the fact that maximizing expected log-wealth would apparently forbid the bet outright because of the ruin scenario, however small its probability. The expected-utility side should have to explain what, if anything, is non-arbitrary about whichever utility function it proposes instead, and why "almost sure" long-run dominance arguments should carry no weight even though the foundation's endowment really will be reinvested and put at risk many more times over its lifetime. Each character should respond directly to the specific points the other raises, concede minor ground where it is due, and stop short of simply restating their own framework in response to every objection.
