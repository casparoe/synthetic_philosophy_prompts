I sit on my hospital's New Procedures and Technology Committee, which has to sign off on any interventional technique before it can be offered outside a research protocol. The spine service is asking us to approve system-wide use of a minimally invasive percutaneous technique for stabilizing painful vertebral compression fractures in osteoporotic patients. There is no completed randomized trial behind it; a sham-controlled trial is enrolling but is at least two years from reporting. What we do have is momentum: the technique is now used at roughly three-quarters of the hospitals we benchmark against, up from almost none five years ago, and our own surgeons' enthusiasm rests heavily on site visits where they watched colleagues elsewhere get good results. The department chair's proposal leans on the breadth of that adoption as evidence in itself, and warns that further delay will cost us surgeons and referrals to hospitals that already offer it.

The committee split in a way that didn't track the usual alliances. Our clinical epidemiologist pointed out that the interval between a hospital's first site visit and its decision to adopt has been shrinking with each wave of new adopters, and argued that this pattern -- convergence driven increasingly by watching what others have already decided rather than by each hospital generating its own evidence -- looks less like accumulating proof and more like an information cascade, which would look exactly as wide and confident whether or not the procedure actually works. Other members thought this was unfair to the surgeons: watching a colleague's outcomes first-hand is a form of evidence too, and three years of accumulated clinical experience shouldn't be waved away as mere imitation. We ran out of time before resolving it.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before our next meeting in six weeks. It should recommend one concrete course of action -- full approval, approval with specific conditions, or continued restriction to the research protocol -- attached to a timeframe, and it should take the "accumulated clinical experience is itself evidence" position seriously rather than simply asserting that widespread adoption tells us nothing.
