Two people in my philosophy of science study group got into an arguement right after our lecture on the replication crisis, and I haven't been able to stop thinking about it. (1) Dev said switching from frequentist to Bayesian stats would've basically prevented the whole crisis: p-hacking works by peeking at your data and stopping once it's significant, but Bayesian updating doesn't care about your stopping rule -- the posterior only depends on the likelihood of the data you actually got, so that failure mode just disappears. (2) Claire said that's a shell game: you've just relocated the hacking from the stopping rule to the prior, since picking a flattering prior after peeking at your data is no more principled than picking a flattering stopping rule. (3) Dev came back that it's not actually symmetric -- with enough data the prior gets swamped and the posterior converges on the same answer regardless, whereas optional stopping under a true null can push the p-value below .05 with probability 1 if you're willing to sample long enough, which is a strictly worse failure mode. (4) Claire said nobody in the real replication crisis was sampling forever -- the guilty studies were small and underpowered, exactly where prior choice has the most leverage and doesn't get swamped, and the actual driver was undisclosed flexibility in exclusions/covariates/DVs, which infects Bayesian analyses just as easily as frequentist ones.

Neither of them would budge and I don't know enough stats to referee it myself. Can you go through moves 1-4 and say who's right (or more right) at each one, rather then just handing out an overall winner? Also curious if there's some consideration neither of them raised that actually matters here.
