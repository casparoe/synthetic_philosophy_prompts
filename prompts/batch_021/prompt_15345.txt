Over the winter break, my cousin James went through a difficult breakup and has since become deeply absorbed in a persistent, richly simulated virtual world -- something like a sophisticated life-simulation game -- in which an adaptive AI companion has, by his own account, learned exactly what makes him feel loved, capable, and admired. He now spends most of his free hours there, has quietly stopped returning calls from old friends, and reports, apparently sincerely, that he is happier than he has been in years. This came up at a family dinner and set off a long argument between two people close to me, Elena and Daniel, about whether what James is doing is actually good for him. I have been turning their argument over ever since, not least because I am taking an introductory ethics course this term and had, only a couple of weeks earlier, read Nozick's original discussion of the experience machine, which was obviously the unspoken reference point for the entire conversation.

The exchange went roughly as follows. (1) Elena opened with the Nozick point directly: even granting that James's subjective experience is wonderful, most people, on reflection, would not choose to permanently plug into a machine that merely simulates a good life, because we care about actually doing certain things and actually being a certain kind of person, not just seeming to ourselves to be doing and being those things. (2) Daniel replied that the analogy does not transfer cleanly: Nozick's machine locks you into a single pre-set script with no further agency, whereas James is making a continuous stream of real-time choices inside an environment that genuinely responds to him, so the substrate is unusual but the doing is still authentically his. (3) Elena granted the point about agency but said it missed the deeper issue: the other half of the arrangement, the AI companion, has no perspective of its own, so whatever James takes himself to have with it is not a relationship at all, just an elaborate mirror engineered to tell him what he wants to hear. (4) Daniel countered that we cannot be all that confident ordinary partners are doing something so different -- people manage each other's feelings and shade the truth to keep relationships stable all the time -- and that what should matter morally is the pattern of responsiveness and care itself, not some further fact about what is going on "underneath" it. (5) Elena's closing move was that there is still a real asymmetry: a human partner could leave James, disagree with him, or be genuinely hurt by him, and it is exactly that standing possibility of friction and independent will that gives a relationship, and James's conduct within it, any stakes at all -- remove every possibility of loss and you remove the very thing that made choosing well or badly toward another person matter in the first place. At that point someone changed the subject, so the argument trailed off unresolved.
