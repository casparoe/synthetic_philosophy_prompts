I spent my working life as an experimental physicist — mostly low-temperature solid-state work — and it is only since retiring that I have had time to read philosophy and logic properly, rather than in the stolen hours I used to grab between runs in the lab. Lately I have been chasing down a thread that starts with something I half-remembered from a logic course thirty-odd years ago — Löb's theorem, the result that if a formal system S proves the sentence "if S proves φ, then φ" for some particular φ, then S already proves φ outright — and ends up, to my surprise, in present-day discussions of self-modifying artificial agents. Writers on so-called "tiling agents" (I gather the framework comes largely from Yudkowsky and Herreshoff's work at MIRI, the Machine Intelligence Research Institute) point out that an agent that only acts, or only builds a successor, once it has a proof that the action or the successor is safe runs straight into Löb's theorem: it cannot, in general, prove the schema "whatever my successor's proof system proves is true," because granting that kind of blanket trust to a system no weaker than itself would force it to prove every sentence there is. They call this the Löbian obstacle, and the whole cluster of problems about how an agent should reason about a smarter or future version of itself is known, at MIRI, as "Vingean reflection."

I raised this at a small reading group I belong to and got two flatly opposed reactions, which I would like to see played out properly rather than simply left hanging when the group broke up. One member held that the obstacle is a genuinely deep result: every way round it that has been proposed — bounding the trust schema by proof length rather than stating it in full generality, stacking an unending sequence of theories in which each one may vouch for a strictly weaker one but never for itself, or giving up on provability altogether in favor of calibrated probability, as in reflective oracles and logical induction — either quietly surrenders the very generality that made the original trust worth wanting, or simply relocates the same problem a level up or down. The other member thought this a case of demanding too much: no reasoner, human or artificial, ever needs unbounded and exact self-trust, and a scheme that supplies enough bounded trust for the next indefinitely long stretch of self-improvement is not a consolation prize but the most that "trust" could ever sensibly have meant.

Could you write me the dialogue this reading group never quite had — say, the two of them continuing the argument by email once everyone else had gone home — in which each responds to the specific technical move the other has just made, rather than restating a general position? I would like the skeptic to have a real rejoinder to the "bounded trust is enough" position (something that engages with what happens once the bound must be allowed to grow without limit across a genuinely open-ended sequence of self-modifications), and the other logician to have a real rejoinder to the "you have only relocated the problem" charge (something that engages with why a relocated problem might not be the same thing as an unsolved one). If you can, have the exchange end not with a concession but with the two of them trying to work out whether they disagree about a piece of mathematics at all, or only about how much certainty a self-improving agent is entitled to demand of itself before it acts.
