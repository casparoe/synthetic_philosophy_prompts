Over the past year I have been working through analytic philosophy on my own in the evenings — I have no formal training in the subject; my background is in law, which perhaps explains why I keep gravitating toward arguments with a lot of moving parts — and lately I have been stuck on Bernard Williams's paper on internal and external reasons. I understand the basic machinery well enough: an internal reason statement, "A has a reason to φ," is true only if A could be brought to be motivated to φ by a sound deliberative route from something already present in A's subjective motivational set S, which Williams is careful to say includes not just narrow appetites but projects, loyalties, and patterns of concern; and his case against external reasons turns on the claim that we cannot make sense of what it would be for a reason-statement to be true of someone for whom no such deliberative route exists. On his view, anything that looks like an external reason claim is either false or is really doing some other job — expressing the speaker's own values, say, or predicting what A would want if he deliberated correctly. I even worked through the well-known case of the man who wants a gin and tonic and reaches for a glass that actually contains petrol, just to see how internalism is supposed to handle a case of false belief.

What I cannot shake is a suspicion that the argument only goes through by quietly sliding between two different things "reason" might mean: the sense in which a reason is whatever explains why someone acted as they did, which plausibly does have to connect to some actual or reachable motivation, and the sense in which a reason is whatever genuinely counts in favor of acting, whether or not the agent could ever be brought to care about it. Williams seems to need the first, psychological sense to give his talk of "sound deliberation" and "reaching" a motivation from S any real content, yet he needs his conclusion to hold for the second, normative sense, since that is the sense the external-reasons theorist actually cares about defending. I mentioned this to a friend who studied philosophy at university; she agreed it sounded like the kind of worry that comes up in discussions of people raised from childhood to be indifferent to others' suffering, but she could not say offhand whether the equivocation has ever been pinned down precisely, or whether Williams has some way of blocking it that I am missing.

Could you set out the strongest, most precise version of the argument that Williams's case for internalism about reasons equivocates in just this way, such that once the explanatory and normative senses of "reason" are properly separated, nothing in his treatment of S, sound deliberation, or the correction of false belief actually rules out there being genuine external reasons in the normative sense? Please say exactly where in the argument the slide happens rather than just gesturing at it, and engage with the most natural internalist comeback — that "sound deliberation" was never meant as a merely predictive or causal notion but is already a normative one — explaining why that comeback does or does not succeed in saving the argument.
