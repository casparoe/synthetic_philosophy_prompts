I recently joined a decentralized finance (DeFi) startup as a smart-contract engineer, where I help build "liquidity pools": users deposit pairs of tokens, and an algorithm automatically sets exchange rates and pays depositors a share of trading fees plus newly minted tokens, with no human lender, borrower, or agreed interest rate anywhere in the process. It has brought back memories of Aristotle's discussion, in the Politics, of why he regarded interest (tokos, literally "offspring") as the most unnatural mode of acquiring wealth: it makes money breed money directly, rather than serving its proper function as a medium for exchanging things people actually need. I would like your best reconstruction of how Aristotle would judge a system like the one I am building, focusing on two points I am unsure how he would handle. First, his critique seems to depend partly on the corrupted character (pleonexia) of someone who comes to love wealth for its own sake, so I do not know whether removing any human creditor from the picture, and replacing him with a self-executing algorithm, would change his verdict, or would simply be irrelevant to it. Second, in our system the "goods" being exchanged are ultimately just more tokens, rather than food, tools, or other things people actually use, so I wonder whether he would see this as an even purer case of the unnatural wealth-getting (chrematistike) he criticizes in retail trade and usury, rather than as something categorically different. Please ground your answer directly in his arguments in the Politics and Nicomachean Ethics, rather than in generic remarks about usury.
