A disagreement broke out at dinner among two people from my philosophy program, and I haven't been able to settle it since. A friend, Theo, told us about finding a stranger's wallet on the subway with about $400 in cash, tracking down the owner from an ID card inside, and mailing it all back. Almost as an aside, he mentioned noticing a security camera in the car and figuring the loss could be traced to him if he'd kept the cash -- and that's what set off Daniel and Maya.

(1) Daniel said this matters a lot: if part of why Theo acted was fear of getting caught, the act wasn't really honest, and he asked whether Theo would have done the same thing with no camera in sight.
(2) Maya said the camera thought only occurred to Theo after he'd already decided to find the owner, so it couldn't be why he acted.
(3) Daniel said that's suspicious rather than reassuring: people are bad at accurately reporting their own motives right after the fact, so nobody's confidence that "it wasn't why" settles much.
(4) Maya said fine, but what was obviously doing the real work was Theo picturing the owner's distress at losing the money, and that if any incidental self-interested thought disqualifies a good act, nothing anyone does would ever count as good, since real motives are always mixed.
(5) Daniel answered that there's a difference between a thought that's merely present and one that's load-bearing, and whether Theo's confidence about the camera would actually have changed his behavior is a further fact that nobody -- maybe including Theo -- can settle just by introspecting.

Neither budged. Could you go through the exchange move by move, say who has the better of it at each point, and note what, if anything, both of them are missing?
