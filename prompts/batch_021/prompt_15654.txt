So I'm a philosophy PhD student (ethics / history of ethics, though my actual dissertation is on something else entirely) and I've fallen down a bit of a side-quest I can't shake. Everyone who's made it to the end of Sidgwick's Methods of Ethics knows the "dualism of practical reason" bit -- he's got two axioms, rational egoism and universal benevolence, both seemingly self-evident, with no way to rationally rank one over the other when they conflict, so the whole system threatens to collapse into what he apparently calls "universal scepticism" unless you can assume some kind of moral government of the universe (basically God plus personal immortality, so virtue and self-interest coincide in the end even when they obviously don't in this life). What I didn't clock until recently is that this same guy was also the founding and longest-serving president of the Society for Psychical Research, and spent literal decades -- with his wife, plus Myers and Gurney -- testing mediums, running telepathy experiments, doing that whole "census of hallucinations" thing, basically trying to find hard empirical evidence for survival after bodily death. And by his own account it never really panned out; he kept not quite finding what he needed.

That feels like way more than a fun biographical footnote to me: it looks like he treated the "moral government of the universe" postulate as something you could actually go out and empirically test, not just a premise you're allowed to wave in at the end of a book. I brought this up with my advisor and she thinks I'm reading way to much unity into what was honestly a pretty common Victorian pastime for eminent Cambridge men. So I want an outside opinion: write me an essay on whether Sidgwick's psychical research was the rational (even admirable) thing for him to do given his own premises -- he clearly took the demand for evidence seriously, he was famously one of the most skeptical, fraud-catching guys in the whole SPR -- or whether there's something actually confused about trying to settle a foundational axiom-ranking problem in ethics via seance-room empiricism, like maybe it shows he didn't really believe those two axioms were self-evident after all, or maybe it's just a category error, smuggling an "ought" question in through an empirical "does the universe reward virtue" back door. Don't just lay out both sides and shrug -- tell me which reading you actually find more convincing and why. Extra credit if you can say something about what your answer implies for how seriously we should still take the unresolved dualism today: is it the kind of gap that further facts could in principle close (so Sidgwick's approach was basically sound, he just ran out of evidence), or is it a permanent structural feature of practical reason that no amount of parapsychology was ever going to fix?
