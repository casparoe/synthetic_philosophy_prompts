Question (20 points): Consider a game in which a fair coin is tossed repeatedly until it lands heads for the first time. If the first head occurs on toss n, the player receives a payoff of (−1)^(n+1)·2^n/n dollars (so the payoff is +2 if the first head is on toss 1, −2 if on toss 2, +8/3 if on toss 3, and so on). This is the Pasadena game, due to Hájek and Nover. (a) Compute the game's expected value by summing the toss-by-toss terms in the natural order (n = 1, 2, 3, ...), and identify the series this sum represents. (b) Explain in what sense the Pasadena game's expected value fails to exist in a stronger and more troubling sense than the St. Petersburg game's merely infinite expected value; your answer should engage with the relevant convergence properties of the series from part (a). (c) A standard response to the St. Petersburg paradox holds that a rational agent's utility function for money must be bounded. Assess whether adopting this response also settles the difficulty raised by the Pasadena game.

Student's answer: (a) The probability that the first head comes up on toss n is (1/2)^n. Multiplying this by the payoff for toss n gives (1/2)^n · (−1)^(n+1)·2^n/n = (−1)^(n+1)/n, so summing toss by toss in the natural order gives 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ..., the alternating harmonic series, which converges to ln 2 ≈ 0.693. (b) This result is fragile, though, because the alternating harmonic series is only conditionally convergent: the series of its absolute values, 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/4 + ..., is the ordinary harmonic series, which diverges. By the Riemann rearrangement theorem, the terms of a conditionally convergent series can be reordered to converge to any positive real number we like. So while St. Petersburg's expected value is infinite, at least that verdict doesn't depend on the order of summation, since all its terms are positive; Pasadena's "expected value" could just as well be declared to be 1, or 1,000, depending on how we choose to add up the very same possible outcomes, so there is no fact of the matter about what it is. (c) This same fix disposes of the Pasadena puzzle as well. Once the payoffs are run through a bounded utility function rather than left as dollar amounts, the terms of the expected-utility series are all bounded, and a probability-weighted sum of bounded terms is guaranteed to converge absolutely no matter how it is arranged, so the order-dependence problem simply disappears: the game acquires a perfectly well-defined expected utility once it is stated in the right units. So Pasadena turns out to be a St. Petersburg-style problem in disguise, solved by the same move.
