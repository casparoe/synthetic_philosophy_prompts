I got kind of nerd-sniped this week while background-reading for a piece I'm writing on decision theory beyond Newcomb's problem, and I can't shake this older puzzle a couple of the philosophers I interviewed kept bringing up: "Death in Damascus" (I gather it goes back to a 1978 Gibbard and Harper paper, but don't quote me on that). Quick version in case it's useful context: a guy in Damascus runs into Death, who gives him a startled look, so he assumes Death's coming for him tomorrow and books it overnight to Aleppo. Except Death is basically a perfect predictor of where people will go and had *already* arranged to be in Aleppo the next day — the look in Damascus was just Death's own surprise at running into him early. So fleeing doesn't obviously help, but staying doesn't obviously help either, since by the same logic Death could've predicted that instead. The annoying bit, apparently, is that if you reason "evidentially" — picking whichever act is better news, given what it would tell you about where Death already is — you never land on a stable answer; you flip from "stay" to "flee" and back forever, because updating on your own pick always makes the other option look safer. And a couple of my sources insisted it's not just an evidential-decision-theory embarrassment: the case was apparently built to show that even causal decision theorists get burned once they also insist the chosen act has to be "ratifiable" (stable once you've learned you've chosen it) — a requirement plenty of people find independently plausible.

So now I'm trying to work out, mostly for my own sanity, which of the various responses to this actually earn their keep versus just kicking the can down the road. Could you rank the following by how well they actually resolve Death-in-Damascus-style cases — meaning they give one principled, non-flip-floppy recommendation rather than a hand-wave: (1) plain causal decision theory, using prior/unconditional probabilities and no ratifiability requirement, (2) causal decision theory with ratifiability bolted on, (3) plain evidential decision theory, (4) evidential decision theory patched with a ratifiability requirement, (5) evidential decision theory patched with something like a "tickle defense" (a hidden trait that's supposed to screen off the act-prediction correlation), (6) functional/logical decision theory, where you choose as though you're picking the output of your whole decision algorithm, predictor included, and (7) just precommitting to an act (or flipping a coin) the moment before Death shows up, as a practical dodge rather than a theory.
