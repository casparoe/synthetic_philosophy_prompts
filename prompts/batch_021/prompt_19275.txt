And here's the student's answer, copied exactly as submitted:
"(a) Minimizing free energy and minimizing prediction error are essentially the same process looked at two ways. In a hierarchical generative model, each level tries to predict the input to the level below it; the mismatch between the prediction and the actual input is the prediction error, which gets passed up the hierarchy to update higher-level representations. Since free energy is basically a measure of the surprise associated with sensory input (or an upper bound on it), and prediction error is the quantity telling the system how surprised it is, minimizing one is mathematically equivalent to minimizing the other, whether the system does this by updating its beliefs (perception) or by acting on the world to bring about the sensory states it expects (action).
(b) The dark room objection says that if organisms are always minimizing prediction error, they should seek out a dark, unchanging room, since there's nothing there to be surprised by — yet organisms obviously don't do this. Active inference answers this by pointing out that agents don't just minimize free energy in the present moment; they minimize expected free energy over future action sequences, and expected free energy includes an epistemic value term that rewards actions which resolve uncertainty about the world. Since a dark room offers no new information, it has low epistemic value, so the model predicts organisms will explore instead.
(c) In psychosis, prediction errors are given anomalously low precision, meaning the brain stops updating its model in light of incoming sensory evidence. This is what produces hallucinations, and it's also why delusions are so hard to shift with counter-evidence — the beliefs have effectively been walled off from being corrected by new prediction errors."
