Okay, I need a referee for an argument me and my friend Priya got into this weekend that neither of us actually won. We were talking about Frederick Douglass and somehow ended up way deep in the weeds on his actual legal relationship to the Constitution, not just the "was he against slavery" thing (obviously yes) but the specific argument he made about the text itself. Basically: for his whole early career Douglass was a Garrisonian, and Garrison's whole line was that the Constitution is a pro-slavery document, full stop — "a covenant with death and an agreement with hell" is the phrase I remember, hence the "no union with slaveholders," dont vote, dont run for office, disunion-now approach. Then sometime around 1851 (might have the year slightly off) Douglass does this pretty dramatic public break with Garrison — I think it lines up with when he renamed his paper Frederick Douglass' Paper and started taking Gerrit Smith's money — and spends basically the rest of his career arguing close to the opposite: that the Constitution, read properly, is actually an anti-slavery document, that the three-fifths clause, the fugitive slave clause, and the slave-trade clause are compromises of practice rather than the Constitution itself creating or recognizing "property in man," and that the Preamble's "We the People"/"establish Justice" language is doing real work pointing the other direction. His big point, if I remember right, is that the words "slave" and "slavery" never actually appear anywhere in the original text, unlike actual slave codes of the era, and that this was very much on purpose on the framers' part.

Priya's take is that this was basically motivated reasoning: Douglass wanted to move into electoral politics instead of doing Garrison's moral-suasion-only thing, so he needed a legal-sounding story that let him do that, and reasoned backwards into it (with an assist from Lysander Spooner's arguments, which I gather were floating around at the time). My take is that the actual textual argument is pretty serious as constitutional interpretation in its own right and beats the Garrisonian "covenant with death" reading, if you take the framers' well-documented squeamishness about the word "slave" seriously. Could you lay out the two readings properly against each other — what each side actually does with the specific clauses, and how each handles the gap between the bare text and what everybody involved knew the text was for — and then actually pick a side on which is the stronger reading? And separately, do you think Douglass's conversion looks more like principled reinterpretation or convenient rationalization? Don't just give me the "Douglass was a great abolitionist, the end" summary, I already know that much — I want the actual mechanics of the disagreement.
