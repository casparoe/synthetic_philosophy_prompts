Had a whole arguement with my mate Dan last night that's actually relevant to something I need to write this week. I sit on my state's forensic science oversight commission, and an internal review at one of our crime labs found fingerprint examiners were way more likely to call a borderline print a 'match' when the file mentioned the suspect had already confessed, versus examiners shown the identical print with zero context. There's a proposal to require 'blind verification': the second examiner who signs off on a match isnt told anything about the case or what the first examiner concluded.

Dan's position was that this is just garden-variety confirmation bias and blind verification obviously fixes it. Mine was that if the contextual info is changing what examiners actually perceive in the ridge patterns, not just how willing they are to call an ambiguous case a match afterward, then blinding the second look doesn't touch the real problem, since the damage already happened at the first look. We went around in circles for an hour and got nowhere.

I have to bring a recommendation to the commission Thursday. Can you draft the memo? It needs a specific procedure and timeline, not 'more research needed', it has to take the lab's objection seriously -- that this is expensive and basically accuses their examiners of incompetence -- and it can't dodge the perception-vs-judgement question, since that's what actually decides whether the proposal on the table solves anything.
