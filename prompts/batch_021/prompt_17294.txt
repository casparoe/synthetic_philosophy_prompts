So this happened in my ethical theory seminar last week and I still haven't managed to let it go. We'd spent the first half of class on the bit in Kant's Groundwork where he says the guy who's naturally sympathetic and helps people because it gives him a warm feeling gets no real moral credit for it, while the cold, unsympathetic guy who does the exact same good deed purely from duty is the one who actually displays moral worth. That passage always gets a rise out of people, but this time someone brought up Huck Finn, and it turned into a running argument between two of my students, Mara and Devon, that basically ate the rest of the session.

Devon's opening shot was that Huck's decision not to turn Jim in has no moral worth at all on a Kantian reading, because Huck isn't acting on any judgment that what he's doing is right — by his own lights (he's a kid raised to believe helping a runaway slave is theft and a sin) he thinks he's doing wrong, and just can't go through with turning Jim in. That's not virtue winning out over a bad rule, Devon said, it's akrasia that happens to land somewhere decent, and if we credit Huck for it we'd have to credit a kid who does turn a Jim-figure in even more, since at least he's acting on principle. Mara shot back that this gets the case exactly backwards: if a theory implies Huck deserves less credit for helping Jim than he would for betraying him, that's a problem for the theory, not for Huck. What's admirable, she said, is precisely that Huck's feeling for Jim as a person overrides a rotten piece of moral education, and that responsiveness to what Jim actually needs is what moral worth should track, not whether Huck's act matches his own badly corrupted principles. Devon pushed back again — fine, he said, but now moral worth has come completely unmoored from the agent's own reasons for acting, which just makes it luck dressed up as credit; if motive-content doesn't matter and only downstream responsiveness counts, why not credit someone whose right action was caused by a private grudge, or plain squeamishness, instead of anything recognizably moral? Mara's answer, right as the bell went, was that motive isn't irrelevant, its that Huck's motive — real care for Jim as a person — is itself a moral motive, just one that isn't routed through a principle, and that's different from a grudge or squeamishness, neither of which is tracking the feature that actually makes helping Jim right.

They wanted me to just declare a winner, which I ducked, so I'm handing it to you instead. Could you go through the exchange move by move (four moves, more or less as I've laid them out) and say who has the better of it at each stage, rather than just giving an overall verdict — and flag anything important you think is doing work in the background that neither of them said out loud? I've got a nagging feeling this whole exchange basically reconstructs a case Jonathan Bennett made famous decades ago (I recall he runs Huck alongside Himmler, of all people, as a study in sympathy versus conscience) and that Mara's move is close to what Nomy Arpaly says under some label like "inverse akrasia" — but it's been a while since I read either closely enough to swear I'm not misremembering, so don't take that framing as gospel.
