I recently built a resource-arbitration service at work that splits contested capacity — bandwidth, GPU quota, that sort of thing — between competing clients, and I based the allocation rule on the Nash bargaining solution because "maximize the product of utility gains over the disagreement point" was easy to justify and straightforward to implement. While reading around the topic afterward, I came across the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution, which keeps most of Nash's axioms intact but drops independence of irrelevant alternatives in favor of an individual monotonicity axiom instead.

I can follow the formal definitions, but I don't yet have a gut-level feel for why those two axioms pull against each other, or what a bargainer actually gives up by choosing one over the other. Could you build up that intuition for me, ideally by walking through a single bargaining problem where the two solutions visibly diverge and explaining what work the "ideal point" is doing in producing that divergence? I've also seen it claimed that David Gauthier's "minimax relative concession" principle in Morals by Agreement is essentially the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution repurposed as an account of fair moral bargains; if that's accurate, I'd like that connection unpacked in the same terms, since it would tell me something about the fairness intuition I've been passing over by defaulting to Nash.
