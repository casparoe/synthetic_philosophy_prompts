This semester I'm taking a seminar on Maimonides, and we've just spent two sessions on his treatment of animal sacrifice in the Guide of the Perplexed. As I understand Guide III:32, Maimonides doesn't think sacrifice has any intrinsic religious value: the generation that left Egypt was so thoroughly habituated to Sabian (star- and idol-worshipping) sacrificial practice that abolishing the institution outright was never really an option, so God's wisdom instead redirected the existing practice toward Himself, in keeping with Maimonides' general principle that nature only ever manages drastic changes -- weaning, for instance -- gradually. The implied trajectory, reinforced by the later discussion of the grades of worship in III:51, is one of progressive interiorization: from sacrifice, to prayer, to a form of worship that is wholly internal and barely needs external ritual at all.

A few of us ended up arguing after class about how this squares with the Mishneh Torah, where Maimonides codifies the sacrificial laws in painstaking detail and, in Hilkhot Melakhim, has the future Messianic king rebuild the Temple; the Code's very last lines describe that era, via Isaiah's prophecy, as one in which the whole world will be filled with the knowledge of God. If sacrifice really is just a concession to a mentality that the Torah's own developmental logic is trying to move people away from, it seems odd that Maimonides' picture of maximal human enlightenment involves reinstating the practice rather than finally retiring it.

Could you write a dialogue between two Maimonides scholars, both texts open on the table between them, working through this puzzle together? One should argue that it reveals a genuine and possibly unresolved tension in Maimonides' thought -- perhaps even a deliberate one, given his own remarks in the Guide's introduction about concealing certain views from most readers -- while the other argues that the tension is merely apparent and dissolves once we distinguish giving the historical reason for a law from specifying whether that law remains valid going forward. I'd like each of them to engage with the strongest version of the other's position rather than a caricature of it, and to end with either a provisional verdict on which reading is more defensible or a clear account of why the texts alone can't settle the matter.
