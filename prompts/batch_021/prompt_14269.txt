I co-host a small podcast about AI and society with a colleague from work; by day I'm a backend engineer at a conversational-AI startup, and neither of us has any formal training in philosophy, so we try to make up for it by over-preparing. Next week's guest is a philosopher who has recently argued for a precautionary, credence-weighted approach to the moral status of digital minds: roughly, the view that we owe a system moral consideration in proportion to our credence that it has morally relevant properties — sentience, valenced experience, preferences of some kind — multiplied by the magnitude of what's at stake if we turn out to be wrong, rather than waiting for certainty or insisting on a sharp yes-or-no verdict. I don't want the episode to just be her restating the framework, which by now I could probably summarize myself after reading the handful of papers and reports that everyone in this space cites.

Could you draft nine or ten interview questions that push on the places where I think this position is most exposed? I'd like at least one question on each of the following four points, with a short follow-up for each keyed to the reply she's most likely to give: (1) the worry that taking any non-negligible credence seriously commits you to extending some sliver of moral weight to almost every sufficiently complex piece of software, including simple feedback-control systems like thermostats, and whether there's a principled place to stop short of that; (2) the structural resemblance between this framework and Pascal's-mugging-style fanaticism, once a tiny credence gets multiplied by an astronomically large stake; (3) the fact that a system's self-reports about its own experience can be trained or fine-tuned to say more or less anything, so it's unclear what would even count as evidence moving the credence up or down; and (4) what the framework implies once a digital mind can be losslessly copied a thousand times over a weekend — whether the moral weight gets multiplied across the copies, divided among them, or something else entirely.

For the closing question of the set, I'd like something that presses her directly on falsifiability: what observation, argument, or thought experiment would be enough to make her abandon the credence-weighted approach altogether, in favor of either flat skepticism about digital minds or full, unconditional inclusion.
