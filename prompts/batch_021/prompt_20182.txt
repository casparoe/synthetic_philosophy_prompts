So I'm a phil PhD student (metaphysics / phil of physics track), and my reading group just spent two sessions on Max Black's 1952 paper on the identity of indiscernibles - the one with the two spheres alone in an otherwise completely empty, symmetric universe. Funny enough, the paper itself is written as a dialogue between two characters, which I thought was a nice touch given what I'm about to ask for. Everyone in the group agreed pretty fast that the cheap responses (just posit primitive haecceities, or just stipulate that there are two and move on) don't really engage with what's interesting about the example. Where we actually split was over the "weak discernibility" move that came later out of the philosophy of physics literature, mostly associated with Simon Saunders and then Muller & Saunders after him if I'm remembering the reading packet right. The idea is that the spheres don't need any qualitative, monadic difference between them to count as discernible, because they still stand in an irreflexive relation to each other (each is some distance from the other, and nothing is that distance from itself), and that alone is enough to rescue a real version of Leibniz's principle. Half my cohort thinks this is a genuinely clever, non-question-begging solution; the other half (me, most days) thinks its basically a trick that smuggles in the very distinctness it's supposed to be explaining.

Can you write me a dialogue between two philosophers hashing this out? Picture them at the pub after the seminar, still going at it. One should make the strongest possible case for weak discernibility as a real vindication of PII (feel free to bring in the identical-particles-in-QM motivation for the move, and maybe Ian Hacking's old suggestion that Black's universe can be redescribed as containing only one sphere, if either is useful ammunition), and the other should press the hardest objections available, e.g, that almost any two distinct things can be trivially "weakly discerned" by their sheer non-identity to each other, so the move risks proving too much or nothing at all. Neither one should be a pushover: they should keep actually responding to the specific thing the other just said rather than delivering pre-packaged speeches past each other, and I'd rather the dialogue end with the real point of disagreement laid bare than with one side quietly converting the other.
