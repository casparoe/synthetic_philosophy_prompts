Ok this has been bugging me while I'm reading for my decision theory chapter, curious what you make of it. Take the Pasadena game (Nover & Hájek): you flip a coin till it first lands heads on toss n, and the payoff is (-1)^(n+1) * 2^n/n. "The" expected value works out to the alternating harmonic series, ln(2), but since that series is only conditionally convergent, Riemann rearrangement means you can reorder the exact same set of outcomes to make the sum literally anything, or make it diverge. Most people I've read (Colyvan, and Nover & Hájek themselves, iirc) take this to show the game just has no determinate expected value, full stop, and treat that as a real problem for expected utility theory as a normative theory of rational preference. I want you to argue against that conclusion - make the strongest case you can that the coin-toss outcomes actually DO come with a privileged, non arbitrary order (the temporal order they'd occur in if you actually played the game), so there's a uniquely correct EV after all, and the rearrangement worry is a red herring. Please deal with the obvious objection - why should that ordering be privileged over any other - rather than just asserting your way past it.
