So I've been slowly working through this old book called "The Aims of Education" by Alfred North Whitehead (my father in law recommended it to me, he taught high school for like 35 years) and I keep getting stuck thinking about his idea of "inert ideas" and that rhythm thing he describes, where kids first fall in love with a subject during a "romance" stage, then get drilled on the technical details during "precision," and only later are supposed to pull it all together into something actually useful during "generalization." Its a genuinely beautiful way of thinking about learning, way more interesting then most of the education chat I hear from actual teachers at my kids school.

Anyway, my son's middle school just rolled out one of those adaptive AI tutoring programs that drills him on math and grammar until everything is "mastered," skipping whatever he already knows, and separately I know plenty of kids just use ChatGPT to bang out essays in ten minutes. It got me wondering what old Whitehead would've actually made of stuff like that. Obviously he never could have imagined any of it, the guy died in 1947, but he did have really strong, specific opinions about cramming, about knowledge just sitting inert in a kid's head without ever getting used, and about external exams warping what teachers actually teach.

Could you take a real stab at reconstructing his actual position here, grounded in those specific ideas rather than just a generic "here's what a smart guy from the past might say"? Like, would he see an adaptive AI tutor as basically a fancier, more efficient version of the drilling he already distrusted, or could it actually slot into his "precision" stage just fine? And what do you think he'd say about kids outsourcing the actual essay-writing to a chatbot specifically, is that basically skipping the "generalization" stage of the rhythm entirely, or is there some way he might've been okay with it?
