I'm a software engineer who spends a lot of my time on data-visualization and icon-system design at work, and reading about the history of the field led me to Otto Neurath's Isotype project: his idea that a sufficiently disciplined "picture language" -- built from standardized pictograms, showing quantity through repetition rather than by resizing a single icon, and avoiding perspective or individualizing detail -- could work as something like an international, wordless way of stating facts. Neurath treated it as a supplement to word-language rather than a full replacement, which suggests there's a real boundary somewhere, but I've never seen anyone locate it precisely. Construct two concrete statistical claims about, say, a country's workforce over a decade: one that a strict Isotype-style system could render faithfully, if awkwardly, and one that it structurally cannot render without quietly borrowing something word-language supplies for free. Then spell out exactly what the second claim needs that mere arrangement of icons can't give it.
