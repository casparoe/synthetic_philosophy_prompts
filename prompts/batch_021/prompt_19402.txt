Okay so this has genuinely been eating at me for like two weeks now, which is a weird thing to have happen because of a philosophy of science paper but here we are. I'm a couple years into a physics PhD (numerical GR is my actual thesis work, but I've been flirting with switching into canonical quantum gravity) and a few of us started an informal reading group, mostly just for fun. We did the Earman and Norton hole argument paper a couple weeks back, and I "knew" the gist walking in - GR is diffeomorphism invariant, so if you drag the metric around inside some matter-free hole region H using a diffeomorphism that's the identity everywhere outside H, you get a second solution, g* = d*g, that agrees with the original on literally everything outside the hole but disagrees on what happens inside it. Actually sitting with it properly kind of wrecked me though, because it seems like taking spacetime points to be "really there" independent of the metric (manifold substantivalism) commits you to a totally unphysical indeterminism, and the only way out anyone in the room could agree on was denying that diffeomorphic models like these represent genuinely different possibilities - which is really just relationism (or "sophisticated" substantivalism) wearing a different hat. My advisor's response when I brought it up was basically "diffeomorphisms are gauge, don't loose sleep over it," which is probably fine advice for actually doing physics but feels like its dodging what Earman and Norton were actually pushing on.

What I'd love is a dialogue between a manifold substantivalist and a relationist standing at a whiteboard where someone's sketched the hole H with g and the dragged-apart g* inside it, each one genuinely trying to talk the other out of their position rather than just restating their own view past each other. I don't want either side to be a pushover - the substantivalist needs a real comeback to "this indeterminism is just an artifact of your bad metaphysics of points," and the relationist needs a real comeback to "you've just defined the problem out of existence by fiat, that's not the same thing as solving it." And if it comes up naturally, I wouldn't mind them getting into what any of this is supposed to mean for finding diffeomorphism-invariant (Dirac) observables once you try to quantize the theory, since that's the bit that's actually relevant to what I might be doing my thesis on - but don't force it in if it doesn't fit.
