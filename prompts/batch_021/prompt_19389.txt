In my seminar on personal identity, I've noticed that students can recite the standard teletransportation puzzle without ever feeling its force: you step into a booth, are scanned and painlessly destroyed on Earth, and an exact physical and psychological replica of you is assembled on Mars, and we then debate whether the person who wakes up there is you or merely someone exactly like you. What tends to land harder is Parfit's own elaboration of the case in Reasons and Persons (1984), usually called the Branch-Line Case: the scanner malfunctions, so that instead of being destroyed you are left alive on Earth with your heart badly damaged, and your doctors tell you that you have only a few days to live, while your replica on Mars, unaware that anything went wrong, has already begun living your life there and can be reached by video-link. For a handful of days, there are two of you: one fading, one flourishing, each carrying every memory up to the instant of the scan.

I'd like a short story of roughly 1000 to 1500 words set during that overlap, told from the point of view of the person whose body is failing on Earth, culminating in the video call between the two of them. Rather than a piece that simply stages the standard arguments about personal identity, I want the philosophical stakes to surface through what the dying character notices, remembers, and chooses to say in what may be his last conversation, so that a reader comes away unsettled about whether he is watching himself survive, watching a stranger inherit his life, or facing a question for which "survival" and "death" are both the wrong words. You may invent names, a plausible near-future setting for the colony and the transmission, and whatever physical detail brings the scene to life.
