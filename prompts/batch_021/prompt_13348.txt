I've been working through the Seventeenth Discussion of al-Ghazālī's *Incoherence of the Philosophers* for a reading group on classical Islamic philosophy, alongside Averroes's rebuttal in *The Incoherence of the Incoherence*, and I keep getting stuck on how the two sides would actually respond to each other's strongest moves rather than simply restating their own starting positions. I understand the headline dispute well enough: Ghazālī denies that fire burns cotton by any power intrinsic to the fire itself, treating the constant conjunction of contact and combustion as a matter of God's habitual practice (al-ʿāda) rather than necessary causation, partly in order to preserve the possibility of events like Ibrāhīm's survival in the fire, while his Aristotelian and Avicennan opponents insist that natures really do necessitate their effects. What I'm missing is the texture of the actual exchange.

Could you write a dialogue between two philosophers who are equally sophisticated defenders of each side: one arguing for Ghazālī's occasionalist treatment of the cotton-and-fire case, the other for the view of real, nature-grounded causal powers that Averroes marshals against him? I'd like the occasionalist to press the claim that we only ever observe temporal concomitance between contact and burning, never a necessitating power in the fire itself, and to explain why even granting fire some causal power for the sake of argument wouldn't settle the matter, since God could simply withhold the cotton's receptivity to being burned. I'd like the defender of real causal powers to push back with the worry that if nothing has a nature that necessitates anything, induction and demonstrative science become unintelligible, and to ask why the world would display such reliable regularities at all if none of them were grounded in the natures of the things involved. Please make sure each character directly engages the other's best point rather than talking past it, and feel free to let the exchange end without a tidy resolution.
