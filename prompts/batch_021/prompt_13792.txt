Simpson's Paradox is typically introduced through examples like the Berkeley admissions data or the kidney-stone treatment statistics, with the moral that the disaggregated, stratified comparison is the one to trust while the aggregated comparison is dismissed as a statistical artifact of confounding. But there are well-documented cases that run the other way: in the so-called birth-weight paradox, stratifying on low birth weight makes maternal smoking look protective against infant mortality, even though it isn't, and here the aggregated comparison is actually the one closer to the truth. As I understand it, this all turns on whether the variable being conditioned on is a confounder or a collider. Rather than the formal apparatus of causal graphs, though, I'd like the underlying intuition: why does that distinction make such a difference, and why can't it ever be settled just by staring harder at the statistics themselves, independent of assumptions about how the variables are causally related?
