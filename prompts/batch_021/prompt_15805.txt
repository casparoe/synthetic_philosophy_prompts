I have been reading George Berkeley's "The Querist" for a seminar on the history of economic thought, and I was struck by how little it resembles the immaterialist metaphysics for which he is normally remembered. Rather than "esse est percipi," Berkeley spends several hundred numbered queries worrying about Irish unemployment, banking, and the circulation of credit, and he keeps returning to a strikingly deflationary account of what money actually is. In one of the best-known queries he asks whether "the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter" for recording and transferring power over other people's labor and goods, rather than a commodity valuable in itself; on this basis he was willing to entertain a national bank for Ireland whose credit would rest on the nation's land and industry rather than on any stock of gold or silver. Yet in his earlier "An Essay Towards Preventing the Ruin of Great Britain," written in the aftermath of the South Sea Bubble, he is scathing about paper credit and speculative finance that outrun real productive activity, and he treats the public's willingness to trust purely symbolic instruments of value as resting on civic and religious virtue rather than on clever institutional design alone.

Given these two strands of his monetary thought, how do you think Berkeley would have reacted to two developments he could not have anticipated: the final severing of state currencies from any metallic backing after 1971, and decentralized cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin, which dispense with any national bank or sovereign guarantee whatsoever? Would his "ticket or counter" theory of money's nature make him relatively receptive to fiat currency, and perhaps even to crypto, as further steps along a trajectory his own account already pointed toward? Or would his South Sea Bubble-era conviction that a credit system needs some trustworthy public guarantor, together with his suspicion of speculative paper divorced from productive industry, make him hostile to precisely the features that crypto's proponents consider its chief virtues? I would like you to work through this using the actual structure of Berkeley's arguments in these two texts, rather than relying on the more familiar link to his idealism about perception.
