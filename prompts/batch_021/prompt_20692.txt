As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I've spent the past few weeks reporting on the revival of "sin taxes" and default-enrollment retirement policies -- soda taxes, automatic enrollment in retirement plans, and the like. After a panel on the subject, I ended up listening to, and eventually getting pulled into, an argument at the reception afterward between two attendees: an economist who works on consumer behavior and a philosopher who works on decision theory. It never really got resolved, and I still can't decide who had the better of it, so I've reconstructed the exchange as faithfully as I can below.

(1) The economist opened by defending critics of soda taxes: someone who buys the sugary drink knowing exactly what's in it is, by revealed preference, valuing taste and convenience over the health cost, so a tax that pushes them off that choice makes them worse off by their own lights.

(2) The philosopher objected to "worse off": utility, in the textbook sense, is just a number attached to whichever option gets chosen, defined only up to any transformation that preserves the ranking, so it carries no meaning beyond the ranking itself. "The tax lowers utility," on this view, says nothing more than "people would rather buy the untaxed soda" -- the same fact in a welfare costume, smuggling in a claim (that the original choice was good for the chooser) that bare choice data can't support.

(3) The economist granted that the numbers themselves are arbitrary but said the welfare claim doesn't need them: with the tax, the consumer's budget set is a strict subset of the old one -- everything still affordable was affordable before, and some things that were (soda at the old price) no longer are -- so on pure revealed-preference grounds the consumer is at least weakly worse off, no cardinal psychology required.

(4) The philosopher agreed the budget-set logic works but said "weakly worse off" shows only that the tax doesn't expand anyone's options, not that it harms them in the sense that matters for policy, since that still means treating today's ranking as the one worth protecting. If sugar is taxed precisely because the in-the-moment chooser discounts future health more steeply than the self who lives with the consequences, then citing that same in-the-moment ranking as proof of harm assumes what's in dispute -- and ordinary consistency checks like the weak axiom of revealed preference, applied to choices at the register, aren't built to catch a conflict between selves, since each purchase looks perfectly consistent on its own.

(5) The economist replied that the alternative is worse: once some "later self" or planner can overrule the person actually at the register, any policy can be justified by positing whichever hidden preference makes the paternalist's preferred answer come out right, whereas the choice actually observed is at least a fact about the world rather than an inference about a self nobody gets to interview.

I'd like your help adjudicating this properly. Could you go turn by turn and say, for each of the five moves, whether it's basically sound, overstated, or mistaken, and why -- rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'm also curious about something broader: do you think this disagreement is ultimately about what "utility" and "preference" mean as technical terms, or about a substantive empirical and ethical question dressed up in that vocabulary? And is there a distinction neither of them reached for that would have moved the conversation forward?
