I spent nearly forty years as a physicist working in statistical mechanics, and since retiring I have finally had the leisure to read philosophy properly rather than just borrow its vocabulary the way working scientists do. Lately I have been working through Armstrong's What Is a Law of Nature? and some of Lewis's essays on Humean supervenience. I have become preoccupied with their dispute over what a law of nature actually is - Lewis's best-system account against Armstrong's picture of laws as relations of necessitation between universals. I know there are other parties to this argument - dispositional essentialists, primitivists like Maudlin - but Lewis and Armstrong seem to me the cleanest two poles against which to test a specific case. Below are notes toward a short essay that tries to do that, using my own field as the test. I would value your comments, particularly on whether the central move actually works or whether I have simply rediscovered a well-known dead end.

Every physicist is taught the Second Law as though it were exceptionless, yet we all know, from Boltzmann's H-theorem and from Loschmidt's and Zermelo's old objections to it, that entropy's increase is a statistical fact about overwhelmingly likely microstates rather than a dynamical necessity: a sufficiently rare fluctuation, or a sufficiently long wait for a Poincaré recurrence, could in principle see it decrease. Nothing in the microdynamics forbids this; only the arithmetic of probabilities makes it fantastically unlikely. I used to treat this as a footnote. I now suspect it might be the whole problem.

If that is right, the Second Law looks like a natural fit for Lewis's account: it counts as a law not because some relation of necessitation rules out the rare decreasing trajectories, but because "entropy does not decrease" (or its probabilistic cousin) belongs to whichever true, simple, and powerful system of axioms best summarizes the entire actual arrangement of matter across history. On this view the rare fluctuations are not violations of anything; the summary was never claiming to exclude them.

But when I tell a student that the ice cube on the table cannot spontaneously unmelt, I mean something stronger than "this has not happened, and no simple summary predicts it will." I mean that it is not a live possibility for that room - a claim that feels much closer to Armstrong's picture of laws doing real modal work than to Lewis's bookkeeping. I cannot tell whether this is a genuine pressure point for the Humean, or whether I am merely undergoing the ordinary experience of a very small probability feeling like an impossibility, which the Humean can concede without losing any ground.

I would be grateful for your comments on three things: whether I have represented Lewis and Armstrong fairly enough to be worth arguing with; whether the statistical character of thermodynamic law is actually a good test case for this dispute, or whether I have picked one that is too friendly to one side; and whether the tension I describe in the last paragraph is a real difficulty for Humeanism, or one with a standard answer I have not yet come across.
