Ok this is going to sound petty but it's been living in my head for weeks. A few of us in my board game group did a chain of trades with a promo card over about two months: I traded my alt-art card to Dan for his card plus $2, then a few weeks later traded that for Priya's card plus another $2, and then last month Priya offered to trade back for my original card plus $2 "to make it even." Each trade felt like a genuine upgrade at the time, that's the only reason I said yes, but I only realized afterward that I'd payed $6 total and ended up holding the exact same card I started with. My roommate, who's doing a philosophy MA, told me I'd basically reenacted a textbook "money pump," and that this kind of loop is the classic argument for why rational preferences have to be transitive (no A>B>C>A cycles) -- otherwise you're just a walking ATM for anyone patient enough to keep trading with you.

That sent me down a rabbit hole, and it turns out the argument is way shakier than my roommate made it sound. The naive version -- re-evaluating your preferences fresh at every single trade -- does get pumped every time, sure. But apparently there's a real dispute over whether a "resolute" or "sophisticated" chooser can keep the underlying cyclic (or even incomplete) preferences and still dodge the pump, just by foreseeing where the trades are headed and declining to finish the loop -- or whether that's basically cheating, since if you won't act on your ranking once it actually matters, it's not obvious you still have that ranking at all. There's also a separate fight about whether being exploitable over a whole sequence of trades (diachronically) really tells you anything about how your preferences have to be structured at a single moment (synchronically), or whether that's smuggling in an answer to one question by pretending it's the other.

Could you write me an essay that actually works through this instead of just asserting the textbook line? Lay out the strongest version of both sides -- the money-pump-therefore-transitivity side, and the resolute/sophisticated-choice-blocks-that-inference side -- including how much weight the synchronic/diachronic distinction can really bear, and then commit to an actual verdict instead of leaving it at "reasonable people disagree." A hedged conclusion like "money pumps establish X but not Y" is fine, as long as you argue for it rather than just gesturing at it.
