My aunt Miriam, a hospice physician for thirty years, was fanatical about one distinction that most of the families she cared for never even noticed she was drawing: increasing a morphine drip to control unbearable pain, even knowing it might shorten a dying patient's life by hours, was something she would defend before any ethics committee; deliberately giving a lethal dose to end a life, even at a patient's own pleading, was something she said she could never do. Foreseeing a death and intending one, she used to tell residents, are not the same act wearing different clothes, and she taught them the distinction with the trolley problem: diverting a runaway trolley onto one man to save five is not, morally, the same as pushing a stranger off a footbridge to achieve the identical result.

At her wake last night, a friend of mine who works in palliative care and I fell into a real argument about whether Miriam's whole doctrine of double effect amounted to principled ethics or a beautiful piece of self-deception, since she maintains that properly titrated opioids almost never actually hasten death, which would make the dilemma Miriam wrestled with for decades largely hypothetical. Neither of us was persuaded.

I am delivering her eulogy tomorrow morning to a room of family and colleagues, almost none of them philosophers, and it should run no more than a few minutes. Please write it so that it makes clear exactly what Miriam believed and why the line she drew mattered to her, without turning into a lecture on the doctrine of double effect, and without quietly settling my argument with my friend in either direction -- that is not mine to decide from the podium.
