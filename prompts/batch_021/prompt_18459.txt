I'm a software engineer (mostly backend work) with no formal training in philosophy, but a few colleagues and I run a monthly reading group that occasionally wanders from tech books into applied ethics, and this month's pick was John Taurek's short, well-known 1977 essay "Should the Numbers Count?" Its central scenario is a scarce-resource dilemma: rescuers can reach only one of two groups of equally endangered strangers, who differ in nothing morally relevant except how many of them there are. Two people in the group, Dana and Wes, kept arguing about it long after the meeting broke up, using a rescue scenario of their own devising to fight over the paper's argument, and I've never been able to settle in my own head who had the better of it, so I'm hoping you can referee.

Their exchange went roughly like this. (1) Dana said the answer is obvious: if a helicopter can reach only one of two distress calls before the storm closes in — a single stranded climber, or a separate party of five facing the identical risk — it should go to the five, because an outcome where five people survive and one dies is simply better than one where one survives and five die, and denying that is just refusing to add up harms that are otherwise exactly alike. (2) Wes objected that this "adding up" move is precisely what Taurek says we're not entitled to make: there is no single sufferer who experiences "five deaths" as one big loss, so each of the five loses only their own one life, no more and no less than the lone climber loses his; describing the group's loss as five times worse quietly invents a collective victim so that sheer numbers can override a claim that, taken individually, is no weaker than anyone else's. (3) Dana replied that no collective victim is needed to reach her conclusion: pair one member of the five against the lone climber and their claims cancel out exactly; that still leaves four more people whose claims are just as weighty as his, with nothing left on his side to weigh against them, so the leftover claims are reason enough to send the helicopter to the larger group without ever summing anyone's suffering into a total. (4) Wes answered that the pairing move doesn't avoid the problem, it just reintroduces the conclusion under a new name, since "unmatched claims should be satisfied" is only compelling if a bigger pile of separately-matched claims is already assumed to win; he said this is exactly why Taurek instead favors giving both sides an equal chance, such as flipping a coin between "go to the one" and "go to the five," since that is the only procedure that treats the lone climber's claim as genuinely equal rather than letting a headcount decide his fate for him. (5) Dana said the coin flip is not neutral either, since it drops each of the five from a 100% rescue probability under her rule to 50%, so that on average fewer of the six survive under Wes's supposedly fair procedure than under her own; a rule that predictably leaves more people dead so that the lone climber can do better than his one-in-six share, she said, doesn't deserve the name "fair" just because a coin was involved.

Neither of them budged, and I'd genuinely like to know who is right. Could you go through the five moves and say, for each one, who has the stronger of it and why, and flag anything importantly wrong or missing from both sides taken as a whole? Please close with your overall judgment on its own line, in exactly this format: \answer{Dana}, \answer{Wes}, or \answer{Neither}, depending on whichever of them ends up defending the more defensible position once all five moves are accounted for.
