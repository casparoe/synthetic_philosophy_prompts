So I fell down a Wikipedia hole last weekend that started at "unit testing" and somehow ended on a metaphysics page about dispositions, and I havent been able to stop thinking about it since. The gist, as I understood it, is that people used to think you could define something like "fragile" just as "would break if dropped" -- the "conditional analysis" -- but then philosophers cooked up these counterexamples called finks and masks that break it. A fink is basically like a live wire hooked up to a sensor gadget that detects a conductor about to touch it and instantly kills the wire right before contact, so the wire really was live even though "if you touched it, it would conduct" comes out false. A mask is a different animal: a wine glass wrapped in three inches of styrofoam is still exactly as fragile as it always was, it's just that dropping it won't break it because the packaging runs interference on the manifestation. There's apparently a guy named C.B. Martin who gets credit for the fink examples, and David Lewis wrote a whole paper trying to patch up the conditional analysis so it could survive them.

The second I hit the wine-glass example I thought of our staging environment. We had a nasty prod incident last month -- a race condition in the checkout service -- that had clearly been sitting in the code for months (git blame says 8 months) but never once showed up in CI, because CI mocks out the payment gateway call that creates the timing window in the first place. Separately, we've also got a couple of "heisenbugs" that reproduce reliably until you attach a debugger or add a logging line, at which point they mysteriously stop happening -- which sounds a lot more like a fink to me than a mask, but honestly I'm not confident I'm using the words right.

Anyway, could you describe the most important implications of the finks-vs-masks distinction (and Lewis's patched-up conditional analysis, if it actually holds up) for how much epistemic weight a green test suite should get as evidence that our code actually has the properties we think it has? I'm especially curious whether "fink-like" failures and "mask-like" failures call for genuinely different fixes on the engineering side, or whether I'm just dressing up ordinary testing problems in fancy philosophy vocab that doesn't actually cash out to anything.
