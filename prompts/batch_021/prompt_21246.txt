Robert Aumann's 1976 agreement theorem shows that two Bayesian agents who share a common prior and whose posterior probabilities for some event are common knowledge cannot "agree to disagree": their posteriors must coincide, even without either side disclosing the private information behind its estimate. A closely related result holds that purely speculative trade between two parties who are commonly known to be rational should be impossible. John Maynard Keynes died three decades before Aumann's paper appeared, but he had already argued, in *A Treatise on Probability*, that probability is an objective logical relation between evidence and hypothesis rather than a subjective degree of belief, and had already explained, via the "beauty contest" analysis of speculation in Chapter 12 of the *General Theory*, how sophisticated investors can rationally disagree and trade against one another for long stretches. How do you think Keynes would have responded to Aumann's theorem? Would his logical theory of probability incline him to accept or resist the common-prior assumption the proof relies on, and would he read persistent market disagreement as showing that the theorem's idealizations (common knowledge of rationality, shared priors) simply don't hold of real investors, rather than as any challenge to the theorem itself? Answer by engaging with Keynes's own arguments, not a generic gesture at "animal spirits."
