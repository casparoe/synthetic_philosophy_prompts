Here's the answer, more or less as submitted (worth 20 points, and no partial-credit key got written in advance, that's on me): "Popper's original worry, developed mainly in the 1970s, was that the theory of natural selection doesn't really count as a testable scientific theory in his sense, so he labelled it a 'metaphysical research programme' instead. The problem was that 'survival of the fittest' threatens to collapse into 'survival of the survivors': since fitness just gets defined by who actually ends up surviving and reproducing, the claim that fitter organisms survive doesn't forbid anything, so it can't be falsified. This is really just one instance of a broader problem Popper had with theories about unrepeatable, one-off events in deep history, which can't be tested by direct experiment the way a physics hypothesis can, and that's why he grouped Darwinism's logical status together with things like psychoanalysis and astrology, as theories constructed so that they can't be refuted by any possible observation. Later on, though, Popper walked this back. In a lecture from the late 70s (the one that became the paper 'Natural Selection and the Emergence of Mind,' I think) he said flat out that he'd changed his mind and wanted to recant, since once fitness is specified independently for a given trait and environment, natural selection does generate testable predictions after all."
