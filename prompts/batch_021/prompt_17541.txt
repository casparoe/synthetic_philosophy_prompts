I am a second-year PhD student in social psychology, and for the past month I have been working through the classic literature on situational determinants of prosocial behavior for my qualifying exams — the Darley and Batson seminary-student study, the "dime in the phone booth" experiments, and the rest of that tradition showing that small, seemingly irrelevant features of a situation predict whether someone stops to help a stranger far better than any measure of their disposition does. Until recently I found this material interesting in a fairly detached, academic way, but something that happened a couple of weeks ago has made it feel much more personal, and I would appreciate an outside perspective.

I was hurrying to catch a train that would get me to a conference on time, already anxious about a tight connection, when I passed an elderly man on the sidewalk who had dropped a bag of groceries and was struggling to gather everything up in the rain. I felt a sharp pang of guilt, hesitated for a couple of seconds, and then kept walking so as not to miss my train. What has stayed with me is not really the incident itself but something a close friend said when I mentioned it to her: she confessed that she had done almost exactly the same thing once, while rushing to a family emergency — and she is someone I would otherwise describe, without any hesitation, as one of the most caring people I know.

When I recounted all this to one of my committee members, she mentioned offhandedly that findings like these have historically been used by some philosophers to attack a whole way of thinking about ethics that puts a great deal of weight on people possessing stable character traits, though that is not her area and she could not say much more. That comment has been nagging at me ever since. If situational pressure can produce identical behavior from my friend and from me, despite what I would have confidently described beforehand as very different levels of "kindness" as a personal disposition, I am no longer sure what grounds there are for saying that she has that trait and I lack it, or that either of us has anything like a stable character rather than just a string of situationally determined responses we retrospectively narrate into a personality. Am I wrong to take this incident as evidence about my character specifically, and is there a serious, principled way of holding onto the idea that people have real character traits like kindness given this kind of evidence, or does whatever position my committee member was gesturing at actually win the argument?
