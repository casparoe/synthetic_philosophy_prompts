For an extension project in my AP Computer Science class, I've been reading around the edges of classic problems in artificial intelligence, and I've fallen down a bit of a rabbit hole with something called "the frame problem." The first explanation I found was fairly narrow and technical: back in 1969, McCarthy and Hayes pointed out that if you want a computer to reason about the effects of actions using formal logic, you can't just tell it what an action changes — in principle you also have to tell it everything the action leaves unchanged (that pushing a chair across a room doesn't alter the color of the ceiling, that opening a refrigerator doesn't change how many moons Jupiter has, and so on for an enormous number of trivial facts), and there's no obvious way to state all of that without the axioms exploding in number.

Then I found one of Dennett's essays that uses the exact same name for something that felt much bigger and fuzzier to me: how does any reasoner, human or machine, ever manage to sort out which of the countless things it believes are actually relevant to a given decision, without getting stuck considering irrelevant possibilities forever? He illustrates this with a little fable about a series of robots that each fail in a different way — one ignores an obvious side effect of its own plan, and the next, built specifically to avoid that mistake by working out the implications of its actions in advance, just sits there endlessly deducing harmless facts about all the things that stayed the same. When I brought this up with my teacher, she said these were basically just two ways of describing the same underlying issue, but that answer struck me as too quick, since the first version comes with actual formal proposed solutions (like McCarthy's own circumscription, or Reiter's successor-state axioms), while the second sounds like a much more general claim about the limits of any relevance-bounded mind — one that Fodor later leaned on to argue that computers might never be able to do open-ended "central" reasoning the way brains apparently do.

So here is what I'd like your help thinking through: please compare the narrow, logic-based frame problem from AI research with this broader "philosophical" frame problem that Dennett and Fodor describe. Are they really the same problem in different clothing, as my teacher suggested, or is that too quick a way to put it? In particular, if someone fully solved the narrow version — found a completely general formal trick for specifying non-effects — would that constitute any real progress on the broader worry about relevance, or would the hardest part of the puzzle be left entirely untouched?
