I sit on my hospital's triage committee, which sets standing policy for breaking ties when our severity-of-illness scoring system ranks two patients equally for one remaining ICU bed. Our rule currently breaks such ties by order of arrival, and we are facing a case in which the first patient got there ahead of the second only because her local clinic happened to have a working ambulance that day. One committee member, who trained in decision theory, defends keeping the arrival-order rule by invoking Harsanyi's aggregation theorem: since the scoring system has certified the two patients as identical in expected medical benefit, any way of combining their interests into a single ranking that aggregates their expected utilities consistently is mathematically indifferent between the arrival-order rule and a coin flip, so switching to a lottery would change nothing about aggregate welfare and would be merely symbolic. Other members insist a coin flip is owed anyway, since it gives the second patient an equal chance that the arrival-order rule flatly denies her, and that this fairness consideration is exactly what an aggregate-welfare calculation is not built to register. Draft the committee's decision memo for Friday's meeting: it should say what happens to the two patients now tied, commit the hospital to one specific tie-breaking rule for future cases, and take seriously — rather than simply talk past — whichever side of the argument the memo ends up rejecting.
