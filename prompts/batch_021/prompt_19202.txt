I collect real arguments for dialogue the way other people collect stamps, and I got a great one at my cousins wedding last month that I still can't settle in my own head. The best man, Gareth, is famously the most anxious person any of us know -- stammers ordering coffee, that sort of thing. He was backstage looking like he might actually bolt, had two glasses of champagne to cope, and then got up and gave, no joke, the best man speech of the decade. Confident, funny, perfect timing, zero stammer. Afterwards two people at my table, Dana and Colin, got into a whole thing about what this actually shows about Gareth, and it went a few rounds:

(1) Dana: "That proves there's a genuinely confident, funny guy in there -- the anxious stammering thing is the mask, and the champagne let the real Gareth through for ten minutes."

(2) Colin: "Disagree. The champagne didn't reveal anything, it just switched the nerves off chemically. He's exactly as anxious a person as he was that morning, we just didn't get to watch it for ten minutes."

(3) Dana: "Then where did the jokes come from, the callbacks to stuff from their college years, the timing? Alcohol doesn't write material. And if this happens every single time -- if he's reliably great after two drinks and only after two drinks -- at some point don't we just have to say the confidence is a real thing about him, it just needs a specific key to unlock?"

(4) Colin: "People say all kinds of things on nitrous at the dentist too, that's not 'the real them' coming out, just different chemistry producing different output for a while. Here's a better test though: if someone in the crowd had heckled him about something actually raw, like him being out of work all year, would drunk-Gareth have crumbled exactly like sober-Gareth, or handled it fine? Crumbled the same way means the anxiety was there the whole time, just papered over. Handled it fine means something in him actually changed for those ten minutes."

(5) Dana: "That test's not fair, sober Gareth might pass or fail a heckle depending on the day too, so it doesn't actually prove anything either way."

My housemate's doing a philosophy PhD, and when I told her this she said it's exactly the kind of case where philosophers argue about a distinction between "finks" and "masks" for dispositions, though she couldn't remember enough on the spot to say which one, if either, actually fits Gareth. Can you take it from there? Go through Dana and Colin's exchange turn by turn and say who's got the better of it at each step, not just overall, bringing in that fink/mask stuff if its actually doing real work here. Then end on its own line with \verdict{Dana}, \verdict{Colin}, or \verdict{Neither}, for whoever's overall picture of Gareth is closer to right.
