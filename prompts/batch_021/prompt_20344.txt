I somehow ended up on the investment committee for the small liberal arts college I went to as an undergrad -- they wanted an alum who "understands finance," which is generous of them. We're an unpaid volunteer board, we meet quarterly, and normally we just nod along to whatever the outside consultant recommends, but this quarter's meeting got surprisingly heated. The consultant wants us to pull about 8% of the endowment out of our passive index sleeve and give it to a "special situations" manager who trades small and micro-cap stocks that big institutions supposedly overlook; the pitch deck shows ten years of backtested outperformance and the guy presenting it is very smooth. One trustee, a retired economics professor, keeps objecting on grounds that I think are actually a version of a "no-trade theorem" argument (I had to look that term up afterward) -- her point is that if whoever's on the other side of this manager's trades is itself a big, rational, well-informed institution, the mere fact that a trade clears at all should make us more suspicious of our side of it, not less, and ten years of gaudy backtested numbers doesnt get around that, because you'd see exactly the same numbers if the whole strategy were a data-mined artifact or the guy just got lucky in a way that looks like skill in hindsight. A couple of the other trustees find this move maddening, because taken at face value it seems to prove no active manager anywhere could ever be legitimate, and yet somebody has to be doing price discovery or markets wouldnt be informationally efficient to begin with -- which doesn't exactly settle what to do with our specific 8% next month.

We vote at the meeting in three weeks and I got voluntold to write the discussion memo everyone will read beforehand. Can you draft it for me? I need it to land on an actual recommendation -- full allocation to the new manager, staying fully passive, or some middle option like a small trial allocation with hard conditions attached -- with a concrete timeframe attached, not a "let's study this more" non-answer. And please don't let the memo just wave at "markets are basically efficient" as settled wisdom and move on; the professor's argument deserves a real response on its own terms, including the strongest reason it might not be as decisive as it sounds, rather than a quick dismissal.
