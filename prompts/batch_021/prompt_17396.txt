For the past couple of years I've been teaching myself evolutionary biology purely out of curiosity. I have no science background at all — I manage warehouse operations for a shipping company — so most of what I know comes from popular essay collections and long detours through Wikipedia and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. One example that has stuck with me, from an old Stephen Jay Gould essay, is the story of the malleus and incus, the two tiniest bones in our middle ear: in our distant reptile-like ancestors they were jaw-hinge bones, and only much later, once the mammalian jaw joint moved elsewhere, did they get repurposed for transmitting sound.

That example sent me down a rabbit hole into what philosophers mean when they say a body part "has a function" at all, and I learned there's a real disagreement here. Etiological (or "selected-effects") theories say a trait's function is whatever effect explains why it was selected for and preserved by natural selection; causal-role theories say the function is just whatever a part presently contributes to some capacity of a system we happen to be interested in, without any appeal to history. What bothers me is that the ear-bone case seems to be a genuine problem even for the more careful etiological views, like Peter Godfrey-Smith's proposal that we only need to look at relatively recent selective history rather than ultimate origin. That move still has to specify which of a bone's many effects counts as "the" selected effect at any given stage, and it isn't obvious that can be done without first making exactly the kind of causal, ahistorical judgment that a causal-role theorist makes explicit from the start.

So here is what I'd like: please make the strongest case you can that causal-role notions of function are conceptually prior to etiological ones — that etiological theories, however sophisticated, can only pick out a determinate selected effect by quietly presupposing the very causal-role judgments they claim to be able to do without. Use the middle-ear bones, or a similar case of a repurposed trait, as your main example, and address directly how a defender of Millikan's or Godfrey-Smith's version of the etiological view would try to block the argument.
