As a software engineer, I like to sanity-check philosophical puzzles with actual numbers, so I recently worked through the classic Allais paradox in detail. Most people prefer a certain $1 million to a gamble giving a 10% chance of $5 million, an 89% chance of $1 million, and a 1% chance of nothing, yet those same people prefer a 10% chance of $5 million (90% chance of nothing) to an 11% chance of $1 million (89% chance of nothing). This reversal is presented as a violation of the independence axiom that undermines expected utility theory as a whole, but it seems to me the puzzle dissolves once you replace raw dollar amounts with a concave utility function, such as log(x), since that's exactly the kind of curve a risk-averse agent's utility should follow. Given that plugging in a concave utility-of-money function is a standard, well-motivated move, why do decision theorists still treat the Allais paradox as a serious open problem for expected utility theory rather than a modeling detail that a sensible utility function already resolves?
