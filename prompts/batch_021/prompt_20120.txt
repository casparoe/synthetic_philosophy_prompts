So I'm a cog sci PhD student (philosophy of mind track) and I TA an intro seminar that covers classical AI and its critics. Every year when we get to the frame problem, about half the class comes away thinking its basically a solved engineering nuisance (non-monotonic logic, STRIPS-style frame axioms, done and dusted) and the other half just parrots Dennett's old robot-with-a-bomb story back at me -- R1, R1D1, R2D1, or however he numbered them -- without really engaging with why he thought the real problem generalizes into something much scarier than "how do you formally specify what doesn't change after an action."

So this time I want to try giving them something that isn't a textbook explanation. Could you write me an original short story -- not a retelling of Dennett's robot parable, and not a "robot goes haywire" cliche -- that dramatizes the frame problem in its deep, epistemological form? I mean the version where the trouble isn't just logically specifying non-effects, but the more basic question of how any finite reasoner is supposed to figure out, out of the effectively unbounded set of things it believes or could infer, which ones are even relevant to a given decision, without either checking all of them (intractable) or quietly leaning on some unexamined background sense of "obviously irrelevant" that it can't itself justify. I want the main character to be smart and competent -- no technical bug, no dumb oversight -- so that their failure (or near-failure) is legible, on reflection, as this exact structural problem, in a way that makes clear why more compute, more facts, or a bigger context window wouldn't obviously fix it. It doesn't need to namedrop "the frame problem" or cite anyone, honestly I'd prefer if it didn't read like a lecture in disguise. If you want you can gesture at the fact that people disagree about whether this is a live problem for something like a modern language model, or whether embodied, skill-based engagement with the world (a la Dreyfus) sidesteps it entirely -- but you don't have to resolve that, I'd actually find it more interesting if the ending left that tension sitting there instead of wrapping it up neatly.

Length-wise something like 1000-1700 words seems right, and you've got free reign on genre, setting, time period, and whether the main character is human, an upload, an AI, an alien, whatever fits the idea best.
