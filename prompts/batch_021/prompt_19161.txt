So I've kinda gone down a decision theory rabbit hole the last few weeks (started with Newcomb's problem like everyone does, then kept clicking related stuff) and I landed on this older puzzle called "Death in Damascus." The original version is that old fable where a servant in Damascus runs into Death in the marketplace, panics because Death gives him a startled look, and gallops off to Aleppo to escape — except Death later tells the merchant the look was just surprise, because he had an appointment with that exact servant in Aleppo that very night. Apparently some decision theorist (Gibbard and Harper maybe? could be misremembering) turned this into an actual formal problem: Death is basically a near-perfect predictor of what you'll do, you have to choose whether to stay put or flee, and you die if and only if you end up in the same city Death does. What gets me is that unlike Newcomb's problem, there's no option that just wins no matter what here — staying isn't "dominant" and neither is fleeing, it only depends on where Death is, so a causal decision theorist can't just wave the usual dominance argument around like they do with the boxes.

The part that's really been messing with my head is the supposed fix on the causal side, this "ratifiability" idea, where an act only counts as rational if you'd still be glad you picked it once you supposed you were actually going to do it. Apparently neither "I'll definately stay" nor "I'll definately flee" survives that test, since whichever one you become confident of is exactly the one Death would've predicted, and so the "stable" answer people give is that you're supposed to flip a coin and randomize 50/50 between the two. Which sounds tidy, but also feels like cheating? If Death is such a great predictor, why doesn't he just predict the coin flip too and show up regardless — and if he can't predict the coin flip, in what sense was he ever such a perfect predictor to begin with. And from what I can tell the evidential decision theorist isn't off the hook either, since on their view becoming sure of literally any option is automatically bad news about where Death is, so it's not even clear they can point to a determinate action at all.

I'd love to actually see this argued out properly instead of me half-piecing it together from random summaries. Could you write me a dialogue — like the two of them are stood right there in the marketplace with the guy, trying to talk him (and each other) into a choice — between a causal decision theorist defending the ratifiability/randomize answer and an evidential decision theorist who thinks that whole move is a dodge? I want them evenly matched and actually engaging with each other's specific points (including my coin-flip worry above, and whatever the best comeback is to the "EDT can't even output an action" problem), not just each restating their own side past each other.
