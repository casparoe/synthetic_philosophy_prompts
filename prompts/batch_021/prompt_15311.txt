For the past several months I have been working on my first full-length literary translation -- a novel originally written in Portuguese -- and I keep getting stuck on words such as saudade, which seem to resist any single English equivalent. This has left me more anxious than I expected about whether the choices I am making are genuinely faithful to the source, so in the evenings I have started reading translation theory on the side to see how much this should actually worry me.

At dinner last week, a friend who studied philosophy in college told me that this whole worry rests on a mistake. She brought up Quine's indeterminacy of translation, and the gavagai example in particular: the field linguist who cannot tell, from a native speaker's behavior alone, whether an utterance produced every time a rabbit runs by means "rabbit," "undetached rabbit part," or "rabbit stage." Her claim was that this generalizes -- there is never a unique fact about what a sentence in one language really means in another, so any manual of translation that is consistent with how people actually use their words is, strictly speaking, exactly as correct as any other such manual. On her view, the honest response is to stop chasing some elusive "most faithful" rendering of saudade and just pick whichever English version reads best, since nothing further is actually being gotten right or wrong by doing so.

I found this strangely comforting for about a day, and I would be glad to adopt it as my working method for the rest of the book if it holds up. But it does not sit well with how the translators and bilingual readers I most respect talk about their work, where some renderings really do strike everyone as better than others, and not merely as differently flavored. If my friend is right about what Quine's argument establishes, how should I actually go about choosing between competing translations of a given sentence, now that faithfulness to the original is apparently not among the considerations available to me?
