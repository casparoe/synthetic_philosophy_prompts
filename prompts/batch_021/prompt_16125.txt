I'm a comparative-cognition researcher recently appointed to my university's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, and my first case, due at Thursday's meeting, is a colleague's protocol to run discrimination-learning trials on octopuses using mild electric shock as an aversive stimulus, ending with the animals euthanized under anesthesia for tissue collection. Octopuses are invertebrates, so they fall outside the Animal Welfare Act's definition of a covered animal: none of the anesthesia, endpoint, or enrichment requirements we'd impose automatically on an identical rat protocol actually apply here -- even though the same experiment would trigger mandatory ethical review in the UK or EU, where cephalopods are legally protected research subjects and, in Britain's case, formally recognized as sentient since 2022. Half the committee wants to hold the protocol to vertebrate-equivalent standards anyway, citing octopus nociception and flexible problem-solving; the other half worries that once "behavioral evidence of sentience" replaces the legal species list as our real criterion, there's no principled place to stop before crabs, bees, or worse. Please draft the written opinion I'll circulate before the meeting: recommend specific conditions for approval or clear grounds for denial, attach a concrete timeline for the PI to respond, and engage seriously with the floodgates worry rather than dismissing it as squeamishness about invertebrates.
