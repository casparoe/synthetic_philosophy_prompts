I've been working through some of the Machine Intelligence Research Institute's older technical material on self-modifying agents, particularly the discussion of what's called the Löbian obstacle to reflective self-trust (the term comes from Yudkowsky and Herreshoff's work on "tiling agents," though the underlying logical point is older and more general). As I understand it, the obstacle reduces to Löb's theorem: for a sufficiently strong formal system T equipped with its own provability predicate Prov_T, and for any sentence φ, if T can prove the conditional "Prov_T(⌜φ⌝) → φ" (informally, "if T proves φ, then φ"), then T can already prove φ outright. Applied to an agent whose decision procedure only sanctions an action, or the design of a successor, once its proof system has certified it safe, this seems to rule out a seemingly innocuous move: getting the system to accept, for some specific safety-relevant φ it has not already established on its own, the bridging claim "if my (or my successor's) proof system certifies φ, then φ holds." Accepting that bridge for any φ not already provable would, by Löb's theorem, force the system to prove φ directly anyway, so nothing is gained by deferring; and trying to secure the bridge across the board simply amounts to the system asserting its own soundness, which no consistent system can do (Gödel's second incompleteness theorem is just the special case where φ is a contradiction).

Please describe what you take to be the most important implications of this obstacle for present-day AI safety proposals that lean, in some form, on formally verified self-improvement or on chains of systems certifying the safety of their successors — whether under the old "tiling agent"/seed-AI framing or under more recent proposals sometimes discussed under banners like "provably" or "guaranteed" safe AI. I am less interested in a rehearsal of the proof itself than in an assessment of how much weight the obstacle should actually bear: whether it shows that any proof-based safety case for self-improving systems is doomed in principle, or only that a naive version of one is; whether replacing proof-theoretic certainty with probabilistic or Bayesian notions of reflection genuinely dissolves the difficulty or simply relocates it to a different formal setting; and where, if anywhere, you think the obstacle tends to get overstated or misapplied in popular discussion of AI self-improvement.
