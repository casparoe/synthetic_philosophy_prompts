The question and her answer both concern Lara Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility theory (REU) and how it is supposed to handle the Allais paradox. The question reads:

"(a) Using the standard Allais choice pairs — a certain $1 million (A) versus a 10%/89%/1% gamble over $5 million/$1 million/$0 (B); and an 11%/89% gamble over $1 million/$0 (C) versus a 10%/90% gamble over $5 million/$0 (D) — explain why no assignment of a cardinal utility function to monetary outcomes can make the typical pattern of preferences (A over B, D over C) consistent with expected utility theory. (b) State the formal definition of Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility, and explain how it can accommodate the typical Allais preferences while still respecting stochastic dominance. (c) Buchak argues that being risk-averse in her sense, over and above having a concave utility function for money, can be fully rational. Explain what work the risk function is doing that a concave utility function is not already doing, and why getting this distinction right matters for how we should interpret the Allais paradox."

Here is her answer in full:

"(a) Under expected utility theory, A ≻ B iff u($1M) > 0.10u($5M) + 0.89u($1M) + 0.01u($0), which simplifies to 0.11u($1M) > 0.10u($5M) + 0.01u($0). The same manipulation applied to C and D shows that D ≻ C iff 0.10u($5M) + 0.01u($0) > 0.11u($1M) — that is, D ≻ C if and only if NOT(A ≻ B). So under expected utility theory, A ≻ B and D ≻ C cannot both hold for any u, however concave: the common 0.89-weighted consequence cancels out of both comparisons and leaves the identical inequality governing each, just reversed. This is why the paradox is structural rather than a matter of degree — no amount of extra risk aversion, in the diminishing-marginal-utility sense, can rescue expected utility theory here.

(b) Buchak's theory replaces the linear expectation with a functional that weights outcomes by rank rather than by raw probability. Ordering a gamble's outcomes from worst to best, x_1 ≤ ... ≤ x_n with probabilities p_1,...,p_n, REU(g) = u(x_1) + Σ_{i=2}^{n} r(p_i + ... + p_n)·[u(x_i) − u(x_{i-1})], where r: [0,1] → [0,1] is strictly increasing with r(0) = 0 and r(1) = 1. Each r-term is applied to a decumulative probability — the chance of getting an outcome at least as good as x_i — rather than to x_i's raw probability. When r is the identity, REU is just expected utility; when r(p) ≤ p throughout, Buchak calls the agent 'risk-avoidant,' since she discounts the chance of climbing to a better rank and so gives disproportionate weight to whatever is secured for certain. This is exactly what lets REU accommodate the Allais pattern without touching stochastic dominance: A is weighted by r(1) = 1 with no discount at all, while B's outcomes are weighted by r(0.10) and r(0.99) rather than the raw 0.10 and 0.89, so a sufficiently risk-avoidant r can make the 1% chance of nothing cost B more than plain EU would predict. In choice 2, neither C nor D offers anything for certain, so the discounting affects both in a structurally similar way and needn't flip their ranking. Because r is only ever applied to the cumulative probability of doing at least as well as a given outcome, and never changes which outcome is ranked higher, a dominated gamble can never come out ahead of the gamble that dominates it.

(c) A concave u already implies that an extra dollar matters less the richer you are, which produces some aversion to fair bets on its own, but part (a) shows that no degree of concavity lets expected utility reproduce the Allais pattern — the inconsistency is structural. The risk function supplies a genuinely separate source of risk aversion: it is tracking the well-documented fact that people don't weigh probabilities linearly when evaluating a gamble, assigning extra subjective weight to a small chance of ending up with nothing and discounting the more likely good outcomes accordingly. Put differently, r does for probabilities what u does for outcomes, except what it captures is a systematic distortion in how probabilities are subjectively registered, which a utility function defined purely over dollar amounts could never capture by itself. Getting this right matters because it reframes the Allais paradox as being less about the rationality of the outcomes at stake and more about a predictable bias in probability perception that shows up whenever a certain option is on the table. It is also what connects Buchak's theory to Quiggin's rank-dependent utility theory and to cumulative prospect theory, which use the same cumulative-weighting device: all three make room for this probability-weighting effect inside a single, coherent preference ordering. Because the weight attached to the 1% chance of nothing depends on what else is on offer, strict transitivity of preference — the requirement that one ranking hold across every pair of gambles — has to be relaxed to a comonotonic version that only demands transitivity among gambles that already agree on how their outcomes are ranked. That relaxation is what makes room for the certainty effect driving the Allais pattern in the first place."
