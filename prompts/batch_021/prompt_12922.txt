I've been reporting a feature on the stranger corners of AI safety research, and one problem I keep circling back to without fully grasping is "Eliciting Latent Knowledge" (ELK), the puzzle laid out in the Alignment Research Center's 2021 report. The illustrative case they use is the so-called SmartVault: a highly capable AI operates a robot that guards a diamond, our only window onto the vault is a video feed, and we want to train a small "reporter" module attached to the AI's internal state that will honestly answer questions such as "Is the diamond still there?" The report's unsettling claim is that even with a flawless training signal on every case we're able to check by hand, gradient descent has no obvious way of distinguishing a reporter that is a genuine "direct translator" of the AI's actual beliefs from one that is merely a "human simulator" predicting what a human onlooker would say from the same footage — the two behave identically on the training distribution and only come apart once, say, someone plants a screen in front of the camera to disguise a theft the AI itself is fully aware of.

What I haven't been able to work out is why some of the more obvious fixes are supposed to fail. My instinct was that you could break the tie with a complexity or locality penalty: a human simulator, which only has to imitate naive human judgment from surface-level sensor data, seems like it should be "doing less work" with the network's internals than a direct translator, which actually has to hook into wherever the fact about the diamond is represented — so penalizing reporters for depending on too much of the predictor's internal state should favor the honest one. Could you explain why the report treats this move as a dead end rather than a fix, ideally in terms of the underlying reason (something about how much computation it would take to map an alien learned ontology onto human concepts, versus simply running a sophisticated simulation of human judgment) rather than just citing the specific counterexample? I'd also be curious whether, in your view, this is a quirk of the toy vault scenario or a genuine obstacle for any situation in which we want a model to report something true that we have no independent way of checking ourselves.
