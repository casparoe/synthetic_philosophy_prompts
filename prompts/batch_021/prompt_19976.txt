Question: In De Senectute, Cato is made to answer four charges commonly brought against old age. Name the four charges, then explain in more detail how he answers the charge that old age deprives us of pleasure and the charge that old age is not far from death, noting how his reasoning on the latter relates to the views of the philosophical schools he draws on.

Student answer: Cicero gives the speech to Cato, the same rigidly principled statesman who would later defy Caesar and take his own life at Utica rather than live under a tyranny. Cato tells Scipio and Laelius that old age is commonly charged with four faults: it withdraws us from active affairs, it weakens the body, it strips us of nearly all pleasure, and it stands close to death.

On pleasure, Cato does not deny that appetite fades with age; he argues this is a gain rather than a loss, since bodily desire is what most clouds judgment, and its ebbing frees the old for the steadier pleasures of conversation, memory, and modest enjoyments like wine at table or the daily cultivation of a farm, which he praises at length.

On death, Cato's argument is essentially the Epicurean one: either the soul perishes with the body, in which case there is no one left to feel any loss, or it survives, in which case death delivers us to something better; on either branch there is nothing left to dread, so the old, who have already lived out a full life, have even less reason to fear death than the young. He closes by picturing his reunion with the great men who have gone before him.
