Three weeks ago I tried a sensory-deprivation float tank for the first time: ninety minutes suspended in body-temperature salt water, in total darkness and silence, the water dense enough that I quickly lost any feel for where it ended and my skin began. About halfway through, I lost track of my limbs entirely, with no clear sense of whether they were touching one another—yet I never once stopped being aware that I existed and was the one having the experience. Describing this afterward to a friend who studies medieval philosophy, I learned I had more or less stumbled into Avicenna's "flying man" (also translated the "suspended man"), a thought experiment from his eleventh-century Book of the Soul: imagine someone created fully formed but suspended in open air, unable to see any part of his own body or feel one limb touch another, who has never had any sensation of any kind prior to that moment. Avicenna's claim is that such a person would still affirm his own existence without affirming the existence of any bodily part or organ, and he takes this as evidence that awareness of self is independent of, and therefore not identical with, awareness of body.

I'd like you to write a short story of roughly 800–1,200 words that uses this scenario as its actual engine, not as set dressing. Give it a character who loses, through some plausible mechanism, all sensory and proprioceptive contact with their own body while their sense of being a self persists, and let the plot genuinely test what Avicenna's inference does and doesn't license, rather than defaulting to a cogito-in-a-tank retread. Part of what makes his version distinct from Descartes's is that his subject has no sensory history whatsoever to doubt away, and I'd like the story to take that seriously. You don't need to name Avicenna, or state the argument outright, anywhere in the story itself—I would rather the ideas be load-bearing in the plot than explained by a character. It needn't resolve whether the inference from "affirmable without body-awareness" to "distinct from body" actually works, but it should leave a reader who already knows the argument with a genuinely new angle on it.
