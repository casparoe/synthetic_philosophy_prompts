I cover tech and ideas for a mid-size online magazine, and thanks to a moment of poor judgment in a meeting last year I somehow ended up on our newsroom's standards and ethics committee. We're voting next week on a proposal from the product team: a box that would run under every story, labeled something like "How Readers Are Reacting," which uses an AI model to digest our comments section plus whatever's circulating about the piece on social media into one tidy paragraph presented as a readout of public opinion. Product says it's just a service, the kind of thing readers already do for themselves by scrolling comments, we'd just be doing it faster and cleaner. A couple of committee members hate it on vibes but can't quite articulate why beyond "it feels gross."

I happen to be re-reading Kierkegaard's Two Ages for a totally unrelated piece on nineteenth-century gossip papers (my partner is already sick of hearing about it at dinner), and it's given me actual language for the vibes objection. His argument, as I understand it, is that "the public" isn't a real body of people at all but a phantom the press conjures into existence, an abstraction with no members and nobody answerable for what it supposedly thinks, and that manufacturing this phantom is central to what he calls leveling: the process by which press chatter flattens the real differences between people into one interchangeable, opinion-having mass. He wrote this not long after getting mercilessly mocked himself by an actual satirical paper, so its not like he was theorizing from some comfortable remove. Anyway, that's basically what our feature would automate: an official-sounding "public" verdict stamped under every headline. I don't want to be the committee member who kills a genuinely useful feature by waving around a dead Danish philosopher, though, especially since there's a real counterargument on the table: if we don't summarize reaction responsibly, X or Reddit will do it anyway, just worse, with zero editorial fingerprints on it and nobody to answer for that either.

Could you write the actual memo I'd bring into that meeting? I need an explicit recommendation -- adopt, reject, or adopt with conditions -- with reasoning that engages Kierkegaard's specific argument about the public and the press rather than the "the crowd is untruth" bumper-sticker version, and that takes the responsible-aggregation counterargument seriously instead of just dunking on it. If the answer is conditions, spell out what they'd actually have to be, ideally including something like a trial period with a real review point, not just "do it thoughtfully." Bullet points are fine, but it has to be able to survive being read aloud to people who then have to vote.
