I teach a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and this week's unit on imprecise credence left the room evenly split, so I would like a considered outside opinion to bring back before we reconvene. We motivated the topic with Ellsberg's urn: thirty red balls, and sixty more that are black and yellow in an unknown ratio, over which most people's betting preferences cannot be captured by any single probability function without invoking something like ambiguity aversion. Half the seminar wants to draw the moral that rational credence should sometimes be represented not by one real number but by a set of probability functions, on the grounds that forcing a single value onto evidence that doesn't single one out amounts to false precision. The other half pushed back with two arguments we had covered earlier in the term: Adam Elga's case that an agent with imprecise credences, following a permissive decision rule, can be walked through a sequence of individually defensible choices into an outcome guaranteed to be worse than an available alternative -- a problem that seems to disappear only if she quietly breaks ties using some sharp probability after all; and Roger White's worry that any interval meant to represent symmetric ignorance about a proposition invites the same question one level up (is the evidence also symmetric about where in that interval the truth is likely to fall?), so that the imprecision either regresses without end or collapses back into a sharp fifty-fifty. There is also dilation to reckon with: cases in which conditionalizing an imprecise credal state on evidence that is obviously relevant can widen the resulting interval rather than narrow it, a consequence that struck almost everyone in the room as at least mildly disturbing.

Write an essay that adjudicates this dispute. Lay out the strongest case for treating imprecision as a rationally appropriate -- perhaps even required -- response to certain evidential situations, and the strongest case that it either collapses into disguised sharpness or licenses bad behavior once decisions must be made in sequence rather than in isolation. I am not looking for a survey that ends in equanimity: reach and defend an actual verdict, even a qualified one. Something in the spirit of "imprecise credences are coherent as a synchronic doxastic state but rationally unstable as a basis for planning across time" would count as taking a position; "thoughtful people land on both sides" would not.
