I'm a PhD student in philosophy currently TAing an upper-level undergraduate seminar on aggregation and distributive ethics, and I'd appreciate a second, more disinterested read on one of the take-home midterm answers before I submit grades. The question the students were given was:

"In 'Should the Numbers Count?' (1977), John Taurek argues that when we can use a scarce resource to save either a smaller number of people or a larger number, but not both, we are not thereby required to save the larger number. Reconstruct Taurek's argument for this conclusion, explaining the role played by (a) his claims about the separateness of persons and (b) his proposed use of a chance procedure. Then explain how Derek Parfit, in 'Innumerate Ethics' (1978), responds to Taurek. (20 points; 500-word limit.)"

One student submitted the following answer, reproduced in full apart from the name:

"Taurek's target is the assumption, shared by utilitarians and many of their critics alike, that when we cannot save everyone, we simply ought to save as many as we can. He denies this. The argument turns on the idea that a loss is always a loss to some particular person, and that there is no further, impersonal fact about how bad it is, period, that five people die rather than one. Nobody undergoes the 'loss of five lives' as such; each of the five only loses his or her own single life. So we cannot add the five losses together and weigh the sum against the one person's loss, in the way we could add five sums of money together and weigh the total against a smaller sum, because there is no one who owns or suffers the combined total the way a single person owns a combined sum of money. Since the numbers therefore give us no reason, by themselves, to prefer saving the five, Taurek concludes that the fairest response to a forced choice between two groups of equal size is to give each side an equal chance, for instance by flipping a coin, rather than simply favoring one side over the other.

Parfit's 'Innumerate Ethics' rejects this conclusion and defends the common-sense view that the numbers should count after all. He argues that Taurek's position cannot be right because, taken to its logical conclusion, it would commit us to being indifferent between saving one person and saving any number of others, however large -- a result Parfit takes to be a reductio of Taurek's view. From this, Parfit concludes that we should return to the aggregative view that we ought always to maximize the number of people saved, treating Taurek's point about the separateness of persons as a correct but normatively inert observation about personal identity, one which turns out to have no real bearing on how we should act once its implausible consequences are exposed."
