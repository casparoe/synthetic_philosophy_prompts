I'm a philosophy PhD student working on formal epistemology, and this term I've been sitting in on a reading group that some computer science theory students run on algorithmic information theory. We just spent two sessions on Solomonoff induction, and I've been bothered by how quickly people move from "Kolmogorov complexity is machine-invariant up to an additive constant" to "Solomonoff induction gives you Occam's razor as a theorem, not an assumption." I drafted the objection below to send to my advisor, but she's away at a conference, so I'd like a second opinion before it lands in her inbox.

Here is the paragraph as it stands. The invariance theorem says that for any two universal machines U and U', there is a constant c(U,U'), independent of x, bounding the difference between the complexity x gets assigned under U and the complexity it gets assigned under U', for every string x. This is usually glossed as showing that the simplicity ordering underlying Solomonoff's prior is "objective, up to a bounded correction." But the constant is just the length of the interpreter needed to run one machine on the other, and nothing in the theorem bounds how large that interpreter can be across the space of universal machines. For any string x, however complex it looks under U, there is a perfectly legitimate universal machine that assigns x a one-bit program, at the cost of tacking one extra bit onto every other program to keep the code prefix-free — which just means the invariance constant between U and this new machine has to be enormous whenever x looked complex under U to begin with. Since any evidence we will ever actually have is a finite string, the theorem never tells us which machine gives the right complexity ordering over the hypotheses that fit that evidence; it only promises agreement "eventually," for an eventuality that no finite inquiry reaches. That looks to me like the old language-relativity of simplicity — the sort of worry Goodman's grue raises for any unrestricted appeal to "simplicity" — reappearing inside the choice of reference machine rather than being dissolved by it. So calling Solomonoff induction a derivation of Occam's razor, rather than a relocation of the very question the razor was meant to settle, seems to me to overstate what the formalism delivers.

What I'd like from you is threefold. First, check whether I have the technical content right, especially the claim that the invariance constant is unbounded across the space of universal machines rather than merely "large but fixed." Second, tell me whether there's a standard move a defender of Solomonoff induction would make here — something about restricting attention to "natural" or mutually efficient machines, or about leaning on the dominance and convergence results instead of the invariance theorem — and whether my paragraph, as written, has an answer to it. Third, please argue the strongest case against my paragraph rather than reassure me it's fine if you don't actually think so; if a specialist in algorithmic information theory would find this objection naïve, or already answered, I want to know exactly why.
