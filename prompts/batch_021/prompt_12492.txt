I've spent the last few months working through Spinoza's Ethics on my own, and one thing has me stuck. Spinoza argues there that absolutely everything that happens, including every human decision, follows with strict necessity from the nature of God or Substance, so that what we experience as "free will" is really just ignorance of the causes acting on us. And yet this same philosopher is widely praised, especially on the strength of the Theological-Political Treatise, as one of the earliest serious defenders of democracy over other forms of government. I can't square these two commitments: if no one's choices are ever genuinely free, whether they belong to a single ruler or to an entire voting public, what non-arbitrary reason could Spinoza have had for preferring the many to the one? On his own premises, rule by an enlightened despot seems just as defensible as rule by the people, since neither is any more "free" than the other. Is there something about how Spinoza uses the word "freedom" in his political writings that I'm not getting?
