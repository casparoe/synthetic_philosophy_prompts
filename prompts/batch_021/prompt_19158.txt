I'm drafting a scene for a novel that turns on a long-running philosophical quarrel between two old friends, and I'd like to get the philosophy right before I put words in anyone's mouth. Miriam, a former philosophy lecturer who quietly returned to religious faith after her husband's death, and Daniel, a science journalist who has spent two decades debunking pseudoscience, are driving home from a funeral when their old argument resurfaces. Daniel takes a strict Cliffordian line: that it is wrong, always and for everyone, to believe anything on insufficient evidence. Miriam answers him with William James's "The Will to Believe," and I want her rebuttal to be a serious piece of philosophy rather than a straw man, so let me set out how I currently understand James's argument and ask you to check it: whenever a question genuinely cannot be settled by the evidence one way or the other, James holds that we are entitled to let our hopes and "passional nature" tip the balance, since refusing to decide is itself a kind of choice with its own risks and costs.

If I have that right, it seems to follow that Miriam can run exactly the same argument for something much smaller than God. For ten years she has been writing a novel that four agents have already turned down, and privately, against the discouraging run of evidence so far, she believes it will eventually be published and vindicated. Nothing conclusively rules this out any more than anything conclusively rules out an afterlife, so on my reading of James she ought to be just as entitled to let hope decide the matter here as in the religious case. Is that actually a faithful use of James's position, or is there something in the conditions he places on what he calls a "genuine option" that would let him -- and Daniel -- grant her the first belief while still holding her to ordinary evidential standards on the second?
