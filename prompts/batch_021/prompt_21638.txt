The following short essay concerns a little-discussed corner of Robert Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia: his brief remarks on how far the side-constraint theory of rights, developed in Chapter 3 to ground a Kantian conception of persons, extends to non-human animals. I would like comments on it before developing it further.

Nozick's case against utilitarianism as a foundation for rights rests on treating persons as bearers of side constraints rather than as loci of value to be maximized: because a person can conceive of and pursue a plan for her own life, and thereby give that life meaning by her own lights, she may not be sacrificed, however slightly, to increase the aggregate welfare of others, no matter how many others or how large the increase. Side constraints are not one good among others to be traded off in a social calculus; they bound the calculus itself.

Nozick is alive to the awkward fact that almost nobody, including him, is prepared to extend this same inviolability to animals. In a brief aside he considers, without fully endorsing, a two-tier resolution: our treatment of persons should be Kantian, while our treatment of animals may remain broadly utilitarian, weighing an animal's suffering against the benefits its use produces for others, precisely because animals lack, or possess to a much lesser degree, the capacity for rational self-direction that grounds side constraints for persons in the first place. Crudely: Kantianism for people, utilitarianism for animals.

The trouble is that the capacity doing all the work here comes in degrees and does not track species membership cleanly. Some non-human animals arguably possess more of it than some human beings — infants, or people with profound cognitive impairments — who nonetheless retain full inviolability on Nozick's view. Either moral status tracks the underlying capacity, in which case the two-tier view collapses into a single graded scale with uncomfortable implications for such marginal-case humans, or it tracks species membership as such, which is just the kind of arbitrary boundary the capacity-based argument was meant to avoid.

There is a further, more internal problem. Elsewhere in the book, Nozick's case against utilitarianism includes the "utility monster": a being whose marginal utility is so high that maximizing aggregate welfare would require everyone else's near-total sacrifice to it. Part of the force of that objection is that no story about how much utility is produced can justify what is done to those aggregated over. But this is exactly the license Nozick is willing to grant with respect to animals. If aggregative reasoning is rejected for persons partly because of what it could in principle authorize, it is unclear why the same worry, applied to a sufficiently large coalition of human beneficiaries, does not equally indict aggregation for animals. The two-tier view seems to need the capacity distinction to carry the entire justificatory weight, which returns us to the boundary problem above.

I would like three kinds of comments: first, on whether this is a fair reconstruction of Nozick's actual position, since I am working from memory rather than the text itself and may have some details, or its place in the chapter, wrong; second, on whether the tension drawn out in the last two paragraphs is one his view has the resources to answer, or is a genuine unresolved cost of it; and third, on whether there is a more charitable version of "Kantianism for people, utilitarianism for animals" that avoids the boundary problem without collapsing into either full aggregation for everyone or bare species membership as the criterion of moral status.
