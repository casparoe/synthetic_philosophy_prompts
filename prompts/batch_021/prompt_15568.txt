Newton's bucket is the usual entry point for the debate between substantivalism and relationism about space and time: because the water's surface turns concave under rotation regardless of what it is or isn't rotating relative to, there seems to be some further fact about rotation—rotation relative to space itself—that only a substantival space can supply. In a reading group I'm part of, we've since moved on to the more contemporary version of this dispute that arises within general relativity, via Earman and Norton's "hole argument," and I'm having trouble seeing exactly how the technical machinery is supposed to generate a metaphysical conclusion.

Could you walk through, in reasonably precise terms, how the hole argument is actually constructed, and why it's meant to be a distinctive problem for substantivalists about the spacetime manifold rather than for relationists? I'd like the explanation to engage with the moving parts: what an active diffeomorphism is doing when it's used to build a second model that agrees with the first everywhere outside some designated "hole" region but disagrees inside it; why this move is supposed to saddle the substantivalist, but not the relationist, with a radical indeterminism, depending on whether one accepts or rejects something like "Leibniz equivalence" between diffeomorphic models; and how "sophisticated substantivalism" (the Butterfield-style response) is supposed to block the argument by denying that diffeomorphic models represent distinct possibilities while still insisting that spacetime points are genuine substances. Finally, I'd be curious for your own assessment of whether that last move is a principled way of individuating spacetime points, or whether, as some critics allege, it just relabels relationism—or a structuralist view of spacetime—in substantivalist-sounding language, and whether any of this ultimately vindicates or undercuts the intuition that originally seemed so compelling in the bucket case.
