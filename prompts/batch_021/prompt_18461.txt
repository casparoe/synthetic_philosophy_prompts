I write about artificial intelligence and cognitive science for a general-interest science magazine, and on the side I host an interview podcast where I get researchers to defend their work in front of a lay audience rather than their usual peers. Next week I'm recording with a researcher who has spent the past several years building and stress-testing "debate" as a scalable oversight protocol: the proposal that we can keep a check on AI systems whose capabilities eventually outstrip our own by pitting two competing systems against each other in front of a human (or weaker-model) judge, on the theory that a lie is more expensive to sustain under cross-examination than a truth is to defend. I've spent the last couple of weeks in the technical literature and its critiques rather than just the original pitch, and I want the conversation to start from where the theory actually strains rather than from the version she gives at conferences.

Draft eight to ten interview questions for her that go beyond having her restate the pitch. I want at least one question on the obfuscated arguments problem -- the worry that a dishonest debater can win by burying its flaw inside a long chain of sub-claims that each look locally fine, so that the honest debater can't cheaply locate and point to the one that's rotten -- with a follow-up keyed to whichever fix (recursive decomposition of claims, forced cross-examination, and so on) she's likely to reach for first. I want at least one question on the gap between the complexity-theoretic case for debate -- the analogy to interactive-proof systems, and the newer results suggesting a bounded judge can verify debates efficiently under the right assumptions -- and the fact that real debaters are shaped by self-play and gradient descent rather than behaving like idealized provers, with a follow-up asking what would have to be true of the training process for that guarantee to survive contact with actual models. And I want at least one question on the suspicion that training a debater against a fallible judge selects for whatever is persuasive to that judge rather than for honesty as such, with a follow-up pressing on whether the human-judge experiments run so far actually show the truth-tracking advantage the theory predicts, or something considerably weaker. Close the set with a single question asking what result, if it arrived tomorrow, would convince her that debate does not scale and should be dropped from the scalable-oversight toolkit.
