My mother retires this spring after thirty-four years on our state's court of appeals, and my siblings have delegated me — I teach philosophy for a living, which apparently qualifies me — to give the toast at her retirement dinner. Among her fellow judges and clerks she is known, with a mixture of admiration and exasperation, for a habit she never broke: on a three-judge panel, she always insisted that the elements of a claim be decided one by one — was there a duty, was it breached, did the breach cause the harm — and let the verdict fall out of that vote, rather than having each judge simply cast an up-or-down vote on who should win and letting the majority decide. Lawyers call this the doctrinal paradox and philosophers call it the discursive dilemma: a panel of individually consistent judges can be pushed toward two different, equally well-reasoned verdicts depending on whether judgments are pooled issue-by-issue or instead by a direct vote on the outcome, and there is at least one case, still discussed by her former clerks, where her own panel split along exactly this fault line. She used to tell her clerks that she would rather lose a case than lose the reasons for it.

I'd like your help drafting this toast. Of the roughly ninety guests, perhaps thirty are judges, lawyers, and former clerks who will recognize immediately what is being alluded to if I lean on this; the rest are neighbors, her choir friends, and three grandchildren who have never heard the phrase "judgment aggregation" and have no need to. This is close enough to my own work in social epistemology that I could write a competent lecture on it in my sleep, which is exactly the failure mode I want to avoid: I don't want the discursive dilemma dropped in as a fun fact about Mom's quirks, I want it doing real work, shaping the toast the way it shaped her career, so that both halves of the room come away understanding, each at whatever level of detail suits them, why anyone would care this much about how votes get counted and not just what they add up to.

One thing matters to me here, and would matter to her. She has said publicly, more than once, that List and Pettit's impossibility result for judgment aggregation — which shows, in a spirit close to Arrow's theorem for preferences, that no procedure for pooling several people's separate judgments into one collective judgment can be guaranteed to preserve every property we would want it to have — means her issue-by-issue habit was a considered wager about what to give up, not a solution to the problem and not evidence that colleagues who voted the other way were being careless. The toast should celebrate the wager without ever implying she solved something that provably cannot be solved. I'm picturing something like six or seven minutes read aloud, warmer than a lecture, ideally with at least one moment that makes the lawyers in the room glance at each other.
