My sister, who wrote her dissertation on Émilie du Châtelet, is getting married next month, and I've been asked to give the toast. She has spent years insisting that du Châtelet's essay "Discours sur le bonheur" (Discourse on Happiness) got it right: against the Stoic ideal of a tranquil, passion-free soul, du Châtelet argued that a happy life requires strong passions and even illusions -- a willingness to exaggerate the importance of what one cares about -- and that the real skill lies in knowing when a passion has to be allowed to change form rather than be kept intact or simply renounced. About a third of the guests are from her graduate program and know the essay well, including its inconvenient wrinkle: du Châtelet's own love affairs and her serious gambling debts were not exactly a tidy advertisement for the theory. Everyone else just wants a toast that sounds like a normal wedding toast. Please write a three-to-four-minute version that uses du Châtelet's actual, specific view of happiness rather than a generic substitute for it, and that lands, for the family, as a toast rather than a lecture, and, for the specialists, as an honest account rather than a whitewash of the theory's rougher edges.
