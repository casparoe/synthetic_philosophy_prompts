Two friends of mine, Leah and Omar, are in the same graduate seminar on normative ethics, and they got into a long argument over dinner recently that I still haven't been able to settle in my own head. The disagreement concerned a mutual friend, Dan, who had promised months in advance to be best man at his oldest friend's out-of-town wedding. Two days before the wedding, his grandmother, who had largely raised him, was moved into hospice with only days left to live, and it turned out that the wedding and the hospice visit fell on the same weekend in different cities, so Dan could not attend both. He went to the wedding, since he had given his word; his grandmother died before he had another chance to see her, and he has since told several people that he still feels he owes her something, even though he doesn't think going to the wedding was the wrong call.

Here, as best I can reconstruct it, is how the argument went -- it turned technical fast, probably because of a unit on moral dilemmas they'd just finished in the seminar. (1) Leah said this is exactly what a genuine moral dilemma looks like: Dan was truly obligated both to keep his promise and to be there for his grandmother, he could not satisfy both obligations, and so whichever he did, he was going to do something wrong -- which is exactly why his continuing guilt is appropriate rather than mere leftover sadness. (2) Omar objected that this can't be right: if Dan was really obligated to do both, he was obligated to do their conjunction, and since that conjunction was flatly impossible for him, "ought implies can" rules it out; so at most one of the two was ever his actual, all-things-considered duty, and the other was a prima facie duty that got outweighed once the standing promise and the timing were factored in. On his view, Dan's guilt is real but doesn't show he did anything wrong -- it's just the residual pull of an unfulfilled prima facie duty, the same way someone can feel bad about missing a friend's small favor even while knowing they were right to prioritize something else. (3) Leah answered that this explains away exactly what needs explaining: if there really was no wrongdoing, why does Dan describe himself as still owing his grandmother something, rather than simply wishing things had gone differently? People don't normally feel they owe amends for having correctly ranked their priorities. (4) Omar replied that the intensity of a feeling is a bad guide here, since people can clearly feel implicated in an outcome, and even feel they owe something, without having done anything wrong -- as when someone feels terrible about a blameless accident they caused -- so the guilt doesn't settle the question either way. (5) Leah's last word was that if this kind of response is always available, "genuine dilemma" becomes a position nothing could ever count as evidence for, which looks like a cost of Omar's view rather than a point in its favor, since the whole dispute is supposed to answer to cases exactly like this one.

Neither of them struck me as confused, and both seemed to be drawing on real material from the seminar rather than improvising.
