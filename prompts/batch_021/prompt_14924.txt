Here's one of the answers I got back (cleaned up slightly for typos, otherwise as submitted):

"Hamilton's rule states that a gene for altruistic behavior will spread through a population whenever rB > C, where r is the coefficient of relatedness between actor and recipient, B is the fitness benefit to the recipient, and C is the fitness cost to the actor. This was Hamilton's solution to a problem Darwin himself flagged: how can natural selection favor sterile worker castes in ants and bees, who give up their own reproduction entirely? Hamilton's answer, usually called kin selection, is that a gene can still increase in frequency by boosting the reproduction of relatives who carry copies of that same gene, even at a cost to the individual bearing it. Since workers in a hive are close relatives of the queen and of each other, helping raise siblings can be a better route to genetic posterity than reproducing directly. In this sense kin selection is really a species of group selection: it privileges the reproductive success of the family or hive group over that of the lone individual, which is exactly the sort of higher-level selection that Wynne-Edwards had in mind and that Williams and others later pushed back against.

This consensus was upended, or at least seriously challenged, by Nowak, Tarnita and Wilson's 2010 Nature paper, which showed mathematically that Hamilton's rule is false as a general description of when altruism will evolve, and argued that standard natural selection models (which track allele frequencies directly rather than going through inclusive-fitness bookkeeping) are the only fully general framework. The paper triggered a huge backlash, including a joint response signed by well over a hundred biologists defending inclusive fitness theory. In my view the challenge succeeds more than most discussions let on: since Hamilton's rule has been mathematically falsified, and Dawkins's whole gene's-eye-view picture of evolution is built on the inclusive-fitness framework Hamilton devised, the 2010 paper effectively undermines the gene's-eye view too, not just eusociality research narrowly. Given how central that perspective has been to popular and philosophical discussions of evolution since the 1970s, that's a bigger deal than the paper usually gets credit for."
