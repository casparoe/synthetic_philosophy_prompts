I am a software engineer rather than a philosopher, but while reading about Kurt Gödel's life outside the incompleteness theorems, I was struck by three of his lesser-known convictions: he was a committed mathematical Platonist; in correspondence with his mother, he argued that the sheer rationality of the world made some form of afterlife almost logically necessary; and in his 1951 Gibbs lecture, he set out a disjunction under which either the human mind can never be matched by any finite computing machine, or there exist absolutely undecidable Diophantine problems — a disjunction he privately believed was resolved in favor of the mind, against mechanism.

Gödel died in 1978, well before "mind uploading" and whole-brain emulation became serious topics in philosophy of mind and transhumanist circles. Given the three convictions above, how do you think he would have reacted to the claim that a sufficiently detailed computational emulation of a person's brain could count as that person's genuine survival after death? Would he have welcomed uploading as a technological vindication of his rationalist case for an afterlife, or would his skepticism that minds reduce to machines have led him to dismiss any such copy as a mechanical simulacrum with no genuine access to the Platonic realm he thought minds could perceive? Commit to a definite answer, and defend it by engaging with the real tension between these strands of his thought rather than describing what a generic "rational" or "religious" thinker might say about artificial intelligence.
