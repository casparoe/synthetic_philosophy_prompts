I've spent years reading rationalist blogs and forums, and one thing that has always struck me about the way people there argue about ethics is how freely everyone moves between object-level moral claims and perfectly ordinary logical operations on them, such as modus ponens, contraposition, and reductio, as though moral sentences behaved in arguments exactly the way factual claims do. At the same time, a large share of that community describe themselves as anti-realists, and quite a few lean toward some form of expressivism: pressed on it, they will say that asserting "torturing animals for fun is wrong" is expressing an attitude of disapproval rather than describing a mind-independent fact, more or less in the spirit of the classic emotivism and prescriptivism associated with Ayer and Hare.

This combination started to bother me during a long comment-thread argument about factory farming, in which someone ran an entirely unremarkable-looking syllogism (if an action causes gratuitous suffering, it is wrong; factory farming causes gratuitous suffering; therefore factory farming is wrong) and another commenter replied that the argument only holds together if one is secretly smuggling in moral realism, citing something called the Frege–Geach problem, which I gather traces back to a 1965 paper by Geach responding to Ayer- and Hare-style non-cognitivism. I looked into it enough to grasp the basic shape of the objection — that an expressivist owes some account of what a moral sentence contributes when it appears embedded and unasserted inside a conditional, given that the same sentence asserted on its own is supposed to be nothing more than an expression of feeling — but I still cannot tell what actually follows from it for someone in my position.

I am not a professional philosopher, just someone who would rather not keep running arguments I cannot account for. Could you set out the most important implications of the Frege–Geach problem for me, given that I want to go on arguing about ethics using ordinary inference, modus ponens included, while remaining an expressivist about first-order moral claims? I would especially like to know whether the standard responses on offer, such as Blackburn's quasi-realism and Gibbard's norm-expressivism, actually rescue the practice of moral argument as we ordinarily conduct it, or whether they merely relocate the difficulty somewhere less visible.
