I co-run the Effective Altruism Club at my high school, and each winter we send whatever we've raised that term to charities chosen by the membership, in keeping with our constitution's instruction to fund 'the interventions that do the most good per dollar, as best we can determine.' No one has ever had to decide what that phrase actually means until this year. We raised $2,640 through a bake sale, a sponsored walk, and a small matching grant from the PTA, and the allocation vote this coming Tuesday has turned unexpectedly heated: our cause-prioritization subcommittee ran the usual expected-value numbers and recommended sending the entire amount to the Against Malaria Foundation, which has the most robust, well-evidenced case per dollar of anything we track.

The complication is that over the summer I attended a short fellowship on effective altruism where a seminar leader spent an afternoon on an idea called evidential cooperation in large worlds: roughly, the thought that if the universe (or multiverse) is large or infinite, there are vast numbers of agents out there who reason the way we do but hold very different values, and that because our decision procedure is correlated with theirs even though we can never causally interact, we have reason to act as if we had struck a bargain with them, each putting some weight on values we don't personally hold, in the hope that they are doing the same for ours. Two members — the head of our animal-welfare subcommittee and one of our AI-safety discussion leads — ran into the same idea independently over break and are now arguing that we should split the $2,640 three ways, between the Against Malaria Foundation, The Humane League, and the Long-Term Future Fund, on the grounds that this, and not the subcommittee's single-charity pick, is what actually maximizes expected value once the correlation argument is taken seriously. Other members, including our resident decision-theory enthusiast, think this smuggles in evidential decision theory through the back door, doesn't survive if you're a causal decision theorist instead, and conveniently funds two causes its proponents couldn't otherwise have won on a straight vote — on a donation so small that it is surely evidentially weightless at whatever scale the argument needs to work.

Our faculty advisor has asked the co-presidents to circulate a short decision memo before Tuesday's meeting so that however the vote goes, everyone feels their argument was actually engaged with rather than simply outvoted. Could you write that memo for me? I'd like it to recommend one specific allocation of the $2,640 across some combination of the three charities (or make the case for giving it all to one), to explain, in terms the club could actually follow, why that allocation does or doesn't take the evidential-cooperation argument seriously, and to state plainly what you take to be the strongest reason the other side could give against your recommendation, rather than just dismissing it.
