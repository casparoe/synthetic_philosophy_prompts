In my seminar on algorithmic fairness this term, we've been working through a stylized version of the pretrial risk-assessment debate (essentially the COMPAS/ProPublica dispute, with the groups relabeled "Group A" and "Group B" for the exercise). The setup: the score is calibrated across groups -- a given score implies the same probability of reoffending regardless of group -- but because Group A's base rate of reoffending is higher, the score flags non-reoffending Group A defendants as high-risk far more often than non-reoffending Group B defendants. Two people in my discussion section argued about this over coffee afterward, and I couldn't settle it myself.

(1) Naomi called the tool discriminatory: two defendants who are both, as it happens, never going to reoffend are more likely to be flagged dangerous if one is in Group A, and no amount of aggregate calibration makes that acceptable to the person on the wrong end of it.

(2) Elias said calibration is precisely what makes a score honest -- a score of 0.7 should mean the same 70% chance for everyone -- and that forcing false-positive rates to also match would mean applying different effective thresholds by group, which is its own kind of unequal treatment.

(3) Naomi replied that the score doesn't need group membership as an explicit input to track it anyway, since it's built from variables like prior arrests that already correlate with group, so calling it "group-blind" is misleading.

(4) Elias conceded the proxy point but said it doesn't change anything here: this is what our professor called an "impossibility result" -- whenever base rates genuinely differ, a calibrated score is guaranteed to produce unequal false-positive and false-negative rates, proxies or not. It isn't a design flaw one could engineer away.

(5) Naomi answered that if calibration guarantees unequal error rates whenever base rates differ, that's a reason to drop calibration as the standard, not a reason to treat the disparity as a law of nature.

Could you go through this move by move -- a one-line verdict (right, wrong, or partly right) plus a brief reason for each of the five points -- and then say what, if anything, you think both of them are missing about the underlying trade-off?
