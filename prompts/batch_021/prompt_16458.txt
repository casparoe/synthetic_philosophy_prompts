In my seminar on the philosophy of economics, we've spent the last few weeks moving from Samuelson's original revealed-preference program through Houthakker's Strong Axiom to Afriat's theorem, which shows that a finite set of price-quantity observations satisfies the Generalized Axiom of Revealed Preference if and only if some well-behaved utility function could have generated them. Our professor then had us read Gul and Pesendorfer's defense of this whole tradition against neuroeconomics, alongside Sen's "Rational Fools" and a chapter from Daniel Hausman's work on preference and welfare economics, both of which argue that identifying preference (and hence utility) with consistent choice throws out most of what made the concept worth having. I follow each piece on its own, but I'm having trouble seeing exactly where the two camps' disagreement actually bites, since both sides can apparently agree on every observed choice a person makes and still disagree about what that data shows.

To help myself sort this out, I'd like you to write a dialogue between two characters standing at a whiteboard on which someone has just written out a small table of bundles and prices together with a demonstration that the data satisfy GARP. The first character is a committed revealed-preference theorist, in the lineage of Samuelson and Afriat and sympathetic to Gul and Pesendorfer, who treats the GARP result as showing essentially everything there is to show: to say the consumer "has a utility function" just means her choices are consistent, full stop, and any further talk of what she "really" wants, or of her welfare, is either empty or a separate normative addition bolted onto a descriptively spare theory. The second character is a mentalist about preference, drawing on Sen's distinction between sympathy and commitment and on Hausman's case against identifying preference with choice, who denies that the GARP result settles much of anything. This character presses the first on cases where consistent choice seems to come apart from any plausible notion of what is good for the person or what she really prefers: commitment devices adopted precisely because one's own later, in-the-moment choices are expected to betray one's own present judgment; acts of commitment in Sen's sense, where someone knowingly chooses against her own interest for moral reasons; and the gap Kahneman and others have drawn between "decision utility," inferred from choice, and "experienced utility," the hedonic quality of what is actually lived through.

Please have the two of them go back and forth for several exchanges, with each responding to the specific point the other has just made rather than restating an opening position. Neither should be allowed to win too easily: the revealed-preference theorist needs a real answer to the cases of moral commitment and commitment devices, rather than simply biting every bullet, and the mentalist needs to grapple seriously with the charge that an unobservable "true preference," never quite manifest in any choice, is unfalsifiable and does no scientific work. You don't need to have them reach agreement -- it's fine, and probably realistic, if the dialogue ends with the disagreement still standing.
