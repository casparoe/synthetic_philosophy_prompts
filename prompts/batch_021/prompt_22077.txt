I teach a graduate seminar on value theory and practical reason, and two of my colleagues nearly hijacked our end-of-term dinner with a disagreement I still haven't managed to settle in my own head. The occasion was a former student's job decision. Daniel had been offered a tenure-track position at a small teaching college -- secure, but with a 4/4 load and almost no room for research -- and, simultaneously, a three-year fellowship at a well-funded research institute, which offered prestige and protected writing time but ended after three years with no guarantee of anything afterward. He agonized for weeks before eventually taking the fellowship, and it was the nature of that agonizing that Nora and Julian could not agree about.

Nora, who wrote her dissertation on incommensurability, argued that Daniel's difficulty was due neither to missing information nor to the two options being equally good; in Ruth Chang's sense, she said, the jobs were "on a par" -- genuinely comparable, yet related by none of better-than, worse-than, or equal-to. Julian, a philosopher of language by training, replied that this is just Raz's old incommensurability point in fancier dress: positing some fourth basic value relation alongside the familiar three explains nothing that plain incomparability doesn't already explain. Nora came back with the small-improvement argument -- sweeten the teaching job a little, say by cutting the load to 3/3, and it still isn't determinately better than the fellowship, whereas if the two had merely been roughly equal, an improvement that small should have tipped the balance, so something more precise than "incomparable" must be going on. Julian answered that this shows only that "better than" is vague in this neighborhood, à la Broome: a small improvement can fail to break a tie for the same reason one grain never turns a non-heap into a heap, so we don't need Chang's exotic fourth relation, just ordinary semantic indeterminacy. From there they moved on, no more conclusively, to whether Daniel's decision could even be called rational, given that on neither of their pictures was there any fact about which job was actually better.

Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of the argument at each stage, rather than simply declaring an overall winner? I would also be curious what, if anything, you think both of them are overlooking about hard choices and incommensurable value.
