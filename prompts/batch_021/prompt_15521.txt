For my epistemology seminar, we've been reading recent work on higher-order evidence and its connection to epistemic akrasia. As I understand it, since believing P while also believing that one's evidence doesn't support P counts as irrational, it follows straightforwardly that whenever you acquire higher-order evidence casting doubt on your own reasoning, rationality simply requires bringing your confidence in P down to match that assessment. Take the sort of case we discussed in class: I work through a tricky derivation and feel sure of the result, but I am then truthfully told that I've been given a drug that impairs logical reasoning in about half of those who take it, with no way to tell introspectively whether I am one of them. Given that akrasia is irrational, doesn't rationality obligate me to lower my confidence in the derivation accordingly? I'm having trouble seeing what would motivate someone like Lasonen-Aarnio to resist this and to treat such cases as posing a genuine dilemma, rather than as settling the matter in favor of always deferring to higher-order evidence. Am I missing something?
