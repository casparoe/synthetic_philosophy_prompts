I've spent the past few weeks reading through Bernard Williams's ethical writings alongside the essays collected in On Opera, the posthumous volume his wife Patricia assembled from decades of reviews and program notes (he sat on the board of the Royal Opera House for years, so this was never a dilettante's sideline), and I've drafted the core of a longer piece arguing that this criticism is less a hobby detached from his philosophy than a continuation of it in another medium. Before I go further, I'd like a careful read and some honest pushback.

The claim, in brief: Williams spent his career resisting the idea that ethical life can be captured by a single, consistent, impersonal theory - that's the thread running from his attacks on utilitarian and Kantian systematizing, through "moral luck," to his insistence on "thick" ethical concepts that resist reduction to thin, rule-governed ones (an argument that keeps resurfacing whenever rationalist-adjacent blogs relitigate whether consequentialism can be made to work). My contention is that his scattered opera essays run the same argument in a different register: specific operas are treated as demonstrations, in the flesh, of ethical material that resists propositional summary, and music is what lets an opera preserve that resistance instead of collapsing into a stated moral.

Three examples I'm relying on:

1. Don Giovanni: the vitality Mozart's score gives the Don survives, and arguably outruns, the moralizing final sextet, in which the survivors sing that the wicked always meet an end that matches how they lived. If the opera's ethical content were really exhausted by that closing verdict, it's odd that generations of productions felt free to cut the sextet or play it as an ironic afterthought, as though the real argument of the piece had already finished with the Don's death.

2. Strauss's Elektra: the music inhabits Electra's monomania so completely, and for so long, that it never has to endorse or condemn it; it simply renders the state of mind available, at a length and intensity a purely verbal account would struggle to sustain without turning into a lecture or a case study.

3. Wagner's Ring: the cycle has attracted allegorical readings (Shaw's socialist Ring, assorted Jungian and political ones) that all feel suggestive and reductive at once, because the drama does something with renunciation, oath-breaking, and power that never cashes out as one extractable thesis.

My tentative conclusion is that Williams found opera philosophically serious for the same reason he found moral theory overrated: some ethical truths are available only to a trained, particular sensibility rather than to calculation, and music is a way of exercising exactly that sensibility.

I'd welcome comments on three things: whether this is a fair reading of Williams rather than something I'm projecting onto him; whether the throughline works as an actual argument rather than just a suggestive analogy; and any counterexamples, from these operas or others, that complicate it.
