Naturally I went down a rabbit hole reading about "split-brain" patients, and half the pop-science stuff I found just straight-up claims the surgery leaves you with two seperate consciousnesses in one skull, which majorly freaked out my mom before I made her stop reading random reddit threads at 1am.

Trying to find something more solid than clickbait, I ended up comparing two philosophers who actually took this seriously and land in pretty different places. Thomas Nagel's old essay on brain bisection basically concludes that the data doesn't support a clean answer of "one mind" or "two minds" — more like our ordinary concept of a unified subject just wasn't built for this kind of case, so insisting on a definite headcount is close to a category error. Roland Puccetti, on the other hand, argued way more confidently that there are already two minds (he'd say two persons) in every normal, intact brain, cooperating quietly through the corpus callosum, and that the surgery doesn't create a second mind so much as unmask one that was there the whole time.

Could you actually compare these two positions for me — not just summarize them side by side, but tell me which one holds up better against what's actually known about how split-brain patients behave day to day, not just in the classic lab experiments where they flash something to one visual field?
