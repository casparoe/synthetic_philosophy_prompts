Two friends of mine, Nadia and Ben, fell into a disagreement at dinner over a third friend, Renata, who is chasing a marathon time fast enough to qualify for Boston — her recent tune-up races put her odds at roughly one in five. She has told everyone she has decided to fully believe she will hit it on race day, since she thinks confidence measurably affects pacing, pain tolerance, and the will to keep pushing late in a race.

(1) Ben said this is Clifford's shipowner in running shoes: talking yourself into a belief because it is comfortable is exactly what "The Ethics of Belief" condemns, and Clifford's own point was that the shipowner would be just as guilty even if the ship reached port safely, since the fault is in how the belief was formed, not how things turn out. (2) Nadia said the analogy fails at the crucial point: the ship's seaworthiness does not depend on the shipowner's belief, whereas Renata's performance might — closer to the climber in James's "The Will to Believe" who needs faith that he can make a leap in order to make it, since hesitation itself produces the fall. (3) Ben answered that this proves too much: if "it would help my performance" were sufficient license, a mediocre interview candidate could equally believe he is the best-qualified person in the room whenever that confidence would help him — which looks like self-deception, not virtue.

(4) Nadia replied that James's license is not that broad: he restricts it to "genuine options" — forced, living, and momentous — and specifically to questions that cannot be settled on intellectual grounds at all, with religious faith as his paradigm case. Renata's one-in-five is a specific unfavorable estimate, not an evidential standoff, so nothing in James obviously licenses overriding it. (5) Ben's rejoinder was that this restriction cannot bear the weight Nadia puts on it: almost no real question is permanently undecidable on intellectual grounds in that strict sense, since further evidence is always conceivable — so either the restriction excludes nearly everything, religious faith arguably included, or it has no principled way of keeping Renata's case out.

Neither budged, and I would rather not fall back on the generic "epistemic vs. instrumental rationality" framing I have seen on rationalist blogs — I want a verdict on this specific exchange. Could you go through moves (1) through (5), labeling each Right, Wrong, or Right-but-incomplete with a brief justification, then close with your own view on whether Renata's policy is closer to a legitimate exercise of the will to believe or closer to the self-deception Clifford had in mind?
