This semester I'm taking a practicum seminar called "Effective Giving and Applied Ethics," in which our class doubles as the grant-making committee for a $25,000 fund that an alumna donated specifically so students could practice this kind of reasoning with real stakes attached. We've spent most of the term narrowing dozens of applications down to two finalists: (A) a global-health charity that distributes insecticide-treated bednets, whose case rests on randomized-trial evidence about as strong as evidence in this area gets, and (B) a small longtermist research organization whose case rests on reducing existential risk from advanced AI over the coming decades. Everyone assumed the final debate would pit rigorous near-term evidence against speculative long-run stakes, and for most of the semester it did.

Then one committee member, who's also a philosophy major, pointed out something that has since derailed us: the "cluelessness" objection to consequentialism doesn't stop at the speculative long-termist option. We can be fairly confident the bednets prevent malaria deaths in the near term, but we have no idea what saving those particular lives does, compounded over decades, to regional population growth, local labor markets, or even land use and wild-animal welfare — effects that could in principle dwarf the direct benefit and that nobody on the committee has any principled way to sign, let alone weigh against the trial evidence. Borrowing a distinction from the literature, she argued that this is "complex" cluelessness, not the kind of symmetric, safely-ignorable uncertainty that attaches to almost any action anyone could take, and that it applies to option A just as much as option B, just less obviously. The committee has since split three ways: some want to bracket unforeseeable long-run effects entirely and fund whichever option has the best near-term evidence; some think cluelessness shows expected-value comparison has broken down here and want to split the money between both finalists on other grounds; and some think the objection, taken seriously, would paralyze every donation anyone has ever made, so it must be resisted somewhere even if we can't yet say exactly where. Our professor has refused to break the tie, on the grounds that owning this disagreement is the actual point of the course.

I've been asked to draft the memo the committee will vote on this Friday, and I'd like your help with it. It should recommend one specific allocation of the $25,000 (a split between the two finalists is allowed, but only if it's actually argued for rather than used to dodge the disagreement), along with a timeline for disbursing it, and it needs to engage honestly with the strongest form of the cluelessness objection to whichever option it ends up recommending, rather than acknowledging the worry and then quietly setting it aside.
