I've been reporting on AI "reward hacking" and specification-gaming stories lately, and Goodhart's Law keeps coming up as the go-to explanation, usually stated as "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Funny thing I learned while digging around for the piece: that punchy phrasing apparently isn't even Goodhart's own words — it's usually traced back to the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern, decades after Goodhart's actual 1975 point, which was really about statistical regularities in monetary policy collapsing once you lean on them for control. Anyway, that's a tangent I probably won't even use.

What I actually can't get straight in my head is a taxonomy I keep seeing referenced in alignment-adjacent writing (I think it traces back to Scott Garrabrant, maybe together with David Manheim) that splits Goodhart's Law into four seperate failure modes: regressional, causal, extremal, and adversarial Goodhart. Every source I find just lists the four names with a one-line gloss and moves on, and the glosses all start to blur together after a while — like, isn't "the correlation breaks down because optimization pushed you into an extreme, weird part of the distribution" basically the same complaint as "the correlation was only ever partial, so selecting hard on it picks up noise"? And isnt "someone is actively exploiting the gap between measure and goal" just a special case of any of the other three, dressed up as its own category?

Could you actually walk me through the four categories in a way that makes the mechanistic difference between them click, ideally with a distinct concrete example for each one (an AI/ML example where you can manage it, but a non-AI one is fine too), and say something about why a fix that works for, say, extremal Goodhart might do basically nothing for adversarial Goodhart? I want to actually understand the underlying mechanics well enough to explain it to a reader, rather than just handing them four vague synonyms for "the metric stopped working."
