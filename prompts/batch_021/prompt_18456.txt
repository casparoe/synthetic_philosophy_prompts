Question (20 points): Kant famously denies that human beings have any direct moral duties toward animals, yet he also insists that gratuitous cruelty toward them is morally wrong. Explain how Kant reconciles these two claims, and briefly evaluate whether the reconciliation is satisfying.

Student answer: For Kant, only a being with reason and self-consciousness can be an "end in itself," and therefore the kind of being to which duties can be directly owed. Since animals cannot set ends for themselves or act on principles, nothing is, strictly speaking, ever owed to an animal itself; direct duties run only between rational agents. This might seem to license any treatment of animals whatsoever, but Kant blocks that conclusion by introducing duties regarding animals, which he distinguishes from duties to them. These are indirect duties: formally, they are duties to oneself, even though they concern how one treats an animal. Kant's own example is a man who shoots his dog once it is too old to work: the dog is not wronged, since a being that cannot judge cannot be wronged, but the man's character is, because indulging cruelty toward a helpless creature is the same disposition that, once exercised, makes a person harder and less humane toward other people as well. In effect, this is the same insight Bentham made more famous a few decades later: that the morally relevant question is not whether a creature can reason, but whether it can suffer. Because the Kantian duty is grounded entirely in what the act does to the agent's own character, rather than in anything owed to the animal, it also follows that a single act of cruelty committed where it could have no effect on the agent's future conduct toward anyone else -- no witness, no habituation, no consequence -- would not, on Kant's own terms, be wrong. This is arguably the weak point in the whole picture: whatever practical protection animals end up getting from Kant is a side effect of human self-cultivation, contingent on empirical facts about how character is formed, rather than anything the animal itself could be said to deserve.
