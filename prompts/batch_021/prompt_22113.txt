I spent about 35 years as a comparative physiologist before I retired, and back then we threw around the word "function" constantly without ever worrying what it meant -- the function of the swim bladder is buoyancy control, end of story. Now that I've gotten into philosophy of biology as a retirement hobby, I've learned theres a real fight over what that sentence even means: one camp (the "etiological" one) says the function of a trait is tied to why natural selection kept it around, the other (the "causal role" camp, after a philosopher called Cummins I think) says function is just whatever the part currently contributes to some capacity of the system, no history required. What's really got me stuck is a swampman-type case, borrowing that term from the philosophy-of-mind crowd: picture a heart-like organ that materializes by pure freak chance, molecule for molecule identical to a normal heart, but with zero evolutionary history behind it. The etiological camp seems forced to say that organ has no function at all even though it pumps blood perfectly well, which sounds nuts to me. Could you walk me through why they're actually committed to that, whether the weirdness survives once you look at it closely, how the causal-role camp would handle the exact same case, and whether anyone's patched up the etiological view to dodge the problem without it just quietly turning into the causal-role view?
