A few of us on my school's math team run a small, informal betting pool for our varsity basketball team's biggest games — nothing serious, just a few dollars between friends. Before last month's rivalry game, my friend Jake, who isn't on the team but likes to join in, told me privately that he was about 70% confident our school would win, and we agreed on a bet at odds that matched his number: he put up $7 against my $3, winner takes the whole pot.

A couple of days later I found out that Jake had also told our other friend Priya, separately, that he thought there was a 60% chance the other school would win, and made a second bet with her using odds that matched that number: he put up $6 against her $4. When Priya and I compared notes, two things jumped out at us. First, his two percentages add up to 130%, which seemed like it shouldn't be possible. Second, when we actually worked through both bets side by side, we realized that Jake is guaranteed to end up exactly $3 poorer no matter which team wins the game — we checked both outcomes separately and got the same number each time.

When we showed him this, Jake didn't think we'd proven anything. He said all Priya and I had done was find a way to combine two ordinary bets against him, not identify anything actually wrong with either belief on its own — he'd have been completely comfortable making either bet by itself, and since he told us the two percentages separately, at different times, he was never consciously holding contradictory beliefs at any single moment. I've just started the probability unit in AP Statistics, which is probably why the 130% caught my attention in the first place, but I don't have a good response to him. Did Priya and I actually show that Jake's underlying confidence levels were irrational, or did we just show that two people who compare notes can out-maneuver someone who doesn't? And does it matter that he never stated both numbers in the same conversation?
