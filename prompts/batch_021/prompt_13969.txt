I've been down a rabbit hole for a piece on personal identity and neuroscience, and it started when I sat in on a support-group meeting for people who've had corpus callosotomies ("split-brain" surgery, usually for severe epilepsy) and got talking afterward to a philosopher who was there doing her own fieldwork. She mentioned a fight that apparently still hasn't died down among people who work on this: is a split-brain patient one person or two? I already knew the pop-science version -- Sperry and Gazzaniga's old experiments, flashing a word to one visual field so only the mute right hemisphere sees it, the left hand drawing what the mouth can't name -- but apparently there's a real camp, going back to a philosopher named Puccetti I think, arguing the surgery doesn't create two minds so much as unmask two minds that were there all along, normally so well-coordinated through the corpus callosum that nobody notices -- on this view we're all sort of dual already. Then there's the opposite camp, which says this reads way too much into some very contrived lab conditions -- flash a chimeric half-face for a split second with the eyes fixed and of course you get a bizzare dissociation, but the same patients drive cars, hold conversations, and don't seem to be sharing a skull with a stranger at breakfast, so something clearly stays unified even when information-sharing between the hemispheres breaks down. (I also vaguely remember a Nagel paper arguing we shouldn't even expect "one mind or two" to have a determinate answer, which just confused me more.)

Could you write me a dialogue between two people holding these opposing views, running into each other at the bar after they've both just given talks at a consciousness conference? I want several real rounds of back-and-forth where they actually engage each other's strongest evidence -- the chimeric-faces stuff, cross-cuing, Gazzaniga's "interpreter," the cases where the two hands seem to want different things, the argument from ordinary daily functioning -- rather than just restating their opening positions, and neither one should be an easy strawman for the other. It's totally fine if they don't resolve it by the end.
