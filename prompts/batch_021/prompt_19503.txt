I have been sitting in on a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind this term, and the last few sessions were devoted to panpsychism, and in particular to what is usually called the "combination problem" (a term most often traced to William Seager, later broken down into several distinct sub-problems by David Chalmers): the difficulty of explaining how the micro-level experiential, or merely proto-experiential, properties that panpsychists attribute to fundamental physical entities could ever combine into the kind of unified, richly structured consciousness enjoyed by macroscopic subjects such as ourselves. One thing that became clear over the course of the seminar is that "the" combination problem is really a cluster of related but distinct puzzles (roughly: how many micro-subjects could compose a further, genuinely new subject; how micro-level qualities could combine into richer macro-level qualities; and how the relevant causal and phenomenal structure is preserved across levels), and that the responses on offer in the literature handle these sub-puzzles with very different degrees of success, often by quietly redefining what "combination" is even supposed to mean.
