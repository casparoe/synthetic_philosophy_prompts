I'm in a seminar this semester devoted to close reading Nietzsche's *On the Genealogy of Morality*, and we've just spent three weeks working through the Second Essay line by line. For my term paper I've drafted an argument about a tension there that doesn't get discussed much outside the specialist literature, and before I take it any further I'd like an honest outside read.

Here is the argument as it currently stands. In sections 16 through 19, Nietzsche describes "bad conscience" as arising when the cruel, aggressive instincts of pre-social humanity, no longer able to discharge themselves outward once they were enclosed within society, turned inward and began preying on their own possessor; he is explicit that this is a sickness, a kind of self-torture humanity was more or less forced into. Yet only a few sections earlier, in sections 2 and 3, he describes what looks like the very same raw capacity for self-directed constraint -- the ability to hold oneself to a promise across time, against one's own momentary inclinations -- as the mark of the "sovereign individual," which he treats not as pathology but as something like the "ripest fruit" of the whole process, an achievement rather than a wound. What makes this more than a coincidence of my translation is that Nietzsche uses the same word, Gewissen, for both: what the sovereign individual feels proud ownership of in section 2 he calls "conscience," and what gets analyzed many sections later as sickness is schlechtes Gewissen, bad conscience -- so the tension seems built into the text itself rather than into any particular rendering of it. My essay tries to reconcile the two passages by arguing that Nietzsche's sickness/health vocabulary is always relative to the constitution of whoever is doing the internalizing: the same redirected cruelty that is mere sickness in the reactive majority becomes, in the rare individual strong enough to shape it, the material of self-mastery rather than self-laceration. Our professor mentioned in passing that some readers instead treat the sovereign-individual passage as ironic, or even as a covert target of Nietzsche's critique, given how oddly it sits next to his general hostility toward talk of a free, self-authored will elsewhere in his work -- and I'd like to know whether that reading is stronger than my reconciliation allows for.

Could you look this over and tell me candidly where it's weakest, rather than just being encouraging? I'm specifically unsure whether the "relative to the constitution of the sufferer" move is doing real philosophical work or is just Nietzsche's own vocabulary dressed up as a solution, and whether there's a more obvious textual response to the tension that I'm missing entirely. I've mainly worked from the Kaufmann and Hollingdale translation but checked the passages in question against Clark and Swensen's, since I suspect the translation choices matter more here than they usually would -- so please flag it if you think I'm leaning on a phrase that doesn't actually hold up once you look past the English.
