Kurt Gödel's 1951 Gibbs lecture to the American Mathematical Society advanced what has come to be called "Gödel's disjunction": either the mathematical power of the human mind exceeds that of any fixed formal system (any "finite machine," in his phrase), or there exist absolutely unsolvable mathematical problems that no mind or machine could ever settle. Gödel presented the disjunction itself as a rigorous consequence of his incompleteness results, while remaining officially agnostic in the lecture as to which horn is correct. His later remarks and conversations, however, recorded for instance by Hao Wang, suggest that he personally leaned toward the first horn, in keeping with his mathematical Platonism and his suspicion that the mind is not a mechanism.

I have separately been reading about a puzzle from a rather different corner of logic that strikes me as a distant cousin of this one: the "Löbian obstacle," as it is named in Yudkowsky and Herreshoff's work at the Machine Intelligence Research Institute on self-modifying "tiling agents." Roughly, a consistent formal reasoner cannot adopt, as a blanket policy, the principle that whatever a system sharing its axioms proves must be true. Löb's theorem shows that a system able to prove this principle for a specific statement must already be able to prove that statement outright, and applying the same argument to a guaranteed falsehood shows that no consistent system can prove its own consistency, in effect recovering Gödel's own second incompleteness theorem by another route. In practice, this means an AI cannot place blanket trust in the reasoning of a successor built on its own axioms, or even in its own future self, without either collapsing that trust into having already settled the matter directly, or risking outright inconsistency. Löb's theorem itself dates to 1955, so it was available within Gödel's lifetime; its use to diagnose an obstacle facing self-improving AI, however, is a much more recent development that he never had occasion to consider.

Given all this, how do you think Gödel would have reacted to the Löbian obstacle, and how might he have tried to fit it into his disjunction? Would he most plausibly have read it as further, more concrete support for the "minds outrun mechanisms" horn, on the grounds that it exposes a limitation of purely formal self-trust that an intuiting mind need not share? Or, given his characteristic rigor, would he more likely have resisted that reading, on the grounds that the obstacle is really a fact about any sufficiently self-referential deductive system, mechanical or not, rather than a result that specifically favors minds over machines? I would find an answer most useful if it ties the prediction to specific things Gödel is on record as having believed, rather than resting on a generic guess.
