The question we were given was: "Dennett distinguishes three stances we can take toward a system in order to predict its behavior: the physical stance, the design stance, and the intentional stance. Using this framework and Dennett's notion of 'real patterns,' explain what it would take for it to be correct to say that a large language model (the kind of AI behind chatbots like ChatGPT) believes that Paris is the capital of France. In your answer, address whether adopting the intentional stance toward such a system commits us to saying it really has beliefs, or whether belief-talk here is best understood as a convenient fiction."

Here is Maya's answer: "Dennett first draws this three-way distinction in Consciousness Explained: there are three stances we can take to predict what a system will do. The physical stance predicts behavior from the laws of physics, the way we predict a dropped rock will fall. The design stance predicts behavior from what a system is designed to do, assuming nothing is broken — we predict a calculator will show '4' after '2+2' because that's what it's built to do, without thinking about its circuitry. The intentional stance is the most powerful of the three: instead of tracing mechanism, you treat the system as a rational agent with beliefs and desires and ask what such an agent would do. This is how we predict a chess program's next move without inspecting its code: we assume it 'wants' to win and will make whatever move a rational player who wanted to win would make.

A language model counts as believing Paris is the capital of France exactly when the intentional stance predicts its outputs better than a stance that doesn't invoke belief at all: if this attribution lets us correctly anticipate not just that answer but a whole family of related outputs (about French geography, the Eiffel Tower, what language is spoken in the capital, and so on), then, on Dennett's view, the pattern is real in the same sense a center of gravity is real, even though nothing in the model's weights is literally a sentence saying 'Paris is the capital of France.' Since Dennett's whole point in 'Real Patterns' is that these patterns are objectively there and not just projected onto the system by us, his answer to whether this is 'mere fiction' is no: it tracks something genuine, not just a convenient story. And since the pattern is exactly as real as the pattern of beliefs we attribute to other people, it seems fair to say the model has its own primitive way of experiencing that belief, even if we have no idea what, if anything, that experience is like from the inside."
