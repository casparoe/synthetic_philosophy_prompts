For the last couple of months I've been turning over something that came up at a Bible study I attend, when the leader described Jesus's death as "penal substitution" and moved on as though the phrase settled the matter. It didn't settle it for me. I wanted to know whether the underlying idea — that punishing an innocent, willing substitute can somehow satisfy the debt owed by the guilty party — actually holds together as a piece of reasoning, independently of whether you accept it on faith. Looking around online for anyone who had tried to pick the idea apart logically rather than theologically, I kept seeing the same short paper cited: "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" by a philosopher named David Lewis. (I'll admit I spent a slightly embarrassing amount of time assuming this was the C. S. Lewis of Narnia fame, before realizing it's an entirely different, later Lewis, who is apparently much better known for work on possible worlds and decision theory than for anything to do with theology.)

I found a copy of the paper, and on the whole I can follow Lewis's reasoning, but there is one passage near the start that I have read several times without managing to pin down exactly what move he is making. It reads:

"We can say, if we like, that the offender 'owes a debt of punishment'. But the metaphor is misleading. As we mostly conceive of them, the condition of owing a debt and the condition of deserving to be punished are not alike. In the case of debt, what is required is that the creditor shall not suffer a loss of the money he lent; what happens to the debtor is beside the point. Whereas in the case of a 'debt of punishment', what is required is that the debtor shall suffer a loss; there is no creditor. (Society? - Not really. The creditor is supposed to be the one who suffers a loss if the debt is not paid. But sometimes, what with the cost of prisons, society will suffer more of a loss if the debt is paid.)"

What I can't work out is the parenthetical remark about society. Lewis seems to be testing whether "society" could stand in as the creditor to whom the offender's debt is owed, and rejecting the suggestion on the grounds that the cost of imprisonment sometimes makes society worse off, not better off, precisely when the debt gets "paid" through punishment. I don't see why that particular fact about prison costs is what disqualifies society from being the creditor, rather than just being an unrelated gripe about the expense of the justice system. Could you explain exactly what test Lewis is applying to decide whether something counts as a "creditor" in this sense, why the point about prison costs is supposed to fail that test, and how this whole debtor/creditor comparison is meant to prepare the ground for the rest of his argument about whether penal substitution really makes sense?
