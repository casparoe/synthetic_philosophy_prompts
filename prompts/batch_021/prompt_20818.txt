I sit on the grants committee of a small EA-adjacent foundation a few of us set up a while back—basically a shoestring Open Phil, funding stuff across global health, animal welfare, and a handful of longshot biosecurity/AI safety grants. We're rewriting the internal guidance our evaluators use to compare grants whose payoffs land on totally different timescales (bednets that pay off in a year or two vs. biosecurity research that might not pay off for decades, if it pays off at all). Our quant lead wants to bake in a flat ~1.5%/year "pure time preference" term, on top of the seperate discounts we already apply for growth/diminishing marginal utility and for the probability that a given intervention's beneficiaries never materialize. His reasoning is basically: nobody, including us, actually treats the year-2200 future as counting the same as next year, so we should stop pretending otherwise in our models and just put an honest number on it. Two other committee members are pushing back hard with the Sidgwick/Ramsey line that there's no rational basis for discounting a benefit just because of when it occurs, and that writing pure time preference into policy just enshrines the exact scope-insensitive bias we're supposed to be correcting for.

Can you draft the memo I'll send round before the quarterly meeting? I need it to actually recommend something — whether the pure-time-preference term stays, goes, or gets swapped for something else — not just "reasonable people disagree." It should keep the three components (pure time preference, growth/diminishing-marginal-utility, and existence/probability discounting) clearly distinct rather than letting them blur into one number the way half our old grant writeups do, and it needs to take seriously the strongest version of the quant lead's worry, which I don't think is really about impatience — it's more that a hard zero on pure time preference, combined with how we already handle cluelessness and tiny probabilities of astronomical payoffs, quietly commits us to something like permanent patient-philanthropy-style deferral, or to whichever grant proposal claims the largest slice of the far future. This whole fight feels like a rerun of the Stern-vs-Nordhaus argument over climate discount rates, except now it's playing out over our AI safety grants instead of carbon.
