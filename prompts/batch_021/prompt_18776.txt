Three weeks ago I fed a piece of soft evidence into a diagnostic Bayesian network at work using Judea Pearl's virtual-evidence method, and the marginal probability on an unrelated node moved in a direction none of us could intuitively justify. A colleague with philosophical training recognized the pattern immediately and pointed me to van Fraassen's Judy Benjamin problem, and I've spent most evenings since working through the probability-kinematics literature it spawned. I now host a small interview podcast for practitioners -- data scientists, actuaries, quantitative analysts -- on the philosophical foundations of the probabilistic tools we use daily, and next week's guest is a formal epistemologist whose research program defends minimum-cross-entropy updating as the uniquely rational way to handle conditional testimony of exactly the kind Judy receives about Red Territory, a position she holds squarely against van Fraassen's own verdict that the example refutes any rule of that kind. Draft eight to ten interview questions that don't just let her re-derive the standard solution: I want at least one question on why an entropy-minimizing update is entitled to move her credence in Red Territory itself when the testimony seems to bear only on the breakdown inside it, one on the objection that her answer depends on which partition of possibilities she treats as basic, and one on whether her rule is meant to fix, or merely explain away, the order-dependence practitioners see when feeding in several pieces of soft evidence in sequence. For each, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, and close with a question asking what finding -- philosophical or empirical -- would actually change her mind.
