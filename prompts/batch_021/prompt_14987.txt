I just got appointed to my state's Gaming Control Board a couple months ago (mostly because of my accounting background, not because I know anything about gambling law), and my very first real case has turned into a genuine headache. A vendor wants us to license a new electronic table game called "Pot Doubler" for the state's casinos: you place a $5 bet, the terminal starts a pot at $1, and every time the flip comes up heads the pot doubles and it flips again; the instant it comes up tails, the game ends and pays out whatever the pot has grown to. Our staff actuary took one look at the filing and said, half-laughing, "you know this is just the St. Petersburg paradox, right?" — apparently a 300 year old puzzle about a game whose expected payout is technically infinite even though almost everyone who plays it walks away with next to nothing. The vendor's application leans hard into this, wanting to advertise "uncapped, mathematically unlimited jackpot potential," while quietly asking us to let them cap the real payout at some fixed maximum "for solvency reasons" without adjusting the $5 price down to match.

The board is now split in a way thats stalling everything. About half of us think there's nothing to see here: the odds and payout structure are fully disclosed on the machine, nobody's being lied to about the mechanics, and refusing to license an otherwise legal game just because its expected-value math feels paradoxical is us substituting our own philosophical discomfort for actual consumer protection. The other half think advertising "unlimited" expected value while quietly capping the real payout, and pricing the bet as if that uncapped number means something, is deceptive in a pretty specific way — it leans on a number that was never a sensible guide to what anyone should pay in the first place. I have to circulate a short decision memo before we reconvene, and I don't want it to just be "we have the authority to approve or deny this, full stop" — I want it to actually take a side (approve, reject, or approve with specific conditions on pricing, the cap, or the advertising language) and back it up with reasoning solid enough that either the vendor's lawyers or my own dissenting colleagues could actually push back on it. Can you draft that memo for me?
