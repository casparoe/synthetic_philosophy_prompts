I'm currently taking a seminar on nineteenth-century philosophy, and we just spent two weeks on Kierkegaard's polemics against "the public" and the press — mainly the closing section of *Two Ages: A Literary Review* (the part often excerpted in English as "The Present Age"), together with some background on the Corsair affair, in which he was mercilessly caricatured for months in a Copenhagen satirical weekly, in a conflict he had more or less provoked himself. What struck me most was his account of "leveling": the idea that the press manufactures a single, homogeneous abstraction called "the public" — something with no meeting place, no concrete existence, and no one who can ever be held accountable — which nonetheless exerts a tyrannical pressure that flattens individual distinction and substitutes anonymous chatter for genuine commitment or action.

My question is about how well that diagnosis actually survives contact with today's media environment, rather than the more obvious "Kierkegaard basically predicted the internet" observation my classmates kept making in discussion. Two features of contemporary social media seem to cut against his original picture rather than simply confirming it. First, algorithmic personalization means there is no longer one undifferentiated "public" being leveled, but countless narrow, mutually invisible feeds and echo chambers — each perhaps homogenizing internally, but not obviously flattening everyone into the same abstraction the way a shared newspaper-reading public would. Second, and maybe more importantly, Kierkegaard's public was made up of readers and spectators of a press written by a handful of named journalists, whereas on social media almost everyone is simultaneously a spectator and a publisher, actively feeding the very mechanism that levels them. Given those two disanalogies, would Kierkegaard most likely treat today's landscape as leveling in an even more advanced and insidious form, as something categorically different that would force him to revise the concept, or as a development that makes his proposed remedy — inwardness, indirect communication, the demand that the single individual answer before God rather than before "the public" — even harder to formulate than it already was? I'd be genuinely interested in how you think he would size up this specific mismatch, rather than a general verdict on whether he'd have approved of social media.
