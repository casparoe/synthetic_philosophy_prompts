As a journalist who writes about biology and evolution, I host a podcast on the philosophy of science, and next week's guest is a philosopher of biology who defends the etiological, or "selected-effects," account of biological function -- the view that what makes pumping blood the heart's function, rather than just something it happens to do, is that hearts were selected by natural selection because they pumped blood. Draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having her restate the view: I want at least one question each on the Swampman-style worry that her account denies any function at all to an organism's molecule-for-molecule duplicate that pops into existence by sheer cosmic accident and so has no selection history of its own, on the puzzle of a brand-new trait that seems to help the very first individual who has it before natural selection has had any chance to act on it, and on a case like feathers, plausibly selected at first for insulation long before they were ever any good for flight, which raises the question of whether current usefulness or selection history really fixes what a trait is for -- and for each, a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. Close with a question about what kind of finding, if any, would count as evidence against her account and in favor of a rival like Cummins' causal-role view, on which a trait's function is just whatever it currently contributes to some capacity of the organism.
