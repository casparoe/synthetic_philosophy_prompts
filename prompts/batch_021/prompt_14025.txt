I spent thirty-two years as an experimental condensed-matter physicist before retiring, and over the past year I have drifted into philosophy of science, beginning with Eugene Wigner's old essay on the "unreasonable effectiveness" of mathematics, which I had not reread since graduate school. That rereading turned into a small monthly podcast I now record out of my study, in which I mostly talk to people willing to indulge a retired scientist's newfound philosophical curiosity. Next month's guest is a philosopher of physics who has just published a paper arguing that Wigner's puzzle largely dissolves once you correct for selection bias: physicists remember the mathematics that happened to fit nature and quietly forget the much larger pile that did not, so what looks like an uncanny match is really only what she calls a "streetlight sample" of successes.

Could you help me draft nine or ten interview questions that do not simply invite her to restate that thesis? I would like at least one question that presses her on cases where the relevant mathematics — non-Euclidean geometry, say, or the group theory later used to classify gauge symmetries — was worked out for its own sake decades before anyone knew physics would need it, so that it is unclear what exactly was being "selected for" at the time it was developed; one on Wigner's related observation that a piece of mathematics, once adopted for comparatively modest reasons (his own example was Newtonian gravitation, fitted to a fairly meager set of planetary data), often goes on to license correct predictions far beyond anything that motivated its original adoption, which looks like a different phenomenon from merely forgetting failures after the fact; one asking her to reconcile the selection-bias story with episodes like Dirac's equation, where a physicist was guided by mathematical elegance well ahead of any confirming evidence, and the elegant choice turned out to be the right one; and a final question asking her to specify, concretely, what a genuine and non-deflatable instance of "unreasonable" effectiveness would look like, if none of Wigner's own examples are allowed to count. For each question, please add a short follow-up anticipating the likeliest way she will try to sidestep it.
