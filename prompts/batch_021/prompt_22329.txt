I'm taking an undergraduate seminar this semester on seventeenth-century philosophy of science, and this week's unit is devoted to Hobbes's rather idiosyncratic views on the certainty of geometry, partly as groundwork for next week's session on his notorious dispute with John Wallis over squaring the circle. While preparing for class, I came across a short passage from the Epistle Dedicatory of Hobbes's *Six Lessons to the Professors of the Mathematiques* (1656) — English Works, vol. VII, p. 184, in the edition on reserve at our library — that our instructor flagged as central to Hobbes's whole philosophy of science, but which I have not been able to work through fully on my own:

"Of arts, some are demonstrable, others indemonstrable; and demonstrable are those the construction of the subject whereof is in the power of the artist himself, who, in his demonstration, does no more but deduce the consequences of his own operation. The reason whereof is this, that the science of every subject is derived from a precognition of the causes, generation, and construction of the same; and consequently where the causes are known, there is place for demonstration, but not where the causes are to seek for. Geometry therefore is demonstrable, for the lines and figures from which we reason are drawn and described by ourselves; and civil philosophy is demonstrable, because we make the commonwealth ourselves. But because of natural bodies we know not the construction, but seek it from the effects, there lies no demonstration of what the causes be we seek for, but only of what they may be."

I follow the general gist: Hobbes thinks geometry and civil philosophy are uniquely certain because we ourselves construct their objects, whereas natural philosophy can only reason back from observed effects to hidden causes. What I am struggling to reconstruct is the underlying argument for why authorship of a construction should confer this kind of certainty on our reasoning about it. Could you walk me through this more carefully: what exactly does Hobbes mean by calling something "demonstrable" here, and how does that notion connect to his broader account, developed elsewhere, of reasoning as a kind of computation with names and definitions? I would also like to know whether the analogy between drawing a geometrical figure and "making" the commonwealth is doing real philosophical work for him, or whether it is mainly a rhetorical move that lets him defend his (as it turned out, mistaken) geometrical results and his political theory in one stroke. Finally, is this "maker's knowledge" idea distinctively Hobbesian, or was he drawing on some existing current of thought that, as my professor mentioned in passing, resurfaces later in Vico's verum factum principle?
