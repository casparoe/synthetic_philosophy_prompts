As a science and ideas journalist currently researching Isaac Newton's private theological writings, I keep returning to one episode that reveals more about the man than any summary of his "secret Arianism" could. Convinced that the doctrine of the Trinity was a corruption that had entered Christianity only in the fourth century, Newton spent years building a textual-critical case that two of its favorite proof-texts, 1 John 5:7 and 1 Timothy 3:16, were not authentic scripture but later interpolations. In 1690 he sent the finished argument, An Historical Account of Two Notable Corruptions of Scripture, to John Locke, intending anonymous publication on the Continent — then, at the last moment, asked for it to be suppressed. I'd like a short story, roughly 900 to 1,200 words, that stays close inside Newton's reasoning during the days he weighs whether to let the manuscript go to print, dramatizing the collision between his confidence as a textual critic and the concrete risk facing a Cambridge don who, for the previous fifteen years, had been excused from ordination and its doctrinal oath precisely so that his private heterodoxy would never have to be tested. (His own successor in the Lucasian chair, William Whiston, would not be so fortunate twenty years later.) Please avoid a generic "great man conceals his true self" arc: root the drama in the actual substance of Newton's argument, not in unspecified "heresy."
