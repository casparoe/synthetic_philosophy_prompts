I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for long enough to have absorbed one of their near-axiomatic commitments: that you should never prefer a comfortable illusion to an uncomfortable truth. The Litany of Tarski is the cleanest statement of this, and Yudkowsky's old post "Doublethink (Choosing to Be Biased)" goes further, explicitly raising the possibility that self-deception might make people happier and rejecting it anyway — the recommended policy is to want your beliefs to track reality regardless of how that makes you feel, on the grounds that an illusion you shield from scrutiny today is a car crash, a bad bet, or a bad relationship you didn't see coming tomorrow.

This came up recently when a friend who's into eighteenth-century French philosophy lent me Émilie du Châtelet's "Discourse on Happiness," and I was struck by how directly her actual argument cuts against that rationalist commitment — more directly, and more carefully, than the usual "some people just prefer comfortable lies" objection. She names five things a person needs in order to be happy: freedom from prejudice, virtue, health, tastes and passions, and, as a distinct fifth item, susceptibility to illusion, since she thinks illusion is responsible for most of what we enjoy in life, and that losing the capacity for it leaves us worse off, not better. Crucially, on her account illusions aren't beliefs you adopt at will the way you might adopt a policy; they're byproducts of passion that you can protect or fail to protect, but never manufacture from nothing. Nor is she indiscriminate: she rejects ambition because it makes your happiness hostage to other people's opinions, while she's comparatively fond of glory and the love of study partly because illusion lets you enjoy the feeling of having accomplished something without needing anyone else to actually confirm it. She even lets reason intervene sometimes — her example is the gambler whose passion is sustained by the illusion that luck favors him, which she treats as exactly the sort of self-deception that reason needs to keep in check.

Taking that whole, qualified picture seriously rather than a flattened one, how do you think du Châtelet would judge the rationalist community's blanket rule against ever preferring a comforting illusion to the truth? Would she read it as simply generalizing the rational check she already grants — reason stepping in whenever illusion turns self-destructive — and so more or less endorse it, or would she say the rationalists have mistaken a narrow emergency brake for a general policy, and are needlessly demolishing the ordinary, harmless illusions that most everyday pleasure runs on? And separately: do you think she'd predict that a culture built around this norm quietly readmits illusion under some other name, or would she bite the bullet and say a community that actually lived up to the ideal should expect, on her own hedonistic terms, to end up less happy than one that let people keep their harmless self-flattery intact?
