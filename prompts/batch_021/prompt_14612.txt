I'm a PhD student working on classical Indian philosophy of mind, and I keep returning to a minority position in Abhidharma scholasticism that most survey treatments of anattā skip over: the Pudgalavādins, who broke with rival schools by insisting that some minimal "person" (pudgala) must be admitted — one that is neither identical to the five aggregates nor separate from them — in order to make sense of memory, moral responsibility, and rebirth without collapsing into either a Brahminical self or a bare, unconnected series of momentary events. Their opponents pressed a dilemma I find genuinely hard to dissolve: if the pudgala can be located within the aggregates it does no explanatory work, and if it can't, it is indistinguishable from the ātman the Buddha explicitly rejected. Rather than another summary of the arguments, I'd like you to write a short story set in a monastery in classical India that dramatizes this controversy through concrete personal stakes — a disputation held at a dying elder's bedside, or a monk's private doubt on the eve of a public debate — rather than through dialogue that just restates the positions. Let the philosophical tension emerge from what the characters stand to lose, not from exposition.
