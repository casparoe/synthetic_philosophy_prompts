Illusionism about phenomenal consciousness holds that phenomenal consciousness -- the intrinsic, ineffable, subjectively "felt" quality that experiences are ordinarily supposed to have over and above their causal-functional role -- does not exist, and that what actually needs explaining is only why creatures like us are introspectively disposed to represent our experiences as having such properties. Keith Frankish has given the view its name and its most systematic defense; Daniel Dennett is usually read as an ally, if not always under that label. I've been sitting in on a philosophy of mind reading group where this came up, and the discussion kept returning to what several people took to be the view's central weakness: even granting that phenomenal properties are illusory, doesn't it still seem some way to the subject for the illusion to occur? If there is something it is like to undergo the introspective misrepresentation, then consciousness hasn't been explained away, only relocated into the "seeming" itself; and if there is truly nothing it is like, it becomes mysterious why anyone ever found consciousness puzzling in the first place, since a mere false belief about one's own states doesn't obviously generate the appearance of ineffable qualitative character. Illusionists try to escape this dilemma by distinguishing a merely functional, representational sense of "seeming" from the phenomenal sense, and insisting that only the latter is illusory; critics reply that this distinction is asserted rather than earned, and amounts to changing the subject mid-argument.

Write an essay that adjudicates this specific dispute rather than the illusionism debate in general terms. Reconstruct the strongest version of the self-refutation objection and the strongest illusionist reply to it, engage with whether a principled (non-question-begging) functional or representational notion of "seeming" is actually available to do the work illusionists need it to do, and consider what is gained or lost by recasting the whole dispute in terms of David Chalmers's "meta-problem of consciousness" -- the project of explaining, in purely functional terms, why we judge and report that consciousness is puzzling, whether or not it actually is. The essay should not conclude merely that reasonable people disagree; it should state and defend a specific verdict on whether illusionism, in its most defensible form, survives the seeming-based objection, is refuted by it, or turns out on inspection to be a notational variant of the very realism about consciousness it was meant to replace.
