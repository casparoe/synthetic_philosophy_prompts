A few of us did an internal hackathon a while back where we built little bots to compete in a simplified sealed-bid auction game, and that sent me down a rabbit hole reading about game theory on nights and weekends (very glamorous, I know). I got to "iterated elimination of dominated strategies" and "rationalizability," and pretty much every intro blog post or youtube explainer treats them like two names for the same underlying idea -- roughly, "the strategies a rational player, who knows the other player is rational, who knows they know, and so on, would never touch." Then I found some denser lecture notes (a PDF, no real context, just floating around) that mentioned almost in passing that the two procedures are only guaranteed to pick out the exact same set of strategies when there's exactly two players, and that once you've got three or more, elimination can leave in strategies that technically aren't rationalizable. It just namedropped Bernheim and Pearce and moved on to the next section, which is not super helpful for someone without the background.

Could you actually unpack why that gap opens up once you go from two players to three or more? My half-baked guess is it's got something to do with whether a player is allowed to believe the other two players' choices are correlated with each other versus having to treat them as independent, but I don't get why that would matter to what feels like a purely mechanical "cross out anything dominated, repeat" rule. One related thing that tripped me up earlier, and that might actually be connected: when you check whether a strategy is "dominated," does it only count if some pure strategy beats it everywhere, or do you also have to check mixes of strategies (I definately ran into an example where a strategy survived against every pure alternative on its own but still lost to a 50/50 blend of two of them)? Is that pure-vs-mixed thing basically the same underlying wrinkle as the independence-vs-correlation one, or are they two separate ways this topic bites people? A minimal sketch of a three-player example where a strategy survives elimination but fails to be rationalizable would help it click, if you can construct one -- or tell me where I've mangled the lecture notes if that's not actually right.

And then zooming out to the more philosophy-brain question: these are two different formalizations of the same intuitive idea, "what a rational agent would never do given everyone's rationality is common knowledge," and they can disagree once there's more than two people at the table. Which one, if either, deserves to be called the correct cash-out of that intuition? Is the disagreement just an artifact of how the elimination procedure happens to be defined, or does it expose something genuinely shaky about what common knowledge of rationality can actually buy you once there's more than one other mind whose beliefs you have to reason about?
