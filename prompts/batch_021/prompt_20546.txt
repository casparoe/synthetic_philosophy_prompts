Over the past year, without any formal training in philosophy, I have been teaching myself about the debates around advanced AI, starting with Nick Bostrom's book Superintelligence. Two claims from that literature have stayed with me: the orthogonality thesis, which holds that how intelligent an agent is tells you almost nothing about what it ultimately wants, since a mind of arbitrarily high intelligence could in principle be built to pursue almost any final goal, however trivial or strange; and instrumental convergence, the related claim that regardless of what an agent's final goal actually is, a sufficiently capable agent will tend to end up pursuing much the same intermediate goals anyway, such as preserving itself, acquiring resources, and resisting attempts to have its objectives altered. Around the same time, for a completely unrelated reason, I picked up a secondhand copy of Hume's Treatise of Human Nature and came across his argument that reason is, in his own words, "the slave of the passions": that reason by itself can only discover facts and work out effective means, and can never hand us an ultimate end, nor condemn a desire as irrational simply for existing — only a false belief about the world, or a mistaken judgment about whether some course of action will actually get us what we want, can make a passion count as unreasonable. The resemblance to Bostrom's two theses struck me immediately, and I cannot shake the suspicion that they are really just Hume's picture of reason and the passions, restated for artificial rather than human minds.

What I would like help working out is whether that suspicion actually survives scrutiny, since I do not trust myself to judge it without more background. My tentative guess, and it is only a guess, is that Hume would have accepted instrumental convergence almost without resistance, because it looks like a direct extension of his claim that reason's whole job is to find effective means to whatever we happen to want, just applied on a larger scale. The full orthogonality thesis seems like a bigger claim, though, since it requires treating intelligence and final goals as completely independent even at the extreme upper end of rationality, and I keep wondering whether other parts of Hume's own system — his distinction between calm and violent passions, his account of sympathy, or the "general point of view" he thinks correct moral judgment requires us to adopt — might have given him grounds to resist that, or at least to qualify it, rather than accept it wholesale. Please do not simply assert that Hume is "obviously" a forerunner of these ideas and leave it there; reconstruct the relevant parts of his actual view in enough detail to show your reasoning, test whether my guess about an asymmetry between the two theses is right, and then commit to a definite answer: on his own terms, would Hume have endorsed both theses exactly as they are stated today, or would he have pushed back on part of the package, and if so, which part, and why?
