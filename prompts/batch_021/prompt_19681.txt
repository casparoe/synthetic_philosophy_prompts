I was covering a public panel on unprovable statements in mathematics -- built around Gödel and Cohen's work on the continuum hypothesis (CH) -- and the sharpest exchange of the night happened afterward, over drinks, between two of the panelists. (1) Nadia, a set theorist, raised the argument known as Freiling's axiom of symmetry: assign each real number x a countable set f(x) of reals; picking two reals independently at random, the chance either lands in the other's assigned set should be zero, so some pair x, y should always escape each other's sets -- x outside f(y), y outside f(x). This principle turns out to be mathematically incompatible with CH, and Nadia said she finds it far more obviously true than CH itself: real evidence, to her, that CH is false rather than simply undecided. (2) Peter, a philosopher of mathematics, objected that "picking a real at random" isn't a well-defined procedure, so the probability talk is a metaphor doing the work an argument should do. (3) Nadia replied that the metaphor is dispensable: stripped of any mention of probability, the claim is just that no single rule can entangle every pair of reals with each other, and that alone still struck her as compelling. (4) Peter countered that under CH, the very function that refutes the principle comes straight from a well-ordering of the reals, and the graph of such a well-ordering is a textbook non-measurable, choice-built object of the sort that has repeatedly defeated naive intuition before (Vitali sets, Banach-Tarski) -- so the principle's "obviousness" is being tested exactly where such intuitions are known to fail. (5) Nadia answered that this cuts both ways: intuitions about choice-built objects have misled us in both directions before, so noting the refuting function's pedigree doesn't tell us whether to distrust her principle or the well-ordering CH itself needs -- Peter owed her an independent reason to blame her axiom in particular, and hadn't given one.
