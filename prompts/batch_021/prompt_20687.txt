For the past few years I've volunteered as a trustee of a small hardship fund that the guild of piano and harpsichord restorers I belong to has run for decades. It's a modest arrangement: members pay a little into a shared pot each month, and if illness stops someone from working, the five trustees vote a weekly payment that gets reviewed every couple of months for as long as it's needed. The fund's rules, written back in the 1970s, say the payment is for a member "genuinely incapacitated by sickness," and until now that phrase never caused any trouble — a broken wrist, a heart attack, chemotherapy all settled the question on their own. This review cycle, though, we have a case that has split the five of us down the middle. A member named Elena, who is in her fifties and restores harpsichord cases, has been unable to do bench work for eight months. She has a settled diagnosis of myalgic encephalomyelitis, sometimes called chronic fatigue syndrome, backed by letters from two doctors describing crushing exhaustion after light activity that confines her to bed for days afterward; neither doctor disputes that she currently cannot work. But there is no scan, blood test, or biomarker underneath the diagnosis — it rests entirely on her reported symptoms and their pattern over time. Two of the trustees argue that "genuinely incapacitated by sickness" was always meant to cover bodies that can be shown to be malfunctioning, not conditions identified solely by what a patient says she can no longer do, however sincerely. The other two point out that the same standard would have refused every migraine and most chronic pain conditions before imaging caught up with them, and that what should matter is how much function a person has lost, not whether medicine currently owns a test that pictures the cause.

I've done a fair amount of reading on my own since this dispute came up, and it turns out this sits on a real fault line in how disease itself gets defined: whether something only counts as an illness once there is a demonstrable, statistically abnormal malfunction inside the body, or whether the harm and loss of function a person actually lives with is what makes it a disease, whatever medicine eventually finds or fails to find behind it. As the trustee asked to write the recommendation the other four will vote on this week, please draft the short memo I'll circulate beforehand. It should take a clear position on whether Elena's payment should continue, at what level, and for how long or until what kind of review, and it should engage seriously with the strongest form of whichever position it doesn't adopt, rather than simply asserting mine.
