I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on formal and social epistemology, and I was recently elected the graduate student representative on my university's Research Integrity Committee, which hears contested allegations of misconduct against graduate students and postdocs. Like most such bodies, ours defines a finding of "responsible" conjunctively: the panel must conclude both (a) that the conduct was a significant departure from accepted practice in the discipline, and (b) that it was committed knowingly, intentionally, or recklessly rather than through honest error. Until now we've simply voted up-or-down on the overall verdict after deliberating together, but a case last month convinced me that this procedure harbors a hidden incoherence. Two of the five members voted "responsible," persuaded on both (a) and (b). Of the three who voted "not responsible," one accepted (a) but not (b), one accepted (b) but not (a), and one rejected both. When I laid these five positions out issue-by-issue afterward, I noticed that a majority had in fact affirmed (a), and a different majority had in fact affirmed (b) — so a vote taken question-by-question, rather than on the verdict as a whole, would have produced the opposite result under our own stated rule. I recognized this from a seminar last year as an instance of what List and Pettit call the discursive dilemma (the lawyers on the committee know a version of it as Kornhauser and Sager's "doctrinal paradox" for multi-member courts), but recognizing the pattern hasn't told me what the committee should actually do about it.

The chair has asked me to write the discussion memo for our retreat next month, where we're meant to adopt a standing voting procedure rather than keep deciding cases ad hoc. Please draft that memo. It should recommend either a premise-based procedure (vote separately on (a) and (b), then derive the verdict from the results), a conclusion-based procedure (vote on the verdict directly, as we've effectively been doing), or some other procedure you can defend, and it should justify the recommendation on its merits rather than by appeal to precedent or committee custom. Above all, don't let the memo quietly bury the strongest objection to whichever procedure you choose: if you recommend premise-based voting, you owe the committee an honest answer to the worry that it can force individual members to vote on, and be bound by, a sub-question they regard as moot given their view of the other premise; if you recommend conclusion-based voting, you owe us an honest answer to the worry that it lets the committee reach verdicts that no majority can actually justify with a shared piece of reasoning.
