The philosophical puzzles buried inside general relativity have never really been settled, and for a long-form piece I'm reporting on this, one thread keeps pulling me back to Einstein's own words rather than the tidy paraphrases the philosophers of physics I've been interviewing keep offering me. The specific puzzle is the "hole argument": because Einstein's field equations hold in any coordinate system whatsoever, you can take a solution, leave everything untouched outside some small matter-free region — the "hole" — and smoothly drag the metric around inside it, producing a second, mathematically distinct solution that agrees with the first everywhere outside the hole, and on its entire causal past, yet disagrees inside it. If spacetime points have an identity of their own, independent of the metric field draped over them, this seems to show that no amount of physical information about the rest of the universe could ever fix what happens at a point — a strange, empirically idle indeterminism that Einstein worried over between 1913 and 1915 before talking himself out of it. In his 1916 review paper he offers what's since become known as the "point-coincidence argument" as the resolution. I tracked the passage down in an English translation I hadn't seen before, done, notably, by a young Satyendra Nath Bose — later famous for Bose–Einstein statistics — for a 1920 Calcutta volume, a few years ahead of the better-known Perrett and Jeffery translation:

"This condition of general covariance which takes away the last remnants of physical objectivity from space and time, is a natural requirement, as seen from the following considerations. All our well-substantiated space-time propositions amount to the determination of space-time coincidences. If, for example, the event consisted in the motion of material points, then, for this last case, nothing else are really observable except the encounters between two or more of these material points. The results of our measurements are nothing else than well-proved theorems about such coincidences of material points, of our measuring rods with other material points, coincidences between the hands of a clock, dial-marks and point-events occurring at the same position and at the same time.

"The introduction of a system of co-ordinates serves no other purpose than an easy description of totality of such coincidences. We fit to the world our space-time variables x1, x2, x3, x4 such that to any and every point-event corresponds a system of values of x1...x4. Two coincident point-events correspond to the same value of the variables x1...x4; i.e., the coincidence is characterised by the equality of the co-ordinates. If we now introduce any four functions x'1, x'2, x'3, x'4 as coordinates, so that there is an unique correspondence between them, the equality of all the four co-ordinates in the new system will still be the expression of the space-time coincidence of two material points. As the purpose of all physical laws is to allow us to remember such coincidences, there is a priori no reason present, to prefer a certain co-ordinate system to another; i.e., we get the condition of general covariance."

I can sense the shape of the move Einstein is making — something like: physical content attaches only to coincidences, never to bare points or coordinate labels, so the two "disagreeing" solutions inside the hole were never disagreeing about anything physical in the first place — but I can't yet reconstruct the actual steps well enough to explain them to readers rather than just gesture at them. Could you walk me through the logic in more detail: how exactly does the premise that all measurement reduces to coincidences get Einstein from "there is no preferred coordinate system" to a dissolution of the indeterminism the hole argument threatens? Underneath the physics, is this basically a relationalist or verificationist move about the identity of spacetime points, and if so, does it sit uneasily with treating the metric field itself as a real, dynamical entity, which is how most physicists now talk about gravity? And in the hands of philosophers of physics like Earman and Norton, is Einstein's own argument generally judged to succeed on its own terms, or does it just relocate the problem it was meant to dissolve?
