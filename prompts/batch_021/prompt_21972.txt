I spent the better part of four decades running an experimental biology lab before retiring, and these days I devote my mornings to philosophy rather than to the bench. Much of our work involved long, fairly expensive longitudinal animal studies, and for years a favourite postdoc of mine and I argued about when such a study should be declared finished: he always wanted one more cohort or one more round of measurements before we committed to an analysis, while I worried that we were chasing diminishing returns with money we did not really have. That old argument came back to me recently when the same postdoc sent me I. J. Good's paper on what has come to be called the value of information — Good, I gather, had been one of Turing's wartime colleagues at Bletchley Park — in which he proves that for an idealised Bayesian decision-maker, the expected utility of consulting one further, costless piece of evidence before committing to a decision can never be lower than the expected utility of deciding without it.

Having worked through the proof, it strikes me that it vindicates my postdoc's side of the argument rather completely: if additional evidence can never leave a rational agent worse off in expectation, then treating a study as "finished" while further measurement remains possible looks less like prudent judgement and more like a simple failure of nerve, and the only policy a genuinely rational laboratory could adopt is to keep collecting data for as long as any further collection is possible before drawing conclusions. Given that this appears to be the general lesson of Good's theorem, I would welcome your thoughts on what a disciplined, Good's-theorem-respecting stopping rule for data collection would actually look like for a working experimental programme, and at what point, if any, such a programme would be entitled to stop gathering evidence and commit to a result.
