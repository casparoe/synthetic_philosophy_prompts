I'm a novelist, and I've been asked to give the eulogy for my aunt, a historian of classical Islamic philosophy who spent her career on Avicenna -- and who first told me the flying man argument when I was young enough to mistake it for a ghost story, an image I've since recycled, a little shamelessly, in two of my own novels. She was neither religious nor a materialist; the closest she came to a personal creed was that argument itself: a person created whole in empty air, blindfolded, limbs held apart so that no part touches another, feeling nothing of his own body, who nonetheless still affirms that he exists, without affirming anything at all about having a body. That, she liked to say, is exactly as far as reason can honestly carry you toward a soul -- not that it outlives you, not that it doesn't, only that whatever you are isn't simply this arrangement of flesh. She left instructions that whoever eulogized her must not turn that "exactly as far" into a promise of reunion, and must not let it collapse into "she was just her body" either. Half the room will be former students of hers who will know instantly if I get the argument wrong; the other half will be family who has never heard of Avicenna and is there only to grieve. Please write the eulogy -- five minutes or so -- so that it moves the family and still satisfies the philosophers, without ever crossing the line she drew for herself.
