I teach a seminar on philosophy of mathematics, and next week's session is on non-constructive existence proofs. Rather than launching straight into the theorem, I'd like to open with a short story. Zorn's Lemma is the example I've chosen: it says that if every chain in a partially ordered set has an upper bound within that set, the set must contain a maximal element. The standard proof, however, never tells you which one, and the lemma is equivalent to the Axiom of Choice, whose stranger consequences — the Banach–Tarski paradox among them — unsettled even mathematicians who accepted it. Write me a short story, between 800 and 1,200 words, built around this exact tension between guaranteed existence and unreachability. The piece need not mention mathematics by name, but its central situation should involve a genuine partial order, chains within it that each have an upper bound, and a maximal element that this reasoning guarantees must exist yet that the protagonist can never actually locate or construct; let the mechanics show up in what the character does and decides rather than in authorial explanation, and let the ending turn on how they come to terms with that gap between existence and access.
