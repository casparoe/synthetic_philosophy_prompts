Question: In his 1955 paper on cardinal welfare and interpersonal comparisons of utility, Harsanyi proves that if the individuals in a society, and "society" itself, are all expected-utility maximizers whose preferences range over one and the same set of lotteries over social outcomes, and if society's ranking of those lotteries respects a Pareto condition (roughly: if every individual weakly prefers lottery p to lottery q, and at least one individual strictly prefers p, then society strictly prefers p), then society's utility function must be an affine combination of the individual utility functions, with nonnegative weights on each individual. (a) Reconstruct the argument for this conclusion, saying what work is being done by the assumption of a common domain of lotteries, by the expected-utility axioms, and by the Pareto condition, respectively. (b) Explain the sense in which this result is, and the sense in which it is not, a vindication of utilitarianism. (c) Present one serious objection to using the theorem as an argument for utilitarian aggregation, and evaluate whether it succeeds.

Student's answer: Harsanyi's aggregation theorem is best understood by way of the thought experiment that motivates it. Imagine you do not yet know which member of the society you are going to turn out to be: you have an equal, 1/n chance of being any one of the n individuals, and behind this veil you must rank the available social lotteries. Since you are, by hypothesis, an expected-utility maximizer, and each individual i's own preferences over lotteries satisfy the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms (completeness, transitivity, continuity and independence) and are represented by a utility function u_i unique up to positive affine transformation, your ranking from behind the veil is forced to be represented by the mathematical expectation of the u_i's, i.e. a weighted sum Σ a_i u_i in which each individual's utility is weighted by the probability of turning out to be them. Harsanyi then adds the Pareto principle — if every individual prefers lottery p to lottery q, so should society — to guarantee that none of the weights a_i can be negative, since a negative weight would let the ranking go against a lottery that every single individual favors.

This is why the theorem is widely presented as an argument for utilitarianism: any observer who is rational in the expected-utility sense and respects the Pareto principle is thereby committed to ranking social lotteries by a sum of individual utilities, which is the utilitarian formula. And because the weights here are fixed by construction to be the equal probabilities 1/n rather than left open, what Harsanyi's proof delivers is not some watered-down "weighted utilitarianism" in which the relative importance of persons is left for further argument, but classical, equal-weight utilitarianism itself.

The theorem's most influential critic, Amartya Sen, objects that this whole argument trades on an equivocation about "utility." The u_i that appear in the proof are von Neumann-Morgenstern utility functions, and these are indices of an individual's attitude to risk, pinned down only up to an arbitrary positive affine transformation for each person separately; nothing in the expected-utility axioms says that a unit of u_1 represents the same amount of well-being as a unit of u_2. So even after we know that the social ranking is representable as a sum of the u_i's, we have not been told how to fix each person's scale relative to the others, and the appearance of a determinate utilitarian ranking is doing more work than the premises can support. Sen's own conclusion is only that Pareto-respecting, rational aggregation must be additively separable across individuals — a conclusion compatible with several non-utilitarian views, including versions of prioritarianism that apply a concave transformation to each u_i before summing.
