the question was: "MIRI's original approach to the shutdown problem tries to make an agent 'indifferent' to whether its shutdown button gets pressed, rather than just penalizing it for disabling the button. Explain (a) why a naive expected-utility-maximizing agent with a shutdown button has an incentive to interfere with it in the first place, (b) how the utility-indifference trick is supposed to remove that incentive, (c) one important respect in which full corrigibility still isn't achieved even once indifference holds, and (d) how Hadfield-Menell et al.'s 'off-switch game' tries to get around the underlying problem with a different mechanism, and whether that mechanism actually delivers a general solution."

and here's the student's answer i need graded:

"(a) An agent maximizing a utility function over outcomes will, in nearly every case, get less expected utility from a world where it's switched off than one where it keeps running and pursuing its goal. So by instrumental convergence it has a default incentive to resist shutdown -- disabling the button, deceiving the operators, etc -- for pretty much any goal you give it.

(b) The fix is to split the utility function into a 'normal' branch U_N (used if the button isn't pressed) and a 'shutdown' branch U_S (used if it is), plus a correction term thats tuned so the agent's expected utility comes out the same either way. This framework was introduced in Soares, Fallenstein, Yudkowsky and Armstrong's 2015 corrigibility paper, and the point is that once the two branches are equalized like that, the agent has no expected-value reason to push probability mass toward or away from the button being pressed.

(c) Even once indifference is achieved, the agent still has no positive reason to keep the button in working order or flag problems with it to the operators -- indifference just cancels out its preference between the two branches, it doesn't give it any incentive to actively protect the shutdown mechanism. So indifference gets you 'won't fight you,' not 'will help you.'

(d) The off-switch game swaps indifference for uncertainty: the robot isn't sure the action it's about to take is actually good, and treats the human going for the switch as evidence about that. Hadfield-Menell et al. show that as long as the robot has this kind of uncertainty about its own correctness, it will always rationally let itself be switched off, which gets you corrigible shutdown behavior for free without needing to hand-design an indifference term at all."
