I'm a retired geophysicist, and since I stopped working a couple years back I've fallen down a philosophy rabbit hole -- no formal training, just reading on my own and pestering my more patient friends. Lately I've been stuck on something that grew out of a popular history I read about how plate techtonics went from Wegener's laughed-at "continental drift" idea to the reigning paradigm by the late 1960s. The book's account of why it finally got accepted was basically a narrative: certain people running certain ship surveys, funding patterns shifting after the war, the magnetic striping on the seafloor finally winning over the Cambridge crowd, that sort of thing -- lots of contingent detail about who convinced whom and when, and no general law anywhere in sight. That bugs me, because in my own work "explaining" something always meant deriving it from a general law plus initial conditions. If a historian's story doesn't do that, is it really explaining anything, or just describing it well?

So I went and read a bit of the philosophy of history literature and now I'm more confused, not less. I gather Hempel argued in a famous 1942 paper that historical explanations really are covering-law explanations underneath, just usually incomplete "explanation sketches" where the laws are left unstated because everyone already takes them for granted. But then Dray apparently pushed back hard, arguing that explaining human actions runs on a completely different logic -- something he called "rational explanation," where you explain what someone did by showing it was the sensible thing to do given how they themselves saw the situation, not by fitting them under an empirical regularity. And there's apparently a third idea floating around, "colligation," where historians explain by binding a mass of events together under an organizing theme rather than either a law or a reason. I honestly can't tell if these are three genuine competitors or if a couple of them secretly collapse into the same move.

Could you write me an essay that actually sorts through this properly? Lay out the strongest version of the covering-law case and the strongest version of the narrativist/rational-explanation case (bring in colligation wherever it actually earns its keep), and then land on a real answer instead of a wishy-washy "both have a point." I want it to actually take a side on whether historical explanation -- whether of a scientific revolution like the plate tectonics one or of some ordinary political event -- needs backing from general laws to count as genuine explanation, or whether a narrative can be doing real explanatory work entirely on its own terms. A hedged verdict is fine (something like "laws are doing the real work for X kind of case but not Y") so long as you actually argue for it rather than just listing considerations on both sides.
