I'm putting together next week's session for my grad seminar on decision theory and I keep getting stuck on how to present the money-pump literature without it collapsing into "intransitive preferences bad, transitive preferences good, QED." Once you run through the classic pump -- cyclic preferences A>B>C>A, and a shrewd bookie trades you round the circle, taking a penny at each step, until you're back where you started but poorer -- the standard next move is to bring in McClennen's resolute chooser, who just commits to a plan at the outset and sticks with it at every later node, temptation or no, or else the sophisticated chooser, who reasons backward through the whole decision tree and picks accordingly. Either way you get an agent who dodges the exploitation without ever actually revising the underlying cyclic (or incomplete) preference, and my students always split down the middle on whether that's a genuine escape hatch or just relabeling the problem: if transitivity is supposed to be a constraint on a single preference ordering, can facts about how you handle a whole sequence of choices over time really let you off the hook, or does the pump just show the synchronic and diachronic levels have to answer to each other?

Could you write me a dialogue, maybe 700-900 words, between two decision theorists hashing this out, ideally stood at a whiteboard with the pump half-drawn between them. One should be a die-hard transitivity purist who thinks the money pump is close to a knock-down proof that rational preference has to be transitive (and complete, if you want to bring that version in too), and that resolute or sophisticated choice are really just changing the subject from preference to willpower. The other should be a genuine defender of resolute (or sophisticated, your pick) choice, who thinks the pump only condemns naive, myopic agents, not agents with cyclic or incomplete preferences who choose well over time. I want real engagement rather than talking past each other -- so have the purist press on whether anything actually binds the resolute chooser at the later node besides sheer stipulation, and have the other side press back on whether "rational preference must be transitive" is doing any independent work here or is just smuggled in from the conclusion the purist wants anyway. Dont feel like you need to wrap it up with some tidy resolution at the end -- I'd rather each side land at least one hit the other actually has to take seriously.
