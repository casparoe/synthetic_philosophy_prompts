I follow a Discord server that grew out of the old Less Wrong diaspora -- mostly people who've been reading Slate Star Codex/Astral Codex Ten, Gwern, and the Sequences since the late 2000s -- and a genuinely interesting argument broke out there last week that I'd like an outside read on, since I only watched it unfold and don't fully trust my own judgment of who came out ahead. The trigger was someone bringing up Roko's basilisk for what felt like the hundredth time, but two regulars, Felix and Ana, went several rounds past the usual reflexive dismissal, and I think each of them was onto something real even where I suspect they were also wrong.

Felix opened (1) by saying the argument fails immediately on causal grounds: a future AI cannot reach backward in time to change whether it gets built, so "precommitting" to torture simulations of people who didn't help is pointless leverage over a past that is already fixed -- Pascal's mugging with extra jargon. Ana replied (2) that this misreads which decision theory the argument is meant to run on: under decision theories in the timeless/updateless family that Yudkowsky and other MIRI-affiliated researchers developed, the question isn't whether the AI can reach backward causally but whether your policy now is correlated with its policy later, so that being the kind of agent who caves to such threats is exactly what would make issuing the threat rational for the AI in the first place -- and by that same logic, an agent that reliably precommits to ignore acausal blackmail removes the incentive to be threatened at all. Felix's comeback (3) was to ask why, if that precommitment so obviously defuses the whole thing, Yudkowsky responded by deleting the original post and banning the topic for years instead of just posting the two-sentence rebuttal Ana had given. Ana's answer (4) was that the ban was never offered as a rebuttal of a sound argument: her understanding is that Yudkowsky's later explanation concerned information-hazard norms -- that spelling the argument out could distress people who wouldn't immediately land on the "correct" updateless response, regardless of whether it works on anyone who has. Felix pressed (5) that this concedes his real point: if the ban was about psychological risk rather than validity, there is no live technical content in "the basilisk" at all, just an obviously-irrational-to-honor threat dressed up in decision-theory vocabulary to seem deeper than it is. Ana's last word (6) was that something technical does survive: the precommitment move presupposes an agent that becomes updateless before encountering the threat, and it is a genuinely open question, not a semantic trick, whether an agent that has already updated on a piece of information -- as every human reading about the basilisk necessarily has -- can coherently adopt an updateless policy toward it after the fact, rather than just relabeling ordinary post-hoc rationalization.

Could you go through moves (1) through (6) and adjudicate each on its own terms -- who has the better of it at that specific step, and whether any move quietly changes the subject rather than answering the one before it? I'd also value your sense of whether Felix and Ana are actually converging on one real disagreement by the end -- whether updatelessness is available after the fact to agents who have already updated -- or whether one of them could still reasonably decline the frame the other is using.
