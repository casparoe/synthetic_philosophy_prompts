I write about science and ideas for a living, which is how I first came to know Professor Eleanor Voss: eight years ago I profiled her for a magazine feature on philosophers who still defend theodicy in earnest, and what began as an interview became a friendship that lasted until she died of cancer last month. She was one of the few people I have met who argued for Leibniz's "best of all possible worlds" thesis without a trace of irony, and in our later conversations she was almost as insistent on the difference between "the Leibniz of the seminar room" and "the Leibniz of Voltaire's Pangloss" as she was about her own prognosis. Her family has asked me to give the eulogy, partly, I think, because I am not a philosopher and will not turn it into a lecture -- but I do not want to repay that trust by flattening what she actually believed into comfortable platitudes.

The difficulty is that most of the room will know "best of all possible worlds" only as the punchline of Candide: a fool insisting all is well while the world burns around him. A handful of her former colleagues, meanwhile, will notice immediately if I get the actual position wrong -- the claim that God selects among whole bundles of jointly compossible things rather than maximizing each good in isolation, the distinction between what God antecedently wills and what he only consequently permits, and the three-part taxonomy of metaphysical, physical, and moral evil around which the Théodicée is organized. Eleanor's last months involved real, undiminished pain, and she never once suggested that the theory required her to call that pain good in itself, only that it belonged to the best possible whole. Could you write the eulogy I should give: one that puts Leibniz's actual argument to work rather than a softened, Panglossian stand-in for it, so that it says something both technically honest and genuinely consoling to a room that is only half philosophically literate?
