I've been trying to work through the identity of indiscernibles on my own, starting from Max Black's thought experiment about a universe containing nothing but two perfectly symmetric iron spheres, and then tracing the underlying principle back to Leibniz, who is usually credited with formulating it. In his fourth letter to Samuel Clarke, Leibniz writes:

"Tis a thing indifferent, to place three Bodies, equal and perfectly alike, in any order whatsoever; and consequently they will never be placed in Any order, by Him who does nothing without Wisdom. But then, He being the Author of things, no such things will be produced by him at all; and consequently there are no such things in Nature."

I can follow the individual sentences well enough, but I haven't been able to reconstruct the reasoning as a single line of argument to my own satisfaction. Why should its being "indifferent" which order the three bodies are placed in mean that none of the orderings ever gets chosen, rather than one of them simply being chosen arbitrarily? And how does that license the much stronger conclusion that indistinguishable bodies cannot exist in nature at all, rather than just that we could never tell how they were arranged? It would also help to know whether it matters that Leibniz's case involves three interchangeable bodies while Black's later example uses only two. I would like to understand the argument well enough to see exactly which step Black's spheres are meant to undercut. Could you walk me through it?
