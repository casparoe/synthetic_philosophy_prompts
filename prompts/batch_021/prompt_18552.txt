I'm finishing a novel told in five parallel strands, each following a different life that my protagonist, Nadia, might have led after the same afternoon in 1994 when she either boards a train to Lisbon or doesn't. Early readers keep asking a question I'd like to address head-on in an author's note: are the five Nadias really the same woman living out different possibilities, or are they five distinct-but-similar women who merely share a childhood? I've been trying to ground my answer in the transworld identity/counterpart theory debate from modal metaphysics, and I've drafted the reasoning below. Before I commit to it in print, I'd value your comments.

Here is my current understanding. On David Lewis's counterpart theory, no concrete individual exists in more than one possible world; to say Nadia could have stayed in Lisbon is really to say that some other-worldly individual similar enough to her in the relevant respects — a "counterpart," related to her only by resemblance, not identity — stayed in Lisbon. On the rival view I've been attributing to Kripke, there is no such substitution: it is the very same particular, Nadia herself, who exists across the different worlds, and no further analysis of "sameness" in terms of qualitative similarity is needed or wanted. I've been telling early readers my five strands work on this second, Kripkean model: one woman, several histories.

But I keep running up against Kripke's own objection to Lewis — the one built around Hubert Humphrey and the 1968 election — which I understand as the claim that a counterpart's fortunes can't be what we're tracking when we ask what might have happened to a particular person, since presumably that person has no special stake in a merely similar stranger's fate. This seems to support the view I want (that my strands must be strictly the same Nadia to carry any emotional stakes at all), but it also feels too quick: if the objection simply defeats counterpart theory, I don't see why philosophers would still be debating the point decades later, and I suspect I'm missing whatever move lets the counterpart theorist absorb it. I've also been reaching for an analogy — that the counterpart relation is something like what holds between a comic-book hero and their various reboots or recastings across continuities, "the same" character only in a loose, family-resemblance sense — but I worry this analogy just imports the ambiguity rather than dissolving it, or isn't even the right kind of case to illustrate the point at all.

Could you look this over and tell me:
1. Whether I have the Lewis/Kripke contrast and the Humphrey objection right, and where exactly I'm oversimplifying.
2. Whether the reboot/recasting analogy actually illuminates the counterpart relation or trades on a different notion of "sameness" entirely.
3. Whether there's a more defensible position in this literature than the stark binary I've set up — and, relatedly, whether my five-strand structure (one shared past, several equally "actual" continuations within the story) is even the right kind of case for this debate, as opposed to something the modal metaphysics of counterparts wasn't built to handle in the first place.
