Two of my classmates, Maya and Dev, got into an argument at lunch that neither of them would drop, and I still haven't been able to settle it myself. Maya collects sneakers, and she had just picked up what resellers call a "1:1" replica of a pair everyone has been hunting for: built, she says, from the same molds and materials as the authentic pair, on the same factory lines, just without the brand's own paperwork attached to that particular production run. Here is how the argument went.

(1) Dev said the replica is worthless as the thing people actually want, whatever it's made of, because it isn't the real shoe: it's a forgery of a design, no different in principle from a forged painting hung next to the original.

(2) Maya answered that this cuts against Dev, not for him: if two objects are identical in every physical respect, down to the factory and the materials, then calling one "authentic" and the other "fake" is just a story about missing paperwork, not a fact about the shoes themselves.

(3) Dev countered that this proves too much, since by that reasoning a flawless forged Vermeer would be worth exactly what the original is once nobody could tell them apart, which is clearly false.

(4) Maya replied that paintings and sneakers aren't analogous that way: a painting is a single, unrepeatable object the artist actually touched, while a sneaker's "original" is already just one of thousands of identical factory copies, so there's no unique object for hers to be a forgery of in the first place.

(5) Dev responded that this misses his point: even among thousands of copies, the ones that count as authentic are exactly the ones that went through the run the brand actually signed off on, and Maya's pair fails that test no matter how many machines or workers it shares with the "real" ones, so what matters was never uniqueness but traceable, sanctioned history.

(6) Maya said this just relocates her original complaint: the only difference between the sanctioned run and the unsanctioned one is a contract between the brand and the factory, which is a legal and economic fact, and it's strange for a fact like that to be what secretly fixes something that feels like it ought to be a fact about the object itself, and about how good it is.

Neither of them gave any ground. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each step, including anything you think both of them are overlooking? Finish with a one-line verdict in exactly this format: \verdict{Maya} or \verdict{Dev}, whichever you think is right on balance.
