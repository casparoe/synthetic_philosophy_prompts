Question 4 (20 points). In the Autobiography, Franklin describes a systematic project he undertook in his twenties "to arrive at moral perfection."
(a) [6 points] Briefly describe the method he used to track his progress from week to week.
(b) [8 points] Explain the reasoning behind the order in which Franklin arranged his list of virtues, and describe the circumstance that led him to expand the list from twelve items to thirteen.
(c) [6 points] Franklin illustrates a certain danger to the whole project with a short anecdote about a man and his axe. Explain the point of that anecdote and how Franklin relates it to his own final assessment of whether the project had been worth undertaking.

Student's answer:
"Franklin's method was to keep a small book with a page for each of his thirteen virtues, each page ruled into a grid of seven columns, one for every day of the week. Every evening he reviewed his conduct and marked a small black spot on the appropriate line for any fault committed that day, giving one week of concentrated attention to a single virtue at a time before moving on to the next, and going through the full cycle of thirteen weeks four times a year. He put Temperance first because he judged that the coolness and clarity of mind it produced would make the vigilance and self-command needed for the other virtues easier to sustain, and arranged the rest so that mastering the earlier ones would make the later ones easier to acquire. Order, by contrast, gave him more trouble than any other virtue on the list, since his unusually good memory had let him get along for years without fixed habits of arrangement, and he admits he never fully mastered it even in old age. Humility was not on the original list at all: Franklin tells us that as he worked his way through the other twelve virtues, he became convinced that pride was the one vice most likely to undo the whole project, since a person can be secretly proud of almost anything, including his own virtue, and so he appended Humility as a thirteenth item specifically to guard against that risk. The axe anecdote concerns a man who asks a smith to grind his new axe bright over its whole face rather than just the edge, and who turns the grindstone himself until the labor tires him out and he decides that he likes a "speckled axe" better than a fully bright one. Franklin offers this story as his own final word on the project: he concludes that the extreme nicety he had demanded of himself was really a kind of foppery in morals, and that, like the man with the axe, a person is generally better off accepting a speckled, imperfect character than grinding endlessly after a perfection he will never reach."
