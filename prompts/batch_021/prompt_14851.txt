I'm a junior in high school, and a few of us who follow AI-safety blogs have been running an informal after-school reading group this term. This week we got stuck on the "coherence arguments" that come up whenever people—Eliezer Yudkowsky especially—argue that any sufficiently capable AI system will end up behaving like an expected-utility maximizer. The version that confused us most is the one aimed at incompleteness specifically, rather than at intransitivity: the claim that even if an agent simply has no preference between two options (as opposed to regarding them as equally good), a bookie can still money-pump it, by offering a slightly "sweetened" version of one of the options and walking the agent around a loop of trades that leaves it strictly poorer than when it started, without it ever having accepted a trade that looked bad in isolation.

The reply I can't quite evaluate is the idea that an agent can escape this by choosing "resolutely": instead of deciding trade by trade, it looks at the whole sequence of offers in advance and simply refuses to walk the loop, even though no single step in that loop is one its incomplete preferences rule out. That sounds like a real fix to me, but then I read the objection that it's actually a trick—that recognizing in advance that a whole loop nets a loss, and refusing it on those grounds, requires comparing entire courses of action against each other, and that a policy of never letting a bookie walk you in a loop ends up behaviorally indistinguishable from just having complete preferences all along. If that's right, "resolute" incompleteness doesn't block the conclusion that any exploitation-proof rational agent—including a future AI system—must act like an expected-utility maximizer; it just relabels it.

Could you write me a dialogue between two decision theorists who run into each other at the coffee break of an AI-alignment workshop and end up arguing about exactly this? One should defend the money-pump argument, holding that resolute choice is either question-begging or an empty gesture; the other should defend resolute choice as a genuine way of preserving rational incompleteness. I'd like them to work the disagreement out using a concrete case—something like choosing between a mountain vacation and a seaside vacation that strike the agent as "on a par" rather than equally good, plus a slightly sweetened version of one of the two—and I'd like both sides argued at full strength, really engaging with each other's strongest points rather than talking past each other. If it comes up naturally, I'd also like them to touch on whether any of this actually bears on whether advanced AI systems are bound to converge on expected-utility maximization, but the main thing I want is a real, substantive back-and-forth rather than a tidy resolution.
