Here is the question, worth 20 points (5 per part):

"Zahavi introduced the handicap principle to explain how signals could remain reliable indicators of quality despite the obvious incentive for low-quality individuals to cheat. (a) Reconstruct the core logic of the handicap principle and explain why Maynard Smith and other early critics regarded it as evolutionarily incoherent. (b) Explain how Grafen's formal treatment addressed this objection, stating as precisely as you can the key condition his model requires for a signaling equilibrium to be both stable and honest. (c) Using Maynard Smith and Harper's taxonomy of signal types, explain why not every reliable signal is a handicap, illustrating with one example each of an index signal and a conventional (non-handicap) signal. (d) Briefly assess whether the peacock's train remains a well-supported empirical instance of the handicap principle in light of subsequent field data."

And here is the student's answer:

"(a) Zahavi (1975) argued that a signal can only be a reliable guide to an individual's underlying quality if it is costly enough that low-quality individuals cannot profitably fake it. Because a genuinely fit individual can absorb a cost that would be ruinous for a less fit one, the cost of the signal itself is what keeps the system honest — cheating is deterred by the signal's own price tag rather than by any policing. Zahavi's favorite example was stotting: a gazelle that pronks conspicuously in front of a stalking cheetah is advertising that it has energy and speed to spare, and a weaker animal that tried to bluff in the same way would be gambling with a predator at exactly the wrong moment. Maynard Smith and other early critics thought this was close to incoherent as evolutionary reasoning: if a display's only function is to be expensive, then a mutant that achieved the same honest signal more cheaply should always have higher fitness than a costly signaler and should sweep through the population, eroding the very cost that was supposed to keep the system honest. On the face of it, selection should relentlessly favor cheaper signals, not more expensive ones, so it wasn't obvious that 'waste' could be stably maintained by natural selection at all.

(b) The objection stood largely unanswered for about fifteen years, until Grafen (1990) modeled signaling as a continuous strategic game, borrowing the mathematical structure of job-market signaling models from economics. He showed that a fully honest, separating equilibrium — where every quality type signals at a different, revealing level — can indeed be evolutionarily stable, which vindicated Zahavi's intuition mathematically rather than merely rhetorically. The condition Grafen needs is that the signal be costly enough, overall, that a low-quality individual attempting to match a higher-quality individual's signal would end up worse off than if it had simply signaled honestly at its own level — in other words, the handicap has to be severe enough that cheating stops paying for anyone but the genuinely top-quality individuals. Because low-quality cheats can never clear that cost floor, no low-cost mutant can invade the honest equilibrium, which is exactly what answers Maynard Smith's worry.

(c) Maynard Smith and Harper's taxonomy makes clear that costly signaling of this kind is only one way to keep a signal honest. An index is a signal whose reliability is guaranteed directly by physics or physiology rather than by strategic cost: the roaring pitch and formant spacing of a red deer stag is fixed by the length of its vocal tract, so a small stag simply cannot produce the acoustic signature of a large one no matter how much it would benefit from doing so. A conventional signal, by contrast, is arbitrary in form and cheap to produce, and is kept honest not by its own cost but by external policing: the size of a sparrow's dark throat badge correlates with its fighting ability not because the badge is expensive to grow, but because other sparrows attack badge-holders whose badges overstate their actual dominance, so cheating is punished rather than self-defeating. Both are reliable signals of quality, but neither is a handicap in Zahavi's sense, since in neither case is honesty maintained by the signaler's own differential cost of production.

(d) The textbook case for the handicap principle is Petrie's finding that peahens preferred peacocks with more elaborate trains, and that train elaborateness predicted offspring viability — a clean example of a costly ornament tracking genuine quality. This has been complicated by Takahashi et al.'s (2008) long-term study of a feral Japanese population, which found that peahens actually preferred males with less elaborate trains, the reverse of Petrie's result. That reversal suggests train elaborateness may not be a stable indicator of quality across populations, and that the peacock's train — despite being the field's go-to illustration — may be a shakier empirical anchor for the handicap principle than it is usually given credit for."
