Me and my friend had this whole argument last night about whether longtermism is actually a new idea, and I brought up Frank Ramsey, which did not help. Two things about him stuck with me from a decision theory seminar: in his essay "Epilogue" he says he refuses to feel humbled by the vastness of the universe -- stars are big, sure, but they can't think or love, so size just isn't the kind of thing that should impress you -- he calls this the "human mood" as opposed to the "cosmic mood" (might be misremembering the exact wording). But then in his savings paper he argues that discounting future generations' welfare just because they come later in time is basically indefensible, which economists now treat as the founding argument for taking the far future seriously. My friend's point was that these two Ramseys contradict each other: one tells you to ignore cosmic scale, the other is basically the ancestor of arguments that we should care enormously about trillions of hypothetical future people and treat existential risk as the only thing that really matters. I said they're reconcilable but couldnt say why on the spot. So: if Ramsey had somehow lived to see modern longtermism and Bostrom-style existential risk arguments, would he have been sympathetic, hostile, or somewhere weirder in between? Actually use what we know of his views to argue it rather than just guessing.
