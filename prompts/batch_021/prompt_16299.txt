For a semester-long elective called Political Philosophy, my class just moved from Mill's *On Liberty* and *Utilitarianism* into a much less famous text: his *Chapters on Socialism*. Our teacher explained that Mill got interested in writing about socialism around 1869 but died in 1873 before he ever finished revising the manuscript, so it was only published posthumously in 1879, prefaced by a note from his stepdaughter, Helen Taylor. That backstory already threw me a little, because the Mill I thought I knew from earlier in the semester is supposed to be the great defender of individual liberty against social control, yet in this text he spends pages weighing the pros and cons of Owenite and Fourierist communism almost as if he might come around to it.

A friend in my class and I got stuck on one paragraph in particular, from the chapter titled "The Difficulties of Socialism," where Mill is summing up his worries about communal living, and we ended up disagreeing about what he's actually concluding by the end of it. Here is the paragraph, as our teacher gave it to us:

"But even the dissensions which might be expected would be a far less evil to the prospects of humanity than a delusive unanimity produced by the prostration of all individual opinions and wishes before the decree of the majority. The obstacles to human progression are always great, and require a concurrence of favorable circumstances to overcome them; but an indispensable condition of their being overcome is, that human nature should have freedom to expand spontaneously in various directions, both in thought and practice; that people should both think for themselves and try experiments for themselves, and should not resign into the hands of rulers, whether acting in the name of a few or of the majority, the business of thinking for them, and of prescribing how they shall act. But in Communist associations private life would be brought in a most unexampled degree within the dominion of public authority, and there would be less scope for the development of individual character and individual preferences than has hitherto existed among the full citizens of any state belonging to the progressive branches of the human family. Already in all societies the compression of individuality by the majority is a great and growing evil; it would probably be much greater under Communism, except so far as it might be in the power of individuals to set bounds to it by selecting to belong to a community of persons like-minded with themselves."

My friend thinks that last sentence basically undoes everything Mill just argued, since it implies individuality would be fine under Communism as long as people got to choose their own commune, while I think he is still saying individuality would end up worse off overall and that the exception only covers a narrow, unusual case. Could you help me work through (1) what exactly Mill means by "the compression of individuality by the majority," since he never quite defines it within this paragraph itself; (2) how much weight that final "except so far as" clause is really supposed to carry, and whether it seriously limits his worry or is just a minor caveat; and (3) how this passage is supposed to relate to the Mill who warns about the "tyranny of the majority" elsewhere in his work? I'm trying to figure out whether this text shows him genuinely wavering on individual liberty, or whether he actually thought liberal individualism and some forms of socialism could be made compatible.
