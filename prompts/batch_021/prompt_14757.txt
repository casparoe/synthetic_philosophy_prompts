I've been mulling something over for a few weeks now, and I'd like a perspective that goes beyond my group chat's default answer of "well, that's capitalism for you." I'm a backend engineer at a Series B startup, about ninety people now. A little under two years ago I built the recommendation engine that's since become the core of our product, and our most recent valuation jump was driven almost entirely by metrics tied to that system. Since then the company has rolled out a project-management tool that auto-generates tickets from the backlog and assigns them to whoever it calculates has "capacity" that sprint. I now rarely see a feature through from idea to shipped product, just my slice of it, and I genuinely couldn't tell you what half the other engineers touching our codebase are working on.

Here's the part I can't untangle. My salary is solid, my options are vesting on schedule, and by any ordinary contractual standard nothing about this is unfair: I signed the offer letter with my eyes open, I could leave tomorrow, and I'm paid at market rate for my role. And yet I keep feeling like the value I helped create is being captured several steps away from where I'm sitting, and like the work itself has been carved into pieces in a way that's made me faster but also strangely indifferent to what we're actually building. When I tried explaining this to a friend who co-founded another startup, he said that if a deal is fair when both sides agree to it, there's nothing further to say. That doesn't sit right with me, but I don't have the background to say exactly what's wrong with it -- I studied computer science, not philosophy or economics, and the one microeconomics elective I took never covered anything like this. Is there an actual argument for why my situation could be unfair even though the contract itself is fair, or am I just describing the ordinary trade-offs of working somewhere that's scaling fast?
