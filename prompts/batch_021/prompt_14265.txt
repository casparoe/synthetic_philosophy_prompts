(1) Jay said that number is basically made up: the crisis happened exactly once, so either nuclear war was going to happen or it wasnt, and there's no real "chance" fact about something that only occurs a single time.

(3) Jay said the difference is you can check a weatherman: take every day he ever said 30% and see if it actually rains close to 30% of those days. There's no pile of other missile crises to grade the historians against, so their number has nothing to answer to.
(4) Mia said the forecaster's 30% was already meaningful the morning he said it, before any of those days happened — it was tracking real fronts and humidity, not his future track record. So the historians' number can be tracking real stuff too, like how many off-ramps existed and how the key guys tended to act under pressure, without needing a drawer full of repeat crises to grade it against later. Not being checkable yet isn't the same as not meaningful.

Every missile and every general's mood was just regular physics grinding forward on one exact track, so the "real" chance of war that week was 0% or 100%, we just didn't know which, and dressing our ignorance up as "33%" makes the past sound open when it wasnt.
(6) Mia said that proves way too much — it means a coin flip from 1962 also "really" had 0% or 100% odds the second it left someone's thumb, but he'd never say there's no such thing as a fair coin. So either no past chancy-sounding event ever really had odds, or "chance" is tracking something else here, like how easily small stuff could've tipped it the other way, which is exactly what the historians' number is trying to get at.
