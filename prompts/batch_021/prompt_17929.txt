Over the past few months I've been building a tool-using LLM agent for a logistics-scheduling startup, and I kept running into a maddening class of bugs: after the agent executed an action (rebooking a shipment, releasing a warehouse slot, and so on), it would go on reasoning from a snapshot of the world that no longer reflected the side effects of what it had just done, so later steps in its plan were subtly wrong. A colleague joked that we had "rediscovered the frame problem," which sent me down a rabbit hole into some old AI and philosophy-of-mind papers. The trouble is that "the frame problem" seems to mean two rather different things depending on who is writing. McCarthy and Hayes's original 1969 formulation looks like a narrow logical/representational puzzle: given axioms describing the effects of actions, how do you avoid also having to state, for every action, the countless facts it leaves unchanged (this is apparently what Reiter's successor-state axioms were later meant to fix)? But Dennett, and especially Fodor, seem to use "the frame problem" for something much broader and more epistemological: the problem of how any reasoner bounds the set of beliefs it even needs to treat as candidates for revision, given that relevance can in principle be global and holistic across a whole belief system. Fodor goes further and treats this as evidence for his claim that "central," non-modular cognition is Quinean and isotropic in ways that make it resistant to computational modeling altogether, not merely hard to axiomatize in first-order logic.

I majored in computer science rather than philosophy, so I may well be flattening or misreading the secondary literature here, which is exactly why I'd like your help. Could you compare these two versions of the frame problem in some depth: the narrow engineering problem McCarthy and Hayes posed, and the broader problem of bounding relevance that Dennett and Fodor extract from it? I'm especially interested in three things: first, whether these are genuinely the same underlying problem viewed at different levels of abstraction, or whether AI researchers and philosophers have mostly been talking past one another under a shared label; second, whether an engineering fix to the narrow problem, such as successor-state axioms, or in practice giving an agent an explicit, regularly-refreshed state representation plus re-planning after every action, has any real bearing on Fodor's broader worry, or leaves it completely untouched; and third, whether the fact that current LLM agents muddle through commonsense relevance judgments reasonably well most of the time, without any explicit frame axioms, counts as evidence against Fodor's pessimism about central cognition, or whether that comparison is actually a red herring. Most of what I've found online treats "the frame problem" as a single, vaguely defined obstacle, so I'd appreciate it if you could be explicit about where the strongest version of each position actually lies.
