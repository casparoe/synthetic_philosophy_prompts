I'm the treasurer of my high school's investing club, where each semester we manage a shared virtual portfolio and compete against a few neighboring schools on total returns. A couple of weeks ago, one of my clubmates asked, outside of our normal weekly vote, whether I would privately swap one of my recommended holdings for one of his, saying he had "a good feeling" about a company after reading an earnings preview. I almost agreed, mostly because I had no strong attachment to my own pick, but our faculty advisor, who spent a few years as a trader before he started teaching, overheard us and told us to look into something called the "no-trade theorem" before agreeing to any more private swaps like that.

From what I have been able to piece together, the idea is roughly this: if two people are both rational, would interpret any shared piece of information in basically the same way, and it is common knowledge between them that the only reason either might want to trade is a belief that they privately know something the other does not, then the very fact that one of them proposes the trade should make the other revise their beliefs and lose their appetite for it. So, in an idealized world, trades motivated purely by private information should not actually happen between two equally rational people. This is hard for me to square with what we have covered in economics class about real financial markets, where enormous volumes of shares change hands every day, seemingly because different investors believe different things about value. Our advisor said that tension is exactly the point, and that no-trade theorems are less a claim about what literally happens on Wall Street than a tool for figuring out what trading activity and price movements can and cannot tell us about what other people actually know.

Since our club is deciding this week whether to keep allowing these informal side trades between members, could you lay out the most important implications of no-trade theorems both for a situation like ours and, more broadly, for how much we should trust that someone's willingness to trade with us, or heavy trading in a market generally, reveals real information rather than something else entirely?
