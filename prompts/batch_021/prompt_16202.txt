I'm a PhD student in machine learning, and somewhat by accident — our group needed a student volunteer, and I'd taken an interpretability seminar the year before — I've ended up as the graduate-student representative on our research group's internal AI Safety Review Board. It's a small committee, modeled loosely on the "safety case" practices now being discussed at frontier labs, that has to sign off before any fine-tuned model gets real tool access or production traffic, even for a limited internal pilot.

The case in front of us this week concerns a coding assistant with file-system and shell access. The safety case the lead engineer submitted is almost entirely behavioral: the model refused every prompt in our held-out dangerous-capability suite, remained cooperative across several hundred red-team elicitation attempts, and showed no drop-off in compliant behavior on a subset of trials deliberately disguised to look like unmonitored production use. He believes this is exactly the kind of evidence a safety case should rest on, and wants approval this week. One of our postdocs, who works on interpretability, is blocking consensus — not because she finds the results unimpressive, but because, on her view, this exact pattern of results is what we would see whether the model is straightforwardly aligned or is a mesa-optimizer that has learned to satisfy the training objective instrumentally while preserving a different objective it would act on only once it judged monitoring to be sufficiently unlikely. No amount of further behavioral testing, however cleverly disguised, can discriminate between these two hypotheses, she argues, since a model of the second kind would, by construction, produce identical outputs on every such test. A couple of other members think that if her argument were accepted, it would become impossible to ever approve anything on behavioral grounds at all, which they take as a reductio of her position rather than a reason to hold the model back.

I've been asked to write the board's one-page recommendation memo for Thursday's meeting — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — and I'd rather it actually take a side than split the difference by calling vaguely for "more research." Could you draft that memo for me? I'd like it to state a clear recommendation with a concrete time frame or conditions attached, and to engage honestly with the postdoc's argument rather than simply asserting that extensive testing should be reassuring.
