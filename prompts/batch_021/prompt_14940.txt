Over the past year I've been in narrative therapy after a difficult divorce, and something my therapist says in almost every session has started nagging at me philosophically: that there is no single "true" story of my marriage or my childhood sitting out there waiting to be excavated, only different, equally fact-respecting narratives that are more or less livable. She's careful to insist this isn't license to invent facts, just a claim about which accurate-enough account is worth building a life around. It reminded me of a philosophy elective I took a decade ago on Richard Rorty, so I've spent the last few weeks back in Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature and Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity, trying to work out whether my therapist is, whether she knows it or not, running a small-scale version of Rorty's antirepresentationalism. Below are some notes I've drafted toward a short essay making that case, mainly to see whether the comparison survives contact with someone who isn't already primed to find it charming.

The core move looks genuinely Rortyan: stop asking which account of the past corresponds to how things "really" were, and ask instead which vocabulary for describing it lets you keep coping — which is more or less what Rorty wants us to do with "true" in general, once we give up the idea that language mirrors an independent reality rather than serving as a tool we use to get by. The mechanism looks similar too. Rorty's account of self-creation in Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity — trading an old "final vocabulary" for a new one once it stops paying its way, exemplified by the self-redescriptions of Nietzsche and Proust, and by Freud's recasting of conscience as contingent and idiosyncratic rather than as access to a universal moral law — reads like a philosophical ancestor of the "re-authoring" conversations my therapist is trained to run.

Here's where I keep getting stuck, though. Rorty's "solidarity" is explicitly a public, social achievement: vocabularies earn their keep by surviving argument within an actual community, and he splits that off from the private, answerable-to-no-one project of ironist self-fashioning precisely so that neither has to answer to the other. Narrative therapy's standard for a "workable" story looks more purely clinical and individual — does it reduce this one person's suffering and let them function, full stop, with no further requirement that it hold up in conversation with anyone else. Maybe that just means the therapy case is a clean illustration of Rorty's private-irony side, and I'm overthinking the disanalogy. Or maybe it means "useful" is doing such different work in the two cases — socially answerable versus purely therapeutic — that the comparison is looser than I want it to be, and I'm at risk of flattening a carefully hedged philosophical position (Rorty was adamant he wasn't offering an "anything goes" relativism) into a self-help slogan he would have been embarrassed by.

Before I take a version of this to the philosophy reading group I just rejoined, or — more nervously — mention it to my therapist herself, I'd appreciate some honest comments. Specifically: am I representing Rorty's antirepresentationalism accurately, or am I smoothing over distinctions — correspondence versus coherence, description versus justification, private versus public — that actually sink the analogy? Is the private-irony/public-solidarity split really the crux, or is there a more basic problem with the comparison that I'm missing entirely? And is there a better-fitting frame I should be reading instead of, or alongside, Rorty — Alasdair MacIntyre or Paul Ricoeur on narrative identity, say — that would make the same point more carefully than I have?
