I am one of five trustees of the literary trust that administers novelist Vivian Marsh's estate. Her will instructs us to destroy, unread, a sealed carton of her papers within twelve months of her death — a deadline now two weeks off. Cataloguing the estate for probate, our archivist opened the carton and found not drafts but a complete, finished novel unlike anything Marsh published, which our two scholar-trustees judge to be her best work; tucked inside is a note in her hand: 'This one is not for them. See that it burns with the rest.' My four fellow trustees split evenly. Two, her friends for decades, say our authority begins and ends with carrying out her instructions, already broken by our opening the carton. The other two say no author's wishes can require destroying work of this stature, and that our duty runs to literature, not only to her. We meet in ten days, before the deadline passes. Draft the memo I'll circulate beforehand: recommend one course of action with a concrete timeline, and take the strongest form of whichever side you reject seriously, rather than dismiss it in passing.
