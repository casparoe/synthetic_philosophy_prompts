I spent my working life as a physicist, mostly in statistical mechanics, and one lesson that never left me is that you cannot run a system around a closed cycle, back to exactly the state it started in, and come out the other end with a net gain of useful energy for free; that is essentially the second law of thermodynamics wearing a disguise. Since retiring I have been reading decision theory for pleasure, and I was startled to meet an argument with exactly that shape used to defend the claim that rational preferences must be transitive: the money-pump argument, in which an agent whose preferences cycle (X over Y, Y over Z, and Z over X) can be walked around the triangle and relieved of a little money at each step, ending up back with the very ticket she started with, only poorer. It struck me at once as a flesh-and-blood version of the same no-perpetual-motion logic I spent a career trusting, and I gather it is the preference-side cousin of the Dutch-book arguments used for probabilistic coherence. But the more I have read since, particularly Edward McClennen on resolute choice and Larry Temkin's case that "all things considered better than" need not be transitive, the less sure I am that the argument proves what it is usually taken to prove. An old colleague from my department, over dinner last month, told me I was overthinking a perfectly simple matter.

I would like you to write me a dialogue that stages this disagreement properly, set at a decision theory workshop just after someone has run the demonstration live on a volunteer from the audience with three raffle tickets, who ends up a few dollars poorer and holding the very ticket she started with. Two decision theorists who watched together now argue about what has actually been shown. The first holds that the demonstration is close to decisive: rationality requires transitive, complete preferences, full stop, and any first-order case for intransitive value, Temkin's included, must be going wrong somewhere, whatever its independent appeal. The second agrees the volunteer behaved irrationally, but denies that cyclic preferences were the culprit; the fault was choosing one trade at a time with no foresight. A resolute chooser, who settles on a plan in advance and sticks to it even at moments when, taken in isolation, she would rather deviate, could have exactly the same cyclic preferences and never be pumped for a cent.

Please have the two of them genuinely engage each other rather than talk past one another, since underneath it all this is a question about diachronic rationality: whether the right unit of rational assessment is the single trade or the whole plan extended through time. I want the first theorist to press the second on whether resoluteness is a real vindication of intransitive preference or just a polite name for ignoring, at the moment it matters, the very preference that was supposed to make the trade rational in the first place. And I want the second to press back on whether the money-pump argument doesn't quietly help itself to a trade-by-trade picture of rationality that is exactly what is in dispute. Neither of them should be easy to beat; I would like to come away still unsure who has the better of it.
