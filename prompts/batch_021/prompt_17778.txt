Since taking over the ordering decisions for my aunt's boardwalk stand this summer — we sell sunglasses and ponchos to tourists, and margins are thin enough that a bad week actually hurts — I signed up for a continuing-education evening course, optimistically titled "Business Decisions Under Uncertainty," to stop just guessing. Last week's case study has stuck with me, mainly because of an argument it set off between two people in my study group, Nadia and Callum, that nobody was able to settle.

The case laid out three ordering policies against two possible weekend outcomes. "Sun" means ordering heavily in sunglasses: $300 profit on a sunny weekend, $0 on a rainy one, since unsold sunglasses are a write-off. "Rain" means ordering heavily in ponchos: $0 if sunny, $300 if rainy. "Split" means ordering roughly half of each: a steady $100 whichever way the weekend goes, deliberately less than a plain average of the other two, since the case noted that carrying two product lines instead of one adds real costs of its own. The case's opening question was simply which policies a rational owner could cross off the list before even reaching its more complicated later scenarios, and that is where Nadia and Callum got stuck. (1) Callum said Split can be discarded outright, since there is no scenario, however likely or unlikely a rainy weekend seems, in which it is the right call. (2) Nadia objected that this was too strong: Sun only beats Split on sunny weekends, and Rain only beats Split on rainy weekends, so neither beats it outright, and she did not see how something could be "never right" if nothing straightforwardly beats it. (3) Callum answered that no single policy needs to beat Split outright: imagine flipping a coin each week between going all-in on Sun and all-in on Rain. That plan averages $150, and $150 beats Split's $100 whichever way the weekend actually goes, so the coin-flip plan beats Split even though Sun alone and Rain alone do not. (4) Nadia replied that nobody runs an actual stand by flipping coins; you place one order and live with whatever weather shows up, so a hypothetical randomizing scheme you would never really use cannot show that ordering Split every single week is a mistake. (5) Callum said it shows something real regardless: whatever you privately believe the odds of rain to be, even if you refuse to put a number on it, some non-random policy beats Split, and if Split is never the best answer for any belief you could hold, that is a genuine mark against it, coin flip or not. (6) Nadia said that only follows if you are willing to average over probabilities in the first place; if you honestly have no idea how to weigh the odds and refuse to guess, isn't Split at least the policy that caps your worst case, since it guarantees $100 no matter what happens, while Sun and Rain can each fall all the way to zero?

Neither of them budged, and we ran out of time before the instructor could weigh in. Could you go through the exchange move by move, (1) through (6), saying who has the better of each specific point, and then give your own view of how the disagreement should actually be resolved, including anything you think both Nadia and Callum are missing?
