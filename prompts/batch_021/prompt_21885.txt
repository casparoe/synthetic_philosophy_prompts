While reading about probability outside of class, I came across Bertrand's paradox: depending on whether a "random chord" of a circle is generated by choosing two independent random points on the circumference, by choosing a random radius and then a random point along its length, or by choosing a random point in the disc to serve as the chord's midpoint, you get three different answers (1/3, 1/2, and 1/4) for the probability that the chord is longer than a side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. I then read that Jaynes tried to dissolve the paradox by requiring the answer to be invariant under translations, rotations, and rescalings of the circle, a requirement he argued singles out the random-radius method and its answer of 1/2. Could you first give me an intuitive, non-technical feel for why imposing those particular invariances forces you toward that one method rather than the other two? Second, I would like your own assessment: does Jaynes's argument actually rescue the principle of indifference from the charge of arbitrariness, or does it just push the arbitrary choice back a level, to the question of which symmetries are the "right" ones to demand of the problem? And if it is the latter, would that same worry undermine an attempted invariance-based fix for van Fraassen's cube-factory paradox, where indifference over a cube's side length and indifference over its volume disagree about how likely a "small" cube is?
