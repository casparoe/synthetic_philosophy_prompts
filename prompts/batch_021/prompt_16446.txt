I've spent years reading rationalist blogs and following the forums that grew up around them, so I thought I'd fully absorbed the principle that a decision should be judged by the quality of the reasoning behind it, not by how it happens to turn out. Something that just played out in my own small forecasting group has left me less sure, and I'd like an outside opinion.

A few of us who met years ago through a local meetup still run an informal forecasting circle, staking small sums against each other on a private prediction market. Two members, Priya and Marcus, each independently took a large contrarian position against the group consensus, on two different long-shot questions; by everyone's own account, both reasoned with the same rigor beforehand, and neither had access to information the other lacked. Priya's bet happened to come in, and ever since, people in the group treat her judgment on completely unrelated matters as unusually reliable. Marcus's bet, equally unlikely to pay off, simply didn't, and he's now gently ribbed as someone whose confidence outstrips his judgment; tellingly, Marcus himself says he'd make the identical bet again if he had to redo it, but admits he can't help feeling foolish about how things turned out. When I pointed out that Priya and Marcus reasoned about equally well beforehand, and that a split like theirs is exactly what you'd expect if several equally capable people take long-shot positions, I was told I was overthinking it, and that results have to count for something in the end. I can't tell whether my unease is tracking a real problem with how the group is assessing the two of them, or whether I'm just refusing to update on legitimate evidence about their judgment. Is there a principled way to decide how much a later outcome should be allowed to shape our assessment of the reasoning that produced it?
