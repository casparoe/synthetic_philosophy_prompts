Ok so I've been working on a piece about this whole "is crypto actually money" fight that flares up again every time some stablecoin blows up, and last week I interviewed an economic historian for background who kept circling back to Aristotle's distinction, in the Politics, between money as a plain medium of exchange and money that "breeds" more money — apparently the Greek word for interest, tokos, literally means offspring. That sent me down a rabbit hole rereading the relevant bits of the Politics and the Nicomachean Ethics (the passage where he says nomisma, currency, is called that because it exists by nomos, convention, and not by nature), and now I cant stop wondering how he'd actually rule on things like Bitcoin, algorithmic stablecoins, and DeFi "yield farming," where people lend out crypto to earn more crypto as a return.

Here's the bit I can't work out on my own: Aristotle seems to have two seperate objections that might actually point in different directions once you apply them here. One is about what even counts as money to begin with — a conventional measure tied to some community's real needs for exchanging goods — and that seems like it could disqualify something like Bitcoin outright, since basically nobody uses it to provision an actual household and its value is close to pure speculation. But the other objection, the usury one, is specifically about lending currency to make more currency with no underlying production, and that seems to describe staking and yield farming almost too perfectly, regardless of whether the underlying token would even pass his "real money" test in the first place.

So: which failure do you think he'd treat as the deeper one — is Bitcoin-as-an-asset basically fine by his lights (a weird case study, but not chrematistike in the bad sense) right up until people start lending it out for yield, or is the whole stack corrupt from the base layer because it was never tied to any real oikonomia to begin with? And zooming out further, does his whole apparatus — natural vs unnatural exchange, the self-sufficient household, money as a creature of a political community — even survive contact with a currency that has no issuing state or community behind it at all, or does it just quietly fall apart? Genuinely curious how far you think the analogy holds before it snaps.
