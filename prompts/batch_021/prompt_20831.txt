I don't have any background in philosophy or logic -- I only got interested in this kind of thing as a hobby a couple of years ago, after a friend showed me a puzzle book that included the old liar sentence, "This sentence is false." Since then it has turned into a running argument in my Wednesday board-game group, and last week, while we were waiting for a table, it boiled over into a proper back-and-forth between two of the regulars, Renata and Doug. I wrote down roughly what each of them said afterward because I wanted an outside opinion on who actually had the better of it at each stage -- neither of them would budge, and I honestly can't tell.

Renata opened by arguing that the sentence isn't really paradoxical at all: it's simply meaningless, on a par with asking what color Tuesday is, so there was never a genuine claim there to be true or false in the first place. Doug objected that this can't be right, since we obviously do grasp exactly what the sentence is claiming -- that it itself is false -- and it's exactly that understanding that produces the contradiction no matter which way you turn it; if it were truly meaningless the way her example was, we wouldn't feel any pull toward calling it true or false to begin with. Renata conceded the point about meaningfulness but fell back to a second position: fine, it's meaningful, but it still just lacks a truth value -- it's neither true nor false, a kind of gap, and that's a legitimate status a sentence can have, so no real contradiction ever arises. Doug replied that this doesn't get her out of it either: if the sentence is neither true nor false, then in particular it isn't true, but now take the closely related sentence "This sentence is not true" and run the same move on it -- if it's likewise a gap, then it isn't true, which is exactly what it asserts, so it turns out to be true after all, and the contradiction just resurfaces one step over. Renata's answer was that this only shows there's no single, all-purpose notion of "true" that can be applied uniformly to every sentence, including sentences about which other sentences fail to have a truth value; she said she vaguely remembered this "levels" idea being traced back to a logician named Tarski, and that the "not true" used to describe the first sentence from outside is simply operating one level up from the "false" used inside it, so the two never actually collide. Doug's parting shot was that this feels like a dodge: ordinary people get by with a single word, "true," and never sense themselves hopping between levels when they call something false, so carving out a special higher-level meaning only once the paradox gets uncomfortable looks less like solving the problem than quietly changing the subject.

Could you go through their exchange step by step and say who had the better of it at each stage, and why, rather than just handing one of them the overall win? I would also be curious whether there's some important point about truth or self-reference that neither Renata nor Doug raised at all.
