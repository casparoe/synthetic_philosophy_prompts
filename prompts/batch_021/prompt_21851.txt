I sit on the three-person grant committee for a small AI-safety fund, about the size of the Long-Term Future Fund. Our latest applicant wants funding to build a 'tiling agent': a system that only rewrites its own decision procedure once it can prove, in its own formal system, that the successor preserves a stated safety property. Our resident logician objects that this runs straight into the Löbian obstacle: Löb's theorem says a consistent system can't generally prove 'whatever my proof system proves is true' as a blanket schema, so the self-trust guarantee on offer isn't actually available, and adding that reflection principle as an extra axiom just buys inconsistency. The other two members call this well-trodden ground: they point to hierarchies of successively weaker successor systems, to Vingean reflection on a successor's properties rather than its proofs, and to probabilistic rather than proof-level self-trust, and worry that rejecting on these grounds judges a prototype by the standards of an already-solved problem. We meet again Tuesday. Draft the committee's funding memo -- fund, reject, or fund with conditions -- with reasoning specific enough that the applicant could respond to it point by point, and give the 'well-trodden ground' view a genuinely fair hearing before explaining why you land where you do.
