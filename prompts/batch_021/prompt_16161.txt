I started a philosophy PhD this year, and in our formal epistemology seminar we just spent a week on Bas van Fraassen's "Belief and the Will" and the Reflection Principle he introduces there — roughly, the idea that your current credence in a proposition, conditional on the supposition that your credence in it at some future time will be r, should itself equal r. We went through the diachronic Dutch book argument for why flouting this looks like guaranteed self-sabotage, echoing the synchronic Dutch book case for probabilism, and I found it persuasive enough as an abstract classroom exercise. Then, purely by coincidence, I got a date for a minor surgery next month involving a sedative my doctor cheerfully described as leaving me "awake but with no memory of any of it" — and I realized I was, quite literally, facing the question of whether I'm now rationally required to defer to whatever my post-sedation self ends up believing about how the procedure went.

What I can't work out is how van Fraassen's own fix for exactly this kind of worry is supposed to apply. As I understand it, he restricts Reflection to cases where you expect your future opinion to be the product of rational, evidence-based updating, precisely so that you're not obligated to defer to a future self you expect to be drugged, hypnotized, or otherwise cognitively hijacked. But anterograde amnesia doesn't feel like "irrational distortion" in the intended sense — it's a mundane, predictable side effect, not a breakdown of my future self's rationality — and yet it clearly isn't ordinary conditionalization either, since conditionalizing on new evidence can't make you lose credences you already have. So does a case like mine fall inside or outside van Fraassen's restriction, and what does the answer reveal about whether that restriction is doing real philosophical work, as opposed to just quietly presupposing the desired conclusion in how "rational updating" gets defined? I'd love a fairly intuitive, gut-level walkthrough of where defenders and critics of Reflection really part ways here, including how memory-loss cases like Sleeping Beauty's are usually thought to bear on the same issue.
