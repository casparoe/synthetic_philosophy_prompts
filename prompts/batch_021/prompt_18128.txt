My younger sister is finishing a philosophy PhD on the wrong kind of reasons problem, and I'm giving the toast at her wedding next month. I never studied philosophy myself, but between her explaining her work at every family dinner and my own stubborn reading so I could argue back, I have actually come to understand it. It's the trouble for any theory that says something is good just because it is fitting to admire or desire it: people can have perfectly good reasons to admire or desire something — a bribe, a threat, the demon philosophers like to invoke, who will hurt you unless you admire him — reasons that have nothing whatsoever to do with whether the thing actually deserves it. About half the guests will be her advisor and her cohort, who will catch every reference; the other half are relatives who think she "does philosophy of goodness" and leave it at that. Please write the toast, no more than four minutes long, so that it gets a genuine laugh out of the reasons-for-marrying-someone version of this problem, but still closes as a sincere claim that, whatever remains unsolved about the problem in general, everyone in that room can tell that this particular case is the right kind of reason. It shouldn't curdle into greeting-card sentimentality, and it shouldn't turn into a seminar either.
