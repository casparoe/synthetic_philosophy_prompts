I'm plotting a novel built around two deathbed scenes, and before I write a line of dialogue I want to make sure the philosophical ground under each character is solid. One character will voice something close to Epicurus's argument that death cannot be bad for the person who dies, since no subject remains to suffer it, sharpened by Lucretius's point that the eternity awaiting us after death is symmetrical with the eternity that preceded our birth, which nobody mourns. The other will voice Zhuangzi — not the vague "made peace with death" version, but his actual moves: the skull he meets on the road, which speaks to him that night in a dream and turns down his offer to be restored to life, and Zhuangzi's own deathbed request to be left unburied for the crows and kites rather than given a proper funeral. Compare the two positions on their own terms: do they arrive at "don't fear death" by essentially the same route, or by routes that differ in kind? And tell me which, if either, survives the standard deprivation objection — that death is bad not for anything it contains but for the future goods it forecloses. I want a reasoned verdict I could put in a dying man's mouth, not a survey of both traditions.
