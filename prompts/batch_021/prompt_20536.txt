I'm enrolled in a graduate seminar on the philosophy of time this semester, and this week's session was devoted to Arthur Prior's branching-time treatment of Aristotle's sea-battle problem, including the split he drew between "Peircean" and "Ockhamist" rules for evaluating future-tensed sentences, and the later debate over whether the Ockhamist picture requires a metaphysically privileged actual future running through the branching tree — the so-called "thin red line" that Belnap and Green argued was indefensible. Our professor mentioned, almost in passing, that Prior's choice of the name "Ockhamist" was meant as a gesture of resemblance rather than a claim of strict fidelity, and that comment set off a real disagreement in my study group afterward about how much of Ockham's actual view the label earns.

As I understand Ockham's own solution, from his treatise on predestination, God's foreknowledge, and future contingents, he wants to preserve bivalence for statements about contingent future events — so "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is already true or false — while denying that this settled truth-value amounts to necessity. He gets there mainly through his distinction between what is necessary absolutely and what is only necessary "per accidens" in the way the past becomes fixed once it happens, plus a fairly candid admission that exactly how God knows future contingents as contingent outruns what human logic and language can spell out. The Ockhamist semantics that inherits his name reaches a similar-looking result differently: it simply stipulates one branch of the tree as "the actual" history and evaluates truth relative to it, so that the sentence comes out true or false now because a fact about which branch is actual already obtains, even though nothing at the present moment fixes or necessitates which branch that is.

What I'd like from you is an actual verdict, not a survey of both sides. If you put the formal apparatus in front of Ockham and explained it in terms he'd recognize, would he accept the thin red line as a faithful rendering of his solution, or would he judge that it quietly reintroduces exactly the kind of necessity he was trying to avoid — perhaps making him more sympathetic to critics like Belnap and Green than to the semantics named after him? Please tie your answer to the specific commitments in his own text (the absolute/per-accidens distinction and his admitted agnosticism about the mechanism of divine knowledge) rather than to a generic gloss on "the medieval view," and note what you take to be the strongest reason he might nonetheless have had for resisting the comparison.
