Been going down a rabbit hole for a piece on old "print more money to juice growth" arguments and got stuck on a bit from Hume's 1752 essay "Of Money." He's explaining why a growing money supply seems to pep up an economy even though he elsewhere insists money itself is neutral, just a "veil" over the real stuff of commodities and labor. Here's the passage:

"At first, no alteration is perceived; by degrees the price rises, first of one commodity, then of another; till the whole at last reaches a just proportion with the new quantity of specie which is in the kingdom. In my opinion, it is only in this interval or intermediate situation, between the acquisition of money and rise of prices, that the encreasing quantity of gold and silver is favourable to industry."

Then a couple paragraphs later, on policy:

"The good policy of the magistrate consists only in keeping it, if possible, still encreasing; because, by that means, he keeps alive a spirit of industry in the nation, and encreases the stock of labour, in which consists all real power and riches."

Ok heres what i can't square: the stimulus only exists in the gap before prices catch up to the new money, which sounds like classic short-run-only, neutral-in-the-end stuff. But then his actual policy advice is to keep the money supply rising forever, i.e. never let that gap close. So is he quietly ditching his own neutrality claim, since on this logic there's no "long run" where prices ever fully catch up if the supply keeps climbing? Or is there a cleaner way to read the two bits together that I'm missing? Also, kind of a side question, but is the mechanism he's describing (new money sitting in a few pockets before it spreads out) basically an early version of what people now call the Cantillon effect, or is that a geniunely seperate idea that just sounds similar?
