I'm taking a graduate seminar on scientific realism this term, and I'd like feedback on the central argument of my term paper before I submit it. Here is the section that matters, with just enough background compressed into it to stand on its own.

Laudan's pessimistic meta-induction draws on a list of theories that were genuinely successful in their day and yet are now considered false, with central terms that refer to nothing at all: phlogiston, caloric, the crystalline spheres, the luminiferous ether, and so on. The worry this generates is that our own successful theories are candidates for the same fate, so empirical success is a poor guide to truth. Worrall's structural realist reply concedes the ontological losses but denies that they touch what realism should actually be about. When Fresnel's equations for the intensities of reflected and refracted light—derived on the assumption that light is a vibration in a mechanical ether—turned up more or less intact as a limiting case within Maxwell's electromagnetic theory, what survived the change wasn't the ether but the mathematical relations themselves. Realists, on this view, should commit to the structure rather than to the particular unobservable entities it happened to be attached to at the time, since it's the structure that actually persists across theory change.

Here is my worry about that move. Worrall's evidence that structure persists comes down to a small set of showcase transitions, Fresnel-to-Maxwell above all. As far as I've been able to find, nobody has gone through Laudan's whole list asking, case by case, whether the mathematics outlived the ontology even where the ontology collapsed. Until that survey exists, the claim that structure survives theory change is itself an induction from a hand-picked sample—and that is, if I'm remembering the base-rate-fallacy objection to this debate correctly, exactly the move that makes historical arguments on both sides of the realism dispute untrustworthy in the first place: you can't read off a reliability rate from a reference class you selected because it happens to deliver the answer you wanted. If that's right, structural realism hasn't blocked the pessimistic meta-induction so much as demoted it, from "will our ontology survive?" to "will our structure survive?"—a question the historical record hasn't actually been surveyed carefully enough to answer.

Two things I'd like pushback on. First, is this a fair way to characterize what Worrall's argument needs, or is there a version of the structural continuity claim that doesn't rely on this kind of inductive support at all? Second, does the reference-class objection actually pick out something specific to structural realism, or is it general enough to flatten Laudan's original list too—in which case I haven't found a problem with Worrall so much as a problem with arguing from history at all, which would be a much bigger admission than I want to make in this paper.
