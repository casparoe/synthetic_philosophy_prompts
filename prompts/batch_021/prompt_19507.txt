At a philosophy of mind reading group I belong to, two members have spent several sessions arguing over whether Gödel's incompleteness theorems show that a mind can't just be a sophisticated computer. Roughly, here's how it went:

(1) Priya's opening move: take any consistent formal system F strong enough for arithmetic. Gödel's construction yields a sentence — call it G — that is true but unprovable in F. If a person's reasoning were entirely captured by some such F, then F is exactly what that person could never get past — yet any competent mathematician, walked through the construction, will agree G is true. So reasoning can't just be running one fixed F.

(2) Owen's objection: this only works if we already know F is consistent, and Gödel's second theorem says F can never certify that from the inside. Priya is importing, from outside the system, exactly the assumption she hasn't earned; if our own reasoning "system" happens to be subtly inconsistent, it can grind out G as a mere theorem too, and the whole argument collapses.

(3) Priya's reply: nobody needs a formal proof of consistency from within F — a person can reasonably trust her own basic arithmetic competence on other, informal grounds, and can always step outside any given F to recognize further truths, with no fixed system dictating in advance where that has to stop.

(4) Owen's comeback: a machine can borrow that exact trick, climbing, whenever it hits a Gödel sentence for its current system, to a new system that adds the old one's consistency as a fresh axiom, and repeating indefinitely. And "no fixed stopping point" is asserted, not shown: human mathematics could itself be a long, historically contingent sequence of patched-together systems, no different in kind from the machine's ladder.

Neither budged. Could you go through moves (1) through (4) and say who has the stronger position at each step, note anything you think both of them are missing, and then offer an overall verdict on the dispute?
