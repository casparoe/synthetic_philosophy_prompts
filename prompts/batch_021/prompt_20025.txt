I serve on the clinical ethics committee of a psychiatric hospital, and we have been asked to resolve a case involving what our consult-liaison psychiatrist calls a "Ulysses contract." Six months ago, while clinically stable, a patient with a long history of bipolar I disorder signed a directive instructing the team to hospitalize and medicate her against her contemporaneous wishes if she became manic again, provided that two psychiatrists uninvolved in drafting the document certified that she met behavioral criteria she had chosen herself in advance: several consecutive nights without sleep, initiation of large unplanned purchases, and refusal of outpatient follow-up. She now meets all three criteria, as confirmed independently by two psychiatrists. The complication is that she is neither confused nor incoherent: she converses fluently, passes our standard capacity assessment without difficulty, and gives a clear, reasoned argument for wanting to leave and refuse medication, including the claim that the woman who signed the contract "was afraid of herself in a way I'm no longer afraid of." Our hospital's advance directive policy was drafted with end-of-life care in mind and says nothing about directives that anticipate a future psychiatric crisis, so the committee has no internal precedent to invoke. Roughly half the committee wants to honor her present, apparently capacitous refusal; the other half wants to enforce the contract, on the grounds that its entire point was to withstand exactly this kind of persuasive-sounding reversal, and they have started comparing it to the way a constitution binds a later legislature even when that legislature has the votes to change it.

Could you draft the short memo I will circulate before Friday's meeting? It should recommend, concretely, whether we honor the contract or her current refusal; lay out a specific procedure and timeframe for acting on that recommendation; and take the strongest version of the losing side's argument seriously rather than treating it as an obvious mistake.
