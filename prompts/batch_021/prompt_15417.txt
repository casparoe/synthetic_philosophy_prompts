I cover physics and the philosophy of science for a general-interest magazine, and I also hold a part-time science-communication fellowship at a research university, which comes with a public-interest seat on its Speculative Research Fund — a small committee that awards one-year seed grants for projects too exploratory to survive a federal review panel. This cycle's most contentious application comes from a postdoctoral researcher in the quantum optics group, who is requesting roughly $40,000 to build a tabletop photonic circuit that uses postselection to simulate a Deutsch-style closed timelike curve, the same basic construction behind a handful of lab demonstrations over the past decade or so that claim to model how self-consistency might tame grandfather-paradox-type inconsistencies. Her stated aim is to test, across a range of input states, whether the fixed point that Deutsch's equations pick out is unique and stable, or whether, as in the classic billiard-ball case that Polchinski posed and that Echeverria, Klinkhammer, and Thorne worked out for Novikov-style self-consistency in wormhole spacetimes, there can be several consistent solutions, or occasionally a tie that needs Deutsch's own maximum-entropy rule to break.

Two committee members have dug in, and the dispute has stalled discussion. Her faculty sponsor, a senior experimentalist, argues this is exactly the kind of cheap, rigorous foundations-of-quantum-mechanics work the fund exists for: it takes a century-old puzzle about backward causation and turns "is self-consistency doing any real work here" into a question you can put numbers on. The panel's philosopher of physics, whom we bring in for cases like this one, counters that the framing oversells what postselection can show: conditioning on measurement outcomes can reproduce all sorts of exotic-looking correlations without any system, signal, or bit of information ever running backward around a causal loop, so the experiment would tell us, at best, about fixed points in a particular quantum circuit, and nothing about whether the grandfather paradox has any physical resolution at all. A few other members suspect the real disagreement is over how the abstract and press release should be worded, and would like the funding question settled regardless.

I need to circulate a one-to-two-page decision memo before Friday's vote. Please draft it for me: recommend that we fund, decline, or fund with specific conditions (for instance, restrictions on how the results may be publicly described, or a reporting checkpoint partway through the year), and make sure the reasoning is tight enough that both the sponsor and the philosopher of physics would recognize their actual argument in it, rather than a caricature of it.
