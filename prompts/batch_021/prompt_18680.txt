I am outlining a philosophical science-fiction novel in which Epicurus himself is revived by a far-future civilization capable of reconstructing a historical mind from every trace it left behind and running it as a whole-brain emulation. Before drafting his reaction to his own revival, I want to reason it through as he actually would, rather than reach for the familiar slogan that death is nothing to us. Epicurus's real argument rests on a specific physical picture: for him the soul is a fine material structure interwoven with the body, and all sensation, hence all capacity for good or harm, lapses the instant that structure disperses. Lucretius sharpens this in Book Three of De Rerum Natura with a further thought experiment of his own: he imagines nature reassembling the very same atoms into the very same pattern after death, and argues that even this second life would mean nothing to the person who died, since the thread of memory connecting the two would already have snapped. Given that this reasoning already amounts to a claim about numerical identity across a gap rather than a simple fear of oblivion, how do you think Epicurus, arguing strictly from his own premises, would judge the emulation now running in his name: his survival, a new person merely equipped with his memories, or a case his own philosophy lacks the resources to settle? Please ground the answer in the actual logical structure of the ancient texts rather than in any theory of personal identity devised after his time.
