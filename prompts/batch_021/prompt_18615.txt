I'm taking a formal epistemology seminar and we just got through Jeffrey conditionalization -- the update rule for when your evidence itself is uncertain, like the professor's go-to example of glancing at a swatch of cloth under bad light and ending up something like 70% confident it looks green and 30% blue, then having to update everything downstream (say, your belief about whether the fabric's silk) by mixing your old conditional probabilities on green and on blue according to those new weights, rather than just flat-out conditionalizing on "it's green." Anyway, two people in my study group, Ben and Nora, got into it about this afterward and neither one backed down, and I keep turning the exchange over in my head.

(1) Bens opening move was that the whole rule is kind of arbitrary, because if you get two separate uncertain readings -- your color impression, and seperately an uncertain read on whether the light was candlelight or fluorescent -- updating on them in different orders can leave you with different final confidence about the silk question, and rational belief change shouldn't depend on which order you happened to process your own evidence in. (2) Nora said that's not really a mark against the rule, since Jeffrey conditionalization comes with an actual Dutch-book argument (something about a bookie being able to force a guaranteed loss on you if you deviate from it) showing you're rationally required to update that way, so the order complaint is a bit of a red herring. (3) Ben came back that a Dutch-book argument only pins down that you shouldn't violate the rule *given* some fixed conditional probabilities -- it says nothing about which uncertain input to process first, or which conditionals to hold fixed while you do -- so you could follow the rule perfectly at every single step and still land in two different places, meaning the theory goes quiet exactly where it most needs to talk. (4) Nora's response was that this isn't a gap so much as the model getting something right: your confidence about the lighting and your confidence about the color aren't actually independent, so treating "color-then-lighting" and "lighting-then-color" as processing the same total evidence was already a mistake -- the order you pick encodes a real judgment call about which impression is more basic. (5) Ben's last line was that this just relocates the arbitrariness instead of removing it: now it's "which impression counts as basic" that the rule gives you zero guidance on, so two equally careful Bayesians can walk away from the identical experience with different beliefs, and Jeffrey conditionalization ends up calling both of them perfectly rational.
