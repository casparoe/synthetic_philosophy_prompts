I'm one of two student representatives on my high school's Science Research Review Committee, which has to sign off on any independent project before it can be entered in our regional and state science fairs, including ones that use live vertebrate animals — those get a pre-approval process modeled loosely on a university's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). This year a junior in the program submitted a proposal for a learned-helplessness experiment on mice: one group would get brief, mild, inescapable shocks over several days, a matched ('yoked') group would get the same total shocks but with a lever that lets them escape, and afterward both groups would be tested on a new escape task to see whether the first group has become slower to learn that escape is now possible. The actual research question is whether a week of voluntary wheel-running beforehand makes mice more resistant to becoming 'helpless' in this way. The shock levels fall within what our fair allows, and a veterinary consultant has signed off on the housing and monitoring plan, but our committee's own charter just says approved projects must 'minimize suffering to the extent consistent with the scientific value of the research,' and we cannot agree on what that actually requires here.

For an independent project last spring I read through Kant's Lectures on Ethics, where he argues we have no direct duties to animals at all, since they are not rational or self-conscious in the relevant sense, but that cruelty toward them is still wrong because of what it does to the person practicing it — it dulls the very sympathy that keeps us decent toward other people. Two committee members who find this persuasive want to approve the project close to as submitted, on the grounds that a carefully monitored, scientifically motivated procedure like this one just is not 'cruelty' in the sense Kant has in mind. A friend on the committee who is more of a utilitarian thinks that is beside the point: what matters is how much the mice suffer and for what payoff, and a teenager's science-fair project is a strikingly thin justification for inducing something like despair in a sentient animal, whatever it does or does not do to anyone's character. One of our faculty advisors also mentioned some more recent work — I believe by Christine Korsgaard — arguing that animals count as ends in themselves even on broadly Kantian grounds, which seems to me like it might undercut the first camp's 'we're just applying Kant' defense rather than support it.

Could you draft the short memo I would circulate to the committee before we vote next Tuesday? It should recommend one concrete outcome — approve, reject, or approve with specific modifications — with reasoning that the student researcher and the rest of the committee could actually engage with, rather than a restatement of the fair's pain-and-distress rules. I would especially like it to take seriously whichever side it ends up ruling against, instead of treating that side as an easy loss.
