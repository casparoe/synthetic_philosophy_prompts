My work-in-progress novel is set at a small, slightly odd independent school whose long-dead founding headmaster built the whole place around Alfred North Whitehead's writings on education — mostly *The Aims of Education*, though apparently he'd also worked his way through *Process and Reality* and never quite gotten over it. The book is set in the present day, and the current headmistress, who inherited this Whiteheadian mission somewhat reluctantly from her predecessor, is getting squeezed from two directions: the board wants her to adopt a shiny adaptive-learning software platform that "personalizes the pace" for each kid, and a state agency is threatening to pull funding unless the school submits to a standardized testing regime.

I want a scene where she (or maybe a sympathetic but skeptical old teacher) actually reasons through what Whitehead's framework would say about these two things, rather than just having her mutter "Whitehead would've hated standardized tests" and leaving it at that — that's the exact cliche I'm trying to write my way around. So: using his actual apparatus — the cyclic rhythm of romance, precision, and generalization, the bit about "inert ideas," and his specific, fairly vitriolic gripes about external examination systems dominating school curricula in his own day — can you work out what he'd plausibly say about (a) high-stakes standardized testing tied to funding, and (b) adaptive AI tutoring software that adjusts pace and difficulty per student in real time?

I'm especially interested in whether his objections to external exams would extend cleanly to the AI tutoring case, or whether there's actually a decent argument that this kind of software gets closer to what he wanted then a single teacher managing thirty kids ever could. Don't just split the difference diplomatically — I'd rather you commit to whichever reading of his views is best supported, since I don't want to put a strawman argument in a smart character's mouth.
