I'm a philosophy PhD student TAing an intro philosophy of religion course this semester, and we just got through Hume's "Of Miracles," which sent me down a rabbit hole that I don't think the secondary lit I've read (Earman, Fogelin, that kind of thing) really addresses head-on: how would Hume's argument actually hold up against the bureaucratic machinery the Catholic Church now uses to certify miracles, as opposed to the kind of testimony he was originally arguing against?

Like, at Lourdes there's an International Medical Committee of physicians (not all of them practicing Catholics, from what I understand) that reviews thousands of claimed healings and only ever certifies a tiny handful as "medically inexplicable by current science." Something similar happens for canonizations - a board of doctors has to independently sign off that there's no known natural explanation before a case even reaches the theologians. This seems like it should be a much harder case for Hume than the testimony he actually had in mind, since so much of his "in practice" argument in Part 2 of the Enquiry is really an attack on the witnesses themselves - people driven by "the passion of surprise and wonder," reports that trace back to "ignorant and barbarous nations," rival religions' miracles cancelling each other out, etc. Modern medical boards are trying to be the exact opposite of that: trained skeptics with diagnostic tools Hume couldn't have dreamed of, actively hunting for natural explanations and only demurring when they genuinely can't find one.

So does that kind of institutionalized, quasi-scientific vetting actually satisfy Hume's maxim that the testimony's falsehood would have to be "more miraculous" than the fact it's attesting to, or is there some deeper reason baked into his argument that would make him unimpressed regardless? My office mate, who's way more sympathetic to natural theology than I am, thinks Hume basically looses this one on his own terms, but I can't quite tell if that's right or if it's missing something. Curious what you think Hume himself - not just later defenders like Earman - would actually say if someone sat him down and explained how Lourdes or the Vatican's canonization process works today.
