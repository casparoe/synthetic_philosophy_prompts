I spent thirty-five years as an experimental condensed-matter physicist, and general relativity was, for me, mostly a chapter I revisited only when a qualifying exam demanded it; the metric tensor and the Einstein field equations were tools to be applied, not objects of sustained reflection. Since retiring I have had the leisure to read more broadly, and for the past several months I have been attending a small philosophy-of-science reading group run out of the philosophy department at the university where I used to teach. We recently spent three sessions on Einstein's hole argument, in the form revived by Earman and Norton, and I came away more unsettled than when I started. What looks, from the physicist's side, like a slightly pedantic remark about diffeomorphism invariance turns out, on the philosophical side, to have spawned an entire cottage industry of rival responses, with articulate defenders on every side and no sign of convergence after several decades.

I have distilled what I take to be the six main live responses to the argument's threat that substantivalism about spacetime entails a radical, physically undetectable indeterminism:

- Sophisticated substantivalism: deny haecceitism about spacetime points -- i.e., deny that a point retains a determinate identity independently of the qualitative (metrical, causal) role it plays -- so that models related by the "hole diffeomorphism" represent one and the same possibility. Developed along lines associated with Butterfield.
- Metrical essentialism: hold that a point's metrical properties are essential to it, so that the "shifted" model does not describe the same points with different properties at all, and no indeterminism arises in the first place.
- Relationalism: deny that spacetime points or regions exist over and above the fields defined on them, so that there is simply nothing for the hole diffeomorphism to act upon independently of those fields.
- Structuralism: hold that points have no identity apart from the relational structure they instantiate, so that diffeomorphic models simply represent the same structure under different descriptions.
- Maudlin's response: accept that the argument does generate indeterminism, but deny that a substantivalist has any principled reason to expect determinism at this level of description, treating the indeterminism as a harmless artifact rather than a genuine cost.
- Weatherall's response: argue, on formal grounds, that models related by a hole diffeomorphism are isomorphic and should be individuated as one and the same model, so that the apparent indeterminism is an artifact of counting physical possibilities too finely, and dissolves once models are individuated properly.

One member of the group, a philosophy postdoc, insists that Weatherall's response is obviously the strongest, since it makes the other five look like they are quarreling over a problem that a more careful formalism shows was never really there; another member, an unreconstructed substantivalist, thinks this is close to cheating, since only sophisticated substantivalism, metrical essentialism, and Maudlin's response actually stay on the substantivalist's own turf and answer the question rather than changing the subject. I would like an outside assessment to bring back to the group. Please rank the six responses above from most to least successful.
