I spent thirty-odd years as a physicist before retiring, and only in the last couple of years have I turned seriously to philosophy and mathematical logic — Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach was my way in, and from there I fell into the literature on formalized self-reference. A few former colleagues and I have kept up a small reading group since retiring, and for the last month or so we have been stuck on something the AI-safety literature calls the "Löbian obstacle," after Löb's theorem. As I understand it — I am relying on secondhand summaries of a paper on "tiling agents" that I believe came out of the Machine Intelligence Research Institute, rather than having worked through the original proofs myself — the theorem says that a sufficiently powerful, consistent formal system cannot prove, for an arbitrary statement P, the schema "if I can prove P, then P is true," not because the schema is false, but because a system able to prove it for every P could thereby prove any P whatsoever, which would make it inconsistent. Transplanted onto a reasoning agent that wants to build, or simply become, a successor it can fully trust, this seems to leave only unhappy options: certify the successor by a laxer standard of proof than the one the agent demands of itself, or else take the successor's soundness on faith rather than on proof — either of which seems to forfeit the very rigor the agent was trying to preserve in the first place.

What I would like from you is a short story, well under 2,000 words, that dramatizes this predicament rather than lecturing about it. Set it wherever feels natural to you — a generation ship, a research institute, a lone probe rewriting its own decision procedures en route to a destination it will not live to see — but build in a concrete moment of handoff, in which some reasoning system, artificial or otherwise, must decide how much proof of a successor's soundness is enough, knowing that the successor reasons using essentially the same logic and so cannot be certified by it with full rigor. I would rather readers feel the bind through the situation and its consequences than have a character explain it to them, and I leave the tone — tragic, wry, or something else again — up to you.
