For the last several months I've been quietly obsessed with a question I can't seem to let go of: which animals actually have someone home, so to speak, and which ones are just running on biological autopilot? It started after I watched a documentary about an octopus that seemed to genuinely recognize and even trust the diver filming it, and it got worse once I started reading about the debate over whether shrimp and other farmed crustaceans can suffer — apparently some people take this seriously enough to have founded charities around it, which surprised me. It isn't just idle curiosity either, since it seems to bear on everything from how we farm insects for protein to how we justify pest control. None of my friends have any background in biology or philosophy, so I've mostly been reading around on my own and forming tentative opinions I have no real way of checking.

Here is the question I keep coming back to. Take the following ten creatures: a chimpanzee, a domestic pig, a common octopus, a New Caledonian crow, a honeybee, a Portia jumping spider, a farmed whiteleg shrimp, a garden snail, a zebrafish, and a C. elegans roundworm (the little worm with exactly 302 neurons whose entire wiring diagram has been mapped). For each one, I would like your honest best-guess probability that there is "something it is like" to be that creature at all, in the sense of it having any capacity for felt, subjective experience, however faint, rather than being a very sophisticated stimulus-response machine with nobody home. I am not asking about intelligence, self-awareness, or problem-solving ability as such, just the more basic question of whether the lights are on in any sense at all. I know the underlying science is genuinely unsettled and that any answer involves real judgment calls, but please still commit to actual numbers rather than answering every single one with "it's uncertain" — I would like to see where you actually come down, and why.
