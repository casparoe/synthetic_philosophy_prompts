Below is a question from a graduate seminar exam in formal epistemology, together with one student's answer.

Question: Fitch's paradox purports to show that the Knowability Principle (KP) — the thesis that every truth is in principle knowable — collapses, given one very weak additional premise, into the claim that every truth is actually known.

(a) State KP and the "non-omniscience" premise precisely, using an epistemic operator K (read as "it is known, by someone, at some time, that...") and a possibility operator ◊.

(b) Derive the contradiction that KP and non-omniscience jointly generate. At each step of the derivation that turns on a general property of K, state explicitly which principle about knowledge is doing the work.

(c) State what the derivation is standardly taken to establish, and what options it leaves open to someone who wants to hold on to something in the vicinity of KP.

Student answer:

(a) KP: for every true proposition p, it is possible that p is known: ∀p(p → ◊Kp). Non-omniscience (NO): some truth is never actually known by anyone: ∃p(p ∧ ¬Kp).

(b) By NO, let q be some true proposition such that q ∧ ¬Kq. Since this conjunction is itself true, KP tells us it is knowable: ◊K(q ∧ ¬Kq). Now ask what it would take for that to be known. Factivity tells us that knowing a conjunction requires knowing each of its conjuncts, so K(q ∧ ¬Kq) gives us both Kq and K¬Kq. And since knowledge distributes — what is known is true — K¬Kq gives us ¬Kq. So K(q ∧ ¬Kq) would require both Kq and ¬Kq, which is impossible; the conjunction can't even possibly be known, so ¬◊K(q ∧ ¬Kq). That flatly contradicts what KP gave us two steps back, so the supposition that generated it has to go: there is no unknown truth. KP therefore entails that every truth is actually known.

(c) Since it is obviously false that every truth is known, this refutes KP, and because verificationists and anti-realists about truth are committed to KP, Fitch's argument refutes anti-realism about truth as such.
