A friend and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about a passage in Turing's 1950 paper that never seems to come up in the potted histories of the Turing test. We'd both been experimenting with chatbots lately and, half as a joke, decided we ought to actually read "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" in full instead of relying on the secondhand version everyone absorbs. What derailed the evening was the section where Turing works through a series of objections to his own position (section 6, if I'm remembering the numbering correctly), which includes, with what looks like genuine seriousness, an argument from extrasensory perception: he treats the statistical case for telepathy as too strong to dismiss outright, worries that a sufficiently inventive interrogator could use ESP-style questions to unmask a psychic human contestant, since no machine could be expected to match those powers, and proposes sealing the human away in what he calls a "telepathy-proof room" to neutralize the effect.

My friend waved this off as a period curiosity -- Turing entertaining a fashionable enthusiasm of his day before moving briskly on. I argued it was more damaging than that: if the test only works once every non-computational channel a human might have access to has been stipulated away, then the imitation game was never really a neutral test of "thinking," but something patched together after the fact to survive one embarrassing counterexample, with the patch quietly doing a lot of philosophical work of its own. By the end I was fairly convinced that Turing had, in that short passage, effectively conceded that his own criterion could never be fully decisive -- which leaves me wondering: if the paper's own author saw that crack in the foundations seventy-odd years ago, why does the imitation game still get treated, in philosophy-of-mind courses and popular AI commentary alike, as such a clean and authoritative benchmark?
