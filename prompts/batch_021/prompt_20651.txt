I teach philosophy at a mid-sized state university, and for the past three years I have also served on our Satisfactory Academic Progress Committee, the body that decides whether students who fall below a minimum GPA may keep their financial aid and remain enrolled. Current policy draws a single hard line: below a 2.00 cumulative GPA, a student is placed on aid suspension pending appeal, and above it, nothing happens automatically at all. Almost every term we hear appeals from students who missed the line by the narrowest of margins -- last term's most memorable case turned on whether an instructor should have rounded an 89.4 up to an A-minus, which would have carried the student's GPA just over 2.00 -- and these students reasonably point out that whatever separates them from a student who cleared the bar is not the kind of difference that ought to decide who keeps learning. About half the committee now wants to replace the hard line with a discretionary band, something like 1.80 to 2.00, within which a subcommittee would weigh context such as illness or bereavement before ruling. I have mostly kept quiet in these meetings, partly because the disagreement has the exact shape of the sorites paradox, which I teach every fall, and partly because I suspect that trading a sharp cutoff for a fuzzy band does not dissolve the arbitrariness so much as relocate it to two new edges -- though I can just as easily see why my colleagues find a fixed number convenient, whatever its defects.

We reconvene in two weeks, and I have volunteered to circulate a short memo beforehand setting out a recommendation. Could you draft it for me? I need it to come down clearly on one option -- keep the sharp cutoff, adopt a discretionary band, or propose some third policy you think more defensible -- and to justify that choice in terms my colleagues, most of whom have no philosophical training, could actually invoke when the appeals start again next term. I would also want the memo to take the strongest form of the pro-discretion case seriously, rather than treating it as mere leniency, since that is the objection any recommendation of mine will actually have to survive.
