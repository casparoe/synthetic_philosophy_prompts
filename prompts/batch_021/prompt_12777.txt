I'm working on a piece for my newsletter about all the "you're such a hypocrite!" pile-ons that erupt every time a politician gets caught doing the exact thing they campaigned against, and I went down a rabbit hole into the philosophy literature on "standing to blame" — Cohen's old essay about who even has the right to condemn terrorists, a Jay Wallace paper that ties hypocrisy back to Strawson's stuff on reactive attitudes, that whole cluster of work. The basic idea, near as I can tell, is that if you slam someone for doing X while you're doing the same thing yourself (or something comparably bad), you loose your standing to blame them for it.

Here's the bit I can't get straight for the piece: if hypocrisy really does strip the hypocrite of standing to blame, doesn't that mean the person being criticized is basically off the hook? Like, if the hypocrite's accusation doesn't count, then functionally the politician didn't actually do anything wrong — the charge just evaporates, right? Or is the whole "standing" thing secretly just a fancier, more academic version of the "tu quoque" fallacy, where pointing out hypocrisy is a distraction that shouldn't change anyone's mind about whether the original act was bad? My editor wants two tight paragraphs on this for general readers by Friday, so I'd love help getting straight on what "losing standing" actually does and doesn't imply, and any other spots where people commonly trip up on this.
