So my friend Priya (not her real name, she'd kill me) is coming on my tiny philosophy podcast next month to talk about moral worth -- the old Kantian puzzle about why the shopkeeper who's honest because honesty is good for business doesn't get any moral credit for it, dressed up in contemporary clothes. She defends a version of the "coincident reasons" view: an action has moral worth just when the reasons you actually acted on are the same reasons that make the action right, full stop -- you don't need to believe you're doing your duty, you don't even need a correct ethical theory, your motivating reasons just have to line up with the actual right-making ones. Which is nice because it can say Huck Finn's act has moral worth even though Huck consciously thinks helping Jim escape is a sin and does it anyway out of something more like sympathy than principle. (This whole shape of problem -- getting the right output for the wrong internal reasons -- is basically the alignment worry wearing a philosophy costume, which is probably why it grabbed me in the first place.)

Ive read enough of this literature secondhand through lesswrong-adjacent blog posts that I can predict most of the opening moves, so I don't want her to just restate the view for twelve listeners. Can you draft me 8-10 interview questions that steelman it while still pressing on the sore spots? I want at least one on whether "coincidence" is doing too much work -- cases where someone's motivating reason tracks the right-making reason through what looks like dumb luck rather than anything we'd call good character -- one on the Michael Smith "fetishism" charge and whether swapping the object of concern from "rightness" to "the right-making features themselves" actually dissolves that worry or just pushes it back a step, one on what it even means for two people with flatly incompatible ethical theories to both "coincide" with the right-making reasons (doesn't that quietly assume her theory is the true one, or at least that there is one?), and one connecting to the akrasia literature, since Huck is basically the mirror image of a weak-willed person. For each one throw in a quick follow-up keyed to whatever answer she'll most likely give, the way a good interviewer has one loaded already. Maybe close with a question about what case would actually make her give up the view -- I don't trust a philosopher who can't name one.
