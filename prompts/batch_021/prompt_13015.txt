I do not have a philosophy background, but a running argument with a friend has gotten me interested in something. We built a low wall out of loose bricks, and we cannot agree on whether there is now some further single thing -- "the wall" -- over and above the bricks, or whether talk of "the wall" is just a convenient way of describing the bricks arranged as they are. Neither of us disputes any physical fact; we just seem to want the words "exists" or "object" to carve things up differently. Looking into this, I found that it is a real debate in philosophy, tied to what is apparently called "the special composition question," and that some philosophers, especially Eli Hirsch, think disagreements like ours are "merely verbal" because of something called "quantifier variance" -- roughly, the claim that there is no single, uniquely correct meaning of "exists" for the two sides to actually be fighting over. Others, like Ted Sider, deny this and argue that some candidate meanings of "exists" objectively "carve nature at its joints" better than others, which would make such disputes substantive after all. Could you explain, working through a concrete example, what quantifier variance actually claims, why Hirsch takes it to show that disputes like the wall case are merely verbal, and what Sider's joint-carving reply says against him -- in enough depth that I grasp the actual mechanics of the disagreement rather than just the labels attached to each side?
