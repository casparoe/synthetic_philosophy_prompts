My college roommate Priya is getting married in June and asked me to give the toast, which is a genuinely terrifying ask because she just filed her philosophy dissertation — 250-odd pages that are basically one long argument with David Lewis's book Convention — and about fifteen of the ninety-ish guests are going to be her cohort and her committee, people who will absolutely clock the difference between a coordination equilibrium and a mere accidental regularity if I fudge it. Everyone else is her fiancé Marcus's family, his adult-league soccer team, and my own thoroughly non-academic parents, who just want a nice cry. I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I'm mid-revision on book three right now, which is exactly the wrong week to also be loosing sleep over five minutes of spoken word, but I've sat through enough of Priya's rants over the years that I don't want to just toss off a "haha marriage is a social construct" line and sit down — I want Lewis's actual account to constrain what I'm allowed to say, not just decorate it.

Here's where I'm stuck. I don't think I'm allowed to say they were "meant to be" or that this was always going to happen, because the whole point of a convention on Lewis's view is that it's arbitrary and self-perpetuating rather than necessary — sustained by precedent and mutual expectation, not fate. But then what do I do with the vows themselves? Standing up in front of everyone and swapping promises looks exactly like the explicit agreement that Priya says Lewis was trying to explain conventions without presupposing, since agreement already requires the kind of shared language whose conventionality was the whole thing he was trying to explain in the first place. So is the ceremony even allowed to matter, on this view, or does making a big deal of the vows quietly break the whole framework? They also have this small dumb ritual — Marcus says "see you on the other side" every time either of them leaves for the airport, for a reason nobody remembers anymore — that I'd like to use as my one concrete worked example.

Could you write me the actual toast — five minutes, meant to be read aloud, not sat with on the page? It should reward the fifteen philosophers for catching the real bones of it (arbitrariness, precedent, common knowledge, coordination equilibrium — real vocabulary is fine once or twice) without ever sounding like a seminar, and it still has to make my mother cry.
