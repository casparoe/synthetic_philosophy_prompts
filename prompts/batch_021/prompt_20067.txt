In a graduate seminar on Gödel's informal philosophical writings, I set the class the following question, worth 20 points (8 for part (a), 6 each for parts (b) and (c)):

Kurt Gödel maintained, in correspondence with his mother and in conversations recorded by Hao Wang, that the rationality of the world gives us reason to expect some form of survival after bodily death, quite apart from any religious doctrine. (a) Reconstruct the premises and overall structure of this argument. (b) Explain how it differs, in aim and in method, from Gödel's ontological argument for the existence of God. (c) Explain how it differs from the disjunctive thesis about minds and machines that Gödel advances in his 1951 Gibbs lecture.

One student answered as follows:

"Gödel's argument starts from a premise he took as almost self-evident: that the world is fundamentally rational, meaning that it is organized for a reason and not the product of chance or absurdity. He then observes that a human life is much too short for a person's intellectual and moral faculties to reach anything like full development: we spend decades acquiring a personality, forming judgment, and refining our characters, only to have the process cut off by death just as it starts to bear fruit. If the world really is rational in the sense the first premise claims, Gödel thought it would amount to a strange kind of design flaw for nature to invest so much in a process it then discards unfinished. So the rationality of the world, together with the observed 'waste' of a life's accumulated development at death, jointly license the conclusion that some kind of continuation after death is to be expected. In this sense the argument really is a proof of immortality: given his premises, Gödel takes the survival of the mind to follow the way a theorem follows from its axioms.

This is quite different from Gödel's ontological argument, which is a piece of formal modal logic modeled on Leibniz and Anselm: it defines God as the being that possesses all positive properties, and derives, via axioms governing the logic of such properties, that necessary existence is itself one of them, so that God exists necessarily. The ontological argument is meant to establish God's existence rather than the soul's survival, and it proceeds by symbolic derivation rather than by reflection on how a rational world would probably behave.

The afterlife argument, however, is essentially a restatement of the same disjunctive strategy Gödel used in his 1951 Gibbs lecture. There he argued that either the world is rational, in which case the human mind must be something more than any machine and an afterlife of the kind just described is available to it, or the world is not rational, in which case the mind just is a kind of machine and the question of survival cannot be rationally settled at all. Even in his most technical work on minds and mechanism, then, Gödel's ultimate concern turns out to be the same metaphysical picture that appears in the letters to his mother."
