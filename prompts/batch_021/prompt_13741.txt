I'm working on a feature about how philosophers of physics argue over whether spacetime is a substance in its own right or nothing more than a system of relations among events, and for the past week I've been stuck on something called the hole argument. I have the historical outline down: Einstein stumbled into an early version of it himself in late 1913, while wrestling with why he couldn't get away with generally covariant field equations; he concluded that general covariance itself must be untenable, spent two frustrating years pursuing a more restrictive theory as a result, and only talked himself out of the worry in 1915–16 with what's now called the point-coincidence argument. Decades later, once historians of physics recognized that Einstein's worry had been no mere blunder, John Earman and John Norton recast it, in a well-known 1987 paper, as a challenge specifically to "manifold substantivalism" — the view that the points of spacetime are genuine individuals, not reducible to whatever matter or metric happens to be draped over them.

What I can recite but don't yet feel in my gut is why the argument's central move is supposed to amount to real indeterminism rather than a harmless redundancy in the mathematics. As I understand it: take a solution to Einstein's field equations, apply a transformation — a "diffeomorphism," in the jargon — that acts as the identity everywhere except inside some matter-free "hole," and you get a second, mathematically distinct solution that agrees with the first on every invariant and every observable, yet disagrees on things like whether a given galaxy passes through some particular point inside the hole. A substantivalist, committed to denying that the two count as one and the same possibility (a principle called Leibniz Equivalence), apparently has to say that the theory simply fails to determine which of them obtains. Could you walk me through, in intuitive rather than formal terms, why this is supposed to be a real cost rather than a shrug-worthy artifact of surplus mathematical structure? I'd also like the same kind of intuitive treatment of one of the main "sophisticated substantivalist" escape routes — the one that proceeds by denying that spacetime points have any primitive identity across these transformations in the first place (anti-haecceitism) — and of why critics such as Belot and Earman have argued that this move, for all its substantivalist trappings, quietly collapses into relationism. A philosopher of physics I interviewed once tried to explain the distinction using two identically shaped maps of the same coastline, and I nodded along without really following.
