"Information in the everyday causal sense just means correlation: smoke correlates with fire, so smoke carries information about fire, but nobody calls smoke a 'code' for fire because there's nothing arbitrary about it, it's just physics. A real code, like Morse code or English spelling, needs an arbitrary convention -- the symbol could have meant something else, and nothing about physics or chemistry forces the pairing. DNA looks a lot more like the code case, because the mapping from codons to amino acids is famously arbitrary: there's no chemical reason GAA has to specify glutamate rather than some other amino acid, which is why Crick called it a 'frozen accident' -- it could easily have evolved into a totally different mapping. Sarkar and Maynard Smith both make essentially the same skeptical point here: that most of the 'information' language in biology textbooks is doing rhetorical work rather than explanatory work, and once you strip away the metaphor all that's really happening is ordinary biochemical cause and effect, a sequence of bases causing a sequence of amino acids to get assembled. I don't think that skeptical argument works though, because you can establish DNA is a real code independently of any metaphor: DNA sequences dramatically cut down our uncertainty about which proteins get made, and cutting down uncertainty is precisely what it means for something to carry information in the rigorous, mathematical Shannon sense. So DNA satisfies the strict definition of a code, not just the loose causal one, and the skeptics are basically denying something that can be shown mathematically."
