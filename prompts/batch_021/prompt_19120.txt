I spent forty years running a comparative neurophysiology lab and, now retired, still sit on my university's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee -- alongside a newer occupation, reading philosophy in earnest. We have a protocol before us that the standard 3Rs checklist doesn't settle: chronic multi-electrode implantation in macaques for a basic-science study of visual-cortex computation, with no direct therapeutic aim, and a euthanasia timetable fixed by the study design rather than by any animal's condition. One committee member defends approval along roughly the lines Nozick sketches in Anarchy, State, and Utopia: animal suffering might properly enter a utilitarian calculus, outweighed by sufficient benefit once pain is minimized, while strict, non-aggregative side-constraints are reserved for persons alone. A second member counters that Nozick immediately questions whether that combination can be made stable, and argues that macaques' capacities for social attachment and instrumental planning are precisely what should push some side-constraint-like protection past the boundary of personhood, rather than leaving them wholly inside the aggregate sum. Please draft the memo I will circulate before next week's vote: a concrete recommendation -- approve, reject, or approve with specified modifications -- reasoning the dissenting member could actually engage with, and a fair statement of the strongest consideration against whatever you recommend.
