This semester I'm taking a course on formal epistemology, and we recently spent two lectures on self-locating belief and observation selection effects. I found the material genuinely disorienting, and it's been rattling around in my head ever since, partly because it feels like it might connect to an argument I had with my roommate a few weeks ago that neither of us managed to resolve.

My roommate writes for our university's satirical student newspaper, which has been in continuous publication for 34 years. When word went around that the university was cutting funding for student publications, most of the staff assumed the paper's survival now depended on contingent things like alumni donations, ad revenue, and whether the current editors can secure a cheaper printer. My roommate, though, insisted that we could already say something substantive about how much longer the paper has left, before any of that information comes in, just from the bare fact that it has existed for 34 years. She'd read about a rule along these lines: absent any special reason to think you're encountering something at an unusual point in its history, there's roughly a 50% chance its remaining lifespan falls somewhere between a third and three times its current age, and a 95% chance it falls between about a thirty-ninth and thirty-nine times its current age. Plugged into the newspaper's 34 years, this yields, she claims, a 50% chance that the paper folds sometime between 11 and 102 years from now — a conclusion drawn entirely from its age, before consulting a single fact about its finances or readership.

This feels like a sleight of hand to me: it seems to conjure real predictive content about one specific, idiosyncratic newspaper out of a single number, and yet I couldn't actually pin down what was wrong with it when I tried to argue back. It also reminded me, vaguely, of a much stranger argument about the future of the human species that came up once in an intro philosophy of science lecture, though I never followed the details closely enough to know whether they're really related. Could you help me work through whether my roommate's reasoning is actually sound? I'm less interested in a flat verdict than in the structure of the argument itself: what assumptions it needs in order to go through, whether those assumptions are plausible in a case like this one, and whether there are recognized objections to this style of reasoning that would tell us exactly where, if anywhere, it breaks down.
