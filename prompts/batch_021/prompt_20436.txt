Me and my friend Priya are both in a decision theory seminar this semester, and after class on tuesday we ended up arguing over coffee for like an hour about whether it's ever rational to turn down free information before making a choice. My intuition has always been that this is basically bedrock: if a piece of evidence costs nothing to look at, checking it before you decide can never make you worse off, because you're always free to just ignore it and act like you never saw it -- this is basically the content of something decision theorists call Good's theorem, for expected-utility maximizers. Priya said that's true for plain expected utility, but not in general, and brought up something from Lara Buchak's work on "risk-weighted expected utility" theory (REU) -- the view where, instead of only letting a curved utility function do the work of representing risk-aversion, you also apply a separate "risk function" to the probability of ending up with a given outcome or better, which lets you weight worse-case outcomes more heavily than their raw probability alone would justify, and is how REU accommodates Allais-style risk-aversion while still never violating stochastic dominance. She claimed that once you build in that kind of global risk-weighting, the "free information can't hurt you" guarantee stops holding -- that there are decision problems where an REU-maximizer should rationally prefer to choose blind rather than peek at a costless, relevant signal first. Neither of us could actually produce a case like that on the spot, we just went back and forth on whether it was even possible in principle, and it's been bugging me since.
