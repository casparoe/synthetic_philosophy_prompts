My partner is doing a PhD in classical Indian philosophy, and lately dinner conversation at our place has basically turned into a crash course on Nyaya epistemology whether I ask for it or not (I don't mind, it's actually kind of fascinating). This week it was the four pramanas, and specifically the one that apparently caused the most grief between the Naiyayikas and the Buddhist logicians: upamana, "comparison." The textbook example is the gavaya — someone who's never seen the animal is told "a gavaya is like a cow," later runs into one out in the forest, and recognizes it as a gavaya purely through the perceived resemblance. Naiyayikas apparently use this to insist upamana is a genuinely separate, irreducible source of knowledge, not just perception or inference wearing a costume. My partner keeps saying Dharmakirti and that whole Buddhist tradition thought this was totally unecessary, that the gavaya-cognition can be fully accounted for with just perception, memory and inference, no fourth pramana required.

I tried to get a fair explanation of both sides out of my partner and mostly just got "well, obviously the Buddhists were right" (I suspect some bias there), so now I'm asking you instead. Could you write me a dialogue between a Naiyayika philosopher and a Buddhist epistemologist in the Dignaga-Dharmakirti lineage, actually hashing this out face to face? I want a genuinely fair fight: the Buddhist should give real, specific reasons why the gavaya-cognition reduces to the other pramanas rather than just invoking parsimony, and the Naiyayika should have to say exactly what's epistemically distinctive about upamana that the reductive story misses, not just insist it "feels" different. If it fits naturally, I'd love it if they also got into what's riding on this for the bigger picture — like whether Nyaya's whole four-pramana architecture even survives if upamana turns out to be dispensable.
