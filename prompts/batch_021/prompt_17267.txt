In my seminar on social choice theory and formal epistemology, we recently covered the "discursive dilemma" that List and Pettit describe: a group can reach a logically consistent, majority-supported verdict on each of several connected premises, and yet the majority verdict on the conclusion those premises are supposed to entail can come out the opposite way, depending on whether the group votes issue-by-issue (a premise-based procedure) or on the conclusion directly (a conclusion-based procedure). Our professor mentioned, almost as an aside, that this result is a structural cousin of Condorcet's own work on majority voting — both his jury theorem, where independent, moderately competent voters reliably converge on the truth under majority rule, and his paradox of cyclical majorities, where transitive individual preferences can still aggregate into an intransitive collective ranking. A few of us in the study group have been arguing about that comment ever since, since Condorcet seems to be the one Enlightenment figure who took both the promise and the pathology of collective judgment completely seriously, long before anyone had the vocabulary of "judgment aggregation" or "doctrinal paradox" (the legal cousin of this problem that shows up in multi-member courts).

Suppose you could sit Condorcet down and walk him through the discursive dilemma in full technical detail, including the general result that no aggregation procedure can guarantee a logically consistent outcome while treating every voter and every proposition even-handedly. Would he treat this as a devastating limitation on the kind of collective rationality he was banking on in the jury theorem, one that forces him to retreat from his optimism about suitably structured deliberative bodies — or would his own probabilistic framework already supply the resources to defuse it, for instance by simply insisting on a premise-based procedure, or by arguing that the dilemma only bites under conditions his competence and independence assumptions were meant to rule out? Write out the position you think Condorcet would actually end up taking, and justify your reconstruction by engaging concretely with his own arguments in the 1785 Essai about voter competence and independence, rather than resting on a general appeal to his Enlightenment faith in reason.
