Question:

In his 1958 Harvard Law Review essay "Positivism and the Separation of Law and Morals," Hart discusses the case of a German woman who, under a Nazi statute criminalizing criticism of the regime, denounced her husband to the authorities; after the war, she was prosecuted, and the court held that the Nazi statute could not be recognized as valid law because it offended the basic principles of justice that all decent people share. Hart criticizes the court's reasoning and proposes an alternative way of reaching a similar practical outcome. Fuller, replying to Hart in "Positivism and Fidelity to Law—A Reply to Professor Hart," argues that the Nazi regime's wholesale disregard for what he calls the "internal morality of law" undermines the claim that its enactments were law at all.

(a) Explain Hart's proposed alternative to the court's reasoning, and why he thinks it is preferable.
(b) Explain the principles that make up Fuller's "internal morality of law," and how Fuller uses them to characterize the status of Nazi law.
(c) Hart separately defends a "minimum content of natural law" thesis in The Concept of Law. Does this thesis give Hart a response to Fuller's challenge, or is it addressed to a different question? Defend your answer.

Student's answer:

Hart thinks the German court's approach—declaring the Nazi statute invalid because it offended basic morality—gets the right practical result by the wrong route, because it disguises the real choice being made. Hart's alternative is to treat the Nazi statute as having been valid law, so that the woman broke no law when she denounced her husband, and then have the post-war legislature enact an openly retroactive statute criminalizing her conduct. He thinks this is preferable because it forces us to see and own the price we are paying (sacrificing the principle against retroactive punishment) rather than pretending, via a fiction about what was "really" law all along, that there is no price at all.

Fuller's reply is that Hart's picture cannot make sense of what happened under the Nazis, because the regime did not merely enact wicked statutes but persistently flouted the internal morality that any system of rules must satisfy to function as law in the first place: rules must be public rather than secret, prospective rather than retroactive, reasonably clear and consistent, and actually adhered to by the officials applying them. Because the Nazi state governed substantially through secret directives, terroristic retroactive validations, and courts that answered to political pressure rather than to the written rules, Fuller thinks large stretches of the Nazi "legal" system fail to count as law on independent, procedural grounds—quite apart from the evil content of any particular statute.

Hart's minimum content of natural law thesis is his answer to exactly this challenge. Since Hart agrees that any legal system needs a certain minimum of substantive rules—protecting life, enforcing promises, restraining violence—if it is to serve its basic purpose of making social life possible, he can say that a regime as far in default of these minimum requirements as Nazi Germany forfeits, on his own terms, its claim to be "law." This lets Hart meet Fuller's objection while still keeping the separation thesis intact at the level of individual statutes.
