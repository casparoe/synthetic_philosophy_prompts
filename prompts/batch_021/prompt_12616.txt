I've spent the past few months reporting on a wave of philosophical and scientific work on free will and prediction—neuroscientists who claim to detect decisions before their subjects are consciously aware of making them, AI systems that forecast individual behavior with unsettling accuracy—and it sent me down a rabbit hole into a much older attempt to reconcile comprehensive foreknowledge with genuine freedom: Luis de Molina's sixteenth-century doctrine of "middle knowledge." As I understand it, Molina held that, logically prior to deciding which world to create, God already knows the truth of every counterfactual about how any possible free creature would choose in any possible circumstance—not because he causes those choices, and not as a matter of logical necessity, but as a contingent fact that simply is true, one God finds rather than makes. This is supposed to let God providentially arrange history down to the smallest detail, choosing which circumstances to actualize and whom to place in them, while leaving each choice genuinely up to the creature; contemporary defenders such as Thomas Flint and Alfred Freddoso, and the popularizer William Lane Craig, still treat it as the most promising reconciliation on offer, and even extend it to the problem of evil by arguing that some possible worlds with less gratuitous suffering simply aren't "feasible" for God to actualize, given whichever counterfactuals happen to be true.

What stopped me cold, reading further, was the "grounding objection" that Robert Adams and William Hasker pressed against this picture: if these counterfactuals are genuinely contingent rather than necessary, and true logically prior to any actual choice, decree, or circumstance, what could possibly make them true? Not the creature, who may never exist or face that circumstance; not God, who by the theory's own lights doesn't determine the answer; and, the objection insists, there is nothing else for a contingent truth to be grounded in. Molinists respond that the objection quietly assumes a theory of truthmaking we have independent reason to doubt, or that a libertarian free choice is exactly the kind of thing that can ground a truth about itself even "before" it occurs. Could you write me an essay that actually adjudicates this dispute rather than leaving it at a tidy stalemate? Lay out what is really doing the argumentative work on each side, press the strongest Molinist replies against the objection on their own terms, and then commit to a verdict: does middle knowledge survive the grounding objection, and if it doesn't, what should someone who wants to keep both a strongly provident God and libertarian human freedom say instead? A qualified conclusion is fine if that is honestly where the argument leads, but I don't want a survey that shrugs between the two camps at the end.
