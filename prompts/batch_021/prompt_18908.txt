Spent almost 40 years as a condensed matter physicist, and only got into philosophy after retiring a couple years ago -- currently working through a philosophy of math book for fun. Something about Zorn's lemma has been bugging me. It guarantees that every vector space has a basis, even monsters like the reals viewed as a vector space over the rationals. But in decades of doing physics with infinite-dimensional spaces I never once used a basis like that -- we always built ours by hand (Fourier modes, Gram-Schmidt, whatever), and those are countable and fully explicit. A Hamel basis for R over Q, by contrast, has never actually been written down by anyone, and it's existence is exactly what lets you build non-measurable sets and other pathologies. So what's actually going on mathematically that makes a Zorn's-lemma basis so different in character from a hand-built one -- and at a gut level, does that gap tell us something about what "exists" really means when a Zorn's lemma argument, rather than an explicit construction, is doing the proving?
