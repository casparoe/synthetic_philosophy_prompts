I spent my career in nonequilibrium statistical mechanics, and in retirement I have been sitting in on a philosophy-of-physics reading group at my old department, made up mostly of graduate students who are remarkably patient with my questions. We had just worked through the standard story about the arrow of time — Loschmidt's and Zermelo's objections to Boltzmann's H-theorem, and the usual response via coarse-graining together with some low-entropy "past hypothesis" about initial conditions, itself sometimes explained as an enormous statistical fluke — when I picked up Sean Carroll's The Big Picture and discovered that this same fluctuation logic returns, considerably sharpened, in modern cosmology. Because an eternally accelerating universe behaves like a system held forever at a fixed horizon temperature, given infinite future time it will eventually fluctuate into any configuration whatsoever, including a fully formed brain, complete with false memories of a 13.8-billion-year history, flickering briefly into existence in otherwise empty de Sitter space. Since there is infinitely more time for such "Boltzmann brains" to arise this way than there ever was for ordinary evolved observers like us to arise through the one-time hot big bang, the overwhelming majority of observers with memories exactly like mine turn out, in such a cosmology, to be freak fluctuations rather than people with any genuine past.

What I have not been able to settle, even after several sessions of arguing about it, is how much this should worry us, and it turns out serious people disagree. One side — pursued in different ways by physicists such as Don Page and Leonard Susskind in their work on the cosmological "measure problem" — treats Boltzmann-brain domination as perfectly ordinary disconfirming evidence: if a cosmological model implies that an observer with your data should almost certainly be such a fluctuation, and you have good reason to think you are not one, that tells against the model exactly as a failed prediction tells against any theory. The other side, of which Carroll himself is the best-known advocate, holds that this gets the logic backwards: a theory on which typical observers are Boltzmann brains cannot be assessed against evidence at all, since it would equally undermine the reliability of whatever reasoning led us to entertain the theory in the first place, so such theories must be rejected a priori as "cognitively unstable" rather than weighed against data in the ordinary way. I would like to see this fought out properly rather than settled by assertion. Could you write a dialogue, set in the corridor after a colloquium on the cosmological constant, between two cosmologists of equal skill and equal stubbornness who hold these two positions, in which each is made to confront the strongest version of the other's case — the first pressed on what could possibly license treating self-locating frequency among observers as truth-tracking evidence in the first place, the second pressed on whether this maneuver isn't simply a general-purpose device for placing any unwelcome prediction beyond the reach of evidence? I would rather you leave to one side the further physics question of which measure or vacuum-decay rate is correct, since that isn't the crux; I want the philosophical disagreement itself carried through to whatever real point of clarity, short of resolution, it can honestly reach.
