I sit on my hospital's capacity and complex consent review committee -- the group that gets pulled in when neurology and psychiatry can't agree on whether someone can make their own medical decisions. We've got a case that's genuinely broken our usual process. The patient, mid-50s, had a full corpus callosotomy about twenty years ago (basically "split-brain" surgery) for epilepsy that wouldn't respond to anything else -- a Wada test back then confirmed her speech is entirely left-hemisphere, like most people's. She's seemed like a completely normal, unified person ever since. Now she's facing a fairly serious elective procedure for an unrelated condition, and as part of the capacity work-up the neuropsych team ran the standard lateralized testing: flashing the choice to one half of her visual field at a time so only one hemisphere sees it, letting her answer verbally for whatever her right visual field (left hemisphere) got, and having her point with her left hand for whatever her left visual field (right hemisphere) got. Her speaking side keeps saying yes, go ahead. Her left hand, given the exact same choice, keeps pointing at "no," and it hasn't budged over several repeats on different days.

Half the committee wants to just go with the verbal answer, since legally and socially we've only ever related to her as one person and don't see why a test in a lab should change that now. The other half says that's exactly the move Nagel was cautioning against when he asked whether commissurotomy patients are really one subject, two, or something our ordinary idea of "a mind" just doesnt have room for -- and Puccetti went further and argued the hemispheres are genuinely two people who happen to share a skull, which would make picking the talkative one nothing more than a bias toward whoever holds the microphone. To make it worse, I recall some newer work (a Dutch group, I think?) pushing back on the classic Sperry/Gazzaniga "two minds" story, arguing these patients keep more underlying unity than the lateralized tasks suggest -- which honestly makes me less sure the hand's "no" is a second person's veto at all, rather than just some weird, non-authoritative readout.

Could you draft the actual memo I need to bring to Wednesday's meeting? I don't need a literature review, I need something that lands on one concrete way of handling her consent (and sets a rule for whatever the next case like this looks like), with an actual procedure and timeframe attached, and that takes the strongest argument against whichever way it comes down seriously instead of just mentioning it and moving on.
