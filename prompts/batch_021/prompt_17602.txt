Löb's theorem says that a formal system with a provability predicate for its own theorems (any theory extending Peano Arithmetic that meets the usual derivability conditions) can prove "if I can prove P, then P" only for those P it can already prove outright; push for that guarantee across the board and, applied to a known falsehood, it amounts to the system proving its own consistency — which, by that very same theorem, forces the system into outright inconsistency. Applied to an agent that wants to certify, in advance, that it may trust whatever a successor running its own proof system goes on to prove, this is what Yudkowsky and Herreshoff dubbed the "Löbian obstacle" to self-trusting, self-modifying agents; the partial fixes on offer in that literature, such as ascending a Tarskian hierarchy of ever-stronger proof systems or trading a single self-referential soundness claim for a "parametric," schema-level one, each sacrifice something that unrestricted self-trust would have delivered for free. I am drafting a short piece connecting this obstacle to the older epistemological debate over epistemic circularity (Alston) and the closely related bootstrapping or "easy knowledge" problem (Cohen, Vogel), in which a cognitive faculty's own verdicts are used to underwrite that very faculty's reliability. Please lay out the most important implications of the Löbian obstacle for that debate: does the underlying mathematics give the charge of circularity a sharper, more rigorous form than it has traditionally had, or are the disanalogies between formal provability and everyday epistemic justification too severe for the result to really bear on it?
