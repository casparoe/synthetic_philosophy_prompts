This semester I am running a graduate reading group that pairs Brentano's *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* with Williamson's *Knowledge and Its Limits*, on the theory that the historical and contemporary literatures on self-knowledge illuminate each other more than either side usually admits. At last week's meeting a disagreement broke out that I promised to think through properly before we reconvene, rather than adjudicate from memory, so I would like your help with that.

Brentano holds that every mental act is accompanied by a secondary, incidental awareness of itself, which he calls inner perception, as opposed to the deliberate, attention-demanding inner observation that he thinks is self-undermining. Judgments grounded in inner perception are evident in his strong technical sense: immediate and strictly infallible, unlike judgments of outer perception, which concern mere physical phenomena and can always be mistaken. So more than a century before Williamson's anti-luminosity chapter, Brentano had already committed himself to something close to an unrestricted luminosity thesis about the mental. Williamson's argument turns on a margin-for-error principle. In the case he relies on most, a subject grows gradually and imperceptibly less cold over the course of a morning, and if feeling cold were luminous, then at each pair of subjectively indiscriminable moments, knowing that one feels cold at the earlier moment would force it to be true that one still feels cold at the next -- so chaining this reasoning across the whole series, from clear cold to clear non-cold, produces a contradiction. No non-trivial condition comes out luminous on this argument, including knowing that p, which is exactly what sinks any unrestricted KK principle.

One student in the group insisted Brentano would simply bite the bullet and restrict infallibility to borderline-free cases; another thought his framework already blocks the argument outright. I would like a more careful answer than either of them gave, built only out of resources Brentano actually has, rather than a generic Cartesian stand-in for him. Could he resist by holding that the coldness itself is a physical phenomenon of outer sense, so that only the accompanying inner perception of one's own sensing needs to be evident, not the fine-grained degree of the sensory content -- and if so, is what remains still substantial enough to deserve the name luminosity? Does his thesis that primary and secondary awareness belong to a single unified act of consciousness, rather than to two separate higher-order acts, do any real work against a sorites-style margin-for-error argument, or does it merely dispose of a different worry, the regress problem about iterated awareness, while leaving the gradual-change problem untouched? And could he simply deny that evidence, in his sense, is the sort of reliability-involving state to which a margin-for-error principle could even apply in the first place, or would that amount to defining the problem away rather than solving it? I would like you to actually commit to which of these lines is most promising for Brentano, and to say plainly where, if anywhere, he would be forced to retreat from full infallibility.
