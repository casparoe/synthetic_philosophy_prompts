I'm working on a piece about the rationalist internet's fondness for Aumann's agreement theorem — the way people like Robin Hanson and the LessWrong crowd invoke it to argue that two honest, well-informed people who still disagree after hashing things out must be doing something epistemically wrong ("epistemic modesty" and all that. The question was worth 20 points, and Priya said she's weighting it roughly 6/6/8 across the three asks: "State Aumann's agreement theorem precisely, including the assumptions required for it to hold. Then explain why the theorem is often invoked in popular discussions of 'rational disagreement,' and identify at least one way such popular invocations tend to oversimplify or misapply it."

Here's the student's answer, in full: "Aumann's theorem (1976) says that if two Bayesians share the same prior over the state space, and each updates only on their own private information, then if it's common knowledge what posterior probability each of them assigns to some event, those two posteriors must actually be equal — they can't agree to disagree. The intuitive reason is that the fact that you know my number, and I know that you know it, and so on, is itself information, so if we're both perfect Bayesians we can't stay apart once all of that is common knowledge. Formally, this works because once our two posteriors are common knowledge, it's as if we've each conditioned on the combination of both of our private information sets — the finest partition that pools what both of us individually know — and since we started from the same prior, conditioning on that same combined information has to give the same number. This gets cited a lot by people like Robin Hanson and the LessWrong crowd as a reason why persistent disagreement between two informed, honest people is a mark of bad epistemics, and it's a big part of the case for 'epistemic modesty' — deferring to the average view, or to whoever has thought about the question longer, rather than trusting your own take. I think the popular version overstates things, mainly because it quietly drops the common prior assumption: two people arguing about politics or AI risk didn't start from a shared prior and diverge only because of different evidence — they likely had different priors to begin with for reasons that have nothing to do with evidence, like temperament or which arguments happened to strike them first, and Aumann's theorem has nothing to say about agents like that. It's also worth remembering that Aumann's original result already tells us that if two people just keep talking and re-announcing their updated posteriors to each other, they're guaranteed to converge to agreement within a finite number of rounds — which is a nice, tidy picture, but real disagreements online often don't resolve even after a lot of back and forth, which suggests it's not only the common prior that's usually missing, but genuine common knowledge of each other's posterior too."
