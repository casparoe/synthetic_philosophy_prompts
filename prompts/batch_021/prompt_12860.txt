I spent close to forty years as a comparative anatomist, working mostly on skeletal morphology in birds and their reptilian relatives, and it is only since retiring that I have started reading philosophy of biology in any systematic way. For the past month I have been working through the argument Gould and Lewontin launched in their 1979 "Spandrels of San Marco" paper, and I have drafted the short essay below to sort out my own thinking before I bring it to my reading group. I would be grateful for your comments.

Spandrels, Constraints, and the Limits of a Metaphor

Gould and Lewontin took their central image from architecture: the tapering triangular spaces between the arches that carry the dome of San Marco in Venice are not designed features in their own right but an unavoidable by-product of setting a dome on rounded arches, later filled in with decorative mosaic. Applied to organisms, the moral is that many conspicuous traits may likewise be by-products of some other structural or developmental necessity, fitted out after the fact with a plausible-sounding story about their function. Their real target, though, was less the existence of such by-products than a style of reasoning they dubbed "Panglossian": treating every trait as adaptive by default, and being satisfied with any functional story that is merely plausible rather than genuinely tested against alternatives.

On reflection, that charge bundles together theses that need not rise or fall as a unit. Peter Godfrey-Smith's later distinction helps here: one can believe that selection is empirically the dominant force behind most phenotypes, or that adaptation should be the default explanatory category (with everything else demoted to a mere "constraint" on it), or simply that proposing and testing adaptive hypotheses is good research practice, without being committed to the other two. It is the explanatory thesis that the Panglossian caricature really damages; the modest methodological thesis survives more or less intact, since testing an adaptive story against rivals is exactly what a sound heuristic should invite.

Dennett's reply complicates this tidy division, however. As I understand him, his point is that Gould and Lewontin's own procedure for identifying a spandrel already presupposes some picture of what a purely optimal design would look like -- you cannot call a trait a leftover without a baseline of what it is left over from. Even Gould and Vrba's later coinage "exaptation," for a structure co-opted for a new use, seems to relabel this problem rather than solve it: it still leaves open, case by case, whether current utility or historical origin should govern the explanation, and I am not persuaded the new term supplies any independent way of deciding that.

What has made me take the difficulty seriously is a concrete case: Donald Symons's old suggestion that female orgasm is a by-product of the shared embryonic origins of clitoris and penis, rather than a trait shaped by selection in its own right -- a suggestion Gould endorsed as a paradigm human spandrel, and that Elisabeth Lloyd later defended at length against a whole family of rival adaptationist hypotheses. "It's a spandrel" strikes me as functioning here just as flexibly as the "just-so stories" Gould and Lewontin complained of: compatible with nearly any pattern of comparative or physiological evidence, and no easier to test than the adaptationist stories it is meant to displace.

My tentative conclusion is that the 1979 critique remains a useful standing caution against explanatory laziness, but that it was never equipped to settle any particular case -- including its own favorite one -- and that the decades of argument since have not changed that.

I would welcome your comments, especially on three points: whether I have represented the Gould-Lewontin argument and Dennett's reply fairly, rather than flattening either side; whether the appeal to Godfrey-Smith's distinction is doing the work I think it is, or whether I have simply defined "methodological adaptationism" narrowly enough to guarantee it survives; and whether the female-orgasm case really supports my skepticism or, on closer inspection, cuts against it.
