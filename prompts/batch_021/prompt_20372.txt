As the clinical ethicist attached to my hospital's transplant program, I sit on the committee that resolves disputes like the one we had last month, when a liver became available just as two claims on it arrived within hours of each other: a patient in our own program who would not survive the week without it, and a request from our regional network to route it instead to a partner hospital with four patients on their list who were each individually more stable but, the network argued, collectively had the stronger claim. We kept the liver for our patient, but the dispute was heated enough that I have been asked to draft an actual tie-breaking policy, and have spent the weeks since rereading Taurek's 1977 paper "Should the Numbers Count?" and the responses it drew from Parfit, Scanlon, Kamm, and Broome, trying to work out which of them I actually believe.

I no longer want to know, in the abstract, which position is correct; I need one I could turn into policy language that is consistent, defensible on appeal, and explicable afterward to a family on the losing end of the decision. Please rank the following approaches to numbers-counting dilemmas from most to least defensible as the basis for an actual allocation policy, and give your answer as a JSON array in this form:

```json

  {"principle": "name of the approach", "verdict": "one sentence defending its place in the ranking"}
]
```

Approaches to rank: unrestricted aggregation, where the organ simply goes to whichever side has more people who need it; Taurek's equal-chance lottery between the two claims; Parfit's aggregative rebuttal of Taurek; Scanlon's contractualist view, on which numbers break ties only between claims of otherwise equal weight; Kamm's more permissive, case-by-case partial aggregation; and Broome's lottery weighted by the relative size of the two groups.
