I co-host a small podcast on the history of ethics, and next week's guest is a moral theologian who has built her scholarly reputation on defending Augustine's position — laid out across *De Mendacio* and the later *Contra Mendacium* — that every lie is sinful without exception, including a lie told to protect an innocent person from violence. This came up because a friend and I spent most of dinner arguing about the "murderer at the door" scenario, and I realized partway through that I was really only attacking a caricature of the rigorist view ("lying is always bad, full stop") rather than the actual argument, which turns out to rest on a set of moves that is far more specific — and, I'll admit, harder to dismiss — than either of us gave it credit for.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions for her that don't just invite her to restate the thesis? I'd like at least one question built around Augustine's eightfold ranking of lies by gravity, pressing on why even the mildest kind — told to help someone and harm no one — still counts as a genuine sin rather than a pardonable exception; one on how she squares the view with the two scriptural cases that keep getting thrown at it, the Hebrew midwives and Rahab, both of whom lie and both of whom scripture seems to reward for it; one that exploits Augustine's own definition of a lie as residing in the intent to deceive rather than in the literal truth of the words, and asks whether that definition actually forecloses the "mental reservation" loophole later casuists tried to open up in its place; and one that sets Augustine's rigorism against Kant's in "On a Supposed Right to Lie from Philanthropy," since the two arrive at the same hard conclusion — never lie, even here — from what look like incompatible foundations. For each question, add a short note on the answer she's most likely to reach for and a sharper follow-up that keeps her from resting there.
