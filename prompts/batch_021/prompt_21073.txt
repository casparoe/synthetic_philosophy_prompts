I spent my career as an experimental physicist, so I'm plenty comfortable with the idea that measurements have error bars -- there's a real value out there, we just can't pin it down past a certain precision. Lately I've been reading about vagueness in philosophy (heaps, bald men, all that) and the epistemicist position -- Williamson and co -- sounded at first like it was just saying the same thing: there IS an exact number of grains that makes a heap, we just can't know which one. But the more I read, the more it seems like they mean something weirder than ordinary measurement uncertainty, something to do with a 'margin for error' principle about what knowledge requires in general, not just the limits of our sand-counting equipment. Don't just give me the textbook one-liner on each view -- can you actually unpack what's doing the work in that margin for error argument, why its supposed to be a different beast from garden variety measurement error, and then contrast it with how a supervaluationist looks at that same pile of sand and refuses to say there's any fact of the matter at all?
