So my friend Marco and I got into a whole argument last night about Kurt Gödel -- not the incompleteness stuff, the OTHER weird thing he believed, which is that he apparently had an actual argument for why there must be an afterlife, totally separate from the ontological proof of God he's more known for among philosophy people. From what I remember (this is mostly reconstructed from Hao Wang's conversations with him plus stuff in his letters to his own mother), the rough shape of it is: the world is fundamentally rational, meaning nothing in it is ultimately wasted or pointless; most peoples minds and characters clearly never finish developing by the time they die; and it would be absurd for a genuinely rational world to just let all that unfinished development go nowhere -- so there has to be some further existence where it continues. I even remember one of his weird numbered philosophical theses being something like "the world we live in is not the only one we shall live in," which is basically just the conclusion stated flat out. Marco's take was that this is obviously just Gödel being unable to emotionally accept his own death and reverse-engineering a logic-sounding argument for the conclusion he already wanted, and I said no, there's an actual structural argument in there, closer to a Leibnizian sufficient-reason move than to wishful thinking, even if the premises are shaky.

We never resolved it, but it left us wondering about something neither of us could actually answer: if you dropped Gödel into today and explained mind-uploading research, the simulation-hypothesis discourse, and whatever it is modern AI systems are or aren't doing internally, would he treat any of that as actually bearing on his argument, or would he think it's a category error -- since his "afterlife" was supposed to be a necessary feature of how a rational cosmos works, not a contingent thing you engineer in a lab? And given that he'd already done serious cosmology himself (that rotating-universe solution to Einstein's equations that allows closed timelike curves), how do you think he'd react to the observational case built up since the late 90s for an eternally expanding, heat-death-bound universe -- does that put any real pressure on his "nothing rational goes to waste" premise, or is that a total non-sequitur relative to what he was actually arguing? I'd love an actual reconstruction of his likely reaction given his specific commitments (the rationalism, the Leibniz influence, the mathematical platonism), not just a generic "here's what a rationalist philosopher might say."
