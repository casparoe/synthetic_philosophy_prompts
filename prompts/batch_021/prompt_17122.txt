We have each written out our full reasoning for the other, updated on the same public evidence as it has trickled in, and gone back and forth more than once, yet our estimates have stayed roughly fifteen points apart the whole time. At our last team call, someone more mathematically inclined than either of us remarked that this was "basically a standing violation of Aumann's agreement theorem," and that two people reasoning as carefully as we claim to be ought to have converged by now.

That comment sent me down a rabbit hole of summaries of the theorem, and I came away less settled than when I started. Nearly every popular treatment states the result as some version of "rational agents with common priors cannot agree to disagree," which sounds like a claim about disagreement itself, but I have a strong suspicion that the actual mathematical content is narrower, and stranger, than that slogan lets on.

Could you walk me through what's really going on? Specifically, I would like you to: (a) state with some precision what the theorem assumes -- especially the common-prior assumption, which I gather is doing most of the load-bearing work and is a substantive, contestable premise rather than a harmless idealization; (b) give me the intuitive, gut-level reason the result has to hold, of the sort you might give for why Arrow's impossibility theorem is true, rather than a formal tour through partitions and common-knowledge operators; and (c) settle something the popular summaries never quite disambiguate for me, namely whether the theorem forbids two people from holding different opinions at all, or only forbids them from having common knowledge that their opinions differ, since those strike me as importantly different claims and I would like to know which one my teammate and I are supposedly guilty of. If it's relevant, I would also like to know how this connects to the further, more dynamic result that repeated, honest exchange of opinions should force convergence in finite time even without either side ever stating its underlying reasons -- since my teammate and I did exchange our full reasoning and still failed to converge, and I cannot tell whether that makes us irrational or just shows where the idealization breaks down in practice.
