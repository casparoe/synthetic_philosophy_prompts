I retired four years ago after a long career as an experimental physicist working mainly on condensed matter systems, and I have only recently found the time to read the philosophy of science I always intended to study properly. A friend from my old journal club, who later moved into philosophy of science herself, recently pointed me to Nelson Goodman's discussion of "grue" in *Fact, Fiction, and Forecast*, and I have been turning it over for several weeks now.

As I understand the setup, every emerald examined so far has been green, but it has equally been "grue" — green if examined before some future date, and blue thereafter — so our evidence confirms "all emeralds are grue" exactly as well as it confirms "all emeralds are green," even though only the latter strikes anyone as a sane thing to believe. My instinct, shaped by decades of choosing between competing curve fits and discarding models with suspicious extra parameters, is that this is no different from rejecting an overfit theory or one bloated with ad hoc epicycles: "grue" is plainly the more baroque predicate, since its very definition smuggles in an arbitrary date, so an ordinary simplicity criterion — some version of Occam's razor — should let us prefer "green" and close the matter. Given that this seems to dispose of the puzzle so cleanly, I am puzzled as to why Goodman, and most philosophers after him, reach instead for his more elaborate notion of "entrenchment," and why grue is still treated as a live problem in epistemology rather than a solved textbook exercise from the 1950s. Is there some genuine technical flaw in the parsimony move that I am not seeing, or is this simply a case of philosophers favoring a subtler answer over one that a working scientist would already consider good enough?
