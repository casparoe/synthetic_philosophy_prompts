Question (15 pts): (a) Explain the money-pump argument for why an agent with cyclic preferences over lotteries (A ≻ B ≻ C ≻ A) is exploitable by a sequence of trades. (b) People sometimes point to arguments like this -- basically the whole "coherence theorems" thing thats been argued to death on lesswrong/the alignment forum for years now -- to conclude that any agent that can't be exploited this way must act like an expected-utility maximizer. Identify and explain one significant gap in that inference.

Student answer:
(a) Say I prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A. A trader can start me off holding C, then offer me B in exchange for C plus a penny (I accept, since B ≻ C), then offer me A in exchange for B plus a penny (I accept, since A ≻ B), then offer me C in exchange for A plus a penny (I accept, since C ≻ A) -- and now I'm back to holding C, three cents poorer, having gone in a full circle. This is basically a Dutch book against me: my cyclic preferences let the trader extract money from me for free, forever, with no risk on their end.
(b) The main gap is that the argument assumes my preferences satisfy the independence axiom, and the Allais paradox shows that most people's actual preferences violate independence in a pretty systematic, even defensible-looking way. So the step from "you can't be money-pumped" to "you must be an expected-utility maximizer" doesn't really go through once you take real preferences seriously.
