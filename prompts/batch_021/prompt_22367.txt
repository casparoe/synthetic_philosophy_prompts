I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, but I've spent a fair amount of time lately thinking about the "torture vs. dust specks" problem that made the rounds in internet-rationalist circles: whether fifty years of torture for one person is a lesser evil than a single dust speck in the eye of each of some unimaginably vast number of people, if you take pure aggregation seriously. Wanting to see where this style of reasoning actually starts, I went back to Bentham's felicific calculus in Chapter IV of An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789), and got stuck on the closing steps, which read almost like pseudocode.

After listing seven "circumstances" that fix the value of a pleasure or pain, the seventh being its "extent; that is, the number of persons to whom it extends; or (in other words) who are affected by it," Bentham gives the summing procedure itself:

"Sum up all the values of all the pleasures on the one side, and those of all the pains on the other. The balance, if it be on the side of pleasure, will give the good tendency of the act upon the whole, with respect to the interests of that individual person... Take an account of the number of persons whose interests appear to be concerned; and repeat the above process with respect to each. Sum up the numbers expressive of the degrees of good tendency, which the act has, with respect to each individual, in regard to whom the tendency of it is good upon the whole... Take the balance which if on the side of pleasure, will give the general good tendency of the act, with respect to the total number or community of individuals concerned; if on the side of pain, the general evil tendency, with respect to the same community."

A footnote passes along a mnemonic verse, "framed," it says, not long after the first edition to help fix these points in memory, which compresses the whole scheme and closes with: "Such pains avoid, whichever be thy view: / If pains must come, let them extend to few."

I can follow each individual step, but I can't work out what the procedure actually commits Bentham to in a dust-specks-shaped case. Does treating "extent" as just one more quantity to sum mean that a large enough number of people always outweighs any amount of pain concentrated in one person, no matter how small each individual's share, or does something in how he individuates "a pleasure" or "a pain" put a floor under how small a pain can be and still count at all? And is the closing couplet's advice to keep unavoidable pain concentrated "to few" actually bearing on that trade-off, or is it just restating the same arithmetic for an easier case (same pain, fewer or more people) that leaves the real dust-specks question untouched? I'd like an actual walk-through of the mechanics, not the usual one-line gloss about utilitarians "shutting up and multiplying."
