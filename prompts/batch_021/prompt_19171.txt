I co-host a small philosophy podcast, and next month's episode features a philosopher of mind who defends illusionism about phenomenal consciousness: the view that there is no phenomenal "what-it's-likeness" over and above certain functional, representational states, which merely represent themselves, via introspection, as having a raw and ineffable qualitative character. While preparing for the conversation -- rereading Dennett's old "Quining Qualia" essay alongside some of the responses to Frankish's 2016 Journal of Consciousness Studies special issue on illusionism -- I noticed that our guest has a reliable way of handling the standard "who's being fooled?" objection: the worry that calling something an illusion presupposes a subject to whom it seems a certain way, and that seeming is itself the phenomenal fact the theory was supposed to dissolve. Her stock reply is that a representation need not have the properties it represents, "any more than my thought of a unicorn needs a horn," after which she tends to move briskly on to the next question.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that don't let her simply restate the view or lean on that analogy unchallenged. I'd like a run of three or four questions that press the unicorn analogy progressively harder -- for instance, on the disanalogy between a representation we inspect from the outside, like a photograph, and an introspective seeming that is supposed to be lived through from the inside, which is exactly the feature that made phenomenal consciousness puzzling in the first place. Please also work in one question connecting her position to what David Chalmers calls the "meta-problem of consciousness," one asking her to place her own view on the spectrum between "weak" illusionism (which still grants some deflated form of phenomenal reality) and the more radical "strong" illusionism she is associated with, and one asking why introspection should be considered radically unreliable in this one domain specifically rather than generally untrustworthy. For each question, add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and close with a question about what empirical or phenomenological finding, if any, would lead her to abandon illusionism altogether.
