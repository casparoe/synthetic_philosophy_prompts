I work on backend systems that increasingly hand off consequential decisions -- fraud flags, content takedowns -- to models that learn their own rules rather than execute ones we wrote by hand, and an argument with a colleague sent me down a rabbit hole into the philosophy literature on what's called the "responsibility gap." As I understand it, the original version of this argument (Andreas Matthias, writing in the early 2000s) was specifically about learning systems: because no person actually authored the rule the system ends up following, nobody has the combination of foreknowledge and control we normally require before holding someone responsible for a bad outcome, so blame for the harm has nowhere left to land. But philosophers writing about bureaucracies had a name for responsibility getting lost among many contributors long before machine learning existed -- the "problem of many hands" -- and a fair amount of what I've read since seems to assume that framework already covers the machine-learning case, making "gap" talk mostly a rebrand of an old problem.

Write an essay that takes a side on this question: is the responsibility gap posed by learning systems something genuinely new -- not already handled by how we distribute responsibility across many hands in human organizations -- or is it a difference of degree dressed up as a difference in kind? Weigh the strongest arguments you can construct on both sides, and end with a definite conclusion rather than splitting the difference.
