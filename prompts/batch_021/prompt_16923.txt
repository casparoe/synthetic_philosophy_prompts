So I fell down a bit of a rabbit hole this week after my philosophy of mind seminar touched on Molyneux's problem -- the old thought experiment about whether someone born blind, who's learned to tell a cube from a sphere by touch, would be able to tell them apart just by looking the moment they gained sight. Turns out people have actually run real versions of this now, mostly through Pawan Sinha's Project Prakash program in India, which gives free sight-restoring surgery to kids and adults who've been blind since birth and then tests them right afterward. From what I've read, the newly-sighted subjects mostly couldn't match what they saw to what they'd previously felt in the first days after surgery, but got noticeably better within about a week, and seemed to lean alot more on motion cues than static shape early on.

Anyway, this got me wondering how the original philosophers who argued about this would've done as forecasters. Molyneux, Locke, and Berkeley all predicted the newly-sighted person would fail, though for pretty different theoretical reasons (Locke's stuff about visual judgment being learned, Berkeley's much stronger claim that touch and sight are totally heterogeneous), while Leibniz pushed back and thought the answer was yes, at least in principle, if the person reasoned it out geometrically.
