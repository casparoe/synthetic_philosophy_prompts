My friend Marcus mentioned that his six-year-old asked him how he knows dinosaurs were real if he's never seen one, and he basically said 'I read about it and I trust the people who wrote it down,' and that offhand comment somehow turned into a full-blown argument between two other friends at the table, Elena and Sam, about whether trusting what someone tells you can ever be rational on its own, or whether it always has to be backed up by something else. Neither of them has any philosophy background, and neither do I honestly (I write backend services for a living, so I kept privately reframing the whole thing as a code review question -- do you trust a PR because you personally verified every line, or because you trust whoever wrote it -- but I kept my mouth shut and just took notes on my phone). Here's roughly how it went, in order:

(1) Sam said trusting someone's say-so isn't enough by itself -- you're only rational to believe someone if you've got independent grounds, built from your own experience of the world (people getting caught lying, claims checking out later, etc), for thinking that kind of testimony tends to be accurate. So trust has to be earned as a conclusion from your own track record, not taken for free as a starting point. (2) Elena pushed back with the kid example: a six-year-old hasn't built up any track record like that at all, and yet it seems totally normal, not irrational, for her to believe her dad about dinosaurs -- if Sam were right, basically all of childhood learning would count as irrational, which seems backwards since that's how everyone learns most of what they know to begin with. (3) Sams comeback was that little kids aren't fully 'justified' in the strict sense, just not-yet-culpable, kind of like a new hire following a senior dev's instructions without being able to evaluate them yet -- whereas adults have accumulated a mountain of independent background evidence over a lifetime that people mostly tell the truth and get corrected when they don't, even if nobody's consciously tallying it up. (4) Elena said that 'independent evidence' is smuggling in the very thing it's supposed to avoid: almost everything in that mountain -- that liars get caught, that claims usually check out, even the words you'd need to evaluate any of it with -- you also got from other people telling you things, so it can't really be independent of testimony without the whole thing being circular. (5) Sam said perception gets him out of the circle: he doesn't need anybody's testimony to notice, firsthand, when a source contradicts itself or turns out to be wrong later, and that's enough of a non-testimonial foothold to bootstrap everything else off of. Elena wasn't buying it, but everyone wanted to get back to the game, so it just fizzled out unresolved.
