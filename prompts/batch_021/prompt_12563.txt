So this whole thing started as a dumb argument with my friend at a bar a few months back. We were splitting the check, I added it up in my head twice, got the same number both times, and felt really confident about it - but then she pointed out that pretty much every time I've been that confident after a couple drinks, I've still been wrong something like a third of the time (she keeps annoyingly precise track of stuff like this about me). And the weird part is, even after she said that, I didn't actually get less sure of my total - I was still just as confident in the number, while also believing she was basically right that my "confident after two beers" feeling isn't something I should trust. Sitting there holding both of those at once felt like it might be it's own special kind of irrational, even though I couldn't have told you exactly what the problem was.

Turns out there's a whole corner of philosophy about basically this situation. People call the second kind of thing (evidence about whether your own reasoning is any good right now, rather than evidence about the actual question) "higher-order evidence," and being stuck the way I was - fully believing something while also fully believing that you personally aren't being rational to believe it right now - apparently has its own name too, "epistemic akrasia" (akrasia normally being that willpower thing, like knowing the cake is bad for you and eating it anyway, except here it's applied to belief instead of action). From what I've read there's two real camps on this. One side says that combination is basically never okay, that you're required to actually bring your confidence down once you've got good reason to distrust your own process, otherwise you're defending a state that's not too far off from that Moore's paradox thing philosophers talk about ("it's raining, but I don't believe it's raining" being nuts to say out loud). The other side thinks that's too quick - that sometimes the careful, in-the-moment reasoning you actually did should just win out over a general statistical worry about yourself, and that always caving to the outside view would be its own kind of crazy, since then no amount of careful thinking could ever be enough to secure your own trust in yourself.

Could you write me a dialogue between two people holding those two positions, arguing it out over a case like my bar bill (or a cleaner version of it if there's some "standard" example philosophers actually use for this - I've got a hunch I might just be reinventing something that already exists)? I'd like them evenly matched, actually responding to each other's specific moves instead of just restating their own side, and ideally getting cornered by each other enough that it's clear by the end exactly where the real disagreement sits, or what each one would have to give up to fully convince the other.
