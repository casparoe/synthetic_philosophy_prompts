I spent thirty-five years as an experimental physicist before retiring, and for the past year I have been sitting in on a philosophy discussion circle at the local university, largely out of a curiosity my career never left room for. Relativity was my daily bread for decades, so when the group devoted a session to the metaphysics of time, I expected to find the terrain familiar. What I came away with instead was a long-running disagreement between two of the regulars -- Nadia, who trained formally in philosophy, and Colin, a retired engineer with an amateur physicist's instincts rather like my own -- that has left me genuinely unable to decide who had the better of it. I would value your independent judgment on their exchange, set out below as faithfully as I can reconstruct it.

(1) Colin opened by arguing that special relativity settles the dispute outright: because simultaneity is relative to an observer's inertial frame, an event that is still future for one observer can already be simultaneous with "now" for a second observer merely moving relative to the first, and since no frame is privileged, both sets of events must be equally real -- leaving presentism, which requires exactly one privileged, frame-independent present, flatly inconsistent with known physics. (2) Nadia answered that this inference moves too quickly: relativity fixes which events are calculated as simultaneous within a given frame, an operational fact about measurement, and that does not by itself settle which events are metaphysically real; a presentist can accept every empirical prediction of special relativity while still holding that a single frame is privileged as an undetectable metaphysical fact, a neo-Lorentzian move that reproduces Einstein's predictions exactly. (3) Colin replied that this was a rescue rather than a rebuttal: an undetectable privileged frame invoked for no reason beyond saving presentism, and admitting of no possible evidence either way, simply reintroduces the old ether under a new name. (4) Nadia countered that eternalism carries an unpaid debt of its own: if every moment is equally real and nothing in the world privileges the present, it owes an account of why experience so insistently represents time as passing -- events seeming to come into being, the present seeming to move -- rather than the present merely differing from other moments the way one place differs from another. (5) Colin answered that no further account is required, since "here" feels special to anyone located there without space having a privileged center, so "now" can feel special to anyone located there without time having a privileged moment; the sense of flow, on his view, is simply what it is like to process information from within a temporal position. (6) Nadia pressed back that the analogy fails exactly where it needs to succeed: nothing in spatial experience presents other places as coming into being the way temporal experience presents other times, so labeling passage "indexical" redescribes the phenomenon rather than explaining why time alone produces it.

They left matters there, each unpersuaded, and the exchange has stayed with me since.
