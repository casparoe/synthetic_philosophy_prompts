A few friends and I who met in grad school -- I'm the one who ended up in software engineering, the other two stayed in academia -- run a monthly video call where we work through a philosophy of science book together. We just finished the section of Popper's *Conjectures and Refutations* on verisimilitude, his attempt to make sense of the idea that a false theory can still be "closer to the truth" than another false theory, and it set off an argument between Naomi (finishing a philosophy PhD) and Felix (a physicist) that ran well past our usual hour. I'm not qualified to referee it myself, but I've kept turning it over since, so I'd like an outside verdict.

Felix opened with what seemed to him like an obviously true claim: (1) the old Bohr model of the atom -- a compact nucleus with electrons circling it in fixed orbits -- is closer to the truth than the plum-pudding model that preceded it, even though quantum mechanics has since shown both to be false. (2) Naomi said this is exactly the intuition Popper tried to make precise with his formal definition of verisimilitude, and that the definition is famous for collapsing: David Miller and Pavel Tichý independently showed in the 1970s that, on Popper's own definition, one false theory can never come out with more verisimilitude than another false theory -- the ordering only goes through if the "winning" theory happens to be true. So, strictly speaking, nothing licenses ranking Bohr above plum-pudding, however obvious that ranking feels. (3) Felix answered that this only discredits Popper's specific machinery, not the underlying idea: just compare each model's predictions -- energy levels, charge distribution -- against what quantum mechanics says is really there, using ordinary numerical distance, and Bohr comes out closer on any reasonable measure. (4) Naomi granted that similarity-based patches like this exist, but said they trade one problem for another, since nothing forces a choice of which variables to measure distance over, and redescribing the same physical setup in a different but equivalent parametrization can flip the ranking. (5) Felix called that a purely logical worry with no scientific bite, since nobody redescribes atoms in gerrymandered variables and every competent physicist agrees on which quantities matter. (6) Naomi's rejoinder was that the "quantities that matter" are exactly the ones quantum mechanics itself designates as fundamental, so Felix is judging older theories by borrowing the ontology of our current best theory -- a fine way to track progress relative to where we currently stand, but not the theory-independent notion of objective closeness to the truth that Popper was actually chasing, since none of us has independent access to the truth to measure real distance from it.

Neither of them gave ground, and nobody else on the call had anything useful to add. Could you go through moves (1) through (6) and say, for each, who has the better of it, rather than just handing down an overall winner? I'd also like to know whether there's some important consideration that both of them are overlooking.
