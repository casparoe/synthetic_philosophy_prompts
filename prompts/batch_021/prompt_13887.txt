So I went down a physics YouTube rabbit hole this weekend and ended up watching a bunch of videos about the string theory "landscape" problem, where apparently once you account for all the different ways you could curl up the extra dimensions, the theory ends up compatible with something like 10^500 different possible universes, and there's no real way to know which one (if any) is ours. Half the comments were arguing about whether this even counts as science anymore if nothing about it can be tested. That sent me down a second rabbit hole into philosophy of science, into this idea called "underdetermination of theory by evidence," which kept leading back to a guy named Pierre Duhem — French physicist and philosopher from around 1900, who was actually a thermodynamics guy pushing "energetics" against the atomists of his day, and who apparently was the first to really rigorously argue that you can never test one hypothesis in isolation, because any real test drags in a pile of background assumptions along with it, so when a prediction fails there's no purely logical way to know which piece is actually to blame. Honestly it reminded me of debugging some big distributed system at work — a test goes red and you genuinely can't tell from that alone whether it's your code, a flaky dependency, bad test data, or just the network; the failure by itself doesn't localize the fault.

Anyway, Duhem died in 1916, so he obviously never heard of quantum mechanics, let alone string theory or the multiverse stuff, which feels like its almost tailor-made for his thesis. So I'm curious what you think he'd actually say if you dropped him into the middle of the string landscape debate today. Would he see it as basically vindicating the instrumentalist stuff he already believed — that physical theories are just useful classifications of our experience, never literal descriptions of some hidden true structure underneath? Or would his very specific old objections to 19th-century atomism and mechanical ether models push him toward objecting to string theory for totally different reasons than the standard "it's unfalsifiable" complaint you hear from physicists now? I'd love it if you could actually reconstruct his reasoning in some depth instead of just going with "he'd probably be skeptical" — like, really walk me through how someone with his specific commitments would look at this.
