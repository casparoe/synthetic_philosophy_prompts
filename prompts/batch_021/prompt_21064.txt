As a journalist who covers formal theories of collective decision-making, I spend a fair amount of time sitting in on academic workshops that never quite make it into print. I went to one on voter turnout last month mostly for background on a longer piece about why rational-choice models keep failing to predict actual turnout, but the part that's stuck with me is an argument from the reception afterward, not anything from the formal session. The session's starting point was the familiar paradox of voting: in a genuinely competitive race, the odds that a single ballot actually decides the outcome are vanishingly small — on the order of one in several million even in a swing state — so on a plain expected-utility reckoning (probability of being decisive, times the benefit of your side winning, against the modest cost of showing up) a purely self-interested voter shouldn't bother. Two of the panelists, Sofia (a political economist) and Owen (a philosopher who works on decision theory), spent the better part of an hour afterward arguing about how seriously to take that conclusion, and I honestly couldn't tell you who won.

(1) Sofia argued that the calculation quietly assumes the only benefit worth counting is your own private stake in the outcome. Weight the interests of everyone the winning policy will actually affect, she said, and the relevant "benefit" term stops being your personal payoff and becomes the summed welfare gain across the whole electorate — a quantity that grows with the size of the electorate at roughly the rate the probability of being decisive shrinks, so the product needn't collapse to zero the way the textbook version implies. (2) Owen replied that this rescues the arithmetic only by importing a large, independent premise — that a voter owes strangers' welfare something close to the weight she gives her own — and that even granting it, the cancellation presupposes she actually knows which candidate would produce the larger aggregate benefit; build in realistic uncertainty about that, he said, and the very same vanishing-probability problem just reappears one level up. (3) Sofia countered that "this proves too much" isn't, by itself, an objection: if the expected-value math really does favor big, low-probability, high-stakes acts — voting, lobbying, one more donation to a large campaign — then it's our intuitions about which acts are "worth it" that should give way, not the math; people sympathetic to effective altruism bite exactly this bullet routinely. (4) Owen wasn't moved. He said there's a real difference between biting a bullet you can independently defend and doing so only to save a conclusion you wanted from the start, and that Sofia hadn't shown which camp her voter falls into. They wandered off into a separate argument about ranked-choice voting before either landed a clean hit.

Could you go through moves (1) through (4) and adjudicate them individually, rather than just declaring an overall winner — including whether Owen's charge in (4) that Sofia's move is "ad hoc" is actually fair to her? I'd also be curious whether there's something about the paradox of voting itself that both of them are overlooking, independent of who wins this particular exchange.
