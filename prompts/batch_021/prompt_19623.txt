I am writing a contemporary novel set inside a worker-owned software cooperative, and I want one scene to stage a genuine philosophical disagreement between two of its founding members. Aviva argues that by distributing every dollar of surplus among the people who generate it, the cooperative has done what matters most: abolished exploitation in Marx's sense, since no class stands between workers and the value they create. Whatever discontent still lingers -- the sprint deadlines, the client-driven backlog, the sense that the product answers to the market rather than to any of them -- she treats as leftover alienation, a separate and comparatively minor problem that better job design could still fix. Marcus denies that the two can be pulled apart so neatly. On his reading, Marx treats exploitation as the economic symptom of a deeper estrangement -- from the activity of labor, from its product, from one another, from what Marx called species-being -- so a firm that still leaves workers governed by market discipline and a rigid division of tasks has not touched that estrangement, whatever it has done to the books. He presses Aviva to say what would even count as evidence against her view: could a firm be wholly non-exploitative by the numbers and still be a thoroughgoing engine of alienation, or is that combination incoherent? Write their conversation so that each answers the other's strongest objection rather than simply restating their own position. Ground the exchange in concrete features of the cooperative -- who decides what to build, how tasks are assigned, what happens to a member who wants to leave, how the surplus is actually calculated and split -- and don't let either one land a clean, final blow: I want the disagreement sharpened by the end, not resolved.
