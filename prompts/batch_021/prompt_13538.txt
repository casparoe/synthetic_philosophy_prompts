A friend of mine who works in machine learning and I spent most of dinner last night arguing about the language of thought hypothesis. He thinks the whole program is basically undercut by the success of deep learning: if a network with no combinatorial syntax can still produce systematic-looking behavior, why posit an inner Mentalese at all? I kept coming back to Fodor and Pylyshyn's own line that connectionist networks are at best a story about implementation, not a genuine rival to a classical cognitive architecture, and I also brought up something he hadn't encountered before and found pretty startling: that Fodor's radical concept nativism has him on record holding that concepts like DOORKNOB and CARBURETOR are, in some sense, innate. Neither of us budged.

I co-run a small podcast attached to a university philosophy-of-mind reading group, and next month we're recording an episode with a philosopher who defends a broadly Fodorian language-of-thought view, including radical nativism about lexical concepts. Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that push past the textbook statement of the position? I would like at least one question on whether the systematicity and productivity arguments are actually doing empirical work as an inference to the best explanation, or whether they quietly smuggle in an a priori demand that any adequate theory of cognition must meet; at least one that forces her to actually defend the innateness of concepts like CARBURETOR rather than just gesturing at the "no learning without a prior hypothesis space" argument; at least one on what, precisely, would make a connectionist model a genuine rival to the language of thought rather than "merely" an implementation of one, given that Fodor and Pylyshyn already conceded the implementation point; and at least one on how the view is meant to handle cases of apparent conceptual change, in children or in the history of science, that look like more than just the triggering of a pre-existing concept. For each, please add a short follow-up question keyed to the reply she is most likely to give.
