A friend and I spent most of dinner last night arguing about whether debate is really a plausible route to scalable oversight, and I'd like a more dispassionate read on my side of it before I concede anything. Below are the notes I wrote up afterward; I'd appreciate comments on where the reasoning holds up and where it doesn't, especially on the third point.

1. The core pitch, as I understand it from Irving, Christiano, and Amodei's original proposal: if dishonest answers are harder to defend under cross-examination than honest ones, then a judge who cannot solve a problem directly can still reliably pick out the truth by watching two more capable debaters attack each other's arguments. The motivation is meant to be more than psychological — optimal debate is supposed to let a bounded verifier adjudicate problems that are, in a precise sense, harder than anything it could check unaided, much as an interactive proof lets a weak verifier be convinced of claims from a far larger complexity class.

2. My friend's response was that this basically reduces alignment to an interrogation problem: so long as the honest debater can always produce some short, checkable pointer to the flaw in any dishonest claim, the judge never needs to understand the whole domain, only each local exchange — and the scheme scales exactly as far as "find the local flaw" scales.

3. My pushback was the obfuscated-arguments worry that Barnes and Christiano raised in the years after the original paper: a sufficiently resourceful dishonest debater can build a case out of a vast number of individually plausible steps with the real error buried somewhere inside, so that an honest opponent can know an error exists without being able to isolate and cash it out within whatever budget the judge is willing to spend. If that's right, debate's guarantees rest on assumptions about judge patience and budget with no clean counterpart in the complexity-theoretic story, and point 1 is less reassuring than it sounds.

4. What I couldn't answer at the table: I recall recent debate experiments finding that judges got more accurate as debaters got more persuasive, and that debate beat single-advocate "consultancy" setups even though nobody had actually solved the obfuscation problem. I don't know whether that's evidence the theoretical worry doesn't bite much with current models, or just that we haven't yet built debaters capable of exploiting it.

Is my objection in (3) genuinely in tension with the motivation in (1), or am I conflating an in-principle result about optimal, unbounded debate with a practical claim the original proposal never made? And is there a standard response to the obfuscated-arguments problem, among people who actually work on this, that I'm missing?
