I'm a retired analytical chemist, and I've been spending my retirement finally reading the philosophy I never had time for during my working life. Started with Kripke's Naming and Necessity a few months back and have now moved on to some papers on "two-dimensional semantics," which seems to be the modern toolkit people use to unpack what Kripke was getting at with his necessary a posteriori truths. I think I've got the water example sorted: "water is H2O" feels like it could have gone either way (nobody before Lavoisier had any idea), but once you separate the primary intension -- how water gets picked out epistemically, before you know the chemistry -- from the secondary intension -- what water actually is once you fix which world is the actual one -- the identity turns out necessary after all. We were just empirically ignorant, not tracking some real metaphysical contingency.

Here's what's been bugging me for the last week or so, though. If that move works for water, shouldn't it work just as well for pain and brain states? Pain sure feels distinct from neurons doing their thing, but water felt distinct from two hydrogens and an oxygen too, until the chemistry got sorted out. So by the same logic, "pain is C-fiber firing" (or whatever we update the physiology to) should turn out to be just another necessary a posteriori identity, and being able to conceive of pain occurring without any particular brain state going on should be exactly as misleading as being able to conceive of water without H2O. And yet the very people who developed this two-dimensional apparatus, Chalmers especially, turn around and use it to argue that the mind-body case is fundamentally different in kind, and that the zombie thought experiment actually refutes physicalism rather then getting explained away the same way the water case did. Before I chalk this up to physicalism's critics moving the goalposts, is their an actual principled reason inside the two-dimensional framework itself for treating water/H2O and pain/C-fiber-firing so differently, or is that just special pleading dressed up in technical formalism?
