Okay so I've been kind of obsessed with watching old William Lane Craig debates on youtube for like the past month (zero philosophy background here, I'm just a guy who falls down rabbit holes at 1am instead of sleeping). From what I can piece together there's technically more than one "version" of the cosmological argument — theres the kalam one and then apparently a totally separate Leibniz "contingency" one that people bring up sometimes — but honestly they always struck me as just two ways of dressing up the same basic move: something can't pop into existence uncaused, everything's gotta have a cause somewhere down the line, and if you trace that chain back far enough you land on God.

So heres whats bugging me. In a bunch of these debates the physicist on the other side keeps bringing up stuff like eternal inflation, or Hawking's old "no boundary" idea, where the universe might not actually have a first moment at all — kind of like how theres no point thats "north of the north pole," the question just stops applying. And their whole move is basically: if there was never a beginning, then there was never anything that needed a cause in the first place, so the argument cant even get off the ground. That seems pretty airtight to me. So why do philosophers of religion — and I mean actual academics, not just apologists doing debate tours — keep acting like the cosmological argument survives just fine even if it turns out the universe is past-eternal with no starting point? Is that just moving the goalposts after the fact, or is there something about how these arguments are actually built that means at least one "version" was never resting on the beginning thing to begin with?
