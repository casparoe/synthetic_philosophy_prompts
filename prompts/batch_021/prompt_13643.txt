Over a forty-year career running a molecular biology lab, I used phrases like "the genetic code," "information encoded in DNA," and "instructions for building a protein" every day without ever pausing to ask whether they were meant literally. Since retiring I have joined a small reading group that works through papers in philosophy of biology, and I have discovered that the question of whether such talk is literally true is a live and rather sophisticated dispute, not an idle one. In particular, two clusters of argument have stayed with me. On one side, there is a fact familiar from my own field: the pairing of codons with amino acids is not fixed by any direct chemical affinity between them, but is mediated by transfer RNAs -- Crick's own "adaptors" -- and by the enzymes that charge them correctly, the aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases, so that the resulting code looks, in Crick's later phrase, like something of a "frozen accident" rather than a chemical necessity. Writers in what is now sometimes called "code biology," such as Marcello Barbieri, take exactly this arbitrariness to be the mark of a genuine, conventional code, in much the same sense that the pairing of a word with its meaning is conventional; combine this with John Maynard Smith's argument that natural selection can occupy the role a designer's intentions occupy in fixing the meaning of an ordinary code, and one gets a serious case for saying DNA carries semantic information, not merely correlational information in the thin Shannon sense. On the other side, I have also worked through the skeptics. Sahotra Sarkar and Paul Griffiths both argue, in different ways, that once ordinary molecular causation has been subtracted there is no remaining semantic content to speak of, and developmental systems theorists following Susan Oyama add that if reliable correlation with phenotypic outcomes across generations were enough to make something an information-bearer, maternal cytoplasm, intracellular architecture, and stable features of the embryonic environment should all qualify too, even though we never call them codes -- which suggests that singling out DNA reflects biological tradition more than principled argument (what is sometimes called the "parity thesis").

I raised this over dinner recently with a former postdoc of mine, an unreconstructed gene's-eye-view enthusiast, who simply could not see how anyone could deny that DNA carries information, and I found I could not do justice to the other side on the spot. What I would like from you is a dialogue between two philosophers of biology who hold these opposing positions and argue the matter out properly, rather than talking past one another: one maintaining that DNA is a code in a fairly robust, literal sense, the other maintaining that this is at bottom a useful metaphor doing no explanatory work that could not be done by ordinary biochemistry alone. Please have them engage directly with each other's strongest moves -- the arbitrariness of the codon-amino acid mapping, the appeal to natural selection as a substitute for a designer's intentions, the distinction between Shannon information and semantic information, and the parity thesis from developmental systems theory -- and have each respond to what the other has actually just said rather than restating prepared positions. Neither side should be a pushover for the other; both should be written as equally sharp defenders of their view. I would be especially glad if the exchange managed to isolate more precisely where the two of them really disagree, or where one concedes a specific point while holding their ground overall, rather than ending in a simple standoff.
