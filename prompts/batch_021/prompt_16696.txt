Quick context: our team does blameless postmortems, so the whole exercise is about finding the "real cause" without pinning it on one person, and that's basically what set them off. Marcus's take was that there's no such thing as an organizational or "systemic" cause floating above the individual people involved -- in this case, one engineer skipped a review because he was in a hurry, another approved a deploy on a Friday afternoon, and a third had muted an alert that had been noisy before, and once you've listed everyone's individual actions and reasons, there's nothing left over to explain. Calling it "a culture problem," he said, is just a vague way of bundling a bunch of individual choices together. Renee pushed back that those three calls only made sense given an unspoken, team-wide norm about what counts as acceptable risk, and that norm isn't a fact about any one of them -- it lives in everyone's mutual expectations about how everyone else will act, so no pile of individual-level descriptions, however long, will capture it.

Marcus came back with something he'd read about years ago -- Schelling's old segregation model, where wildly segregated neighborhoods show up on a grid even when every simulated person only has a mild preference against being a small minority on their block -- and said this proves you can always generate this kind of nobody-intended-it, group-level pattern out of purely individual-level rules, no extra team-level ingredient required. Renee's comeback was that the model actually assumes the very thing she was pointing at instead of explaining it away: somebody has to hand the model the categories people sort by and the threshold for "too few of my kind here," and both of those come from outside any individual's head, not from individual psychology. So at best, she said, the model shows individual rules can produce a pattern once you feed it group-level ingredients, not that the group-level stuff was dispensable all along.
