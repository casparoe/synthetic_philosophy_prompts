A philosophy reading group I'm part of recently spent a session working through the nonidentity problem from population ethics, and the shape of the argument has stuck with me since. As I understand it, the puzzle starts from something that looks like plain common sense: an act harms someone only if it leaves that person worse off than they would otherwise have been. The trouble is that many of our most consequential choices, about how to raise a child, settle a territory, or manage a shared resource across generations, don't just make existing people's lives better or worse; they help determine who exists in the first place, since even a small change in timing or circumstance can mean that a different person is conceived rather than the same person simply living under different conditions. So if a community adopts a policy that leaves its distant descendants with harder lives than they might have had under some alternative, it seems we can't say those descendants have been made worse off by the choice, since under the alternative policy they, specifically, would never have existed at all; someone else would have been born in their place. And yet the choice still strikes us as wrong. I find something genuinely vertiginous about the idea of a wrong that wrongs no one in particular.

I'd like you to write a short story that sits inside this puzzle rather than explaining it from the outside. Picture some small, bounded community, a valley settlement, an island, a sealed colony, whichever setting appeals to you, whose founders once faced an irreversible choice between a strategy that let them flourish quickly at real cost to the community's long-term prospects, and a slower, costlier strategy that would have meant a smaller and, crucially, different population coming after them. Set the story enough generations later that the consequences of that founding choice have simply become how things are, and follow a character, perhaps someone piecing the decision together from old records or oral history, as they work toward the unsettling recognition that they cannot coherently resent the founders on their own behalf, since any different choice would not have given them an easier life; it would only have meant that someone else existed in their place instead. I'd rather see that thought worked through concretely, in incident and character, than resolved into a tidy moral, so feel free to leave the ending open.
