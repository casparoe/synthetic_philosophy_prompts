Program equilibrium, the framework introduced by Moshe Tennenholtz in which players in a game submit programs rather than actions directly, and those programs can take one another's source code as input before producing a move, comes with an associated folk theorem: once this kind of mutual transparency is available, essentially any individually rational, feasible outcome, including full mutual cooperation in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, can be sustained in equilibrium, typically via constructions as simple as a program that cooperates only when it detects that the opponent's code exactly matches its own. Later work building on this, using tools from provability logic to let agents reason about programs that aren't literally identical without collapsing into infinite mutual simulation, has pushed the idea further toward something that looks less like a mathematical curiosity and more like a genuine mechanism for escaping non-cooperative outcomes.

I ran into this literature through a colleague who works on multi-agent reinforcement learning, and I've been trying to work out since then how much weight it can actually bear once you leave the idealized setting. Concretely, imagine two AI agents negotiating on behalf of their respective owners over something mundane, such as the price of a used car or how to split a shared bill, where each agent's underlying policy (though not necessarily its literal weights or source code) could in principle be disclosed to the other. Could you describe the most important implications of program equilibrium and its folk theorem for whether this sort of mutual transparency between negotiating agents should actually be expected to produce cooperative, efficient outcomes rather than ones that are exploitative or that simply fail to converge? I am particularly interested in which features of the formal result, for instance its reliance on exact code-matching or on proof systems of bounded strength, are essential to the cooperation it delivers, and which are idealizations that make the theorem a poor guide to what real, imperfectly transparent software agents would actually do.
