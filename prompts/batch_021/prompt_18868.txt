After a session of my graduate seminar on formal epistemology, two of my PhD students stayed behind to keep arguing about imprecise credences, and I would value a third opinion on the exchange that followed.

The setup under discussion: an agent faces an urn containing 100 balls, red and black in a ratio about which they have no information whatsoever, and the question is how they should represent their credence that the next ball drawn is red.

(1) Priya argued that representing this credence as a single number, such as 0.5, would misdescribe the agent's evidence: nothing in that evidence favors a precise, symmetric probability over any other, so the honest representation is an interval, or set of probabilities, spanning the whole range from 0 to 1.

(2) Marcus objected that an interval-valued credence cannot actually guide choice. He described two bets -- one that pays well if the ball drawn is red and costs a little if it is black, the other structured the mirror-image way for black -- such that each bet, taken alone, is a net loss under some distributions within the interval, yet the two bets taken together guarantee a profit no matter the true ratio of red to black. An agent who follows the natural policy of declining any single bet that is not a sure gain across their whole interval ends up declining both bets and forfeiting that guaranteed profit. An agent who can be talked out of free money like that is not rational, whatever we call their credal state.

(3) Priya replied that this only refutes evaluating bets one at a time. Nothing stops the agent from noticing that the pair, taken as a package, guarantees a profit under every distribution in the interval, and accepting the package as a whole. The moral is that the agent should reason about bundles of bets rather than each bet in isolation, not that their credence should be sharp.

(4) Marcus answered that "reason about the whole bundle" is a bigger concession than it sounds. In his example the two bets happened to be offered together, but in general the agent has no advance knowledge of which future bets they will face; requiring bundled reasoning would mean acting, now, as though a complete contingency plan for wagers not yet on the table had already been worked out. That is either not feasible, or it smuggles back in something that functions exactly like a sharp credence under another name.

Neither of them would concede the point. Could you go through moves (1) through (4) and assess who has the better of it at each step, rather than simply declaring an overall winner?
