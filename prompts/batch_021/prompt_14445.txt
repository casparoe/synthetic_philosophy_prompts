Standard coherence arguments for expected utility maximization hold that any agent whose preferences violate the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms can be money-pumped -- exploited through a sequence of trades into a sure loss -- so that rationality requires satisfying those axioms, completeness included. It has been argued that an agent with incomplete preferences (one that sometimes has no preference at all between two options, rather than being indifferent between them) is just as vulnerable to a money pump as one with cyclic preferences. A natural reply is that such an agent can immunize itself with a simple policy: never pay anything to switch away from its current option unless the alternative is strictly better, thereby avoiding exploitation without ever resolving its incompleteness. Write an essay assessing whether this policy genuinely defeats the money-pump argument for completeness or merely relocates the underlying irrationality -- for instance, into a dynamic inconsistency, or into an unprincipled sensitivity to whatever option the agent happens to start from. Reach and defend a definite verdict, and state briefly what it implies about whether a sufficiently capable, reflective agent must eventually converge on maximizing expected utility.
