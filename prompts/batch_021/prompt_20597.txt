In the Tahāfut al-Falāsifa, Al-Ghazālī argued that there is no necessary connection between what we call a cause and its effect: when fire touches cotton and the cotton burns, it is not the fire's own nature that produces the burning, but God, who creates the burning directly on the occasion of the contact, in accordance with a habitual pattern that He could interrupt at will. Suppose Al-Ghazālī had lived to learn of quantum mechanics, and in particular of the mainstream view that certain events—such as the decay of a given radioactive atom at a given moment—are not merely unpredictable in practice but are genuinely undetermined by any prior state of the universe. Would he have welcomed this indeterminacy as scientific vindication of his account of causation, or are there considerations internal to his own theological position that should have made him wary of claiming quantum physics as an ally?
