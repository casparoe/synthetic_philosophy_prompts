Something that happened in my lab a few weeks ago has been nagging at me, and I would like help thinking it through properly rather than simply deferring to whichever of my labmates argued more forcefully at the time. I am a second-year PhD student in social psychology, and I had been running a study on whether a small change in how a request is phrased affects how much money people are willing to hand over to a stranger in a simple economic game. My advisor and I originally planned to recruit about sixty participants, but when the effect was trending in the right direction without quite clearing the conventional significance threshold, she suggested I keep collecting data in batches of twenty and check the result again each time. I did this until, at a hundred participants, the result finally crossed p < .05. Everyone was pleased, until I described exactly how I had gotten that result at the following week's lab meeting.

One labmate, who has become something of a methodological conscience for the lab after spending a summer reading about the discipline's struggles to make published results hold up, said that stopping this way makes the .05 essentially meaningless: because I kept looking, and would have continued had the early batches not worked out, the actual probability of hitting on a "significant" result somewhere along the way is much higher than five percent, regardless of whether the effect is real. A second labmate, midway through a rotation in a lab that approaches statistics quite differently, disagreed just as forcefully: she argued that whatever I would have done under some other, hypothetical run of the experiment is beside the point, since the only thing that can bear on how strongly my data support the hypothesis is the data I actually collected, not the space of experiments I did not run; treating my stopping point as though it contaminates the evidence, on her view, rests on a mistaken picture of what evidence even is.

Both arguments strike me as genuinely compelling, which is unsettling, since they cannot both be entirely right, and I still need to write a methods section defending what I did before I meet with my committee next month. Is one of my labmates simply correct and the other confused, or is this a case where two careful thinkers end up at odds because they do not actually agree on what makes data count as evidence for a hypothesis to begin with? I would like to understand the real structure of the disagreement well enough to know whether, and how, I should be doing things differently going forward.
