I serve on my university's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC), and a protocol has come in that our charter doesn't really anticipate. A cephalopod lab wants approval to study injury response and short-term analgesic preference in octopuses: animals are given a minor tissue injury, then allowed to choose between an enclosure compartment infused with a local anesthetic and one without, as a behavioral test for whether their reaction to injury has a pain-like, affective component rather than being purely reflexive. If the subject were a rat or a zebrafish, this protocol would automatically trigger our full review process: justification under the 3Rs, a veterinary welfare assessment, mandatory analgesia unless scientifically justified otherwise, and a defined humane endpoint. But both the Animal Welfare Act and the PHS Policy that ultimately define our committee's jurisdiction restrict "animal" to vertebrates, so cephalopods fall outside our legal mandate entirely -- we could decline to review this protocol at all, and no external body would ever second-guess that decision. Some members want to review it anyway: the whole reason a protocol like this exists is that there is now a serious, published case for cephalopod nociception and possibly valenced pain, strong enough that the UK's 2022 Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act extended statutory protection to cephalopods and decapod crustaceans on the strength of a commissioned scientific review. Other members think voluntary review is exactly the wrong move: our committee's composition, expertise, procedures, and appeal rights were all calibrated to a vertebrate-based federal framework; improvising a welfare standard with no regulatory benchmark behind it is arbitrary and effectively unappealable; and if uncertain sentience is reason enough to review, the crickets and fruit flies used in at least three other labs on campus would seem to qualify by the same logic -- something the committee has neither the expertise nor the capacity to take on.

I need to circulate a short written recommendation before our meeting in three weeks. Please draft it. It should reach a concrete recommendation for this specific protocol -- proceed without review, review it and under what standard, or something else -- state a principle for which future non-vertebrate protocols would or would not trigger the same treatment so we are not improvising case by case, and take seriously the strongest version of the argument for staying inside our existing vertebrate-based mandate rather than extending review on the committee's own initiative.
