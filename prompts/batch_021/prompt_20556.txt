I've spent the last year or so drifting from backend work into interpretability tooling at my company, mostly wiring up sparse autoencoders (SAEs) to decompose activations from our internal models into cleaner, more "monosemantic" directions, since individual neurons tend to fire for a grab-bag of unrelated concepts (the usual superposition story: the network has more features than dimensions to spend on them, so it packs them into nearly-orthogonal directions and eats some interference). After a long, somewhat heated Slack argument with a coworker about whether the "features" our SAEs turn up are actually in the network or just a convenient fiction we're imposing on it, I wrote up some notes to organize my own thinking, and I'd like an outside, philosophically literate read on them. I have no formal philosophy training, so please don't assume I'm using words like "realism" or "representation" correctly.

Here's the core of it. When we clamp a feature direction up or down and the model's behavior shifts exactly the way you'd predict from the feature's supposed meaning (the now-famous public example being Anthropic's demo where they turned up a "Golden Gate Bridge" feature until Claude couldn't stop bringing up the bridge), that feels like real evidence the feature is there, in roughly the way physicists trust electrons are real partly because they can be manipulated to produce predicted effects, not merely because they show up in some equations. Two things bother me about leaning on that, though. First, if you train several SAEs on the exact same layer with different dictionary sizes, sparsity penalties, or random seeds, you typically get different, non-identical sets of features, and yet each dictionary supports its own successful interventions. If more than one incompatible decomposition "earns its keep" this way, is there really a fact about which features the network has, or does that plurality quietly sink the realist reading? Or is it more like choosing between equally valid bases in physics, which nobody thinks threatens realism about the underlying system? Second, I can't tell whether there's a genuine difference between (a) a direction that's simply a reliable lever we've found for predicting and steering outputs, and (b) a direction the network is actually using as an internal stand-in for something, the way a map is used as a map rather than merely correlating with terrain — or whether that's a distinction I've invented that doesn't cash out in anything checkable.

I'd appreciate comments on whether this reasoning actually engages with real, existing positions in philosophy of science or mind, whether I'm running together things that should be kept apart, and whether the "multiple decompositions" worry has a known answer or analogue elsewhere that I should know about. I'm not looking for the latest ML trivia here — feel free to reason from whatever you already know about interpretability rather than worrying about being current — I'm mainly interested in whether the underlying philosophical argument holds together or has an obvious hole I'm missing.
