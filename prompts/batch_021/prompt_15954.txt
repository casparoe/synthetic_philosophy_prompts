Cicero's *De Senectute* has been the subject of an old Less Wrong-adjacent reading group I'm part of; we've been working through classical texts that keep surfacing, usually as throwaway references, in the community's long-running arguments about aging and death. This round's text was written not long before Cicero's own killing on the triumvirate's orders, framed as a consolation dedicated to his friend Atticus, in which the elderly statesman Cato lectures two much younger men, Scipio and Laelius, on why old age isn't the calamity people assume. Cato's method is to take up four standard grievances in turn: that old age withdraws us from active life, weakens the body, empties life of pleasure, and sits too close to death. He works through each and argues it's overstated or beside the point, provided a person spent their earlier decades cultivating reason and character rather than appetite, and he insists that most of what gets blamed on old age is really the accumulated debt of a poorly lived youth. In the dialogue's closing stretch he goes further still, laying out reasons to think the soul outlasts the body, and adding that even if he's mistaken about that, death will turn out to be a dreamless rest rather than anything to dread.

What I can't stop chewing on is how Cato's framework would actually hold up against the specific modern proposal that this same intellectual community takes quite seriously, running from Aubrey de Grey's damage-repair program to Bostrom's parable of the dragon-tyrant, that senescence is a disease with a cure rather than a season of life to be reconciled with. Would Cicero read successful biomedical rejuvenation as the fulfillment of his own argument, since he's already committed to the claim that decline isn't intrinsic to old age and that a well-tended mind need not fade at all? Or would that read of him miss something load-bearing in the text, given how much of Cato's consolation depends on decline and death being natural, timely, and the sort of thing a well-ordered soul makes peace with rather than defeats outright? And separately, what would he make of schemes to preserve a mind indefinitely through technological means, given that he thought he already had an argument for the soul's persistence that owed nothing to engineering? I'd like your best reconstruction of where Cicero would actually land, built out of the dialogue's own argumentative structure rather than a generic gesture at "ancient wisdom about accepting mortality."
