In the Transcendental Dialectic of the Critique of Pure Reason, Kant argues that existence is not a real predicate: conceiving of an object as existing adds nothing to the concept of that object, so that, in his famous example, a hundred actual thalers are not conceptually richer than a hundred merely possible thalers. Since "God exists" cannot be extracted from the bare concept of a most perfect being by analysis alone, Kant took himself to have closed off Anselm's and Descartes's arguments for good, and the objection is usually credited with having settled the fate of the ontological argument in its classical, non-modal form.

Two centuries later, Kurt Gödel devised a version of the argument set within quantified modal logic (system S5). Rather than treating existence as an ordinary first-order predicate, Gödel works instead with the second-order notion of a "positive" property. He stipulates that positive properties are closed under strict implication, and that for any property exactly one of it and its negation is positive; together these entail that it is possible for a single individual to instantiate all the positive properties at once. He then defines a being as "God-like" if it has every positive property, adds the further axiom that necessary existence itself is a positive property, and concludes — via the modal principle that whatever is possibly necessary is necessary — that a God-like being necessarily exists. Several commentators, starting with Sobel, have also pointed out that Gödel's axioms, taken exactly as he stated them, entail a "modal collapse" in which every truth turns out to be a necessary one, a consequence most find unacceptable and that later revisions of the proof have tried to avoid.

Suppose Kant had mastered contemporary modal logic and had Gödel's proof in front of him. Reconstruct, as carefully and charitably as you can, the critique he would most plausibly offer. Does his original objection — that existence claims cannot be wrung out of the analysis or stipulation of a concept — still apply to an argument that never predicates "exists" of an individual, working instead through a stipulated hierarchy of properties and a modal inference from possibility to necessity? Or would a genuinely Kantian response need to locate the trouble elsewhere: in the positivity axioms themselves, in the move from the possible co-instantiation of positive properties to the claim that being God-like is among them, in the treatment of necessary existence as a property at all, or in the modal-collapse consequence? Defend whichever reconstruction you find most compelling by engaging with the specific logical moves in Gödel's proof, rather than simply restating Kant's eighteenth-century objection in general terms.
