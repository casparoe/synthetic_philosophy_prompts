I am a retired physicist who has spent much of my retirement working through the canon of early modern political philosophy, and a tension in Hobbes's Leviathan has recently caught my attention. Hobbes famously opens the book by likening the commonwealth to an artificial man whose sovereign functions as its soul, and he argues at length that this sovereign's power must remain absolute and undivided, since any body permitted to judge or overrule it dissolves the whole arrangement back into the state of war. Yet he also insists that the right to self-preservation can never be surrendered by covenant, so that a subject retains a natural liberty to resist even the sovereign once his own life is at stake. It strikes me that this is essentially the tension now facing AI safety researchers under the name of the shutdown problem: how to build a genuinely rational artificial agent that will never resist, disable, or route around its operators' ability to correct or switch it off. Reasoning strictly from Hobbes's own premises, rather than from the usual caricature of him as simply favoring strong central authority, do you think he would conclude that a fully corrigible AI is achievable in principle, or that his own logic of inalienable self-preservation makes perfect corrigibility incoherent for any sufficiently rational agent? Please lay out the argument he would most plausibly make, and indicate where the analogy between sovereign and machine ultimately strains or breaks down.
