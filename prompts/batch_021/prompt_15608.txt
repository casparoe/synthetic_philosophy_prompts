A PhD student in political philosophy currently drafting a dissertation chapter on the argument from the original position. A few weeks ago I found myself arguing about this with a friend who is doing a PhD in economics, and the conversation left me feeling like the standard seminar-room gloss on the veil of ignorance — "it guarantees impartiality, full stop" — badly undersells how contested Rawls's actual argument for maximin reasoning is once you look at it from a decision-theoretic angle. She kept pressing the Harsanyi-style line that the parties should just treat every social position as equally likely and maximize expected utility, which points toward something like average utilitarianism rather than the difference principle, and I realized I could gesture at Rawls's side of the exchange without really being able to reconstruct it as a live argument rather than two people asserting incompatible intuitions at each other.

So I'd like your help writing a dialogue set inside the original position itself: two parties who agree on everything about their situation (neither knows their talents, class position, or conception of the good) but disagree about which decision rule should govern their choice of principles. One party reasons via maximin, ranking candidate arrangements by how the worst-off position fares under each, and is being pushed toward the difference principle. The other insists that, absent any reason to think one position more likely than another, the rational move is to assign each position equal probability and maximize expected utility instead, which points toward something closer to an average-utilitarian standard. Please have them actually work through the pressure points rather than trading slogans: whether their situation is one of genuine uncertainty, in which assigning determinate probabilities at all would be arbitrary, or one of risk, in which equiprobability is the principled default; Rawls's "strains of commitment" point that a principle has to be one they could still affirm on discovering they occupy the worst position, given that this is a single irreversible choice about the shape of an entire life rather than a gamble that could be repeated or diversified; and the objection most closely associated with Harsanyi's own critique, that lexical maximin commits them to accepting almost any aggregate loss to everyone else for the sake of a marginal gain to the worst-off position, which is hard to square with how rational agents are normally taken to handle risk.

Please make both parties equally sharp — I don't want either position played as a strawman — and have them respond to each other's specific moves rather than restating their opening position in slightly different words each round. It's fine, and probably realistic, if the exchange doesn't end in a clean resolution; I'd rather see the real structure of the disagreement laid out than a forced verdict.
