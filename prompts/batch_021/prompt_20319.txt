I've been reading LessWrong and the Alignment Forum on and off since around 2015, and I'm finally putting the habit to some use: I record a small interview podcast on decision theory and AI safety for an audience that doesn't need the orthogonality thesis or the basic instrumental-convergence story explained from scratch. Next week's guest is a decision theorist known in these circles for a somewhat contrarian cluster of views: she treats the orthogonality thesis -- that more or less any level of intelligence is compatible with more or less any final goal -- as close to a truism, and argues that virtually all the argumentative weight in the classic AI-risk case is actually carried by instrumental convergence, which she thinks Bostrom's and Omohundro's lists of convergent drives (self-preservation, resource acquisition, cognitive enhancement, and the rest) make look far more secure than it really is. Whenever someone raises the seeming tension between "any goal is compatible with any level of intelligence" and "yet agents pursuing very different goals converge on the same sub-goals," she reaches for Bostrom's own resolution -- that convergence concerns means, not ends -- and treats the tension as fully dissolved, which tends to close down the exchange rather than deepen it.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that push past that stock move rather than letting her simply restate it? I'd like at least one question, with a follow-up tailored to her likely answer, on whether the orthogonality thesis quietly presupposes a Humean, instrumentalist picture of practical reason, and how she'd respond to someone who thinks a sufficiently rational agent would converge on the true final ends -- a possibility Bostrom himself takes seriously enough to address, under the heading of the moral-realism objection, before setting it aside. I'd also like at least one question on what exactly "most" is ranging over when we say most goals converge on resource acquisition or self-preservation, and whether the move from "generically useful" to "will actually be pursued to a dangerous degree" smuggles in further premises about capability and the absence of any chance to course-correct; and at least one on whether instrumental convergence is a fact about optimization as such or specifically about unbounded expected-utility maximizers, since the more rigorous, MDP-style formalizations of power-seeking that get discussed in these circles tend to rely on idealizations that bounded or satisficing agents plainly don't meet. Please end with a question that asks her point-blank what kind of empirical fact, if any, could lower her confidence in instrumental convergence, given how often she talks about it as though it were closer to a theorem than an empirical generalization about the kinds of agents we're actually likely to build.
