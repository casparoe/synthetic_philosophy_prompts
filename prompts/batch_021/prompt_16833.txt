A colleague of mine in the department, Miriam, died very suddenly three weeks ago - a stroke, sixty-one years old, no warning at all. She'd spent her whole career on formal epistemology and decision theory (the Ramsey-de Finetti stuff on subjective probability was basically her bread and butter), and the one bit of philosophy she brought up at every single pub night, completely unprompted, was Frank Ramsey's stray paragraph on the meaning of life: the one where he says his picture of the world is drawn in perspective rather than to scale, so the stars end up no bigger than threepenny bits, and that he flatly refuses to feel humbled by the vastness of the universe, because size just isn't the kind of property that could be impressive - thinking and loving are, and stars can't do either. She used to say that paragraph should replace the reading at her own funeral, and she wasn't really joking. I've been asked to give the address at the department memorial next week, and I want to build the whole thing around exactly that passage, since it was basically her creed, but I'm genuinely stuck on how to pull it off.

Here's my problem. Half the room will be logicians and decision theorists who knew her work and will wince if I flatten Ramsey's actual argument into vauge "it's the little things that matter" mush. The other half will be her sister, her partner, and a bunch of undergrads who don't care about subjective probability and just want to feel like the room is honouring the person they loved. And underneath both of those is a harder problem still: Ramsey wrote that passage as a cheerful, healthy young man who had no idea he'd be dead himself within a few years, so there's something almost dangerous about using his refusal-to-be-humbled-by-scale as consolation at an actual funeral, for someone who really is gone and isn't coming back, rather than as a bit of undergraduate bravado. Could you write me the eulogy? I want Ramsey's specific argument - not cosmic significance, not size, but thought and love - to be doing the real work of comforting the room, rather than sitting there as a quotation I nod at before moving on to the usual eulogy platitudes.
