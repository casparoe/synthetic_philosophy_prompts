Over a forty-year career as a field entomologist, much of it devoted to the behavior of parasitoid wasps, I grew closely acquainted with the sort of spectacle that unsettled Darwin: a caterpillar paralyzed but kept alive, serving as fresh meat for a wasp grub that consumes it from the inside out over several days. Since retiring, I have finally had the leisure to read philosophy in a sustained way, and a small discussion group I joined at the local library has spent the last two months working through the literature on the problem of evil. We went through Alvin Plantinga's free will defense in some detail, and it was presented to us, correctly as far as I can tell, as the argument that finally persuaded most philosophers, including skeptics such as J. L. Mackie in his later work, that God's existence is not strictly inconsistent with the existence of evil, since it may be that every possible free creature suffers from what Plantinga calls "transworld depravity": there is, in other words, no world available for God to actualize that contains significantly free creatures and yet no moral evil.

This came as a real relief to me, because the difficulty that had actually dogged me throughout my career was never especially the wrongdoing of human beings, which I could see might be redeemed by the value of freedom, but rather the sheer scale of suffering that predation and parasitism visit on sentient creatures with no share in that freedom at all. What I would now like help with is extending the free will defense properly to this case: given that it has settled the logical problem of evil, what is the accepted account of how the same reasoning covers a creature like the wasp's caterpillar host, which by all appearances suffers a great deal despite having no free will of its own to exercise well or badly?
