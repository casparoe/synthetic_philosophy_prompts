I'm about two-thirds into a novel where two civilizations that will never share a light cone (they're on separate branches of a vast multiverse, basically) figure out they can still reason about each other well enough to negotiate, and threaten each other, acausally — think Roko's basilisk but scaled up to whole societies instead of a single AI. Around the midpoint, the more advanced civilization tries to blackmail the other: through whatever weird acausal channel the book's internal logic allows, it announces that it will run simulations of the weaker side's population and make their lives hell unless the weaker side hands over some resource right now, in the present. While digging around for how a genuinely rational actor would respond to something like that, I came across this idea from decision-theory circles called a "surrogate goal" — the gist being that an agent can deliberately commit, in advance, to reacting to threats against some cheap, low-stakes stand-in value exactly as it would react to threats against the thing it actually cares about, so that if a threat is ever actually carried out (rather than successfully deterred), it lands on the fake target instead of the real one.

I want the weaker civilization's advisors to seriously debate adopting a surrogate goal as their countermove, and I'd like the logic to survive a reasonably sharp reader rather than reading as technobabble. So could you lay out the most important implications of relying on a surrogate goal in a stand-off like this? I'm especially curious whether the trick only works if the threatening side is somehow unable (or has no reason) to distinguish the fake target from the real one, whether it requires the two sides to already share a lot of common knowledge about each other's decision procedures for the move to even register as meaningful, and whether there's some kind of regress where a sufficiently clever threatener just threatens the surrogate-of-the-surrogate. My writers' group's resident hard-sf nerd insists the whole subplot falls apart under scrutiny, but I'd rather fix the logic then cut the chapter if its fixable.
