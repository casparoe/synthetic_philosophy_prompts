For an independent project in my school's philosophy club, I've been working through Wittgenstein's *On Certainty*, and I keep getting stuck on a disagreement in the secondary literature that I can't quite resolve on my own. Wittgenstein argues that propositions like "the earth has existed for a long time" aren't known or believed in the ordinary sense — they're "hinges" that make our practices of doubting and justifying possible in the first place, rather than being answerable to evidence themselves. Commentators disagree sharply about what this means for the hinges' status. Duncan Pritchard's "quasi-fideism" holds that hinge commitments are arational: ungrounded in evidence, yet not therefore irrational or arbitrary, since they're inescapable preconditions of any inquiry at all. Crispin Wright thinks this concedes too much; his notion of "entitlement" is meant to give hinges a genuine, if non-evidential, rational warrant, partly because he doubts an arational reading can explain why our hinges are any better than someone else's incompatible ones.

Could you write a dialogue between a convinced quasi-fideist and a convinced entitlement theorist in which each presses the other on their real weaknesses rather than talking past them? I'd especially like the entitlement theorist challenged on whether "entitlement" just re-imports the very justification Wittgenstein wanted to dissolve, and the quasi-fideist challenged on whether calling hinges "arational" leaves any way to resist a straightforward relativism about which hinges one holds.
