I'm taking a graduate seminar on formal epistemology this term, and this week's unit covered the "problem of logical omniscience" in Hintikka-style epistemic logic: the fact that, in the standard possible-worlds semantics, an agent's knowledge (or belief) is automatically closed under logical consequence and includes every tautology, simply as a consequence of the K axiom, K_i(φ→ψ) → (K_iφ → K_iψ), together with the necessitation rule that makes every valid formula automatically known. Everyone in the seminar agreed this is a wildly idealized picture of any real cognizer, and we surveyed some of the standard responses in the literature — impossible-worlds semantics, awareness logics, resource-bounded "step" logics — without settling on a favorite.

For my term paper, though, I want to defend a narrower and, I think, more tractable claim: that belief logics are simply less afflicted by the problem than knowledge logics are, so that switching from the latter to the former is itself already a partial solution. The reasoning is that knowledge is standardly modeled with an S5 system (built from K, T, 4, and 5), whereas belief drops the factivity axiom T (K_iφ → φ) in favor of the strictly weaker consistency axiom D, giving the system KD45. Since KD45 is a proper weakening of S5 — it has strictly fewer validities overall, precisely because it no longer forces believed propositions to be true — it seems to follow that agents modeled by KD45 are stuck with correspondingly less logical omniscience than agents modeled by S5: less idealized closure, fewer automatically "believed" tautologies, and so on, simply because KD45 is the weaker logic across the board. Before I build my paper's opening argument around this contrast, I would like help sharpening it into something rigorous: what is the best way to state, formally, exactly how much less logical omniscience a KD45 believer is saddled with compared to an S5 knower?
