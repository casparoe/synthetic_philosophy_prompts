About six weeks ago I moved from a product team into the safety-verification group at the AI company where I work, and the onboarding reading list dropped me straight into Yudkowsky and Herreshoff's "tiling agents" paper and the Löbian obstacle it lays out: the result, following from Löb's theorem, that a system reasoning from a fixed set of axioms can never fully certify, from the inside, that a successor built on those same axioms is trustworthy — because a system that could prove "whatever my successor proves is true," in full generality, could thereby prove anything at all. I've since worked through the proposed patches — weakening each generation's proof system relative to the last (what people on my team call a "consistency waterfall"), or abandoning proof-theoretic certainty altogether for something more like calibrated logical induction — and none of them quite gets rid of the underlying vertigo: every guarantee needs a further guarantor, and the chain never touches solid ground. It is an odd thing to be paid to think about, and it has followed me home more than I expected.

I'd like you to write me a short story that takes this specific obstacle seriously as its engine, in the way Greg Egan's Permutation City works through the metaphysics of copied minds rather than treating the idea as scenery for a more conventional plot — I don't want something that is really just "the AI turns out to be dangerous" with a Gödel reference bolted on. If it helps as a starting point, you could set it inside a research institute racing to certify a new architecture before an older one is retired for good, where the certificate the humans are asking for is exactly the kind of self-vouching Löb's theorem rules out, and the story follows what the outgoing system does once it understands why it can never quite hand over what is being asked of it — though please depart from that setup entirely if you find a sharper angle on the same idea. What matters most to me is that the logical bind itself does real work in the plot, so that a reader who already knows the theorem would recognize precisely which trap the story is dramatizing.
