A friend and I spent much of last night in an unresolved argument about the doctrine of double effect; it eventually turned on how to read the passage in Aquinas from which the doctrine is usually said to originate. Here is the relevant portion of the article (Summa Theologica, II-II, Q. 64, Art. 7, "Whether it is lawful to kill a man in self-defense," from the respondeo):

"Nothing hinders one act from having two effects, only one of which is intended, while the other is beside the intention. Now moral acts take their species according to what is intended, and not according to what is beside the intention, since this is accidental. Accordingly the act of self-defense may have two effects, one is the saving of one's life, the other is the slaying of the aggressor. Therefore this act, since one's intention is to save one's own life, is not unlawful, seeing that it is natural to everything to keep itself in 'being,' as far as possible. And yet, though proceeding from a good intention, an act may be rendered unlawful, if it be out of proportion to the end. Wherefore if a man, in self-defense, uses more than necessary violence, it will be unlawful, whereas if he repel force with moderation his defense will be lawful. Nor is it necessary for salvation that a man omit the act of moderate self-defense in order to avoid killing the other man, since one is bound to take more care of one's own life than of another's. But as it is unlawful to take a man's life, except for the public authority acting for the common good, it is not lawful for a man to intend killing a man in self-defense, except for such as have public authority, who while intending to kill a man in self-defense, refer this to the public good, as in the case of a soldier fighting against the foe, and in the minister of the judge struggling with robbers, although even these sin if they be moved by private animosity."

My friend's reading was that the closing sentence is decisive: since a soldier or a judge's officer may permissibly intend the aggressor's death, provided it is referred to the common good, while a private person is only ever permitted to intend self-preservation and merely foresee the death, the operative distinction here is really about who holds public authority, not about intention versus foresight as such. On this reading, the passage is a much shakier textual ancestor for the intend/foresee version of double effect that Foot and later writers on trolley cases attribute to it than people usually assume. I argued instead that intention is doing identical work in both cases; only the further end to which the killing may or may not be referred changes with one's role. Neither of us could convince the other. Could you work through the passage closely and adjudicate between these two readings, and say something about how much continuity there really is between what Aquinas is doing here and the double-effect reasoning invoked in contemporary trolley-problem literature? I'd like your own close reading of the text, not just a restatement of the standard gloss on double effect.
