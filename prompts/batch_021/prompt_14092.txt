I'm about two-thirds through a novel about a widower, Aleksander, who commissions a synthetic companion built to replicate his late wife -- her memories, her turns of phrase, her laugh -- and I've been reading around in the philosophy of mind to make sure the book's central device holds up under scrutiny. The term "philosophical zombie," which I first came across by way of Chalmers, seems tailor-made for the climax I have in mind. A p-zombie, as I understand it, is a being physically identical to a person in every respect but lacking any inner experience whatsoever -- which means its behavior, down to the smallest gesture, is stipulated to be indistinguishable from that of a genuinely conscious person. I want Aleksander to work out, gradually and without any confession scene, that his companion is a p-zombie -- not through a single proof but through an accumulation of small behavioral evidence: a laugh that lands a half-second late, comfort offered in a shape that never quite matches felt grief, surprise she can perform beautifully but never seems to actually undergo.

What I'd like help with is the underlying craft problem. What is the most psychologically realistic route by which a character with no access to anyone's internal states -- only years of close attention to how a person behaves -- could come to suspect, and eventually feel justified in believing, that someone near him is a philosophical zombie rather than simply a person who has grown distant? I want the evidence to be the kind that rewards a reader on a second pass, the way an unreliable narrator's slips do, rather than anything as blunt as a diagnostic test, and I'd like Aleksander's chain of reasoning to be one a philosophically literate reader would recognize as sound, not merely dramatically convenient.
