So this week's session of my dynamic choice reading group was entirely on money-pump arguments against intransitive preference, and I came out more confused than when I went in, specifically about the resolute-choice move. Writing it out as a dialogue instead of another list of premises usually helps me, so that's what I'm hoping you can do.

Here's the setup I want dialogued out: a Chooser has cyclic preferences over three vacation packages, A over B, B over C, and C over A, each pairwise ranking driven by a different attribute (price, weather, itinerary) that gets weighted slightly differently depending on what it's being compared to — so it's a genuine multi-attribute cycle, not confusion or a typo in someone's preference list, kind of in the spirit of the old Davidson/McKinsey/Suppes setups. A trader sets up a table with all three packages and starts offering sequential swaps — C for B plus a dollar fee, then (now that the Chooser holds B) B for A plus a dollar, then A for C plus a dollar, round and round — and the textbook line is that the Chooser rationally takes every single trade and gets bled dry forever, which is supposed to show the cycle itself is irrational, prior to and apart from any individual trade being a mistake.

I want a dialogue between two people standing right at that table, watching this happen in real time, each trying to talk the other into their reading of what it shows. One is a straightforward "the pump vindicates transitivity, full stop" theorist. The other defends something like McClennen's resolute choice: that the Chooser can keep the cyclic preferences and still dodge the pump by resolving up front to stop after the second trade, and that this isn't some ad hoc patch. I want the transitivity theorist to press the obvious objection — that resolving to turn down a trade you'd still, in the moment, prefer to take just relocates the irrationality rather than removing it — and I want the resolute-choice defender to have a real comeback rather than just restating the view. Then have the transitivity theorist bring up sophisticated (backward-induction) choice as a supposedly cheaper way out that needs no resolving at all, and let the other person explain why that's not the free lunch it looks like (there's something interesting to say about whether the "you're declining what you currently prefer" charge actually lands equally on sophisticated choosers, which is the kind of thing I keep almost seeing and then losing). If it fits naturally, a line on how Rachels, and I think Temkin with his spectrum cases, argue we're forced into real intransitive "better than" judgments by pretty plausible axiological commitments — not just by cooked-up trading examples — would be a nice touch, since it raises the stakes past a toy scenario built to embarrass decision theorists. Both characters should be genuinely sharp, responding to the specific last thing the other one said rather than delivering prepared speeches past each other, and neither should just fold at the end — I'd rather see exactly where they still disagree once the easy shots are used up.
