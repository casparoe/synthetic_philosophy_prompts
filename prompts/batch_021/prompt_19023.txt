I spent some forty years as an experimental physicist before retiring, and I've been using the extra time since to finally read philosophy I always meant to get to. I started with Spinoza's Ethics, partly out of curiosity about a philosopher who thought he could derive propositions about God, mind, and emotion "in the geometrical manner," the way one derives theorems from axioms — a method that felt oddly familiar coming from a physics background. That led me on to the Theological-Political Treatise and then to the unfinished Political Treatise, and it is these latter two that have occupied me for the last few weeks.

What strikes me most is how thoroughly Spinoza grounds his politics in a naturalistic psychology of the affects: on his account, the multitude is moved far more by passions such as fear, hope, and hatred — and by what he calls the "imitation of the affects," roughly, the way people catch one another's emotions simply from perceiving others as similar to themselves — than by reason, so that stable government is less a matter of appealing to reason than of channeling or containing this dynamic. In the Theological-Political Treatise he argues that democracy is the regime most consistent with natural right, since it preserves the most equality and freedom among citizens; yet, as I understand it, he died in 1677 having barely begun the chapter of the Political Treatise meant to work out democracy's own institutional design, leaving us mainly with his much more detailed proposals for aristocracy — large rotating councils, secret ballots, oversight bodies he calls "syndics" — as safeguards against exactly the kind of factional, passion-driven instability that worried him.

So here is my question, and I would like you to reason it through using Spinoza's actual categories rather than the generic platitudes about tolerance and free speech to which he is often reduced: what do you think he would have said about algorithmically curated social media, that is, systems that can identify which passions move which slice of the population and then amplify and exploit those passions asymmetrically, at a scale and precision no seventeenth-century demagogue or pamphleteer could have approached? Would he take this to be a danger to which democracy, of all regimes, is distinctively exposed, given how directly it lets the multitude's affects into government — or might his evident taste for institutional engineering, of the sort he worked out for aristocracy, suggest he would think a democracy could in principle be designed to resist it? I would be curious how you think he might have resolved that tension, and on what grounds.
