I've been on a bit of a Frederick Douglass kick lately, reading his autobiography and some old speeches, and I ran into something that threw me off. Turns out Douglass used to run with William Lloyd Garrison's crowd, who thought the Constitution was basically a slaveholders document through and through - Garrison even burned a copy of it at a rally once, calling it a deal with the devil basically. But later on Douglass broke from that whole camp and started arguing pretty much the opposite: that if you read the actual text plainly, it never even mentions slavery, so the document itself is anti-slavery even though the country wasn't living up to it. That's a massive reversal and I don't totally get how he defended it against his old friends, or if Garrison's side even had a good comeback. Can you write me a dialogue between Douglass, after his change of heart, and one of his old Garrisonian allies, where they actually go after each others arguements instead of just repeating themselves past one another? I want both of them arguing like they've got a real shot at winning, not one of them being the obvious "correct" one.
