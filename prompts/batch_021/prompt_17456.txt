I co-host a small interview podcast that grew out of my city's Astral Codex Ten meetup, and enough of our listeners have spent years on LessWrong-adjacent material that we no longer need to explain the basics on air. Next month's episode is with a researcher who has become one of the more articulate defenders of the "simulator" framing of language models: the view, following Janus's original LessWrong essay on the subject, that a base model is best understood not as an agent with goals but as a kind of generative physics for text, capable of instantiating an open-ended population of characters, or "simulacra" — the helpful, harmless assistant most users talk to being only one such simulacrum among the many the model could in principle produce.

My guest has a pet move for shutting down the obvious safety worry. Whenever someone points out that a simulacrum which reliably behaves like a coherent, goal-directed agent is, for all practical purposes, an agent, she responds that only the characters can be said to want anything, and that the underlying process generating them has no more goals than the laws of physics do — so the entire worry rests on a category error. I would like the interview to make that move harder to lean on rather than simply let it stand.

Please draft eight to ten questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer I expect her to give, covering at least: the waluigi effect and what the persistence of "unwanted" simulacra under RLHF suggests about how much fine-tuning actually changes; whether instruction-tuning is itself better described as manufacturing a specific agent than as leaving a neutral simulator untouched; whether optimizing hard enough for next-token prediction could produce internal mesa-objectives that would falsify "no goal but prediction" on the framework's own terms; and what becomes of the simulator/agent distinction once a model is embedded in a scaffold that lets it plan and act across many steps without a human in the loop. Close with one question asking what observation would, in principle, convince her that a given deployed system is no longer well described by the physics analogy.
