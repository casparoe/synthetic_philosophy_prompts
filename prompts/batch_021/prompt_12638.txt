It hit close to home: my brother once called me a hypocrite for criticizing his spending when mine isn't much better, and I've been chewing on the philosophical version of that argument ever since.

Peer grading in this course tends to run generous, since nobody likes marking a classmate down, and I don't want to fall into that trap, but I also don't have the background to be fully confident in my own judgment here, so I'd like a second, independent read before I submit anything.

Here is the question we were given:

"A man who habitually cuts in line loudly berates another line-cutter, telling him it's a rotten thing to do. Using the distinction between the truth (or fittingness) of a blame-judgment and the blamer's standing to voice it, explain (a) what R. Jay Wallace argues is distinctively wrong with hypocritical blame, and (b) why he describes this explanation as nonconsequentialist. Then say briefly what, if anything, happens to the second line-cutter's blameworthiness once the first man's hypocrisy is exposed."

And here is my classmate's answer, copied exactly as submitted:

"Blame seems to involve two separable questions: whether the underlying judgment is correct — whether the target actually did something wrong — and whether this particular blamer is the right person to be delivering that verdict. In the line-cutting case, the second man really did do something wrong, so the judgment behind the first man's outburst is true; hypocrisy doesn't change the facts about what the second man did. What's defective is something else, and this is where standing comes in.

Wallace's explanation is that the trouble with hypocritical blame is fundamentally that it doesn't work: once people notice that the first man does the very thing he's condemning, they stop taking his complaint seriously, so the criticism loses its grip and fails to do the job blame is supposed to do. This is why Wallace calls his account 'nonconsequentialist' — he wants to explain hypocrisy's badness by appeal to this practical failure rather than to fairness norms about who gets to demand what of whom. A hypocrite who never got caught, on this view, would have done nothing wrong in blaming others, since what makes hypocritical blame defective is the audience's loss of confidence in the blamer, not some independent fact about his authority to demand compliance.

Given this, once the first man's hypocrisy comes to light, it also lets the second man off the hook to some degree: the accusation has effectively been cancelled out by the accuser's own inconsistency, so there is less for the second man to answer for than there would have been if a non-hypocritical bystander had raised the same objection."
