Nate and Farah got into an argument about coherence arguments -- the idea that any agent smart enough to avoid getting money-pumped will basically end up behaving like an expected utility maximizer. I'm going to try to reconstruct it fairly:

1. Nate: if an agent's preferences aren't transitive (prefers A to B, B to C, but then C to A), you can build a "money pump" out of it -- keep offering trades around the loop and drain it dry. So any agent smart enough to notice this happening to itself will eventually act coherently, i.e. like it's maximizing some utility function. That's basically why people say a sufficiently advanced AI will end up with "a goal" it's ruthlessly optimizing for, whether we like it or not.

2. Farah: sure, but that only rules out cyclic preferences. Behaving like a full expected-utility maximizer also requires completeness -- having some ranking, even just indifference, between literally every pair of options -- and that's a seperate assumption from transitivity. An agent could have straight-up gaps, options it refuses to rank, and dodge the pump completely just by never trading away from whatever it currently has unless the new option is one it actually, definitely prefers.

3. Nate: but "never trade away from the status quo unless you definitely prefer the alternative" is itself a preference -- it just means you weakly prefer the status quo over anything you haven't ranked. So completeness sneaks back in through the side door. There's no way to consistently act on a "gap" without that gap collapsing into ordinary indifference.

4. Farah: that's mixing up a policy with a preference. Refusing to trade isn't the same thing as preferring not to -- it's just what happens when there's no preference either way to act on. If any consistent way of behaving counts as secretly revealing a preference, then no agent could ever count as having incomplete preferences even in principle, which makes completeness true by definition instead of something the money-pump argument actually earns.
