In my formal epistemology seminar, we recently covered David Lewis's Principal Principle: roughly, the idea that a rational agent's credence in an outcome should equal the objective chance of that outcome. I think I've found a fairly direct counterexample. Suppose a fair coin is about to be tossed, so the objective chance that it lands heads is exactly one half. Now suppose a completely reliable oracle, stipulated for the sake of the example to be error-free, tells me with certainty that this particular coin will land heads on this particular toss. Surely, on hearing the oracle, I should have credence near 1 that it will land heads, not 0.5. But the objective chance of heads is still 0.5 right up until the coin actually lands, so the Principal Principle seems to say my credence should stay at 0.5 regardless of what the oracle tells me. Either I ignore the oracle and keep my credence pinned to the chance, which seems clearly irrational, or I update to near-certainty and thereby violate the principle.

I raised this in our study group, and we split into two camps. One camp thinks this shows the Principal Principle simply cannot be right as a general norm of rational credence, since any testimony about a specific outcome will generate the same tension. The other camp suspects there must be some restriction on which evidence the principle actually governs, but nobody could say what that restriction is, or why it would be a principled feature of the theory rather than a patch introduced just to dodge examples like mine. Which camp has it right: does the oracle case genuinely refute the Principal Principle, and if it doesn't, what exactly is supposed to block it, and is that move well-motivated or just an ad hoc rescue?
