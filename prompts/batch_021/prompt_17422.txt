I TA our department's intro ethics course, and every fall I trot out Yudkowsky's dust specks vs. torture case to get discussion section going -- one person tortured for 50 years vs. 3^^^3 people each getting a single dust speck in the eye, plus the "shut up and multiply" argument that total-utility aggregation commits you to picking torture as the lesser evil. This year I added a new bit to my slides where I tell them the whole "must bite the torture bullet" problem is really just an artifact of summing utility instead of averaging it: switch to average utilitarianism and the tortured guy's suffering gets spread across all 3^^^3 people, so his contribution to the average rounds down to way less than a single dust speck's worth of disutility, which seemed to me like it let you dodge Yudkowsky's conclusion entirely and just go with the specks instead. My students loved it, it felt like a clean escape hatch instead of the usual "yeah, utilitarianism bites bullets, get used to it."

My co-TA Priya thinks Im overselling this and wants me to cut the slide before we teach the section again next week, but I'm honestly not sure she's right either -- she couldn't quite say what was wrong with the averaging move, just that it felt too easy. Can you settle it for me: does switching from total to average utilitarianism actually change which option comes out ahead in this case, or does the averaging move just not do the work I think it's doing? If I'm wrong about this I'd rather find out from you than get cornered by a sharp undergrad in week 9.
