Kind of a weird one, but here goes. My actual work is on Hume's moral psychology, but I've been down a decision-theory rabbit hole lately (started from AI safety stuff, of all things) and keep running into the argument that any agent whose preferences are cyclical -- prefers A to B, B to C, but C to A -- can be money-pumped out of everything it owns, and that this is supposed to show such preferences are irrational, full stop. Hume obviously thinks reason is "the slave of the passions," and that a passion can only be "contrary to reason" in two narrow cases: when its founded on a false belief that some object exists, or when it picks means insufficient for the end it's aiming at. Given just those two conditions, would Hume actually grant that having a preference cycle counts as irrational, or would he say the guy running the pump is exploiting a psychological quirk rather than exposing any failure of reason? Please don't just land on "Hume was an anti-rationalist about practical reason" and call it a day -- actually work through whether getting money-pumped counts as a means-end failure by his own lights. If you want to go further, his bundle theory of personal identity seems to cause trouble for the standard version of the argument too, since that plays out as a sequence of trades over time -- curious what you make of that angle as well.
