So this week in my epistemic logic seminar we got into the problem of logical omniscience and I'm still kind of chewing on it before the midterm. The setup, if I'm getting it right: in the standard Hintikka-style possible worlds semantics for knowledge, an agent "knows" p just in case p is true at every world compatible with what they know, and this has the side effect that agents automatically know every logical truth (they're true at every world, so trivially known) and every logical consequence of anything they already know. Our professor was pretty insistent this is a real defect and not just a quirky idealization, since actual people obviously don't know every theorem that follows from the axioms of Peano arithmetic just by knowing the axioms. But when we got to the proposed fixes in the readings I honestly couldn't tell if they're substantively disagreeing about how to solve the problem or just relabeling it. Theres the "impossible worlds" approach (Hintikka's own later move, also Rantala) where you throw extra, logically deviant worlds into the model so an agent's epistemic alternatives can include one where a contradiction holds or where two logically equivalent sentences differ in truth value, which blocks the automatic closure. And then theres the awareness/algorithmic knowledge approach from the Fagin-Halpern-Moses-Vardi book, where implicit knowledge stays fully idealized (still logically omniscient in the background) but you define a separate, restricted notion of explicit knowledge tied to what the agent is actually aware of or can compute given their resources.

And I'd like the other character to defend the awareness/algorithmic side and push back by saying impossible worlds don't actually model anything about the agent's reasoning process, they just relabel which "worlds" happen to be compatible with what's known, so you've saved the letter of the semantics without saying anything about cognition.
