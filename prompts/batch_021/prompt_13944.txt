That interest is presumably why a friend who teaches a graduate seminar on rational choice roped me into helping grade this term's take-home midterms. One question asks students to trace Herbert Simon's notion of satisficing and bounded rationality into its later career as a normative position in ethics, and then to engage with the standard objection to it. I have in front of me an answer from one of the stronger students in the seminar, and before I commit to a grade I would like a careful, independent second opinion, ideally one that does not take the overall fluency of the writing as a proxy for the correctness of its content.

Here is the question exactly as it was given:

"Herbert Simon argued that real decision-makers 'satisfice' rather than maximize. (a) Explain what Simon meant by bounded rationality and satisficing, including how the aspiration-level mechanism is supposed to work. (b) Explain how this idea was later taken up as a normative (rather than merely descriptive) claim in ethics, in particular via Michael Slote's satisficing consequentialism. (c) Explain the central objection Philip Pettit presses against Slote's proposal in their joint symposium exchange, and assess whether it succeeds. (400 words or fewer.)"

And here is the student's answer, reproduced verbatim:

"Simon's starting point is that the 'economic man' of classical theory—an agent who surveys every option and computes the expected utility of each before choosing the best—is psychologically unrealistic. Real agents operate under bounded rationality: limits on the time, information, and computational power available to them. Rather than optimizing over the full choice set, an agent sets an aspiration level, a threshold of acceptability given the current situation, searches through options more or less sequentially, and stops as soon as one clears the threshold rather than continuing until the best option turns up. Simon calls this satisficing. The aspiration level itself is not fixed: it adjusts over time depending on how the search is going, rising when good options are easy to find and falling when they are scarce. This picture was later vindicated and extended by Kahneman and Tversky's heuristics-and-biases research, which used experiments to show that the satisficing heuristics Simon described are part of a broader family of shortcuts that systematically bias human judgment away from the utility-maximizing choice recommended by normative decision theory.

Michael Slote imported satisficing into ethics with his satisficing consequentialism: an act can be morally right even without producing the best available outcome, so long as it produces a good enough one. Slote's motivation is to relieve consequentialism of its demandingness—the sense that, on a maximizing view, one is always required to sacrifice a little more for a marginally better result—and to make room for supererogation, acts that are praiseworthy but not strictly required.

Philip Pettit's reply in the same symposium presses a dilemma: either the 'good enough' threshold is set arbitrarily, in which case it is hard to see why an agent should ever stop short of the best option when nothing stands in the way of reaching it, or the threshold is set by appeal to some independent standard, in which case satisficing consequentialism looks like it is just maximizing a different, more complicated quantity under a new name. I think Pettit's dilemma is answered once we notice that the threshold can be fixed non-arbitrarily, by appeal to whatever ordinary moral common sense already treats as 'good enough' in the circumstances. Since the threshold need not be arbitrary, satisficing consequentialism escapes the objection and survives as a genuine rival to maximizing consequentialism, rather than a mere redescription of it."
