In Book IV of The Wealth of Nations, particularly the chapter "Of Colonies," Adam Smith argues that Britain's American colonies were, on balance, an economic burden rather than an asset: the monopoly of colonial trade diverted capital away from more profitable uses, and the expense of defending and administering the colonies outweighed whatever gains a narrow class of merchants extracted from them, at the cost of the nation as a whole. Several of the UK's remaining Overseas Territories today, such as Bermuda, the Cayman Islands, and the British Virgin Islands, supply almost none of the raw materials, agricultural produce, or captive markets Smith had in mind; instead, they function chiefly as offshore financial centers, offering low taxation and corporate secrecy to global capital. Applying Smith's own method of asking who bears the costs of empire and who captures its benefits, how would he likely assess Britain's continued sovereignty over these territories? In particular, consider whether the absence of the costly military and administrative burdens central to his eighteenth-century analysis would lead him to a different verdict than the one he reached about the colonies of his own day, or whether he would diagnose a comparable capture of the national interest by a narrow one.
