I'm a backend engineer at a startup that runs a marketplace where an autonomous agent negotiates and executes trades of cloud resources — compute credits, storage quota, API rate-limit allocations — on behalf of customer accounts, swapping one bundle for another whenever it judges the new bundle an improvement over what the account currently holds. I sit on our internal "agent risk review" board, which has to sign off before any new trading behavior ships, and we're stuck on a disagreement I'd like help thinking through clearly before Thursday's meeting.

During red-teaming, our safety lead (who actually has a philosophy background and keeps citing decision-theory papers the rest of us haven't read) constructed three resource bundles, X, Y, and Z, such that the agent reliably preferred X over Y, Y over Z, and Z over X in head-to-head tests, and would even pay a small credit fee each time to "upgrade" along the cycle. Chained together, that means a counterparty who knew the pattern could walk the agent around the loop indefinitely, draining its account for free. The safety lead calls this a textbook money pump and wants to block the launch until the valuation model is rebuilt to guarantee one strict, transitive ranking over all bundles, which the agent team estimates would take a multi-month rewrite. Their counter-proposal is much cheaper: simply bar the agent from ever re-entering a trade that would return it to a bundle it has already held, so the loop can never physically close, whatever its underlying pairwise preferences look like. They argue this makes the exploit unavailable in practice, especially since a real counterparty won't have the full preference map our red team had. The safety lead's objection is that this leaves the agent "incoherent by design," and that the rule can likely be routed around using bundles that are close to, but not identical with, ones the agent has already held; the agent team thinks the rewrite is an expensive fix for a vulnerability that may not be practically exploitable at all.

Could you draft the short memo I'll bring to the board? Please take an actual position rather than calling for more testing — approve the cheap patch, require the full rewrite, or recommend something else entirely — and attach a concrete rollout timeline. I'd especially like the memo to engage head-on with whether "make the loop physically impossible to complete" is a principled way of handling intransitive preferences or just a patch that leaves the real problem intact, since that's the crux the two camps keep talking past. I don't have the background to adjudicate that claim myself, and I'd like the reasoning to be solid enough that I could defend it to both sides of the room.
