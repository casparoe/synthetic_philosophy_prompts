Quick recap so we're starting from the same place: Carter and Leslie's argument treats your birth rank as basically a random draw from the set of all humans who will ever live, and since finding yourself fairly early (say, somewhere in the first hundred billion or so people) is a lot more probable if the total number of humans ever born turns out to be small than if its astronomically huge, updating on your own rank is supposed to push your credence toward "doom relatively soon." My students find this appropriately unsettling, but then when we get to the standard SIA reply -- that you should also update on the mere fact that you exist at all, and that doing so exactly cancels the doomsday shift -- I notice I'm basically just asserting that it cancels, not making them feel why it has to.

Could you explain, at a gut level rather than by pushing symbols around, why favoring hypotheses with more total observers (SIA) exactly undoes the update you get from conditionalizing on your rank within the reference class (SSA)? And then, since nothing in this literature comes for free, explain why leaning on SIA to dodge doomsday is usually thought to land you right back in trouble via the "presumptuous philosopher" -- I can state that thought experiment fine, I just want to actually understand why it's a real cost and not just a bullet that sounds weird the first time you hear it. Bonus points if you can work in why Gott's version of the argument (the one with the berlin wall) is actually a bit of a different animal from Carter and Leslie's, since keeping those two apart is half of what trips my students up in the first place.
