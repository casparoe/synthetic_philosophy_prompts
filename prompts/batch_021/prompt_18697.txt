I've been re-reading the closing stretch of Cicero's De Senectute, the part where Cato basically free-associates his way through every argument he can find for the soul's immortality before wrapping up the dialogue. What's kind of funny is that it's not really one argument, it's like 8 different, not obviously compatible ones stacked on top of each other — some lifted straight from Plato, a couple attributed to Cyrus via Xenophon, and a couple that are just Cato own gut feelings dressed up as philosophy. I want to rank them by how much actual argumentative work they do (i.e. how much they'd still carry weight for someone who doesn't already buy into Platonic psychology), not by how rhetorically satisfying they are. Here's the lineup:

- the soul's powers (memory, quick uptake of new skills, foresight) are too impressive to belong to something merely mortal
- the soul is self-moving, and a self-mover can never stop moving, so it can never stop existing
- the soul is simple/has no parts, and only composite things can come apart, so it can't perish
- kids pick up hard material so fast it must be "recollection" of stuff the soul already knew before birth
- (via Cyrus) we only ever infer that someone has a soul at all from watching what they do, even in life, so not perceiving it after death doesn't prove anything new
- (via Cyrus) dreams show the soul at its most active and even prophetic, exactly when the body is most shut down, in sleep
- the best and wisest men are consistently both the ones most driven to chase undying fame AND the ones who die most calmly, wich Cato takes as evidence they're onto something
- Cato's closing move that he loses nothing by being wrong, since if the soul isn't immortal he won't be around afterward to be embarrassed about it
