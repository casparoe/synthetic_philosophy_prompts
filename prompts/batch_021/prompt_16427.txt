So I'm reporting a piece on the game theory of cooperation and want to sanity-check something before it goes to print, because an economist source I talked to got a little exasperated with me about it and didnt have time to fully explain why. I've been re-reading Hofstadter's old Metamagical Themas columns, where he walks through Axelrod's computer tournament results right alongside his own idea of "superrationality," and it read to me like two versions of the same underlying proof: that fully rational players will cooperate in the prisoner's dilemma instead of defecting, since defecting only looks smart if you assume your counterpart is dumber than you or reasoning along totally different lines. Axelrod actually ran the tournaments and tit-for-tat came out on top, and Hofstadter worked out the logic for why two identical, equally rational minds should both land on cooperate. Taken together it felt like a pretty clean one-two punch against the classic textbook solution.

My source's reaction was basically "those two things aren't proving the same point, you can't just stack them," but he had to run to a meeting before unpacking it any further. So: if superrationality shows that cooperating is the rational move for a symmetric pair of reasoners, and Axelrod's results show cooperative strategies winning out in practice, why do game theory courses still confidently teach mutual defection as the dominant strategy and the unique Nash equilibrium of the one-shot dilemma? Is there an actual gap between what these two results establish, or is this mostly economists being attatched to a tidy solution concept they like?
