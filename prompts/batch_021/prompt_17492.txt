For the past two years I've been embedded with a mechanistic-interpretability lab, working on a book about whether AI researchers are discovering real structure inside neural networks or just projecting patterns onto noise. The lab's small interdisciplinary workshop -- half interpretability researchers, half philosophers of mind who work on representation -- lost its keynote speaker to illness, and since translating their papers for outsiders apparently counts as a qualification, I've been asked to open it instead. More than one philosopher has already told me, unprompted, that Fodor and Pylyshyn settled this back in 1988. Write me five minutes of opening remarks that state plainly what the two sides are actually disagreeing about -- whether "the network represents X" or merely behaves as if it does -- grounded in specifics like superposition and sparse-autoencoder features rather than staying abstract, and without collapsing into "it depends what you mean by representation," which is the one line I've promised this speech will not become.
