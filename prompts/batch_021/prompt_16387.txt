The question was: "Explain the traditional doctrine of the moral equality of combatants, and how Jeff McMahan's revisionist critique challenges it. Your answer should address: (a) the relationship between jus ad bellum and jus in bello under the traditional view; (b) McMahan's alternative account of liability to lethal force; and (c) at least one major objection to McMahan's revisionist position and how a revisionist might respond." Here is the student's answer in full:

"According to the traditional doctrine, most famously associated with Michael Walzer, the justice of a war's cause (jus ad bellum) and the justice of conduct within it (jus in bello) are entirely separate questions. Because they are independent, a soldier fighting for a state that lacks just cause is not thereby doing anything wrong simply by fighting, so long as he abides by the rules of discrimination and proportionality in battle. This is why combatants on both sides are said to be moral equals: each retains an equal right to kill enemy combatants and is equally liable to be killed, regardless of which side has the just cause. Responsibility for the resort to war rests with political leaders, not the soldiers who carry out their orders.

Jeff McMahan, most fully in Killing in War, rejects this picture. He argues that liability to be killed in war is grounded not in one's uniform but in individual moral responsibility for posing an unjust threat, following the same logic that governs individual self-defense: an aggressor forfeits the right not to be attacked, while his victim does not. Since unjust combatants pose threats that lack a just cause, they are liable to defensive force, whereas just combatants, who are merely resisting that threat, are typically not liable to be killed by the other side. Because liability on this view is simply a matter of objectively contributing to an unjustified threat, it makes no difference whether the unjust combatant was deceived by his government or conscripted under threat of punishment — he is just as liable to be killed as a volunteer who fully believes in his cause. On McMahan's view, then, unjust combatants who kill just combatants are always guilty of murder, even if they scrupulously observe the rules of discrimination and proportionality, because those rules only regulate how a war is fought, not whether one had the right to fight it in the first place.

A standard objection to McMahan, pressed by Walzer and others, is epistemic: soldiers are rarely well positioned to know whether their war is just, so holding them to a standard that requires correct moral judgment about jus ad bellum is unreasonable, and a shared in bello code is necessary for war to be governed by law at all. McMahan responds that this is a good reason for the law of armed conflict to preserve formal equality, but insists that this is a fact about the ethics of law and convention, not about the deep morality of the individual soldier's act."
