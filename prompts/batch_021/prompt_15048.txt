I'm a backend dev with zero formal philosophy training, but I fell down a rabbit hole a while back reading old MIRI papers and landed on this "logical induction" thing (I think Garrabrant was the lead author?) -- basically a way to get well-defined credences on math statements, even open conjectures, by treating them like prices in a market that no trader you could actually run in reasonable time can exploit forever, and its proven that market eventually converges to the truth as proofs become available.

Someone in a comment thread on it (bringing up some old philosopher, Carnap I think?) argued this doesn't actually solve anything philosophically -- not being exploitable just means the prices stay internally consistent over time, which is basically the same deal as regular Bayesian coherence, and it doesn't pin down what the number should actually be for some hard conjecture that has no findable pattern yet. Two completely different logical inductors could spit out wildly different credences for the same open conjecture and the theory has no grounds to call either one wrong.

Write me an essay that actually takes a side on this: does logical induction give us a real, meaningful notion of rational credence for open math questions, or is it just a fancy non-exploitability guarantee wearing an epistemology costume? Weigh the strongest arguments both ways, but don't just leave it hanging -- land somewhere and defend it. "It works for statements with learnable patterns but not truly generic ones" counts as landing somewhere, just commit to an actual verdict.
