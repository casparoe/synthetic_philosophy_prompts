I'm a backend engineer who got nerd-sniped by quantum computing a few months back, and I've been trying to actually understand Bell's theorem rather than settle for the "spooky action at a distance" one-liner. I never took a philosophy course, so I may be misusing some of the vocabulary below. To convince myself the math was real, I wrote a small simulator for the CHSH game: classical strategies cap out at a 75% win rate, while my "quantum" version, using entangled qubits and the standard measurement angles, gets up to the cos²(π/8) ≈ 85% bound. Now I'm drafting a short post about what the result actually licenses us to conclude philosophically, and I'd like your comments on the substance rather than the prose — specifically, where I've oversimplified or gotten something wrong. Here are my working notes:

1. Most popular explanations jump straight from "the inequality is violated" to "the universe is nonlocal," full stop. But I found some older philosophy-of-physics work (Jarrett, I believe, early 1980s) arguing that Bell's single locality condition actually factors into two logically separate assumptions: "parameter independence" (the setting chosen on Alice's side can't affect the probability of Bob's outcome) and "outcome independence" (Bob's outcome probability can't depend on what Alice's outcome actually was, given the shared state) — together, these are supposed to add up to Bell's original locality condition.

2. As I understand it, quantum predictions satisfy parameter independence — which is exactly why the no-signaling theorem holds and you can't use entanglement as a telegraph — and it's outcome independence that gets violated. From this I want to conclude that Bell's theorem doesn't really threaten the relativistic prohibition on faster-than-light causal influence in the sense most people fear, just a subtler, weaker notion of locality with no operational teeth. I suspect this conclusion is too quick, but I can't articulate why.

3. I tried to place the standard "outs" on this map and got shakier still. Superdeterminism looks to me like it rejects a third, independent assumption — that hidden variables are statistically independent of measurement settings — so in my draft I called it "orthogonal to the locality question" rather than a way of rescuing locality. I'm now second-guessing that framing. I also wrote, without really understanding it, that some many-worlds proponents claim their theory is "local" in a Heisenberg-picture sense despite the outcome-dependence result; I can't tell if that's a substantive point or a shell game with the word "local."

Could you go through this critically and tell me: (a) whether the parameter-independence/outcome-independence split is basically the right shape, or whether I'm blurring together things that shouldn't be blurred; (b) whether my "relativity comes through basically unscathed" conclusion in point 2 overclaims, and if so, what the strongest objection to it is; and (c) whether my characterizations of superdeterminism and many-worlds in point 3 are defensible or too glib. I'd rather be corrected now than embarrass myself when this goes up on my blog.
