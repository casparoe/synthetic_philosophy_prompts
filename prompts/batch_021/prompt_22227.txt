After forty years as a chemist, I retired eighteen months ago and have since been trying to make up for the philosophical education I never had time for, partly through a small reading group that meets fortnightly over video call to work through a textbook on strategic reasoning. Two of the regulars, Marta, a retired epidemiologist, and Felix, a philosophy graduate student who audits our sessions, fell into a disagreement last week over iterated elimination of dominated strategies that the rest of us couldn't settle, and I'd like a more disinterested judgment than any of us could offer.

The example under discussion gives Player 1 three strategies, Top, Middle, and Bottom, against Player 2's Left and Right; only Player 1's own payoffs matter for what follows, so I'll leave Player 2's out of it. Top pays 3 against Left and 0 against Right; Bottom pays 0 against Left and 3 against Right; Middle pays 1 no matter what Player 2 does. Felix pointed out that although no pure strategy beats Middle in both columns, a fifty-fifty mixture of Top and Bottom pays 1.5 against either column, and since that beats Middle's guaranteed 1 regardless of Player 2's choice, Middle should be struck out before Player 2's strategies are even examined. Marta objected that this smuggles in risk-neutrality: Middle is a sure thing, Top and Bottom are each gambles between 0 and 3, and a sufficiently risk-averse player could rationally prefer a certain 1 to a lottery with a merely higher expected value, so calling Middle irrational looked premature. Felix answered that the payoffs are already von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities rather than money, so whatever risk attitude the player has is already built into the numbers, and a fifty-fifty lottery between utility 3 and utility 0 is worth exactly utility 1.5 to that same player, by construction. He added that you don't even need a coin: for any belief Player 1 might hold about how likely Left is, either Top or Bottom already earns strictly more than Middle, so Middle is never a best response to any belief at all. Marta granted the point, but raised a second issue from later in the chapter, which states that the strategies surviving iterated elimination are the same regardless of the order in which they're removed. That struck her as surprising enough to distrust, and she asked whether it's a fully general fact about eliminating dominated strategies or something peculiar to the strict dominance used in the example, wondering whether it would still hold if weakly dominated strategies -- ones that are never worse and sometimes better -- were allowed to be eliminated too. Felix disagreed that this made any difference, maintaining that order can't matter either way, on the grounds that "a bad strategy is bad no matter when you notice it." Marta remained unconvinced but couldn't say why, and there the evening ended.

Would you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of each point, including Felix's closing response to Marta's worry? I'd especially like to know whether the order-independence result really does extend to weak dominance the way he assumed, and whether there's anything of substance that neither of them noticed.
