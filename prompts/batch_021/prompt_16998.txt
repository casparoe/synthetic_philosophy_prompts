I recently moved to Rochester, New York, and joined the historical society's programming committee, which is hosting a historian who has built her career defending Frederick Douglass's post-1851 claim that the Constitution, read on its own terms, is an antislavery document rather than the "covenant with death" he and Garrison once called it. I don't want the evening to become a mere restatement of that thesis. Please draft seven or eight interview questions that press on its weak points: at least one on how a plain-text reading of the three-fifths and fugitive-slave clauses survives the fact that everyone at the Philadelphia Convention knew exactly what those clauses were for; one on whether his appeals to the Preamble's language about justice and the general welfare quietly smuggle in natural-law premises that make him less a strict textualist than he claimed to be; and one on whether the timing of his break with Garrison, which coincided with his new alliance with Gerrit Smith, suggests that strategy, rather than principle, drove the change. For each, add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give.
