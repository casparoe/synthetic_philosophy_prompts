Okay, this is a bit of an odd one, but I've been chewing on it ever since a colleague in the math department forwarded me a passage from Gödel's letters to his mother, Marianne, back in Vienna. Buried in there is this argument that because the world is "rationally constructed," it would be absurd for something as elaborate as a human mind to just be extinguished at death -- especially given how little of our potential most of us ever get to realize in one lifetime -- so there has to be some further existence, or "another world" as he put it, where that development can continue. Hao Wang apparently records a version of this on his list of Gödel's core philosophical theses too, so it wasn't just a one-off thing Gödel said to comfort his mom.

I want to be really clear this isnt the (much more famous) ontological proof -- the modal logic one with "positive properties" that Dana Scott circulated after Gödel died -- that one's about God's existence and is a can of worms in its own right. I'm after the seperate, much less formal rationality-of-the-world argument for personal survival specifically, and I've already got a few worries rattling around about it: whether it just smuggles in a full-strength Leibnizian principle of sufficient reason that's independently in trouble (modal collapse and all that), whether "rational" is equivocating between "intelligible" and "just, or fitting," and whether there's some tension -- or maybe an illuminating parallel -- with the optimism about the mind outrunning any formal system that he defends via the disjunction in the Gibbs lecture.

For a talk I'm giving at our grad reading group next month, can you write me an essay that reconstructs the argument as charitably as possible in something like premise/conclusion form, works through the strongest objections against it (mine or better ones you come up with), and then actually commits to a verdict? Something like "it fails as a proof but survives as a coherent expression of his broader rationalism" would count as an answer, but whatever you land on, argue for it -- don't just leave me with "reasonable people disagree."
