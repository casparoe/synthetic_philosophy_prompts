I'm a junior and one of four student officers who run my high school's Effective Altruism club, and our faculty advisor has asked me to bring a short written recommendation to Thursday's meeting on a charter question that has split us down the middle. Last term's ethics elective covered Peter Singer's shallow-pond argument, and afterward two of our officers proposed amending the charter so that anyone who wants to serve as an officer -- not ordinary members, just the four of us -- has to sign a public pledge committing to give at least ten percent of any future income to a top-rated charity for life, essentially our own version of the real Giving What We Can pledge. Our treasurer, who used to support the idea, now argues that it smuggles in exactly what our teacher called "the demandingness objection" to consequentialism, and she's been reading enough on her own to bring up Liam Murphy's proposal that an individual's duty to help should be capped at whatever their fair share would be if everyone else were doing their part too, rather than growing without limit just because other people are free-riding. The two officers who still want the pledge answer with a version of the skepticism Shelly Kagan raises in The Limits of Morality about principled caps on how much morality can demand: the reason to save a drowning child at small cost to yourself doesn't seem to get weaker just because nine other bystanders who could help are standing around doing nothing, so why should a "fair share" defined by an imaginary world of full compliance let any of us off the hook in a world where compliance is nowhere close to full?

Could you draft the memo I need to circulate before the vote? I'd like an actual recommendation -- adopt the pledge requirement, drop it, or adopt some modified version, such as a lower percentage, an opt-out clause, or a sunset review, whichever you think you can actually defend -- together with reasoning our treasurer could genuinely engage with rather than dismiss, and reasoning the pro-pledge officers could take just as seriously if the recommendation goes against them instead. I'd also like the memo to say something about whether Susan Wolf's worry about "moral saints" or Samuel Scheffler's idea of an agent-centered prerogative gives us any principled way to set the actual number, since whatever we decide will end up binding real teenagers for the rest of their adult lives, not just club members for a semester.
