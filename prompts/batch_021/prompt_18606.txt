For a chapter of my dissertation on the prehistory of collective intentionality, I keep returning to a comparison that the secondary literature does not seem to address. Contemporary theorists of joint action disagree over whether shared intention can be explained in purely individualistic terms. Michael Bratman holds that it reduces to a structure of interlocking individual intentions and meshing sub-plans, sustained by common knowledge and mutual responsiveness; nothing in this analysis requires that participants owe each other anything beyond what practical rationality demands, so the shared intention simply lapses if one party's own intention does. Margaret Gilbert denies this: joint action, on her view, requires a primitive "joint commitment," irreducible to any sum of individual commitments, which alone explains why participants acquire genuine obligations to one another that cannot be dissolved by a unilateral change of heart — rescinding the commitment requires the concurrence of all parties.

Rousseau, writing more than two centuries before either position was formulated, describes the founding of a political community as an act that creates a "moral and collective body" possessing its own unity and will, and he insists that this general will is categorically distinct from the "will of all," the mere aggregate of private wills. Given his actual arguments for that distinction, which side of the Bratman/Gilbert dispute do you think Rousseau's considered position supports, and why? Point to the specific doctrines that tell for or against each attribution, and flag anything in his account of the general will that resists assimilation to either camp.
