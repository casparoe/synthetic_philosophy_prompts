So I'm about a third of the way into a novel with a side character, Marguerite, a crotchety old jurisprudence professor who's taught the same Hart-Fuller unit for thirty years now -- rule of recognition, the grudge informer case, all of it. In the chapter I'm drafting, a student derails her seminar by bringing up something that happened well after both Hart and Fuller were dead: the 2016 hack of "The DAO" on Ethereum, where someone found a loophole in the smart contract's code and quietly drained something like fifty-odd million bucks worth of ether out of it, completely within the letter of what the code allowed. The Ethereum community ended up hard-forking the whole blockchain to reverse it, which is also why there's a seperate "Ethereum Classic" still limping along today -- the holdouts who rejected the fork on principle kept insisting the code's output was final no matter how unfair, because "code is law."

I want Marguerite's answer to be the centerpiece of that chapter, and I'd like it to actually be right, rather than having her just mouth some vague "ooh, code is law, very positivist" line and move on. I did a philosophy minor eons ago but never got this deep into legal theroy, so I don't trust myself to fake it convincingly on the page. So: given what Hart actually argued about the rule of recognition resting on the practices of officials rather than on whatever happens to be mechanically written down, would he really have accepted that the DAO's code was "the law" here? And what would Fuller have said this whole mess proved, or disproved, about his eight desiderata and the idea that a legal system owes some minimal fidelity to purpose over letter? Don't give me a wishy-washy "both sides have good points" -- actually commit to what each of them, true to their own arguments, would have said, and where that leaves the "code is law" crowd.
