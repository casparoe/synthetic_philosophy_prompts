So I somehow ended up on my university's Student Judicial Board this year (it's the mostly-student panel that decides sanctions when someone's charged under the conduct code), and we've got a case this Thursday thats got everyone kind of at each other's throats. About fifteen students occupied the lobby of the admin building for something like 36 hours to protest the school dragging its feet on fossil fuel divestment. Nobody got hurt and nothing got broken, but they blocked the main entrance so staff and some visiting donors couldn't get in, and they wouldn't leave when campus police told them to disperse, so now they're charged under the disruption and failure-to-comply sections of the code, which carry anything from probation up to a semester's suspension. The student speaking for the group wrote a statement arguing this was a textbook case of justified civil disobedience and that it should count as a major mitigating factor — she quotes Rawls's conditions for civil disobedience almost verbatim, plus Thoreau and the Birmingham jail letter. About half the board wants to give them the standard sanction because a rule's a rule regardless of motive, the other half wants to basically let them off because it was political speech, and honestly I don't think either half has really engaged with what those texts actually argue.

I'm taking a seminar on political obligation this term and we just wrapped the unit on civil disobedience, so I got voluntold to write the memo the board actually discusses before we vote. Two things are bugging me that I think the memo needs to deal with head on instead of hand-waving: first, Rawls builds his whole account around citizens of a "nearly just" constitutional democracy who can't just exit the system, and its not obvious that transfers cleanly onto a university code that students voluntarily signed onto when they enrolled — maybe the better frame is something like fair-play or associative obligation rather than the natural duty of justice Rawls has in mind. Second, Rawls and MLK both treat willingness to accept the legal penalty as part of what makes disobedience "civil" rather than evasion, so I'm not sure "drop the sanctions entirely" is even the outcome their own arguments support, even if the underlying protest was fully justified. Can you draft the actual memo I'd circulate? It needs to land on one specific recommended outcome (not "here are both sides"), give criteria the board could reuse for the next protest case that comes along, and take the strongest argument against whatever you recommend seriously rather than just gesturing at it before dismissing it.
