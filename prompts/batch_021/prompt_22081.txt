Kind of a random one, but two friends of mine got into a whole argument at dinner last week and I havent been able to stop thinking about it (I'm a philosophy PhD student working on causation, so I was dying to jump in, but made myself just referee it in my head instead). The story that kicked it off: a guy collapsed on a subway platform, a stranger nearby noticed but was on his phone and didn't react in time, the guy stumbled onto the tracks and died -- and there were also a dozen or so other people further down the platform who also just stood there and did nothing.

(1) Nadia said the closest bystander -- the one who noticed and had a couple seconds to grab him -- is a cause of the death, full stop: if he'd acted, the guy lives.
(2) Dev said that can't be right, because then literally everyone on the platform who could've intervened counts equally as "a cause," plus the transit authority for never installing platform doors, plus whoever decided against sensors -- all of those would also have prevented it, so singling out the one bystander is really tracking blame, not causation.
(3) Nadia answered that there's a real asymmetry: the transit authority's decision was made years earlier, and by the time the bystander had his moment, that was the last remaining opportunity to prevent it -- so it's proximity in the causal chain doing the work, not duty or blame.
(4) Dev shot back that "last opportunity" just relocates the arbitrariness instead of fixing it: picture two equally close, equally capable bystanders who both freeze, one a random tourist and one an off-duty lifeguard -- our gut says the lifeguard is somehow more "the" cause even though they're in the exact same causal position, which means duty or role sneaks back in anyway.

Neither of them budged (great dinner party). Can you go through this move by move and say who's got the better argument at each step -- not just an overall winner -- and flag anything you think both of them are missing?
