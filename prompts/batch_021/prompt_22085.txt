I am a philosophy PhD student working in formal epistemology and decision theory, and a reading group on Cicero's De Fato this term keeps intersecting with my own research in ways I did not expect. Chrysippus's reply to the Lazy Argument is that fate does not make deliberation pointless, because an outcome such as recovering from an illness is "co-fated" with the actions that produce it, calling a doctor being the standard example: fate operates through our choices rather than around them. I would like to know what he would say about Gibbard and Harper's "Death in Damascus": a man in Damascus is warned by Death, a perfect predictor, that Death will come for him tomorrow, so he flees overnight to Aleppo, only to find Death already waiting there, having predicted exactly that flight. Would Chrysippus treat fleeing to Aleppo as co-fated with dying in Aleppo, on the same model as calling the doctor being co-fated with recovery? Or does a predictor whose accuracy consists in correctly anticipating the very choice at issue introduce a dependence that his ordinary, cause-to-effect picture of co-fated events was never designed to handle, so that the honest Stoic verdict is that Death in Damascus is not really a version of the doctor case at all? Please work out the actual mechanics of his position rather than simply observing that the Stoics were compatibilists.
