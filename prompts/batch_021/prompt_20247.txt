Okay, this is going to sound niche, but "higher-order evidence" has been stuck in my head since a bad week I had last month. I'm a second-year resident, and I pulled a 28-hour stretch awake finishing documentation on a diagnostic call I'd made, and the next morning my attending forwarded me one of those articles about how past ~24 hours awake your reasoning gets about as impaired as being legally drunk. Which, cool, except when I reread my own reasoning that morning it still looked completely solid to me -- and that's apparently exactly what it would look like whether or not it actually was. I fell down a rabbit hole into the epistemic akrasia literature (basically: is it ever rational to believe P while also believing, on seperate grounds, that you shouldn't believe P) trying to figure out if I was even allowed to trust myself, and I never landed anywhere solid.

Then, completely separately, two of my co-residents, Dana and Marcus, got into it at journal club over a case that hit way too close to home. Their hospital's quality-improvement office had just released an audit showing that when attendings say they're "90% sure" a vague chest-pain presentation is anxiety rather than cardiac, they're only right about 75% of the time for female patients, even though the same "90% sure" calls track fine for male patients. (1) Marcus said that once an attending knows this and then examines a female patient and feels like saying "90% anxiety," they should drag that number down toward 75%, because "I went over the chart carefully and I still feel sure" isn't independent confirmation -- it's exactly what someone whose 90%-confidence calls are secretly running at 75% accuracy would also feel and say. (2) Dana said that can't be right in general: the audit is about a broad, vague reference class, while a given patient might have a specific finding -- say, pain that's reproducible on palpation -- that the audit never isolated, and if group-level bias stats automatically swamped case-specific findings, no diagnosis could survive contact with a bias study, which seems nuts. (3) Marcus said fine, but then "I found the detail that gets me back to 90%" is exactly what a motivated reasoner would also produce, so the doctor shouldn't trust that move either -- the honest thing is to hold two numbers apart, "90%, going by the chart" and "lower, given what I know about people like me," instead of forcing them into one. (4) Dana said that's not modesty, that's just epistemic akrasia with a nicer name -- believing the patient is 90% likely to have anxiety while also believing, by your own lights, that the rational credence is only 75%, is straightforwardly incoherent, and the doctor has to actually pick: revise down for real, or decide the finding legitimately defeats the defeater and stay at 90%, not hover in between and call it humility.

I've read enough now to know they're each echoing real positions in that literature, but I can't tell who's actually getting the better of it at each step, or whether there's a middle position they're both missing entirely. Could you go through moves (1) through (4) and adjudicate them individually rather than just declaring an overall winner?
