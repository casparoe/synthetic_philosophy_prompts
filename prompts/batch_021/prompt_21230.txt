So this is a bit random but it's actually become relevant to my actual job, not just idle curiosity. I'm on a small team building coding agents that periodically draft and redeploy patches to their own orchestration code (basically v_n writes and tests a patch, and only ships it as v_n+1 if it can formally verify the patch preserves a handful of safety invariants). In our reading group this week someone brought up "the Löbian obstacle" from the old MIRI tiling-agents stuff, and summarized it as: Löb's theorem proves a formal reasoner can never trust a successor that reasons from the same axioms, so any chain of agents that keeps verifying and signing off on updates to itself is mathematically guaranteed to break down eventually, no matter how careful each individual verification step is. That summary kind of spooked everyone because it sounds like its saying our whole "each version has to prove the next version is safe before handing off control" approach is a dead end in principle, not just something thats hard to engineer well.

I went and skimmed the actual Yudkowsky/Herreshoff paper plus a couple of "Lobstacle"-type posts afterward, but honestly my logic background isn't strong enough to tell whether thats really what the theorem shows, or whether its a flattened/exaggerated version of a narrower technical point that got telephone-gamed on its way to our reading group. Can you lay out what the Löbian obstacle actually establishes, and be honest about whether it really does rule out the kind of self-verifying update chain we're building, or whether there's a more precise (and maybe less doomy) way to state the real result that I should bring back to my team?
