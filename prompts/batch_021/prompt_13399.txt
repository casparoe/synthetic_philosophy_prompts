I'm a software engineer who got into Bayesian reasoning through prediction markets, and one result that still unsettles me is Aumann's agreement theorem, together with the no-trade results that followed from it: they seem to show that two Bayesians who share a common prior cannot knowingly keep betting against each other once each side's willingness to trade is common knowledge, yet that is exactly what happens on every market I have used. Write a dialogue, set at the moment two forecasters are about to shake hands on a wager, that dramatizes this tension rather than explaining it away. One character defends the Harsanyi doctrine, on which two rational agents can disagree only because they hold different information, and pulls back mid-handshake to insist that their standing disagreement must come down to some hidden asymmetry in what each of them knows -- not to any legitimate difference in priors. The other is a subjectivist about priors who is willing to say two equally rational people can simply start out believing different things, and who suspects the common-prior doctrine is a modeling convenience rather than a genuine requirement of rationality. Have each press the other on their weakest point -- what could possibly ground a "brute" difference in priors, versus whether the common-prior doctrine has any content that could actually be violated -- and do not let either side win by simply restating their opening position.
