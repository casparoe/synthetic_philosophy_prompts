I'm giving the best man speech at my oldest friend's wedding next month, and I've gotten stuck on something I can't quite write my way around on my own. I don't have any background in philosophy or economics -- I work in IT support -- but a couple of years ago I fell down a reading rabbit hole on akrasia, the old puzzle about how a person can genuinely believe one course of action is better and still, freely and knowingly, do the other one, and on hyperbolic discounting, the behavioral-economics idea that we discount rewards far more steeply over a short delay than over a long one, so that a promise which feels easy to keep from a distance quietly stops feeling binding the moment it's actually due. The groom is, affectionately, a living case study in both: something like eleven abandoned gym memberships, a novel that has been "almost finished" for a decade, and a running joke among our friends that future-him is a man who keeps making promises present-him has no intention of honoring. I want to open the speech by calling this wedding his first successful Ulysses pact -- the first time in his life he's tied himself to the mast in advance instead of trusting future-him to show up.

Here is where I keep getting stuck, and a couple of us in the wedding party have tried and failed to write past it together. If I lean on the akrasia framing -- weak will, knowing better and doing worse anyway -- the speech turns into a backhanded accusation, which isn't fair and isn't what I actually think of him. But if I lean on the discounting framing instead, and say that none of it was ever really weakness, just the ordinary mathematics of how a human discount curve behaves, then I've quietly pulled the rug out from under the whole toast, since that same framing says today's vow is exactly the kind of promise that gets discounted into nothing the moment it stops being convenient. I don't want to dodge that tension by staying vague and sentimental about it; I want a toast that actually works through it and leaves the room with a real reason, not a hand-wavy one, to believe this promise is built differently from the gym memberships. Could you write that speech for me? About five minutes read aloud, warm rather than lecture-y, with at least one line sharp enough that any guest who happens to know this literature will recognize exactly what's being done with it, but nothing so technical that his parents, who are hearing both of these terms for the first time tonight, feel like they've wandered into a lecture hall.
