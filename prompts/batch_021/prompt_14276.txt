In the decision-theory literature on acausal interactions -- cases where two agents can never make causal contact but can still shape each other's incentives through mutual prediction, as in discussions of acausal trade and acausal blackmail -- I've come across the proposal that an agent can defend itself against threats by adopting a surrogate goal. As I understand it, instead of trying to make yourself genuinely indifferent to whatever a threatener would actually want to target (which may be difficult, costly, or simply impossible to arrange in a way the threatener would find credible), you manufacture a cheap, essentially worthless goal and commit to responding to threats against it exactly as you would respond to threats against the real one, in the hope that threateners end up directing their threats at the decoy rather than at what you truly care about.

What I can't work out is why this would actually change a sufficiently rational threatener's behavior. If the threatener knows the surrogate goal is a decoy that you don't intrinsically value, why would they ever prefer threatening it over threatening your genuine vulnerability, rather than just threatening whichever target they already expect you to cave on? Please walk through the game-theoretic logic that is supposed to let surrogate goals redirect threats in this way, being explicit about what the threatener needs to believe, want, or be unable to distinguish for the scheme to work, and about what happens to the argument once those assumptions are relaxed -- for instance, if the threatener has any independent stake in inflicting real harm, or is able to tell the surrogate and the real goal apart. I'd also find it helpful to understand how this strategy is supposed to relate to an agent's more basic commitment never to give in to threats in the first place: is adopting a surrogate goal meant to substitute for that commitment, or to patch a specific gap that the commitment alone leaves open?
