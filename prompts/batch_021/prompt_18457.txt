So I fell down a rabbit hole a while back on classical Indian epistemology (started as a tangent off some old LW post on Bayesian "ways of knowing," as these things do) and got stuck on this argument between the Naiyayikas and the Mimamsakas that I can't shake. Nyaya says there's exactly four pramanas (roughly, the valid sources/means of knowledge) — perception, inference, comparison, testimony — full stop. The Mimamsakas say that list is short at least one: arthapatti ("postulation"), whose stock example is always poor Devadatta, who's fat but nobody ever sees him eating during the day, so you're forced to postulate he eats at night, since the fatness is otherwise inexplicable. Their claim is that this isn't just inference (anumana) in disguise, because ordinary inference relies on a vyapti — some already-known invariable connection between a hetu and a sadhya, like smoke and fire — that you then apply to a new case, whereas arthapatti starts from a single fact that's flatly unintelligible and works backwards to whatever removes the anomaly. The Naiyayikas (and iirc the Buddhist epistemologists too) say that's a distinction without a difference, and that you can always cash out the postulation as an ordinary syllogism with a manufactured vyapti bolted on after the fact. I keep flip-flopping on who's actually right here. Can you write me an essay that lays out the strongest version of both sides and then actually commits to an answer — is arthapatti a genuinely separate pramana, or does it just collapse into anumana?
