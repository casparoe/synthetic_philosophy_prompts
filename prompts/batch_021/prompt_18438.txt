As an undergraduate I concentrated in early modern philosophy, and Berkeley's case against the existence of matter in the Principles of Human Knowledge and the Three Dialogues Between Hylas and Philonous was something I could reconstruct from memory without much effort. Since graduating I've moved into product work at a small startup building a stablecoin, and for an internal reading group on the history of theories of money, each of us was asked to bring in an older text we thought was relevant. Half as a joke, I brought in Berkeley's Querist, his long series of rhetorical questions on Irish economic policy, expecting it to be a minor curiosity at best. Instead I found him arguing, decades after his metaphysical works, that coin and specie carry no wealth in themselves, that money is better understood as a kind of ticket or counter for reckoning and circulating credit and industry, and even sketching a scheme for a national bank issuing paper currency backed by nothing but public confidence.

Rereading the two sets of texts side by side, I keep noticing what feels like the same underlying move being made twice. In the Principles, Berkeley holds that the esse of a sensible thing is percipi: its whole reality consists in being perceived and put to use by a mind, and positing some further material substratum beneath our ideas is both unintelligible and unnecessary. In the Querist, he seems to run a strikingly similar argument about money: asking after the "intrinsic value" of coin, over and above its role in enabling exchange and stimulating industry, looks like the same confused demand for a substratum that money simply doesn't need in order to do its job. My reading group meets next week, and rather than show up with a vague impression, I would like to have an actual argument worked out.

Could you argue, as rigorously as you can, that Berkeley's monetary theory in the Querist is best read not as an unrelated foray into economic pamphleteering but as a direct extension of the immaterialist metaphysics of the Principles and the Dialogues, with money and matter playing structurally parallel roles: neither has any being apart from its function for perceiving or acting agents, and demanding an "intrinsic" value or substance behind either amounts to the same philosophical mistake in two different domains? As part of the argument, please also engage with the strongest objection: that money's reality is public and institutional, presupposing agreement among many minds, in a way that a Berkeleian idea had by a single perceiving spirit is not, so the analogy may break down exactly where it would need to hold.
