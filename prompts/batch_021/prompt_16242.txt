I had my appendix taken out about three weeks ago now (it ruptured before they got it out, so it's been a whole saga), and I'm still on light duty at my parents' place, which means I've been around for a lot of dinner conversations I probably shouldn't have started with an offhand comment about my scar. Last weekend one of them turned into a genuine arguement between my dad and my older sister, who's a few years into a PhD in evolutionary biology, about whether the organ they cut out of me actually does anything. I've been turning it over since because neither of them seemed straightforwardly wrong to me.

Here's roughly how it went. (1) My dad opened with: the appendix is just a leftover pouch from when our ancestors ate a lot more raw plant matter and needed extra gut space to digest it, we clearly don't need it now, surgeons remove it all the time with no downside, so it doesn't actually do anything for you anymore. (2) My sister pushed back with something she'd read: there's a hypothesis that the appendix acts as a kind of shelter for good gut bacteria, helping repopulate your microbiome after something like a bad stomach bug wipes it out, and that people who've had theirs out seem to have a harder time with certain recurring gut infections afterward — so it obviously does do something, which means it has a function, end of story. (3) My dad wasn't buying it: "does something helpful" can't be the same as "has a function," he said, or every random side effect of every body part would count as one — his surgery scar is great for winning bar bets, that doesn't make winning bar bets the function of scar tissue. What matters is what the appendix was actually built by evolution to do, and that's digesting plant fiber; any bacterial-hotel benefit now is just it being in a convenient spot, not what it's "for." (4) My sister's comeback was that biologists have actually looked at the appendix across a big pile of unrelated mammal lineages and found that it's evolved independently, over and over, way more often than you'd expect by chance — and it tends to stick around even in species that don't need the digestive version. That's exactly the pattern you'd expect, she said, if it keeps getting re-selected for something else, like immune support, rather than just being dead weight nobody's bothered to clean up — so by my dad's own standard of "what it was selected to do," the honest answer in a lot of these lineages might just be immune support. (5) My dad's response was that sticking around isn't the same as being positively selected for a specific job; it might just not be costly enough to get weeded out. And if "it keeps getting reused for whatever's handy" counts as a real function, he said, then "function" stops explaining anything and just means "whatever it's good for this week." (6) My sister said that standard can't be right either, because then nothing could ever pick up a genuinely new function at all — bird feathers apparently started out for heat regulation and only later got co-opted and refined for flight, and nobody wants to say feathers still have no flight function just because that's not why they first showed up. At some point, she said, being maintained and refined because of a new effect has to be enough, and she thinks the appendix clears that bar. My mom changed the subject to pie shortly after and nobody ever conceded anything.

I can't tell who's actually right at each step, or whether they're both quietly assuming something false about what "having a function" even means. Could you go through the six moves one at a time and say who's got the better of it at each point, not just who wins overall? A short verdict per move would be great — feel free to score each one from -2 (pretty clearly off base) to +2 (basically right) if that's a useful way to organize it — plus whatever you think both of them are missing about the underlying question.
