Over the past year I have been slowly working through a translation of Nagarjuna's Mulamadhyamakakarika on my own, without any background in philosophy, mostly because a friend who meditates got tired of my questions about what Buddhists mean by "emptiness" and told me to go read the source instead. One thing keeps stopping me cold: Nagarjuna's habit of using what the translator calls the tetralemma, or catuskoti. The clearest example is the question of whether a fully awakened being continues to exist after death, where instead of picking one answer he rejects all four of the available options—that it does, that it does not, that it does both, that it does neither. Everything I remember about logic says that once "it does" and "it does not" are ruled out, "both" and "neither" are supposed to mop up whatever is left between them, so rejecting all four together looks to me less like a hard truth to accept and more like a plain contradiction. I do not think Nagarjuna is simply confused, since people who have spent their careers on this clearly treat it as a serious, coherent position, so I assume there is some distinction I am missing—maybe about different senses of "exists," or about conventional versus ultimate levels of truth—that keeps the four-way rejection from collapsing into nonsense.

There is a second puzzle that feels connected to the first. Nagarjuna's central claim is that absolutely everything lacks any fixed, independent essence (I have picked up the Sanskrit word svabhava for this) and is therefore empty. But "everything lacks a fixed essence" is itself a general claim about how things are, and I cannot see how it avoids applying to itself: either it quietly makes an exception of itself, in which case it was never really a claim about everything, or it applies to itself too, in which case the claim that everything is empty is just another empty, dependently arisen piece of language with no better claim to being correct than its denial would have. The introduction to my edition mentions in passing that Nagarjuna wrote a separate, shorter text specifically answering people who accused him of exactly this kind of self-refutation, but the footnote just says he "defuses the objection" and moves on without ever explaining how.

What I would really like is for someone to walk through both of these together, since I suspect they are one puzzle wearing two hats. How is the fourfold rejection in the tetralemma supposed to avoid simply violating the law of excluded middle, and how is the claim that everything—including emptiness itself—lacks inherent existence supposed to avoid undercutting its own right to be asserted? I would especially appreciate it if the explanation did not just gesture at "the two truths" as though naming the distinction automatically resolves the tension, since that is exactly the move every short summary I have come across seems to make right before changing the subject.
