I recently switched the focus of my dissertation to the metaphysics of propositions, and my committee has asked me to write a chapter assessing the "act-based" theories of propositions developed by Scott Soames and Peter Hanks, on which a proposition's unity is secured not by any further relation among its worldly constituents but by the thinker's own act of predication. Both authors present this move as their way of finally escaping Bradley's regress, which threatens any account of a proposition as a mere complex of terms plus a relation, since that relation will itself need some further relation to tie it to the terms, and so on without end. I would like help reconstructing how F. H. Bradley, reasoning from the metaphysics of *Appearance and Reality*, would most plausibly have judged this proposal. Bradley treated the regress not as a puzzle to be patched but as a positive argument that relations, and therefore any "complex" whatsoever, are never ultimately real; given that, would he grant that relocating the unifying work into a mental act of predication truly dissolves the regress, or would he insist that it simply resurfaces one level up, inside the act itself, making "predication" just as question-begging a primitive as the relation it replaced? Please ground your answer in the actual structure of Bradley's argument rather than a general impression of his idealism.
