I'm a software engineer, and over the last few weekends I've been working through the theory-of-computation chapters of a textbook I picked up after a coworker got me nerd-sniped by a tangent about esoteric programming languages. I got stuck, in a productive way, on the section covering the Church-Turing thesis, and when I ran my confusion by a friend who's finishing a CS PhD, her answer didn't fully satisfy me, so I'm hoping for a more thorough take.

Here's the issue. Turing's machines, Church's lambda calculus, Gödel and Kleene's general recursive functions, Post's production systems, and later Markov's normal algorithms were all developed more or less independently, out of quite different intuitions about what "mechanical computation" should even mean, and every one of them was subsequently proven, in the ordinary rigorous sense, to compute exactly the same class of functions. Both the textbook and my friend insist that, despite this, the Church-Turing thesis remains permanently stuck as a "thesis" rather than a theorem, because it equates an informal, pre-theoretic notion — an "effective procedure" — with a formally defined one, and you supposedly cannot give a mathematical proof of an equivalence when one side of it isn't itself a precisely defined mathematical object. That feels like a slightly too-convenient way of dodging the obvious conclusion: if half a dozen independently motivated formalizations of "what can be computed by following fixed mechanical rules" all converge on the exact same answer, isn't that convergence itself a proof that we've correctly captured the informal notion — no less rigorous than, say, the epsilon-delta definition replacing the intuitive notion of a continuous function? So which is it: is there a substantive, principled reason this kind of convergence can never amount to proof, or is "the thesis is unprovable in principle" really just an inherited bit of caution among logicians that nobody has seriously re-examined since Turing and Church's day?
