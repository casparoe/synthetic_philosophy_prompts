I teach a graduate seminar, cross-listed with economics, on the philosophical foundations of bargaining theory, and this week we have been working through the two canonical axiomatizations of the two-person bargaining problem: Nash's original solution and the rival solution proposed by Kalai and Smorodinsky in 1975. Both begin from a feasible set of achievable utility pairs together with a disagreement point, and both impose Pareto optimality, symmetry, and invariance to positive affine rescaling of utility; where they part ways is that Nash additionally requires independence of irrelevant alternatives, whereas Kalai and Smorodinsky replace that requirement with an axiom of individual monotonicity. Informally, this axiom requires that if the feasible set expands so as to raise the most that one party could conceivably obtain, while leaving unchanged the most the other party could obtain, that first party should never end up worse off at the solution. Since these two requirements cannot both hold in general, the two solution concepts must sometimes disagree -- yet several of my students suspect the disagreement is a merely formal curiosity, since every worked example in our textbook happens to involve either a symmetric problem or a linear Pareto frontier, and on any such problem, as it happens, the two solutions always coincide exactly.

To make the conflict vivid for next week's class, I would like you to construct, from scratch, a concrete two-person bargaining problem on which the Nash solution and the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution recommend two distinct outcomes. Please specify the feasible set explicitly -- either as a simple polygon given by its vertices, or as a region bounded by an explicit and clearly non-linear Pareto frontier -- along with a disagreement point, and then compute both solutions for your example with enough precision to demonstrate, numerically, that they diverge. Finally, use your example to diagnose the conflict: state which of the two axioms, independence of irrelevant alternatives or individual monotonicity, is violated by which solution in this case, and comment briefly on why some combination of asymmetry and a kinked or curved frontier was indispensable to your construction -- that is, why no symmetric bargaining problem, and no problem with a linear frontier, could ever have exhibited the divergence you are asked to produce.
