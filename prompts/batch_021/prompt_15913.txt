I've been going down a Plato rabbit hole for a piece I'm writing on drinking culture, and I stumbled onto something in the Laws that's messing with my whole picture of the guy. I always had Plato pegged as the original buzzkill about this stuff -- reason ruling over appetite, the tripartite soul, Alcibiades' drunken entrance in the Symposium basically played for cringe. But in Book 1 (running into Book 2) of the Laws, the Athenian Stranger spends ages arguing that Sparta and Crete are actually wrong to just ban symposia outright, and that a well-regulated drinking party, run by a sober "symposiarch," is genuinely useful pedagogically: getting citizens mildly drunk under supervision works as a kind of training exercise for self-control, the same way exposing soldiers to controlled fear trains courage, and it also strips away people's social mask so a legislator can see what someone's character is actually like underneath. Then it gets even stranger -- he says wine is basically a gift from Dionysus to help old men loosen up enough to sing and dance in the choruses without embarrassment, and that getting properly drunk once you're old is actually fine, even good, like a medicine against the stiffness and gloom of age, softening a person the way fire softens iron.

That's a pretty different Plato then the one I learned about in school -- not "reason must suppress appetite" but something closer to "controlled indulgence of appetite is itself a technology for producing virtue." I mentioned this to a philosopher friend and she said this is a genuine fault line among people who work on the Laws: some read it as Plato in dead earnest, extending his usual interest in habituation and testing virtue into a new domain, others read it as basically a hack -- the Athenian can't ban the symposium the way Sparta and Crete did (its too central to Greek social life), so instead he dresses up a concession as high theory.

So here's what I want from you: an essay that actually takes a side on this. Does Plato's defense of regulated drunkenness in the Laws sit comfortably with his psychology of the soul and his theory of habituation elsewhere in his work, or is it in real tension with it? And more specifically: is the drunkenness itself doing the philosophical work here -- does temporarily losing rational control have genuine pedagogical value on Plato's own terms -- or is all the real work being done by the surrounding safeguards (the sober leader, the legal limits, the age restrictions), with intoxication itself still treated, underneath it all, as something bad that's merely being managed? Weigh the strongest version of both readings, but don't leave me hanging with an "on the one hand, on the other hand" -- land on an actual verdict and defend it.
