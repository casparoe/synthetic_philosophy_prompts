A friend and I spent a long evening arguing over whether two honest, rational people can ever knowingly agree to disagree; neither of us budged, and the conversation kept circling back to Aumann's agreement theorem. I'm a teaching assistant this term for a graduate seminar in formal epistemology where the theorem came up on a problem set, and before I record grades I'd like a disinterested second read on one student's submission.

The question was: State Aumann's agreement theorem precisely, including the role of the common prior and of common knowledge of posteriors. Sketch the proof for the two-agent, finite-state case. Then, in three or four sentences, explain why the theorem is compatible with the fact that reasonable, well-informed people often go on disagreeing.

The student wrote the following. "Aumann's theorem considers two Bayesian agents, 1 and 2, who share a common prior P over a finite state space and who each receive private information represented by a partition of that space. At the true state, each agent's posterior for an event E is obtained by conditioning P on their own information cell. The theorem says that if the two agents' posteriors, q1 and q2, are common knowledge -- that is, each agent knows both q1 and q2, and each knows that the other knows both -- then q1 must equal q2. So two people who share the same prior cannot knowingly disagree about the probability of an event, even if their posteriors rest on completely different private information.

The proof takes the meet of the two agents' partitions, the finest common coarsening of the two, so that the cell of the meet containing the true state is a union of whole cells of agent 1's partition and, separately, a union of whole cells of agent 2's partition, and, by common knowledge, lies entirely inside both the event that agent 1's posterior is q1 and the event that agent 2's posterior is q2. Since agent 1's posterior equals q1 on each of agent 1's partition-cells that make up that meet-cell, conditioning P on the meet-cell reproduces q1 by the law of total probability, since a weighted average of numbers that are all q1 is just q1; the same argument on agent 2's side gives q2 for that identical meet-cell, so q1 = q2.

Despite this, real people go on disagreeing because the premises rarely hold outside idealized models. Aumann's own follow-up work showed that even two agents who start out without common knowledge of each other's posteriors will be driven to it, and so to the same number, if they simply take turns publicly announcing their current probability for E, converging after finitely many rounds. So persistent disagreement mostly reflects people not actually exchanging their honest posteriors, rather than any flaw in the underlying logic."
