So I'm prepping this week's formal epistemology seminar and I keep getting tangled up on something I used to think I understood cleanly: why the "diachronic" Dutch book argument (the one that's supposed to justify updating by conditionalization) gets treated as so much shakier than the plain old synchronic Dutch book argument for probabilism. On the surface they look like the same move — show that violating some constraint on your credences (additivity in one case, conditionalization in the other) lets a bookie build a combo of bets that guarantees you loose no matter how things turn out, so violating the constraint is irrational. But I recall folks like Maher and Christensen (or maybe it was someone else, my memory of the exact papers are fuzzy) basically arguing the diachronic version doesn't do the work its defenders want it to, while still being totally happy with the synchronic one. I'd like to actually be able to explain the asymmetry to my students instead of just hand-waving at it next week.

My vague recollection is that the trouble has something to do with the bookie needing advance knowledge of what your credence will become after you learn some proposition E, which is a much weirder assumption than just knowing your current betting quotients, and also something about a "sophisticated" agent being able to dodge the trap just by refusing to commit to one of the two bets ahead of time — without ever actually conditionalizing — which, if right, seems to show that avoiding the sure loss doesn't specifically require conditionalization at all. Could you walk me through the actual structure of the two bets in the diachronic case (roughly when each one gets offered and how the payoffs are rigged so they guarantee a loss), and then lay out the strongest version of the "this doesn't really show what it claims to show" objection? And if you have room, a sentence or two on how this connects to the broader worry that Dutch book arguments only ever establish a *pragmatic* cost to incoherence rather than a properly *epistemic* one would be great too — that's a distinction I gesture at in lecture but have never nailed down to my own satisfaction.
