I'm a PhD student in economics. A few of us run a joint reading group with some formal epistemology students from the philosophy department, working through the epistemic foundations of game theory, and two members -- Felix, from our side, and Noor, from theirs -- got stuck in an argument neither would let go of. The flashpoint was Aumann's result that common knowledge of rationality forces the backward-induction outcome in finite games of perfect information, which we'd been working through via the centipede game: each round, a player who "passes" grows the shared pot, but risks having the other player "take" it all, and backward induction says the first player should take immediately, leaving almost everything on the table.

(1) Felix said the theorem vindicates backward induction completely: if rationality is common knowledge, no player will ever choose, at any node, an action that isn't optimal given what happens afterward, so working back from the last node forces the outcome all the way to move one. On this view, passing beyond the very first node only makes sense if you doubt that rationality is actually common knowledge.

(2) Noor objected that this can't be the whole story, because it requires saying something about what a player should believe at nodes that, by the theorem's own premise, should never be reached. If it were really common knowledge that everyone is rational, an observed deviation should be impossible -- so nothing in the setup tells us what a player is entitled to infer once they've actually seen one. She said the proof smuggles in, through its own definition of a rational strategy, exactly the conclusion it's supposed to derive.

(3) Felix replied that this rests on a misreading: "rational," for Aumann's purposes, isn't a claim about what a player infers mid-game after a surprising move -- it's a static property of a complete contingency plan, true just when the plan's prescribed action is optimal at every node given the player's information there, whether or not that node is ever reached. Common knowledge, on that reading, is common knowledge of this dispositional fact about strategies, fixed before play starts, so nobody ever has to update on anything, and the circularity Noor is worried about doesn't arise.

(4) Noor answered that this rescues the theorem from inconsistency at the cost of changing the subject: it no longer tells an agent who actually finds themselves at an off-path node, wondering what to make of the other player's deviation, anything about what to believe or do next -- which was the interesting question to begin with. A result that's airtight about dispositions but silent about deliberation-in-the-moment, she said, doesn't earn the right to be described as showing backward induction is "required by rationality" in the sense anyone actually cares about.

They've gone another two rounds since without either side budging. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move -- saying who has the stronger position at each of the four numbered points rather than just declaring an overall winner -- and flag anything you think both Felix and Noor are overlooking about what Aumann's theorem does and doesn't establish?
