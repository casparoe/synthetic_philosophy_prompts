Okay so I had this whole argument with my friend Priya last night about whether Zhuangzi actually thought death was BETTER than life, not just nothing to fear, and we never really settled it so its bugging me. Her whole case rests on that passage in the "Perfect Happiness" chapter where Zhuangzi is riding through Chu, sees an old bleached-out skull by the road, taps it with his whip, and fires off a string of questions about how it died — lost your kingdom and got executed, did something shameful and couldn't face your family, froze or starved, or just plain old age. Then he uses it as a pillow and falls asleep, and at midnight the skull shows up in the dream and tells him that whole list sounds like something a rhetorician would say, that it's all just "the entanglements of the living," and that death has no ruler above, no subjects below, no four seasons to worry about, and that even a king facing south on his throne isn't happier than this. Zhuangzi says he doesn't believe it and offers to get the skull's body and family back for it, and the skull just scowls and asks why it would trade a king's happiness for that. (Paraphrasing a bit here, don't have the book on me, but that's the shape of it.)

Priya says this settles it, death > life, straight from the source. I keep getting stuck on two things: the skull's supposedly rank-less bliss keeps getting measured against a king anyway, which feels self-undermining, and somewhere else in the book Zhuangzi flat out refuses to say hating death isn't just like a kid who's lost and doesn't realize he's actually headed home — which sounds like agnosticism, not a preference. Plus this whole speech comes via a dream, from a guy who elsewhere won't even commit to waking life being more real than dreaming. So who's actually right here — is the skull's speech Zhuangzi's real view, or is the scene built to wreck anyone who wants a tidy answer out of it (us included)?
