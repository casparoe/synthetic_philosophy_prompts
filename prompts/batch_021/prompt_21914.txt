This is the kind of exercise that turns up constantly on rationalist blogs and forums — take a historical thinker's stated principles and run them forward onto something they never lived to see — so I want to give it a careful shot on a case I don't think usually gets done well: Aristotle and cryptocurrency.

His discussion of chrematistike in Politics I.8–10 draws the line fairly precisely: wealth-getting that stays subordinate to oikonomia and bounded by household need is natural; nomisma itself exists only by convention, to serve exchange; and once acquisition is cut loose from use and pursued as an end in itself — his paradigm cases being retail trade and, above all, usury — it becomes the unnatural form of chrematistike. Usury draws his sharpest condemnation because interest (tokos) makes money itself breed more money, "the birth of money from money," exactly what a mere medium of exchange isn't supposed to do.

Applying that framework to Bitcoin: would Aristotle lump mining, buy-and-hold speculation on price appreciation, and interest-bearing crypto lending together as one undifferentiated case of unnatural chrematistike, or would he need a distinction his own categories don't obviously supply — between a currency whose scarcity is fixed by protocol rather than sovereign decree, say, or between speculating on something with no use value at all and trading a good that might genuinely be needed wherever no shared civic currency is available? Give me a specific, defended verdict, not just a list of open considerations.
