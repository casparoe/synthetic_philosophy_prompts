I spent the better part of thirty-five years running a behavioral pharmacology laboratory, and a considerable share of that career was devoted to intracranial self-stimulation (ICSS) experiments, a paradigm going back to Olds and Milner's original rat studies of the 1950s: animals fitted with electrodes in reward-related circuitry, trained to press a lever that delivers a brief pulse of stimulation directly to that tissue. Some of my own animals would return to that lever compulsively, at the expense of food, water, and every other form of behavior an ethologist would call natural. Since retiring, I have finally had the leisure to work through the moral philosophy I always meant to read properly, and I have lately been going slowly through Bentham's "An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation." It struck me, reading his account of how pleasure and pain are to be measured, that my animals were unwitting subjects in a kind of applied hedonic calculus, and that they have an odd modern echo in a worry now current among people who build reinforcement-learning systems: that a trained agent may learn to tamper with the very mechanism that generates its reward signal, rather than doing whatever that signal was meant to encourage in the first place — a failure mode often called "wireheading" or "reward tampering." A friend from my reading group insists that Bentham, presented with either case, would simply have shrugged and pronounced the calculus vindicated: more pleasure, obtained more efficiently, is still more pleasure. Having watched what direct stimulation actually does to a nervous system, I am not so sure.

I would like your help settling this properly. Working from Bentham's text on its own terms, rather than through the objections of later critics such as Nozick, please reconstruct what you take to be his most consistent verdict on both cases — an animal or human self-stimulating a reward circuit, and an artificial agent that has learned to short-circuit its objective in an analogous way. Go through his own seven circumstances for weighing a pleasure or a pain one at a time — intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, and extent — and tell me, for each, whether wireheading comes out ahead of or behind the ordinary, indirect pleasures his calculus was built to compare it against. Then tell me whether the calculus, as Bentham actually formulated it, leaves him any principled resource for resisting the conclusion that a wireheaded mind is thereby living about as good a life as any other, or whether he would have had to amend the framework once confronted with the possibility.
