I'm a novelist in the final stretch of a novel whose climax turns on a daughter forcing her dying, alcoholic father to finally accept responsibility for abandoning his family, rather than hide behind the claim that he simply "turned out this way." I want the scene to earn its catharsis honestly, but Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility, which he insists holds whether or not determinism is true, seems to undercut the premise: since you do what you do because of the way you are, and you can never be truly responsible for the way you are, true desert looks impossible for anyone. Could you describe the most important implications of the Basic Argument for how I write this confrontation, in particular whether Strawson's distinction between "true" or "ultimate" responsibility and the everyday responsibility we assign in ordinary life leaves any principled room for real moral reckoning, or whether the father's excuse is, on this argument, simply unanswerable?
