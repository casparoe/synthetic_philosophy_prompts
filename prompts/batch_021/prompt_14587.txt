Since retiring after thirty-two years running an analytical chemistry laboratory, I have joined a small philosophy reading group at the local university, and this term we have been working through classic material on Bayesian epistemology. I was struck by Richard Jeffrey's observation, in *The Logic of Decision*, that evidence does not always arrive in the tidy form that ordinary conditionalization presupposes, where one becomes fully certain of some proposition and revises accordingly. His illustration was judging the color of a piece of cloth by candlelight: one's confidence that it is green might rise substantially, from, say, one half to four fifths, without ever reaching certainty, and every other belief that depends on the cloth's color has to be nudged along with that partial, uncertain shift. He proposed a generalized updating rule, now usually called Jeffrey conditionalization or probability kinematics, built around exactly this kind of case.

This struck a chord because it describes, almost exactly, the epistemic situation I lived in for three decades. A doublet in a mass spectrum, or a chromatogram peak eluting a shade earlier than the reference standard, very rarely gave a clean yes-or-no verdict on whether a target compound was present; more often it nudged one's confidence up or down by some amount that a bench chemist would estimate by feel and rarely attach a number to. I would be grateful if you could describe the most important implications of Jeffrey conditionalization for how one should reason through a sequence of such uncertain instrumental readings - say, three successive runs on the same sample, each shifting your credence that "compound X is present" without ever pinning it at zero or one.

There are two aspects of the framework that I have not been able to work out on my own and would especially like you to address. First, Jeffrey's rule is typically stated together with a rigidity condition requiring that the conditional probability of the hypothesis on each element of the evidential partition remain fixed across the update; I am not sure what this actually forbids in a case like mine, where a new run often changes not just how confident I am in an earlier ambiguous reading but how strongly I think that reading bears on the hypothesis in the first place. Second, I gather that, unlike ordinary conditionalization on certain evidence, successive Jeffrey updates on different uncertain readings need not commute, so that the final verdict on the compound's presence can depend on the order in which the runs happen to be processed. I would like to understand whether that order-dependence exposes a real defect in how my colleagues and I used to combine ambiguous readings, or whether it simply reflects a genuine path-dependence in how uncertain evidence works, one that any adequate theory of partial belief revision would have to accommodate.
