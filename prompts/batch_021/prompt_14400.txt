I spent thirty-five years doing condensed matter physics before I retired, and it's only in the last year or two that I've actually sat down and read philosophy of physics properly, instead of just absorbing the folklore version the way most working physicists do. Bell's theorem is a good example of what I mean. What I got taught in grad school was basically: Bell proved you have to give up either locality or realism, the experiments come down against locality (and I gather Aspect, Clauser and Zeilinger got the Nobel a couple years back for nailing the loopholes shut), so nature is nonlocal, end of discussion, now back to computing band structures. I mentioned this to an old officemate of mine recently and he just said "shut up and calculate," which is exactly the attitude I'm trying to get past at this point in my life.

The more I read though -- some Maudlin, a Wiseman paper that distinguishes Bell's original 1964 argument from his later, more careful "local causality" version, and the superdeterminism stuff Hossenfelder and Palmer have been pushing lately -- the more that "locality or realism" framing looks like a huge oversimplification. There's really at least four seperate places you can locate the damage: drop locality itself (Bohmian mechanics), drop statistical independence between the hidden variables and which measurement gets chosen (superdeterminism, retrocausality), drop the idea that there's a single determinate outcome at all (many-worlds), or drop the assumption that unmeasured quantities have values in the first place (Copenhagen/QBism-ish views). Each of those has serious defenders, and each one pays a real price for it that doesn't get mentioned in the popular version.

So here's what I'd like: an essay that actually walks through those four escape routes, is honest about what each one costs and not just what it's called, and then commits to a verdict -- which one is least bad, or an argument that we're simply stuck with nonlocality and the rest are just fancier ways of not saying so. Please don't give me the wishy-washy "reasonable people disagree" non-ending so many essays default to -- take a side and defend it using the actual structure of Bell's argument, not the "spooky action at a distance" one-liner.
