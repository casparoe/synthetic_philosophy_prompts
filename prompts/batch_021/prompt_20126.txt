So I'm in a reading group working through List and Pettit on judgment aggregation, and we just got to the discursive dilemma via the classic three-judge contract case: the court has to rule on whether there was a valid contract (p), whether the defendant breached it (q), and on liability, which is stipulated to hold iff p and q both hold. The paradox is that majority voting on p and on q seperately can each come out true while majority voting directly on liability comes out false, so a premise-based procedure and a conclusion-based procedure can hand down opposite verdicts from the exact same panel of judges.

Where my classmate and I disagree is what to make of this. She's convinced by the further move List and Pettit make: because the very same group verdict can be "realized" by all sorts of different individual judgment profiles (lots of different combinations of who-believes-what among the judges add up to the same majority outcome), the group's belief is multiply realizable over individual beliefs in roughly the sense that a mental state like pain is supposed to be multiply realizable over physical states -- and that this licenses treating the group's belief as a genuinely autonomous, sui generis attitude, not just a shorthand for "these particular individuals voted this way, via this procedure." I think that's a bad inference dressed up in respectable functionalist clothes. Can you make the strongest version of my side -- that the multiple-realizability analogy doesn't buy group belief anything like ontological autonomy, that "the committee believes p" stays fully eliminable in favor of individuals-plus-procedure talk even once you grant the discursive dilemma, and that whatever's actually doing the work for multiple realizability in the philosophy-of-mind case (causal/explanatory role, presumably) is exactly what's missing on the group side? Get into the premise-based vs. conclusion-based procedure stuff directly rather than staying at the level of generalities, if you can.
