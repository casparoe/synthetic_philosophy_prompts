A few months ago I developed a fear of flying after a genuinely rough patch of turbulence on a work trip, and I've been doing cognitive behavioral therapy for it ever since. The therapy rests on a fairly explicit picture of what fear is: my therapist has me identify and rewrite the "automatic thought" supposedly driving the fear (something like this turbulence means the plane is going to break apart), on the assumption that revising the belief should dissolve the feeling along with it. I've done the revising, repeatedly and, I think, successfully — I know the safety statistics better than some pilots I've flown with, and at the level of considered belief I don't think the plane is in any real danger — and yet the fear shows up exactly on schedule the moment the seatbelt sign lights up, apparently indifferent to what I "believe." I gather this is a banal experience to anyone who's ever had a phobia, but it's new to me, and it's sent me down a bit of a rabbit hole.

A friend finishing a philosophy PhD tells me this is a stock case of what the literature calls a "recalcitrant emotion," and that cases like it sit right at the fault line between two rival theories of what an emotion fundamentally is. On one side are judgment (or cognitive) theories, in the lineage of people like Solomon and Nussbaum, on which having an emotion essentially involves making an evaluative judgment — fear, on this view, is something in the neighborhood of judging a situation to be dangerous. On the other side are perceptual theories, in the lineage of people like Prinz (who calls his version an "embodied appraisal" theory) and Tappolet, on which an emotion is closer to a perceptual state representing a value property (dangerousness, say) somewhat the way vision represents color — a state that can therefore come apart from what one believes, much as a visual illusion persists even once one knows it's an illusion.

What I'd actually like, rather than a summary of the disagreement, is to watch the two views fight it out over a case like mine. Could you write a dialogue between a proponent of the judgment theory and a proponent of the perceptual theory, set on a turbulent flight, in which the two of them keep being interrupted by (and drawn back to) a nearby passenger who is white-knuckled with fear while calmly assuring her seatmate that she knows, statistically, there's nothing to worry about? I'd like the judgment theorist to take the obvious move of distinguishing the passenger's reflective, endorsed belief from some more automatic or habitual evaluative judgment that's doing the emotional work, and I'd like the perceptual theorist to press on whether that move genuinely rescues the judgment theory or just quietly smuggles in something like their own view under a different name. I'd like the judgment theorist to press back in turn on whether "perceiving danger," in anything like the way one perceives a Müller-Lyer illusion, actually explains anything distinctive about fear, or merely borrows the vocabulary of perception without earning it. Please have both positions argued by someone genuinely sympathetic to them, with real concessions and real pressure exchanged on both sides, rather than one side winning by default.
