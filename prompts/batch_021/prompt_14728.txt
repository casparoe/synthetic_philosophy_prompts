A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about A.J. Ayer and his brush with death. In 1988, a year before he died, Ayer choked on a piece of smoked salmon while in hospital and went into cardiac arrest for several minutes; he later wrote candidly about the strange experience he underwent during that time, and admitted that it had left him, against his own expectations, slightly less certain that his death would simply be the end of him, while insisting the episode had not converted him to any religious belief. My friend maintained that, as a thoroughgoing empiricist, Ayer would have had no choice but to treat any well-documented empirical evidence of awareness during cardiac arrest as a serious challenge to his atheism, and so would have revised his views much further than he actually did, had he lived to see the resuscitation research of recent decades -- multi-center studies, of the kind associated with the critical-care physician Sam Parnia, that monitor brain activity through cardiac arrest and use hidden visual targets to test whether patients can later report real events they should not have been able to perceive once their hearts had stopped and their EEGs had gone flat. I argued this gets Ayer's verification principle backwards: the interesting question for him was never whether unusual experiences occur during cardiac arrest, which he was quite prepared to grant, but whether any such finding, however well-documented, could count as evidence for the metaphysically loaded claim that a person survives their own death, rather than merely showing that the boundary of brain death is fuzzier than assumed. Who is closer to the mark? Reconstruct, as precisely as you can, how Ayer's actual commitments in Language, Truth and Logic, together with his own post-resuscitation remarks, would have led him to assess this later research, and state plainly whether it would have moved him further from, or back toward, his lifelong atheism.
