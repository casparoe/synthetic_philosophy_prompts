So lately I've been going down a rabbit hole reading about the history of logic for fun (no formal background, just wikipedia and a couple library books), and I got kinda stuck on Gottlob Frege, the guy who's usually credited with inventing modern formal logic and the whole sense/reference distinction thing. Turns out the year before he died, in 1924, he kept a private diary that's genuinely ugly - nationalist, anti-democracy, antisemitic, wanting Jews stripped of political rights, that sort of thing. It didn't surface until decades after he died, and apparently even the philosopher most famous for championing his work in English (Dummett, I think?) admitted later he was kind of floored by it.

I brought this up at dinner and it turned into a whole back-and-forth between my sister Rachel and her husband Dan that never really got resolved. Roughly:

(1) Rachel said this should genuinely change how seriously we take the philosophy around the technical stuff - not the proofs themselves, but his big confident claims, like that thoughts are objective and mind-independent, existing (his actual words, apparently) in a "third realm." She said a guy that obsessed with purifying logic of anything "merely psychological," who also wanted the political sphere purified along ethnic lines, isn't a coincidence you just shrug off, and it should atleast make you read his non-formal claims more suspiciously.

(2) Dan said that's a textbook genetic fallacy - whether sense and reference are really distinct has nothing to do with what Frege thought of Weimar democracy, and if we start being "suspicious" of claims based on the psychology of whoever had them, we'd have to throw out huge chunks of math and philosophy, since plenty of major contributors were awful people in one way or another.

(3) Rachel said she's not touching the formal results, she agrees those are true or valid no matter who found them - it's specifically the unprovable, take-my-word-for-it parts of his philosophy where a person's temperament has room to leak in, and noticing that isn't the same fallacy as doubting his math.

(4) Dan said that's a distinction without a difference, because now she just gets to label whatever part of someone's philosophy she wants as "the soft part where psychology counts," and no philosopher's speculative claims would survive that standard once you go digging for personal ugliness.

Neither of them budged. Can you go through this move by move and say who's got the better of it at each step, not just call an overall winner? And if you think they're both missing a better way to frame the actual issue, I'd want that too.
