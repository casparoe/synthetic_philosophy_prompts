Me and a buddy of mine (he's a couple years in to a philosophy PhD, I just write backend code for a living) have been arguing for weeks about whether "emergence" is a real thing or just a fancy word for "stuff too complicated to predict." My go-to example is always Conway's Game of Life: gliders and spaceships show up out of four dead-simple rules, and nobody could've told you in advance what patterns would appear, but every cell's state is still fully fixed by the rules plus the previous board - nothing extra gets added anywhere. That's basically my whole case for thinking any "emergent" property in science - wetness, hurricanes, maybe even consciousness - is what I've since learned people call "weak emergence": surprising, and only predictable by actually simulating the system out rather than some shortcut, but not ontologically novel, no new causal juice added anywhere.

Where I get stuck is that my friend keeps bringing up Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument as support for the OPPOSITE view, real "strong" emergence with genuine downward causation, which trips me up because I thought the whole point of that argument was to make trouble for people who believe in exactly that. His pitch (as far as I can reconstruct it after a few beers) goes something like: if you actually want higher-level stuff to be doing real causal work rather than being epiphenomenal window dressing, and you're not willing to just reduce it away to the physical level, the exclusion argument doesn't refute strong emergence at all - it forces your hand into it, since the only premise left to reject at that point is the causal closure of the physical, and ditching closure is basically what strong emergence with real downward causation amounts to.

I don't think he's right but I can't currently poke a hole in it, so I'd like you to do his side of the arguement justice instead of mine: lay out the strongest possible case FOR genuine ontological/strong emergence with irreducible downward causal powers, specifically working through how Kim's exclusion argument gets repurposed as a premise in favor of that view rather than an objection to it. Don't dumb it down on my account either - I've read enough pop philosophy of science (Nagel's bridge laws, multiple realizability, that whole cluster) to follow along if you want to get into the weeds.
