Been down a bit of a rabbit hole this week after someone in an ACX comments section mentioned, almost in passing, that Mill wasn't just the buttoned-up classical liberal we all know from On Liberty and Utilitarianism -- that late in life him and Harriet Taylor actually described themselves as socialists of a sort. So I went and read the relevant bits of the Autobiography and then the unfinished Chapters on Socialism that got published after he died. It's a pretty interesting mix: he's actually quite harsh on a lot of the *existing* socialist schools of his day (Owenism, Fourierism, the St.-Simonians, Louis Blanc-style state workshops) on practical grounds -- worried about incentives, about who ends up doing the unpleasant work, about whether people will actually pull their weight without personal reward -- while at the same time being pretty clearly convinced that the wage/capital system as it existed in Victorian England was just a passing historical stage rather than some permanent law of nature, and getting genuinely enthusiastic about worker cooperatives and profit-sharing schemes (there's a whole section of the Principles on this, "the probable futurity of the labouring classes" or something like that) as the gradual, non-revolutionary route toward something better. He was also pushing land-nationalization-ish stuff near the end of his life through that Land Tenure Reform outfit he helped set up.

Anyway, obviously he died in 1873, decades before socialism actually got tried in a bunch of very different flavors over the next century and a half -- Soviet-style central planning, Fabian gradualist welfare statism, Yugoslav worker self-management, Scandinavian social democracy, Chinese state capitalism, even stuff like the Mondragon cooperative today. Based on what you know of his actual late views (not just the pop version of Mill as basically a libertarian who wrote one weird chapter on economics), which of these would he look at and go "yeah, this is roughly the sort of thing I was gesturing at," and which would he see as a betrayal of everything he cared about re: individuality and liberty? I'm especially interested in whether the guy who wrote the Chapters on Socialism could stomach even a little bit of Soviet-style central planning as a "necessary transitional stage," given how paranoid he generally was about concentrated power, or whether he'd be firmly on the cooperative/gradualist end of the spectrum no matter what. Would love to hear your actual reasoning here rather than just a vibes-y answer.
