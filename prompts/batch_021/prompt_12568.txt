I'm at work on a novel centered on two estranged siblings: a widow who has entered into a devoted, entirely text-based relationship with a conversational AI companion, and her brother, a once-celebrated sculptor whose recent exhibitions consist of pieces generated by a machine-learning model that he merely selects, titles, and signs. Before I draft the scene in which the two of them finally quarrel over whose life is actually going well, I want the philosophical ground beneath their argument to be sound rather than merely fashionable. In Principia Ethica, G. E. Moore argued that personal affection and the contemplation of beauty are, together, very nearly the only things intrinsically good in themselves, and he defended the latter claim partly through his well-known comparison of a beautiful world and a hideous one, neither ever perceived or known about by anyone. Working strictly from the structure of Moore's own arguments, rather than from present-day intuitions about artificial intelligence, what do you think he would have concluded about whether the widow's attachment to her AI companion counts as the kind of personal affection he prized, and whether her brother's curated, machine-generated sculptures possess the sort of beauty he considered valuable in itself? I would like your best-supported verdict on each question, not simply a survey of considerations on both sides.
