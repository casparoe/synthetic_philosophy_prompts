I am a novelist whose last three books have each turned on a single counterfactual hinge in nineteenth-century European history. I have been asked to give the opening remarks at a week-long residential workshop for historical novelists, nearly all of whom, like me, build their books around the dramatic weight of individual choice at a turning point. Rereading Tolstoy's second epilogue to War and Peace beforehand has left me unsettled: the most celebrated historical novel in the language was written by a man who used that very book to insist that Napoleon's will barely mattered to the war's outcome, that crediting "great men" for what happens is close to superstition, and that history can only become a science by giving up the flattering illusion of individual centrality, much as astronomy had to give up a stationary earth. I don't want to open this workshop by reassuring the room that novelists' instincts about agency are basically sound, which I doubt, nor by conceding that the craft we're all here to practice rests on a discredited illusion, which seems needlessly demoralizing. Write the fifteen-minute talk I should give, pitched to novelists mostly unfamiliar with historiography, that takes Tolstoy's own paradox seriously and reaches an honest, defensible answer to what a historical novelist is entitled to claim in dramatizing a hinge moment.
