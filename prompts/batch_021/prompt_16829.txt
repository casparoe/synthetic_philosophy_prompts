I spent my working life as a physicist, so for years the only thing I knew about Émilie du Châtelet was that she translated Newton's Principia into French and tacked on her own commentary about vis viva -- solid, technical stuff. Since retiring I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the local university (they let old retirees crash the seminar, bless them), and last month we read her much shorter, much more personal essay, the "Discourse on Happiness." I was kind of thrown by it: from what I can tell she argues that being happy actually requires holding on to certain illusions -- that someone who's stripped away every prejudice and sees everything with perfect clarity ends up worse off, not better. She lists health, virtue, taste, strong passions, and a capacity for illusion as the ingredients of a happy life, explicitly pushing back on the Senecan/Stoic idea that you should root out the passions altogether, and she singles out study as the one passion that never lets you down, especially once you're older and love or ambition have faded (which, as a retired guy, I appreciated). She's also refreshingly honest that she herself can't quit gambling, and more or less defends the habit.

Here's what bugs me though: its the same woman whose day job, so to speak, was defending Newtonian mechanics against the Cartesians and writing a physics textbook steeped in Leibniz and Wolff's principle of sufficient reason -- someone who basically staked her reputation on rigorous, clear headed rationality. So I want an essay that actually settles this for me: is her claim that we need illusions to be happy in real tension with those broader rationalist, Enlightenment commitments, or is there a decent way of reading "illusion" in the happiness essay that gets her off the hook? Lay out the strongest version of the "she's being inconsistent" charge, then the strongest version of a defense, and then actually pick one and argue for it. I don't want a namby-pamby "both sides have a point" conclusion, I want an actual verdict.
