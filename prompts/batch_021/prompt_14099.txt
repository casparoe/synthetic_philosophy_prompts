So I've been down a rabbit hole this week that started with some dumb twitter argument about whether a politician has any "standing" to criticize corruption when his own party does basically the same thing, and it somehow ended with me reading old LW/ACX-adjacent threads on hypocrisy and blame, which led me to G.A. Cohen's essay about who has standing to condemn terrorists (the one that leans heavily on the Israel/Palestine case). Theres a distinction that keeps getting made in this literature between whether an act is blameworthy -- i.e. whether the claim "X was wrong to do that" is true -- and whether some particular person has standing to voice blame for it. Everyone agrees "tu quoque" (pointing out that the accuser does the same thing) is a fallacy if you're using it to argue the original act wasn't actually wrong. But in the same breath a bunch of these philosophers (Cohen, and I think theres a rebuttal paper by Fritz and Miller or someone with a similar name going the other direction) say hypocrisy CAN undermine your standing to blame, even though it does nothing to the truth of the accusation itself.

I don't really get how those two claims are compatible without "standing" collapsing into something purely social/rhetorical (like "people just won't listen to a hypocrite," which is more a fact about persuasion than ethics) -- that seems too deflationary given how these philosophers talk, like there's a genuine normative fact about who "gets to" blame whom, over and above whether the blame is true. Could you walk me through the actual mechanism thats supposed to be doing the work here? Like, what exactly is the standing condition attached to -- the act of blaming, the assertion, some implicit claim the hypocrite is making about themselves? And why would violating it not also contaminate the warrant of the underlying moral claim, given that on the face of it hypocrisy seems totally orthogonal to the object-level facts? If it's useful, feel free to lay out a couple of the competing stories on offer (e.g. something about implicitly exempting yourself from a norm you apply to others, vs. something about equal standing/moral address between persons, vs. the whole thing really being a different phenomenon -- like discrediting, or complicity -- that's getting mislabeled as a standing issue). I want the actual logical structure of the puzzle, not a "here's why hypocrisy is bad" essay.
