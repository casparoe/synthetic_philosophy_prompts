At our local ACX meetup's reading group, during a session nominally about Bayesian confirmation theory, two regulars named Elena and Dmitri got into a disagreement about the problem of old evidence that I haven't been able to fully resolve myself.

(1) Elena opened with the standard example: the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion (roughly 43 arcseconds per century) had been a known puzzle since Le Verrier flagged it in the 1850s, and when Einstein showed in 1915 that general relativity entails exactly that figure, everyone treated it as strong confirmation of GTR, even though the evidence long predated the theory. She argued this is a genuine problem for Bayesian conditionalization, not just a curiosity: if an agent already assigns the perihelion anomaly probability 1 as background knowledge, then P(H|E) = P(H) for literally any H, so strict conditionalization implies this couldn't have been real confirmation -- which contradicts everything we know about how physicists actually treated it.

(2) Dmitri responded that the standard fix handles this cleanly: drop the assumption that agents are logically omniscient. Einstein didn't already assign probability 1 to the claim that GTR entails the Mercury figure -- deriving that took real mathematical work. So what actually confirmed GTR wasn't the old evidence E itself, but the new logical fact that H entails E, a proposition the agent hadn't previously been certain of. Once credences can attach to entailment claims and not just to observation sentences, he said, the paradox dissolves and ordinary conditionalization is saved.

(3) Elena granted that this blocks the strict formal contradiction, but said it proves too much: on this story you could "confirm" almost any theory after the fact just by hunting until you find some already-known fact it happens to entail, since a sufficiently rich theory entails no end of true sentences. Unless you can independently say which derived entailments are allowed to count, the move just relocates the mystery instead of solving it. She added that it doesn't match the history either: nobody consciously updated on the discrete proposition "GTR entails the perihelion figure" the way you'd update on drawing a card from a deck.

(4) Dmitri answered that the "proves too much" worry has some force but isn't fatal: what made the Mercury case impressive wasn't merely that GTR entailed an old fact, but that it did so without tuning any free parameters to match it, unlike ad hoc patches grafted onto Newtonian gravity for the same purpose. Build that "no fudge factors" condition into how surprising the entailment would be under some suitably chosen catch-all rival, and the account survives. As for the history, he said that's beside the point, since Bayesian confirmation theory was never meant as a description of anyone's actual thought process.
