I'm currently taking a graduate seminar on social choice theory and judgment aggregation, and as part of a peer-review exercise I've been asked to grade a classmate's written response to one of our practice questions before the instructor reviews our marks. The question was:

"Using the classic three-judge breach-of-contract example, explain what the discursive dilemma shows about aggregating individual judgments on logically connected propositions, and state precisely which condition on judgment-aggregation rules is given up by (a) propositionwise majority voting and (b) the premise-based procedure."

My classmate answered:

"The discursive dilemma, which List and Pettit developed by generalizing the doctrinal paradox that Kornhauser and Sager identified in their work on collegial courts, illustrates how a group of individually consistent judges can produce a collectively inconsistent verdict. Take a three-member panel ruling on a breach-of-contract suit, where liability (R) is stipulated to hold just when there was a valid contract (P) and the defendant breached it (Q), i.e., R ↔ (P ∧ Q). Suppose Judge 1 accepts P, Q, and R; Judge 2 accepts P, rejects Q, and rejects R; and Judge 3 rejects P, accepts Q, and rejects R. Taking a separate majority vote on each proposition yields a majority for P (Judges 1 and 2), a majority for Q (Judges 1 and 3), but a majority against R (Judges 2 and 3) — so the panel's own verdict on R contradicts what P and Q jointly entail. Since the court's ruling on R fails to depend, in the way the connecting rule demands, purely on the separately tallied verdicts for P and Q, propositionwise majority voting here violates independence, the condition that the collective judgment on any one proposition should be a function only of individual judgments on that same proposition. Courts standardly avoid this by adopting a premise-based procedure: they vote on P and Q separately and then simply read off R from the rule, guaranteeing a verdict that respects R ↔ (P ∧ Q). The cost is that this procedure is no longer anonymous, since it treats the panel's collective attitude toward R as answerable to P and Q rather than to any votes cast on R directly. In effect, the discursive dilemma shows that universal domain, anonymity, independence, and collective rationality cannot all be secured together once judges must vote on logically connected questions, and any workable procedure has to relax at least one of them."
