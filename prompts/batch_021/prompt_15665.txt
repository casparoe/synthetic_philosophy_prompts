I spent about thirty-five years as a geochemist, a good chunk of it measuring iridium levels in K-Pg boundary clay (yes, that boundary, the one with the dinosaurs), and now that I'm retired a few of us from the old department have started a little reading group to get through philosophy books nobody had time for during our careers. My daughter thinks its a phase. Anyway we just worked through some classic material on explanation in history -- Hempel's covering-law model versus the Dray/Danto/Gallie narrative-explanation crowd -- and it hit closer to home than I expected, because we used to have almost this exact argument amongst geologists about the extinction event itself, just without ever putting a name to it.

Here's the part I cant get straight in my head though. Hempel says a genuine explanation has to subsume whatever's being explained under a general law, statistical laws included, and if you can't produce the law you're only offering an "explanation sketch" -- basically an IOU that the laws are there even if unstated. Dray and Danto say that's the wrong picture of what's going on -- history explains through colligation, through "narrative sentences" that only make sense in light of later events, through the event's place in a larger story, and none of that is standing in for missing laws, it's doing the explanatory work on its own terms. But every time I try to actually locate the disagreement it slips away from me. Couldn't Hempel just say the impact-winter story for the extinction is a sketch resting on a mountain of unstated physical and ecological generalizations, and couldn't a narrativist turn right around and say that any "law" you'd ever actually cite in a historical explanation is so hedged with exceptions that it's really the narrative arc doing all the work?

So here's what I'm hoping you can help me sort out: is there an actual, adjudicable disagreement between the covering-law camp and the narrative camp once you strip away the rhetoric, or does it dissolve into two vocabularies for the same underlying practice? And if there really is a substantive difference, what's the strongest version of the narrativist case you can make -- one that doesn't just amount to "history feels different from physics"? I'd rather not get the two-paragraph textbook version of "some people think explanation needs laws, others disagree," I can dig that up myself -- I want something that actually grapples with the Hempel/Dray/Danto back-and-forth.
