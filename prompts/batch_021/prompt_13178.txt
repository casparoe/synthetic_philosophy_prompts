Since my retirement from a career in evolutionary biology, I have taken up philosophy in earnest, and within decision theory I keep returning to the idea of "program equilibrium": the proposal, going back to Moshe Tennenholtz's early work on the topic, that if players in a game submit programs rather than actions, each able to inspect its opponent's source code before computing a move, then a suitably designed pair of programs can guarantee mutual cooperation in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma as a genuine equilibrium, with no repeated play and no external enforcer. Some constructions escape the obvious regress ("I cooperate if you cooperate if I cooperate...") by way of a fixed-point trick that leans on Löb's theorem. Having spent my working life on the evolution of cooperation among organisms that cannot read one another's code, I find the whole idea both fascinating and a little suspicious. I would like you to write a dialogue between two colleagues who must decide, within the next few minutes, what disposition to submit on behalf of their joint project to a one-shot, code-transparent negotiation of exactly this kind. One colleague holds that this sort of source-code legibility genuinely solves the general problem of trust between rational agents, so that submitting the cooperative program is simply the rational choice; the other holds that it is an elegant trick that works only under idealizations — common knowledge of the literal running code, unbounded proof search, no possibility of misrepresentation at the level of the interpreter — and that betting the project on it is naive. Please give both characters equal argumentative skill, have them engage directly with each other's points rather than talking past one another, and do not force a tidy resolution if the arguments genuinely do not yield one.
