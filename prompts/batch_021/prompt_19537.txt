My aunt Miriam died last month at eighty-three, and I have been asked to give her eulogy at the memorial service this weekend. She taught Victorian literature for thirty years, and George Eliot was the center of her intellectual life -- not chiefly the novels, though she loved them, but Eliot's own religious trajectory: the loss of her evangelical faith in her twenties under the freethinking Bray circle in Coventry, the rift this caused with her father, and her translations, first of Strauss's Life of Jesus and then, under her own name -- the only book she ever published as herself -- of Feuerbach's Essence of Christianity.

Miriam liked to retell the story of Eliot telling F. W. H. Myers, in a Cambridge garden, that of the three words God, immortality, and duty, the first meant nothing to her, the second she could not credit, and the third was nevertheless absolute and unyielding; this was, more or less, Miriam's own creed as well. She wanted no talk of heaven at her funeral, and no reunion with my uncle "on the other side" -- nothing she would have called a comforting lie. But she also loved Eliot's idea, from the poem about joining the "choir invisible" and from the closing page of Middlemarch, that a person can go on mattering after death only through the difference she made to people who are still alive, and she did not want the eulogy to be cold or merely academic either.

About eighty people will be at the service: her surviving siblings and their families, ordinary practicing Christians who will be listening for whatever counts as comfort in their own idiom, and several dozen former students and colleagues who will recognize exactly what she meant by "the choir invisible" and will notice immediately if I flatten her actual views into generic uplift, or make her sound as though she is delivering a lecture from beyond the grave. Could you write the eulogy? It should run five to seven minutes read aloud, it needs to work for both halves of the room at once rather than address them in turn, it must not promise Miriam anything she spent sixty years declining to believe, and it should read as a eulogy for a specific, loved person rather than as an essay about George Eliot.
