I've been down a rabbit hole on Hilbert's list of 23 problems lately and problem 1, the continuum hypothesis, is the one that messes with my head the most. Hilbert was famously confident that every math problem has a definite answer -- his big line was "we must know, we will know" -- and he even argued that proving a problem CAN'T be solved still counts as settling it, so either way there's no such thing as a question math can't answer (he was arguing against this whole "ignorabimus" idea floating around back then, basically "we will never know"). But decades after he died, Cohen showed, building on Godel's earlier consistency proof, that CH is actually independent of the usual ZFC axioms of set theory, meaning you literally can't prove it or disprove it from them. Hilbert died in 1943, way before Cohen's proof came out in '63, so he never got to react to it himself.

So which is it: would Hilbert count this as a win for his whole worldview (we now have an actual proof that CH can't be settled the normal way, and proving impossibility was always fair game for him), or would it have wrecked him, since "it's literally undecidable from the axioms we've got" sounds a lot like exactly the kind of unanswerable question he spent his career insisting didn't exist? Does you're answer change at all once you bring in that people have gone looking for new axioms to settle CH anyway? Don't just give me a "nobody can really know what a dead guy would've thought" cop-out -- actually get into his specific views on formalism and decidability and commit to a real answer.
