For the past three years, a former colleague of mine, Owen, and I have co-produced and sold a self-paced online course on backend system design. We've always split the revenue 50/50 without much debate, since we contribute roughly equally to keeping it updated. A well-known tech-education company has now offered to acquire exclusive rights to the entire back catalog for $120,000, an amount Owen and I are free to divide between ourselves however we like (we co-own the material outright, so the exclusivity deal requires both of our signatures). Separately, the company also wants to sign one of us to a two-year, $300,000 "staff instructor" contract to build new flagship content; based on their audience testing, they've made clear they want Owen specifically for that role, and it isn't something I could take even if I wanted to. Crucially, the instructor contract only exists as part of the same bundle: the company won't do it unless we also grant exclusivity, and won't grant exclusivity unless I agree too. If we don't reach an agreement on the whole package, their fallback is a smaller, non-exclusive license on the existing catalog worth $40,000 total, split 50/50 as usual — meaning $20,000 each — and the instructor offer disappears entirely.

Owen's proposal is to split the $120,000 catalog payment 50/50, giving each of us $60,000, and to keep the entire $300,000 instructor contract for himself, on the grounds that "that one's mine, you couldn't have done it anyway." That leaves him with $360,000 total and me with $60,000. Something about this nags at me: yes, I can't do the instructor work, but Owen also can't unlock any of that $300,000 without my signing off on exclusivity, and it feels like my agreement ought to be worth more than just my usual half of a licensing fee. At the same time, I don't think I'm owed some kind of half-share of the $300,000 either, since that money really is tied to work only Owen can do. A mutual friend who practices law told us Owen's proposal "sounds about standard," which didn't really settle anything for me, since I'm not sure what principle she was applying. We're meeting the company's lawyers again next week, and I'd like to reason this through properly rather than negotiate from gut feeling. Is Owen's proposed split defensible, and if not, what would a more principled way of dividing this actually look like?
