Ok so I fell down a rabbit hole this week that started with re-reading Robin Hanson's old "homo hypocritus" posts and the hypocrisy chapter of The Elephant in the Brain, where the framing is basically that hypocrisy is a mostly-unconscious, evolved status/signalling strategy rather than some weird glitch in our moral reasoning. I brought this up on a discord with some friends and one of them insisted that this basically dissolves the philosophical question of whether hypocrites "lose standing to blame" people for stuff they do themselves, since its all just signalling anyway, but that felt too fast to me. So I went and looked at what actual moral philosophers say about the narrower question of whether being a hypocrite costs you your standing to blame others for the very thing you're also guilty of (the "who are you to talk" move, aka tu quoque). Turns out theres a whole little mini-literature on this from the last ~15 years, and I've only read abstracts plus skimmed a couple of the actual papers, so I'm probably garbling some details, but here's roughly my understanding of the main camps:

- Wallace's "equal standing" view: hypocritical blame is defective because blaming someone presupposes you'd accept being blamed the same way for the same thing yourself, and the hypocrite implicitly denies the target's equal standing under the very norm they're invoking.
- Cohen's "silencing, not refuting" view (from "Casting the First Stone"): a hypocrite's record undercuts their standing to voice the charge, but that's a purely pragmatic/dialectical defect in the speech act — it does nothing to show the charge itself is false.
- Todd's "commitment" view: by having acted the same way themselves (while presumably not thinking that made them blameworthy), the hypocrite is committed to a claim that's flatly inconsistent with the blame they're now dishing out, so the blame is self-undermining rather than illegitimate for some further, independent reason.
- Bell's skeptical take: there's no genuine free-standing "standing" norm here at all — whatever's doing the real work in these cases is just ordinary facts about sincerity, evidence, or fairness, dressed up as something more exotic.
- Fritz & Miller's sincerity/commitment account: blaming presupposes being genuinely committed to the standard invoked, and a hypocrite's pattern of exempting themselves shows (whatever they say) that they aren't actually so committed.
- Isserow & Klein's deflationary view: there's no special, independent "hypocrisy" norm at all — apparent cases of standing-loss are fully explained by more familiar things like insincerity or unfair double standards.
