I'm teaching a graduate seminar on decision theory this term, and the session that produced the most disagreement wasn't the standard Newcomb problem but Gibbard and Harper's 1978 "Death in Damascus" case. The setup is this: a man in Damascus is confronted by Death, who announces that he has an appointment with the man tomorrow. The man may stay in Damascus overnight or flee to Aleppo, and Death—a perfect predictor—will already have arranged to be wherever the man actually ends up. The trouble for causal decision theory is that neither pure strategy is stable in the way the theory usually wants a rational choice to be: run the causal expected-utility calculation on the supposition that the man has settled on staying, and fleeing comes out looking better, since a predictor tracking that supposition would be fixed on Damascus; run the same calculation on the supposition that he's settled on fleeing, and staying comes out better. So neither act is "ratifiable" in the technical sense causal decision theorists use elsewhere to rule out unstable choices, and the natural fallback—recommending some mixed strategy, coin flip included—has struck some decision theorists as a genuine solution and others as merely relocating the instability, since a sufficiently good predictor can be stipulated to track probabilistic plans as well as deterministic ones.

Write an essay that works through this dispute on its own terms, rather than by re-running the familiar one-box/two-box debate. Explain why Death in Damascus poses a distinctively harder problem for causal decision theory than ordinary Newcomb cases do, assess whether appealing to ratifiability—with or without randomization—genuinely rescues the causal decision theorist, and state what, if anything, the case shows about the comparative merits of causal versus evidential decision theory. Please reach and defend a definite verdict rather than concluding that both theories are defensible in their own way: a conditional verdict is acceptable (for instance, "ratifiability succeeds provided the predictor cannot anticipate genuinely randomized choices, and fails otherwise"), but only if you say exactly what is doing the work in the condition and defend it.
