I have spent a fair amount of time on my own working through Epicurus's argument that death cannot be bad for the person who dies, since there is no subject left to bear the harm once death has actually occurred. In reading around the topic, I came across Lucretius's related but, I think, genuinely separate point: that the infinite span of time before our birth and the infinite span of time after our death are symmetrical, so that if we are untroubled by the first we have no good reason to dread the second. Popular treatments tend to fold these two arguments together as a single "Epicurean" package, but the first seems to rest on the absence of a subject at the moment of harm, while the second rests on a claimed symmetry between two stretches of non-existence, and I am not convinced the two really stand or fall together. Could you state each argument as precisely as possible in its own terms, and then work out whether an objection that defeats one of them, such as the common reply that death is bad because it deprives us of goods we would otherwise have gone on to enjoy, also defeats the other? I am especially interested in whether it is possible to consistently accept that reply as a refutation of Epicurus while still finding Lucretius's symmetry point troubling, or whether accepting it settles the matter for both arguments at once. Please justify your answer rather than simply asserting it.
