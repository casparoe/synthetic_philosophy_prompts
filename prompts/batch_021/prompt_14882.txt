I'm in a grad epistemology seminar right now and we just spent two sessions on the "bootstrapping problem" (sometimes called the problem of easy knowledge) that Vogel and Cohen raise against dogmatist/reliabilist theories of perceptual justification. Quick recap in case it's useful: say your experience that something looks red gives you justification to believe it's red, with no antecedent need to already know your color vision is reliable (that's basically what dogmatism about perceptual justification says). The worry is that you can then rack up a big pile of beliefs like "on this occasion my color perception was accurate," inductively generalize, and conclude "my color vision is reliable" - and you'd get exactly the same string of confirmations whether or not your vision is actually any good. That feels like way too cheap a route to a substantive conclusion about your own reliability, but its genuinely hard to say what rule is being broken.

What's been bugging me (brought it up with my study group and nobody had a great answer) is how similar this is, structurally, to the Cartesian circle that Arnauld raises against Descartes in the Fourth Objections: Descartes wants to use his clear and distinct perceptions to prove God exists and isn't a deceiver, and then use that result to certify the general reliability of clear and distinct perception - which is using the faculty to vouch for itself, same shape as the bootstrapping case. Descartes' actual way out, as I understand it, leans on a distinction between the perception you're having right now, which is supposedly self-certifying while you're actually attending to it, and your memory of having once perceived something clearly and distinctly, which is what actually needs God's guarantee against the demon hypothesis.

So here's what I actually want from you: if someone handed Descartes the modern bootstrapping problem (the red-perception case above, or whatever concrete version you like) and made him solve it using only concepts available to him - no borrowing reliabilism, safety, or sensitivity conditions from the 20th/21st century - do you think his memory-based response to Arnauld actually generalizes to block it? Or does the modern version expose that his answer to the circle never really worked and just relocated the bootstrapping to a different level (trusting memory instead of trusting perception)? Give me your best reconstruction of the Descartes-y response and then tell me straight up whether you think it actually works.
