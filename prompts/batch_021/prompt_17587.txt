I'm a PhD student in cognitive science; my own work is on visual perception, not emotion, which is partly why the following has been bothering me more than it probably should. Last winter, my grandmother was hospitalized for a procedure that turned out to be minor and low-risk, during the exact week a fellowship application of mine was due. After a lot of deliberation, I decided not to fly home and to finish the application instead, on the grounds that she was very likely to be fine and that missing the deadline would set my funding back by a year. She recovered without complications, and when I've discussed the decision since with my advisor, my partner, and my grandmother herself, everyone, myself included, has agreed it was a reasonable call given the constraints I was actually facing. I don't think I did anything wrong, and I'm fairly confident I'd choose the same way again.

And yet, months later, I still feel a distinct stab of guilt every time the episode comes to mind, as though I had abandoned her, even though, on reflection, I don't believe, and perhaps never really believed, that I did. When I mentioned this to a few people in my cohort, half told me the feeling would fade with time and half told me it probably never would, neither of which helped me understand what the feeling actually is. What strikes me as strange is not the guilt itself but its refusal to update: ordinarily, when I decide a fear or a suspicion was unfounded, it fades roughly in step with my being convinced, the way a belief should once the evidence for it disappears, but this hasn't faded at all. I'm less interested in whether I should feel guilty, since I've already concluded I shouldn't, than in what kind of mental state is producing the guilt in the first place. Is it simply a belief I haven't fully revised, lagging behind my own better judgment the way an old opinion sometimes does? Is it closer to a perceptual illusion, something that goes on presenting the situation as wrong even after I'm entirely convinced it isn't, the way a visual illusion persists once you know better? Or is it best understood as a bare bodily reaction with no real content to be true or false, correct or mistaken, in the first place, so that asking whether it fits the facts is the wrong kind of question to ask? I'd be grateful for your take on which of these, if any, actually captures what's going on when a feeling like this outlasts the judgment that was supposed to justify it.
