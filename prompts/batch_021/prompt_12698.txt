I'm writing a novel in which two once-close philosophers -- former doctoral supervisor and student, now estranged and sniping at each other through footnotes -- are seated together at a colleague's memorial reception and, against their better judgment, resume an old quarrel over the rule-following considerations. She has settled into something close to Kripke's skeptical solution: there is no fact about what anyone means by a sign, only a community's practice of endorsing and correcting usage. He calls that a surrender, and holds instead that facts about meaning are real and irreducibly normative -- that no further fact was ever owed to the skeptic in the first place. Write the scene. Let the quus paradox be their common ground, and let the real dispute be whether a communal practice of correction can actually underwrite the normativity of meaning, or merely imitate it from outside. It should read as two people who once admired each other's minds and now can't forgive what's become of them, not as a seminar transcript -- and neither should win by default or be reduced to a foil for the other's cleverness.
