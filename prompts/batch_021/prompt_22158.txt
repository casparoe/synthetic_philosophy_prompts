So this year I'm taking a comparative religion elective and we just spent like two weeks on classical Indian philosophy, and I kinda went down a rabbit hole on the Nyaya school's theory of knowledge (the pramana stuff). They basically say there's four legit sources of knowledge -- perception, inference, comparison, and testimony (sabda) -- but the one that's stuck with me is testimony. From what I read, Nyaya says a statement only counts as real knowledge if it comes from an "apta" (basically someone competent who isn't trying to deceive you), but once that's satisfied, actually understanding the sentence and forming the belief is treated as its own direct process, not something you consciously infer every time someone tells you a fact. My teacher mentioned that some rival schools (Vaisheshika and a couple Buddhist guys) didn't even accept testimony as its own pramana and said it's really just a disguised form of inference, which got me thinking about a totally different debate.

That debate being: in my other class (kind of a Theory-of-Knowledge type thing) we covered this whole Hume vs. Reid disagreement about testimony in western epistemology. Hume basically thought trusting testimony is secretly just induction -- we've noticed peoples words usually line up with reality so we infer this new statement probably does too -- while Reid thought that's backwards, and that we've got this built in "principle of credulity" that just makes us believe testimony by default, totally separate from inference, kind of on the same level as trusting our own perception.

Now I can't stop thinking about whether Nyaya's account of testimony is basically doing the Hume thing, the Reid thing, or something else entirely. Like, does the "apta" requirement mean Nyaya is secretly sneaking in a reliability check that's really just an inference (which would make it more Humean, or even close to what those rival schools said), or does the fact that they still treat the resulting cognition as its own separate pramana, not reducible to inference, make it way closer to Reid? Or is it doing some third thing that doesn't map onto either side of that debate at all? I'd love an actual justified answer for which one it's closer to (or why it's neither), not just a "well it's complicated" cop-out -- this is kind of eating at me lol.
