A couple of writer friends and I were arguing last week about an idea for a short story, and it somehow spun out of a philosophy of language class one of them audited years ago -- specifically Frege's old chestnut about identity statements, the "Hesperus is Phosphorus" business, where the sentence feels like a genuine discovery even though "Hesperus is Hesperus" is just empty repetition, because the two names secretly pick out the same planet. What really got stuck in my head, though, is the extra twist I guess Kripke adds later with a guy named Paderewski (riffing off the actual historical Paderewski, who really was both a famous concert pianist and, later, a Polish prime minister): the puzzle survives even without two different names, because someone can hear about "Paderewski the pianist" and "Paderewski the statesman" at different times, never click that its the same guy, and end up sincerely believing both that Paderewski has musical talent and that he doesn't -- no confusion, no irrationality on his part, just two separate mental folders for one person.

I mostly write literary fiction about con artists and impostors (my last book had a woman quietly running two households under two names), so naturally I want to steal this for a story instead of leaving it in the seminar room. Could you write me a short story, maybe 1500-2000 words, set somewhere very ordinary -- a family funeral, a co-op board meeting, a class reunion, whatever fits -- where two or three characters hold strong, flatly contradictory opinions about "someone," and it emerges over the course of the story that they've been talking about the same individual the whole time under two different guises or reputations. I don't want the cute superhero secret-identity version of this (no capes, please), and I don't want the reveal to just be a twist for its own sake -- I want the actual shape of the story to track what's philosophically interesting about the puzzle, not just the fun of a surprise. After the story, add a short paragraph unpacking which beats of the plot are meant to correspond to which piece of the Frege/Kripke puzzle.
