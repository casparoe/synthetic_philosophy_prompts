Hey, want to sanity check my grip on Jaynes' resolution of Bertrand's paradox. Standard version: "pick a random chord" is ambiguous -- you get 1/3 (random endpoints), 1/4 (random midpoint), or 1/2 (random radius) depending on method, which people love to cite as proof the principle of indifference is hopeless without a privileged parametrization. Jaynes' move was to say the problem only becomes well-posed once you fix an actual physical procedure (tossing straws at a circle from a distance), and then require the resulting distribution to be invariant under translating, rotating, and rescaling that setup -- which apparently singles out the 1/2 answer uniquely. I don't need the full measure-theoretic derivation, just the gut-level reason those invariances are enough to kill the ambiguity when bare "indifference" isn't. What I actually care about pedagogically: does this vindicate the principle of indifference, or does it quietly show that physical symmetry was doing all the real work and "indifference" was just a label we slapped on it after the fact? Push back if you think thats the wrong way to frame the upshot.
