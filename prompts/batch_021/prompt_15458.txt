A gnarly aliasing bug at work last month—two object references that looked distinct everywhere in our logs but turned out, after a long afternoon of debugging, to be the same underlying object—sent me down a rabbit hole that ended with me reading Frege's essay "On Sense and Reference" over the weekend, mostly because a friend with a philosophy PhD keeps bringing it up whenever the two of us argue about names and identity. Frege's puzzle, as I understand it, is that a statement like "the morning star is the evening star" feels like a genuine discovery, while "the morning star is the morning star" is empty, even though both are just claims about Venus; he accounts for the difference by saying the two names share a Bedeutung (reference) but differ in Sinn (sense).

The more I sat with this, the more it looked like exactly the distinction between reference equality and value equality that every working programmer already relies on: two variables can fail a reference-identity check (what Python calls "is", as opposed to "==") while still resolving, once you actually chase them down, to the same object, and it's that gap between "same handle" and "same value" that makes the equality feel like news instead of a tautology. This isn't just a quirk of how we happen to label variables, either: whether two arbitrary expressions or programs pick out the same object is undecidable in general, a well-known corollary of Rice's theorem, so it's not even surprising, on purely computational grounds, that some true identity claims are hard-won and others ("a=a") come for free. Taken together, this makes Frege's "puzzle" look to me like a slightly antique way of describing a distinction that formal semantics and computability theory have long since made precise.

My friend disagrees, but mostly just insists that I'm flattening the sense/reference distinction into something it was never meant to be, and that the genuinely hard cases are exactly the ones where this formal trick runs out, without ever giving me one of those cases in a form that survives translation into code. I don't want to keep winning an argument I might actually be losing for reasons I can't see, so I'd like to know: is there something specifically philosophical that the reference/value-equality picture, plus ordinary facts about computational hardness, actually leaves out? And if so, why doesn't the debate just get reframed in those terms, instead of remaining an open problem that's apparently still generating new philosophy papers a century later?
