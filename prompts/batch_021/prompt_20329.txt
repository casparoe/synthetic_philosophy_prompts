The question the students were given reads as follows: "Karl Popper is well known both for proposing falsifiability as the criterion that demarcates science from non-science, and for a considerably more complicated verdict on where Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection stands with respect to that very criterion. Reconstruct Popper's position on Darwinism and how it developed over time. Your answer should address (a) the alleged tautology in the phrase 'survival of the fittest,' and why this seemed to Popper to threaten the testability of natural selection; (b) what exactly Popper meant, and did not mean, by calling Darwinism a 'metaphysical research programme'; and (c) the sense and the extent to which Popper's later remarks on the topic amount to a recantation of his earlier position."

Here is the answer submitted by one of the students: "Popper's concern begins with the slogan 'survival of the fittest.' If the fittest organisms are simply defined as those that in fact survive and reproduce, then to say that the fittest survive is to say only that those that survive, survive—a tautology that rules out no possible state of affairs and so makes no risky prediction. Since Popper's criterion for a genuinely scientific theory is that it be falsifiable, i.e., capable of clashing with some possible observation, a theory that collapses into a tautology cannot be scientific by his own lights. On this basis, Popper concluded that Darwinism belongs, together with theories like Marxism and psychoanalysis, among the 'metaphysical research programmes': unfalsifiable frameworks that are consequently of no real scientific value, however much prestige they enjoy among working scientists. Later, however, Popper stated in print that he wished to recant his earlier verdict: he had come to think that natural selection could, after all, generate falsifiable predictions once it was applied to a specific population under a specified selection pressure, rather than stated as an unrestricted general law. Given this reversal, Popper's mature position was that the theory of evolution by natural selection is a properly scientific theory in exactly the sense that Newtonian mechanics is, and that his earlier doubts about it were simply mistaken."
