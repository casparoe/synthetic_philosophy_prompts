I'm taking a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of relations and facts this term, and we recently spent two sessions on Bradley's regress and the "unity of the proposition" problem — the question of what makes a relational complex like "the mug is on the table" a single unified fact or claim, rather than just a list of items (the mug, on-ness, the table) sitting side by side. I found this material genuinely disorienting in a way that our earlier weeks on possible worlds and universals weren't, so I've spent a fair amount of time hashing it out with classmates outside of class. Two of them, Ines and Callum, got into a real disagreement about it after our last seminar, and since nobody in the group could settle it, I'd like an outside adjudication.

Here's how their exchange went, as best I can reconstruct it. (1) Ines opened by arguing that the regress is defused once you accept Russell's distinction between a relation occurring "as a term" — something you can name and treat as a further logical subject, like the noun "betweenness" — and a relation occurring "as actually relating" its terms within a genuine fact; since no fact ever contains a relation playing the inert, nameable role, the demand for some further relation to connect the first relation to its terms never actually arises. (2) Callum objected that this distinction doesn't explain anything, it just gives the mystery two labels: saying the relation "actually relates" in the unified case simply restates that unity is present, without saying what makes it present there and absent from a mere list — Ines has redescribed the explanandum, not identified an explanans. (3) Ines replied that every metaphysical account bottoms out in some unexplained primitive eventually, and hers is preferable precisely because it doesn't posit any further connecting item — a "unifier," a special glue-relation — which is exactly the move that generates Bradley's regress in the first place; so parsimony favors her over any rival. (4) Callum granted the parsimony point but denied it was decisive, since an empty explanation isn't rescued by being cheap; he offered Frege's alternative, on which a concept like "is wise" is inherently unsaturated and can only ever occur already joined to an object, so that the unity is traced to a real feature of one of the constituents rather than merely asserted. (5) Ines countered that Frege's own apparatus is no less troubled: he seems committed to saying we can't refer to an unsaturated concept without thereby treating it as a saturated object (his own example: "the concept horse is not a concept"), so his preferred solution carries a comparably serious, well-known cost — making it inconsistent of Callum to fault her view for question-begging while giving Frege's a pass. (6) Callum answered that the concept-horse puzzle is a problem about what a subject-predicate language can say about unsaturated entities, not a failure of unsaturatedness to explain why "Socrates is wise" is a unity to begin with, so the two costs aren't actually parallel — only Ines's account leaves the original question unanswered. Neither conceded, and the conversation dissolved into a dispute about who had "won," which is when I decided to write it up properly instead.
