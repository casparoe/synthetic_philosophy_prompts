I've followed Robin Hanson's case for prediction markets and futarchy since his Overcoming Bias days, and one technical result has always sat uneasily alongside it. The Milgrom–Stokey no-trade theorem applies the logic of Aumann's agreement theorem to markets: if the initial allocation is Pareto-optimal, all traders are rational, and both facts are common knowledge under a shared prior, no trade motivated purely by private information can be mutually agreeable, because a counterparty's very willingness to trade is itself adverse information (the logic behind what's sometimes nicknamed the 'Groucho Marx theorem'). Hanson has separately argued, in 'Uncommon Priors Require Origin Disputes,' that genuinely rational agents should share a prior, making persistent disagreement itself evidence of bias rather than of reasonable differences in starting point. Please argue that these two commitments, taken together, undercut Hanson's own epistemic case for prediction markets: that, on his own premises, the informed speculation he credits with aggregating dispersed information cannot be happening for the reason he thinks, and must instead be powered by traders who are irrational, hold heterogeneous priors, or are trading for reasons unrelated to their information. Take the standard escape routes seriously — noise traders, hedging demand, subsidized market-making, the Grossman–Stiglitz resolution — rather than treating the tension as automatically decisive.
