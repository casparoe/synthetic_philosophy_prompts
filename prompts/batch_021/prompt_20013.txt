I spent thirty-some years studying corvid cognition before I retired, and a good chunk of that career was spent arguing with reviewers about whether it's okay to say a scrub-jay "remembers being watched" and "expects" another jay to pilfer its cache, or whether that's just cheap anthropomorphism for what's really a duller associative mechanism underneath. Since retiring I've fallen in with a little philosophy discussion group that meets at the library, and lately we've been working through Dennett's stuff on the intentional stance, which honestly reads like a direct transcript of arguments I used to have with colleagues, just with animals swapped out for machines.

Last week two people in the group, Owen (he writes code for a living) and Ingrid (finishing a philosophy MA), got into a real back-and-forth over something that happened at Owens place: his kid asked their AI assistant when a certain solar eclipse was, it gave a confident wrong date, got corrected, and then said "you're right, sorry, it's actually [date]" and used the right one from then on. Owen's opening move was that nothing was corrected in any interesting sense: its next-token statistics all the way down, so there was never a belief to be wrong in the first place, only an output that changed, and calling that "admitting a mistake" is projecting drama onto arithmetic. Ingrid said that standard proves too much, since we don't crack open anyone's skull and check neurons before crediting them with beliefs either; what should settle it, she said, is whether belief-desire description is doing real, otherwise-unavailable predictive work, and here it obviously is. Owen came back that there's still a difference in kind and not just degree: for the assistant we already possess the complete causal story start to finish (predict the likely next word), so the intentional-level talk is a nice-to-have we could always cash out and discard, whereas nobody has that full reductive story for a person or a jay, which is exactly why belief-talk stays mandatory for us and merely optional for it. Ingrid's reply was that this move -- "we already know the mechanism, so the higher-level talk is just optional decoration" -- is precisely the inference Dennett spends a lot of effort blocking: physicists don't stop talking about an object's center of gravity just because they can describe every particle in it, and nobody thinks that makes centers of gravity fake; what matters is whether the higher-level pattern buys real predictive power you can't get otherwise, not whether you also happen to know the underlying mechanism. Dessert arrived before Owen could answer that one, and it never got picked back up.

It's been nagging at me since, because I think they were each onto something real and also each getting away with something. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who's got the better of each point, not just declare an overall winner, and flag anything substantive you think both of them are missing? I'd also be curious, given my background, whether this really is "the same" argument I used to have about the jays, dressed up in new clothes, or whether there's a principled disanalogy between attributing beliefs to animals and to language models that neither Owen nor Ingrid managed to put their finger on.
