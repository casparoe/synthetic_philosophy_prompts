I'm prepping my early modern political philosophy seminar and keep getting stuck on how to frame Spinoza's actual view of democracy, because the textbook line ("he liked it, most natural regime, moving on") falls apart the moment you put the Theological-Political Treatise next to the unfinished Political Treatise.

Here's the tension I want an essay to actually wrestle with instead of flattening. In the TTP (ch. 16) Spinoza is pretty unambiguous that democracy is the most natural form of state, the one that stays closest to the freedom everyone has by nature. But the Political Treatise, written a few years later, gives monarchy two chapters, lavishes three increasingly fussy chapters of institutional detail on aristocracy (councils, syndics, staggered terms, all of it) -- and then chapter 11, on democracy, opens by calling it the most "absolute" of all forms of dominion and then just breaks off a few pages in, mid-argument, right as he's working out why women shouldnt count as full citizens. Negri- and Balibar-style readers treat the incompleteness as basically irrelevant, since his whole account of potentia and the multitude was always heading toward democracy regardless of what got left on the page. Other readers think the sheer imbalance in space and care is itself evidence the later Spinoza had quietly cooled on democracy relative to aristocracy.

So: does Spinoza's political philosphy, taken as a whole rather than as isolated quotes, actually support democracy as the best regime, or not? Write me an essay that takes both readings seriously and weighs the main textual and philosophical considerations, but that actually commits to a verdict -- "the texts are just inconsistent" is a fine conclusion if you argue for it, not if it's a shrug to dodge picking a side.
