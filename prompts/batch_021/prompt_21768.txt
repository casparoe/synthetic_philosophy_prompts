So I've been down a rabbit hole for an ideas column on 19th-century abolitionist infighting, and the thing that keeps grabbing me is the Douglass/Garrison split over the Constitution -- its way more interesting than the flattened textbook version ("some abolitionists thought it was pro-slavery, some didn't"). Could you write me a dialogue between Frederick Douglass, not long after his 1851 break with Garrison, and Garrison himself, arguing over whether the Constitution is fundamentally a pro-slavery document or a redeemable one? I want real engagement with the actual text -- the fugitive slave clause, the three-fifths clause, the fact that the word "slavery" never once appears, what counts as legitimate interpretation when the language is deliberately euphemistic -- not just each guy restating his conclusion. Have Garrison throw Douglass's own earlier speeches back at him too, since he can't pretend he always thought this. And if you can, surface whether they're actually disagreeing about what the document objectively means or about what abolitionists should strategically claim it means -- I have a hunch it's murkier than either of them would admit.
