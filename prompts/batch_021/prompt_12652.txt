For the past several months I have been building formal models of multi-agent systems that reason about one another's knowledge, and I keep colliding with the problem of logical omniscience: the standard possible-worlds semantics for knowledge, going back to Hintikka, entails that every agent instantly knows every tautology and every logical consequence of anything it already knows, which makes such models nearly useless for representing any bounded, realistic reasoner. Partly as an escape from this at night, I have been reading Leibniz, whom I previously knew mainly as the inventor of binary arithmetic, and I was struck by his doctrine of infinite analysis: necessary truths, on his account, resolve into explicit identities after finitely many steps of conceptual substitution, while contingent truths involve an analysis that continues without end and is fully transparent only to God, which is why finite minds can know that contingent truths are true without ever demonstrating them the way a geometer demonstrates a theorem. My first thought was that this doctrine is an obvious ancestor of the modern problem, but on reflection the fit seems imperfect, since logical omniscience is usually posed as a problem about an agent's grip on necessary, logical consequences rather than contingent ones, and those are exactly the truths Leibniz thinks a sufficiently diligent finite mind could in principle fully trace out. I would like your best reconstruction of how Leibniz would have reacted had the modern problem, in something like its technical form, been put to him directly. Does his epistemology offer other resources, beyond infinite analysis, that would explain why real finite reasoners fall short of logical omniscience even with respect to necessary truths? And faced with specific modern proposals for weakening the standard semantics, such as awareness logics, algorithmic models of knowledge, or impossible-worlds semantics that let agents entertain epistemically possible but logically inconsistent scenarios, which do you think he would have found congenial to his rationalism, and which would his commitment to the principle of contradiction as inviolable have led him to reject outright?
