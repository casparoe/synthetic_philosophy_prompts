I don't have any formal background in economics or philosophy -- I've just spent a lot of my own time reading about Aumann's agreement theorem, because the idea that two genuinely rational people cannot simply "agree to disagree" has always struck me as fascinating and a little too neat. Recently I watched two friends of mine, both fairly well-versed in this kind of material themselves, get stuck in exactly this argument, and I'd like your help working out who had the better of it.

They had spent an evening debating how likely it is that a mutual friend's new bakery will still be open in two years -- he said 30 percent, she said 80 -- and neither could talk the other down even after each had laid out every reason they had. At that point:

(1) He said that since they were both rational and had now shared everything they knew, Aumann's theorem meant one of them had to be making a mistake somewhere: two rational people with common knowledge of each other's honest probability estimates simply can't end up at different numbers.

(2) She replied that the theorem only bites if they had started from the same prior before either of them saw any evidence about the bakery -- and maybe they never did. She might just be a more optimistic person about small businesses generally, which isn't a mistake, just a different starting point, so neither of them is being irrational.

(3) He said that shouldn't save her: the fact that she still believes 80 percent after hearing his reasons is itself evidence he should fold into his own number, and the same goes for her with his 30 percent. If they each keep updating on the mere fact that the other disagrees, they have to converge eventually, whatever prior either of them started with.

(4) She said his last move fails for the same reason his first one did: the convergence-through-repeated-updating idea he's now leaning on also assumes a common prior, so two people who genuinely start from different priors can trade numbers all evening without ever meeting in the middle. And in any case, actually reaching the kind of common knowledge the theorem needs -- not just stating a number, but each being certain of the other's honesty and reasoning ability, all the way up -- isn't something one long conversation, however frank, ever quite delivers.

Could you go through this move by move, say who had the better of it at each of the four numbered points and to what extent, and flag anything relevant to Aumann's theorem that neither of them brings up?
