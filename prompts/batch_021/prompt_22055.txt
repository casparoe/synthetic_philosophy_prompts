At the end-of-year gathering for my company next month, I've been asked to give a short toast for a colleague, Marisol, who is retiring after twenty-three years as a systems engineer. What the person organizing the event doesn't fully appreciate, I think, is what she was actually doing on the side for nearly that whole time: every Saturday morning, on her own dime and her own time, she ran a free robotics club for kids in the foster care system, and she has always turned down any offer of company sponsorship or a spot on the careers-page blog, on the grounds, as she once put it to me, that being recognized for it would make it "stop being mine." HR wants the toast to double as a gentle nudge for other engineers to "get more involved," which is exactly what worries me: if I frame what she did as simply what any decent person would do, I think I'll be quietly telling everyone else in the room, myself included, that we've been falling short for two decades, and I'll probably annoy Marisol in the process, since she can't stand being held up as a moral example. But if I go the other way and soften it into "she just followed her heart, nothing special, anyone could have done it," I think I'd be underselling just how far beyond any reasonable expectation this actually was.

While hunting for the right way to phrase this (a late-night Wikipedia detour, not any actual philosophical training — I write Terraform for a living, not essays), I came across the term "supererogation": acts that are good to perform but not wrong to omit. It seems to be exactly the tool I need, since the whole point of the category seems to be that an act can be praiseworthy precisely because it wasn't required — which would mean nobody else's non-participation has to count as a failure for Marisol's choice to still look extraordinary. I gathered in passing that utilitarians have a long-running headache with this category, something about the best available act always ending up obligatory, which would leave no room for actions that are optional yet praiseworthy, but I don't understand that objection well enough to know whether it should change how I write this.

Could you write the actual toast for me? It should run about five minutes spoken aloud, and I'd like the concept of supererogation to do real work in it — shaping what the toast can and can't say about praise, duty, and everyone else's choices — rather than just being a word I drop in for flavor. A handful of philosophy PhDs on the research side of the company will be in the room and will clock it immediately if the concept is used loosely, but most of the audience has never heard the term and doesn't need to; the toast has to genuinely work for both groups at once.
