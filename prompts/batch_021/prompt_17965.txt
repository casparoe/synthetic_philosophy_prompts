Frank Ramsey turns up constantly in the rationalist blogs and forums I've been reading for years: the Dutch-book argument for the axioms of subjective probability, the Ramsey Test for conditionals, Ramsey pricing whenever natural-monopoly regulation comes up, even the occasional nod to Ramsey numbers in a combinatorics thread. So I was surprised, working through Cheryl Misak's biography of him, to learn that in 1925 he read a short, informal paper called "Epilogue" to the Cambridge Apostles that amounts to his considered view of the meaning of life. His argument is that human significance has nothing to do with physical scale: he writes that his "picture of the world is drawn in perspective, and not like a model to scale," with people filling the foreground and the stars shrunk to the size of coins, and he insists he feels no humility before the vastness of the heavens, since size and duration just aren't the kind of thing that could make anything more or less important. He runs the same move on time, dismissing the eventual heat death of the universe on the grounds that "its present value at compound discount is almost nothing." He was dead within five years of giving that talk, at twenty-six.

What continues to puzzle me is that this is the very same Ramsey whose 1928 paper on optimal saving calls discounting future enjoyment purely for its remoteness in time "a practice which is ethically indefensible and arises merely from the weakness of the imagination" -- a line still cited against pure time preference in welfare economics today. That looks like a real inconsistency: the essayist waves away the far future by discounting it, while the economist, three years later, denies that discounting the future can ever be legitimate. This isn't only a puzzle for Ramsey scholars, either, since people in my circles take longtermism quite seriously, and arguments like Bostrom's "astronomical waste" paper get most of their force from the sheer number of potential future lives at stake.

Please describe the most important implications of Ramsey's view of the meaning of life for how someone sympathetic to longtermism should treat scale-based arguments for prioritizing the far future. I'd especially like you to address whether Ramsey's claim that scale is irrelevant to significance is really about the same thing longtermists rely on when they invoke scale, whether his two positions on discounting can be reconciled or whether one of them simply has to go, and whether it is coherent to insist, as he seems to want to, that cosmic scale should leave a person emotionally unmoved while still mattering enormously to what that person ought to do.
