Write a dialogue between a precise Bayesian, who holds that rational credence must always be given by a single sharp probability, and an imprecise probabilist, who holds that rational credence can be represented by a whole set of probability functions -- a credal set -- when the evidence warrants it. Stage the exchange in front of Ellsberg's two urns: one is known to contain exactly fifty red and fifty black balls, the other contains a hundred red and black balls in a ratio unknown to both of them, and both characters are asked to state the odds at which they would bet that a ball drawn from the second urn is red. The precise Bayesian opens with a dilemma: naming odds just is stating a credence, so the imprecise probabilist's refusal to name a single number is either a credence of exactly 0.5 in another guise, or an admission that they have no real opinion about the urn at all. The imprecise probabilist has to make good on a genuine third option.

Don't let the exchange stop at the urns themselves. Have the precise Bayesian press on with a sequential-choice argument in the spirit of Adam Elga's case against unsharp credence: across an ordinary run of successive bets, an agent who only ever rules out acts that are dominated relative to every distribution in their credal set can be steered into a combination of choices that is jointly self-defeating in a way no sharp-credence agent would ever fall into, so that any repair strong enough to prevent this ends up smuggling a sharp credence back in. (The imprecise probabilist's natural fallback here is something like Levi's rule of E-admissibility, on which an act is permissible so long as it maximizes expected utility relative to at least one distribution in the set.) Have the imprecise probabilist meet that argument head-on rather than simply insisting that indeterminacy is fine, and then have them counterattack with dilation: cases in which conditioning a credal set on genuinely relevant new evidence leaves it wider rather than narrower, so that learning something seems to make a person less certain instead of more. The precise Bayesian needs a real answer to that, not just the observation that it is someone else's problem.

This tension keeps resurfacing, in various guises, in a formal epistemology reading group I've been part of, where the tidy one-line verdicts on each side -- sharp credence is precise but unmotivated, imprecise credence is honest but can't act -- always seem to dissolve under pressure. Aim for something in the neighborhood of 700 to 900 words, give both figures equally strong material, make sure each response actually engages with the specific point just made rather than restating a prior position, and don't let either side simply cave; it's fine, and probably preferable, if the dialogue ends with the disagreement clarified and sharpened rather than resolved.
