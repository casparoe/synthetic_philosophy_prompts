This semester I'm taking a seminar on medieval political thought, and this week's session paired two of Aquinas's discussions of tyranny that our professor deliberately set against each other: the brief remarks on obedience to unjust rulers in his early Commentary on the Sentences (Book II, distinction 44), and the much fuller treatment of kingship and tyranny in the later De Regimine Principum, addressed to the King of Cyprus. The two passages don't obviously agree. The Sentences commentary is usually read as allowing that a ruler with no legitimate title at all — a pure usurper — may be resisted, even killed, by a private person, on the grounds that he is not a genuine authority but something closer to a violent aggressor against the community. De Regimine Principum, when it takes up the question of tyranny directly, is far more guarded: Aquinas worries at length that any general license to kill "tyrants" will be seized on by ambitious men to depose perfectly legitimate kings under false pretenses, and he wants the correction of tyranny routed through public authority — the community that installed the ruler, or some superior power — rather than through private initiative of any kind.

Our professor's own view is that the two texts are perfectly consistent once you see Aquinas working throughout with two distinct kinds of illegitimacy — what she calls tyranny "of title" and tyranny "of exercise" — but several of us in the seminar weren't fully convinced this dissolves the tension rather than just relabeling it. Rather than settle the question myself, I'd like to see the strongest version of the disagreement staged directly. Could you write a dialogue of around 700 words between two present-day scholars of Aquinas, in which one defends the "unity" reading — that this distinction does real, stable work across both texts, so there is nothing to reconcile — while the other argues that this is a tidy retrospective harmonization, and that the later, more deliberate treatise shows Aquinas's considered position hardening against tyrannicide in ways the earlier, more casual commentary doesn't anticipate. Please make both scholars equally sharp and well-read in the primary texts, have them actually respond to the specific points the other raises rather than talking past each other, and let the disagreement stay genuinely open at the end rather than resolving neatly in one side's favor.
