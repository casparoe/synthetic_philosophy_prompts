Over a long career in statistical and condensed-matter physics, I grew used to a fact I never had occasion to examine philosophically: strictly speaking, a genuine phase transition—a true non-analyticity in the free energy—can only occur in the idealized thermodynamic limit of infinitely many degrees of freedom. No actual, finite system, however large, ever undergoes a real discontinuity; it merely exhibits an increasingly sharp crossover as the number of particles grows. Since retiring, I have been reading in philosophy of mind and biology, where phase transitions are routinely offered as the paradigm case of "weak," unproblematic emergence, licensing the hope that life or consciousness might be emergent in that same tame, ultimately reducible sense. Please describe what you take to be the most important implications of the thermodynamic-limit requirement for that analogy: does the fact that physics's best-behaved example of weak emergence is secured only by an idealization that no real system ever satisfies strengthen the case that mind or life may require a more full-blooded, "strong" notion of emergence instead, or does it merely show that even "weak emergence" was always a claim about idealized limits rather than actual systems?
