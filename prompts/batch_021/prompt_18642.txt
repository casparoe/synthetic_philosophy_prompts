Ok so I'm TAing a formal epistemology seminar this term and got roped into presenting on van Fraassen's Reflection Principle next week, and I've been chewing on an objection I don't think is the standard one, and I want a sanity check before it goes in my handout. Quick recap for context: Reflection says that if you're currently certain your credence in some proposition A at a later time t' will be r, then your current credence in A, conditional on the supposition that your future credence will be r, should also equal r — basically you're supposed to defer to your anticipated future opinions the way you'd defer to a better-informed expert. Van Fraassen backs this with a diachronic Dutch book argument, and the obvious objection everyone raises in week one is the memory-loss/brainwashing case: if I know I'm about to be hypnotized, or to loose my memory of tonight's poker hand, why should I defer to whatever credence I'll have tomorrow? The standard fix, as I understand it (I think this is basically Christensen's point, or maybe it was Maher, my notes from that week are a mess), is to restrict Reflection to cases where the anticipated shift is due to ordinary rational updating on new evidence, not to drugs or memory loss.

My worry, which I want to make the crux of the presentation, is that this fix quietly guts the whole point of the principle. Reflection was sold as a formal, subject-matter-neutral constraint on rationality, like probabilism — you're supposed to be able to check whether someone violates it just by looking at their credences across time, without knowing anything about what they believe or why. But "the shift has to come from rational updating" already requires an independent, substantive standard of what rational updating on your specific evidence looks like, and once you've got that standard, Reflection isn't adding any independent constraint at all — it just falls out of requiring your current credence to already track whatever that independent standard says you should believe once updated. So the Dutch book argument ends up dressing up something that isn't really "structural" in the advertised sense as a substantive, freestanding principle. Am I onto something here, or is there an obvious way for van Fraassen (or a defender) to block this that I'm too deep in the weeds to see? And is this actually a known criticism from the Dutch-book wars of the 80s/90s that I should just be citing instead of presenting as my own reading? Would love a point-by-point poke at the argument rather than just a verdict.
