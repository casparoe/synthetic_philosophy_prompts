I've been an on-and-off reader of LessWrong and the Alignment Forum since the Sequences days, and one idea from that corner of the internet I keep coming back to is the "commitment race": the worry that when two sufficiently sophisticated agents bargain, each has an incentive to bind itself to a tough position as early and as irreversibly as possible, on the classic Schelling logic that the power to visibly tie one's own hands is a bargaining asset — but that if both sides reason this way, each is pushed to commit before it has learned enough about the other to know whether their demands are even compatible, so a process that should converge on a mutually acceptable deal instead risks the strategic equivalent of two doomsday devices going off rather than either side blinking first.

That idea stopped being an abstraction for me last month, because I sit on the internal deployment review board at the logistics-tech firm I work for, which builds negotiation agents that mid-size shipping and warehousing companies deploy to hammer out freight contracts directly with each other's agents, with a human signing off only on final terms. Our engineering lead wants to ship a "credible commitment" feature: on a client's instruction, that client's agent can cryptographically publish an irrevocable floor or ceiling on one contract term before substantive back-and-forth even begins, so the agent can occupy the high ground of "I am no longer able to move from this position." The case for shipping it is concrete: last quarter, a client lost a favorable deal by staying flexible against a counterparty whose agent committed hard and early. The case against it, which two of my fellow board members keep raising, is that once both sides' agents have the capability, each is under pressure to commit earlier and harder than the last round, with no time to discover whether the other side's true walk-away point would have supported a much better deal for everyone — and because the commitments are cryptographically irrevocable, no human can intervene once an agent has locked itself in, even if the mistake becomes obvious minutes later.

I need to circulate a short decision memo before Thursday's board meeting, and I'd like your help drafting it. Please give a clear, time-bound recommendation — ship the feature as proposed, withdraw it, or ship it only under specific constraints (for instance, stake limits, a mandatory delay before a commitment becomes binding, a verification step regarding the counterparty, or a pilot period with a fixed review date) — together with reasoning the engineering team could actually engage with rather than a generic appeal to caution. I'd especially like you to take seriously the strongest argument for whichever side you don't end up recommending, particularly the competitive-disadvantage worry: telling a single firm not to unilaterally commit is cold comfort if its rivals adopt the feature regardless, and I don't want the memo to simply wave that concern away.
