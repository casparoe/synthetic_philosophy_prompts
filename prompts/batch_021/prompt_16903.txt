i can't stop thinking about an argument that broke out in my ML reading group last week. It started from that Anthropic demo where they took one direction in Claude's activations, turned it way up, and the model got fixated on the Golden Gate Bridge no matter what you asked it. One guy said that's about as good as evidence gets for the network having a real internal representation of the bridge -- they isolated a direction, intervened on it, and got a precise, predictable effect on behavior -- what more could you ask for. Someone else said not so fast: a sparse autoencoder trained with a different dictionary size or sparsity penalty gives you a different "bridge" direction that steers just as well, and these features are well known to "split" into finer sub-features as the dictionary grows -- so there's no single privileged decomposition being discovered, just one of many equally serviceable bases laid on top of a tangle of superposed activity, more like drawing constellations than finding stars. He shot back that causal control isn't nothing -- that's basically the same evidential bar neuroscience clears when it credits a neuron with "representing" something, like place cells. She said neuroscience earns that inference through a bunch of independent triangulation -- anatomy, development, consistency across tasks and animals -- and that's exactly what's missing here, since steering success alone is compatible with there being many different, mutually incompatible ways to carve up the same network that all "work." Neither budged. Can you go through it move by move and say who's got the better of it at each step, not just who wins overall, and whether there's something important both of them are missing?
