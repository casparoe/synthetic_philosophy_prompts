I teach in the fiction and nonfiction tracks of a small low-residency MFA program, and this month I've been asked to sit on a three-person faculty panel convened to review a complaint that has split the department. A second-year student's braided nonfiction thesis — already accepted, pending minor revisions, by our affiliated literary journal — turns out to contain at least three scenes that did not happen: a deathbed reconciliation with a grandmother who in reality died alone and estranged from the family, a suicide attempt invented wholesale to anchor the essay's emotional climax, and a courtroom scene, dialogue included, built out of a relative's real but very differently resolved custody hearing. The matter surfaced when the grandmother's sister read an excerpt online and wrote the program director an angry letter. The student's own written response argues that memoir has never been stenography, that braided and lyric forms exist precisely to license this kind of invention in service of a larger emotional truth, and that the panel is drawing an arbitrary line around her essay when half the memoirs on our syllabus reconstruct scenes no one could have recorded word for word. My two co-panelists have settled along roughly that fault line, one insisting this is simply what contemporary creative nonfiction is, the other maintaining there's a real difference between reconstructing a conversation whose substance happened and inventing the substance itself. As a novelist who has published under both labels at different points in my career, I find I don't actually know where I'd draw that line, which is presumably why I was asked to join this panel in the first place.

Only half in jest, I mentioned the case to a friend who works on late antiquity, saying we probably needed a moral theologian more than a program director. She brought up Augustine, who devoted an entire treatise, Contra Mendacium, to arguing that a lie told for someone's good — to save a life, to protect the innocent, to spare a family's grief — is never permissible, no matter how good the end. He was also explicit that what makes an utterance a lie in the first place is the intention that a falsehood be believed, which cuts both ways in our case depending on what the essay's readers were actually invited to take as fact. I need to bring the panel's written recommendation to the program director within the next two weeks, before the journal goes to print and before the thesis can be approved. Could you draft that memo? It should recommend a specific disposition for this student's thesis and journal publication, propose a workable disclosure policy for future nonfiction workshops so the program isn't litigating this case by case, and take the 'all memoir invents a little' position seriously enough that the colleague who holds it would recognize her own argument in your account of it before you explain why the panel didn't simply adopt it.
