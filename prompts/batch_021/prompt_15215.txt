"For Ayer, meaningfulness requires that a statement be either analytic or in principle empirically verifiable, and 'I will survive the death of my body' fails on both counts, so it isn't false, it's meaningless - a bit like saying 2 is jealous. This was basically the standard Vienna Circle line: Schlick makes the identical argument, that since all verification bottoms out in sensations belonging to some body, and my body won't be there anymore, there's nothing left that could count as evidence either way, so the whole dispute between believer and disbeliever just evaporates.

In 1988 Ayer choked on some smoked salmon in hospital and his heart stopped for four minutes; a couple of months later, in the Sunday Telegraph, he wrote 'What I Saw When I Was Dead,' describing a red light in charge of the universe and some incompetent 'ministers' of space. He's careful to add that this wouldn't prove God exists even if it did prove survival. Then in October he published 'Postscript to a Postmortem' in the Spectator, where he goes back over the same ground and reaffirms that the experience really had, in his own words, 'slightly weakened' his conviction that death is the end - he's just adding the caveat that his atheism itself never wavered. So his considered view by the end of 1988 seems to be that the survival question, whatever he thought in 1936, isn't meaningless after all: it's a real question, and one he now thinks is slightly more open than he used to."
