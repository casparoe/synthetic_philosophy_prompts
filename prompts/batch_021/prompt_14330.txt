I'm writing a piece on misinformation and the health of democracies, and I fell down a rabbit hole re-reading Spinoza for background — specifically the bit in the Theological-Political Treatise (chapter 16, if I'm remembering the numbering right) where he makes his case for democracy being the most "natural" form of sovereignty. The part that stuck with me is his argument that its structurally hard for a large multitude to agree en masse on some folly or self-destructive law the way a single monarch or small ruling council might — basically, foolishness doesn't scale the same way collective power does, so a democracy is less likely than other regimes to pass measures that go against its own interest. That argument obviously predates anything like mass media, let alone algorithmically microtargeted political ads, coordinated disinformation campaigns, or filter-bubble social networks that can nudge huge numbers of people toward the same (often false) belief very quickly and cheaply.

So here's my question for you: does Spinoza's case for democracy actually survive contact with this stuff, or does it quietly depend on an empirical premise about how hard it is to manipulate a multitude en masse that just isn't true anymore? I don't want the generic "he'd probably be worried about fake news" answer — I want you to really engage with the mechanics of his own argument (the idea that right is basically coextensive with power, and that sovereignty is grounded in the power of the multitude rather than in the ruler as such) and tell me whether modern information tech actually breaks that argument, forces a revision of it, or turns out to be secretly compatible with it once you think it through carefully.
