I write about science and ideas for a general audience, and I'm currently working on a piece about the growing cultural authority of Bayesian reasoning: superforecasting tournaments, prediction markets, and the broader insistence that a rational person's beliefs should come in precise numerical degrees. Before I try to explain to readers why this practice is supposed to be rational in the first place, I want to get the underlying philosophy right. Two quite different arguments are typically offered for the claim that credences ought to obey the probability axioms. The first is the classic Dutch Book argument, which shows that anyone whose credences violate the probability calculus can be induced to accept a combination of bets that guarantees a loss regardless of outcome. The second is Jim Joyce's non-pragmatic "accuracy-dominance" argument from accuracy-first epistemology, which tries to reach the same conclusion purely from considerations of gradational accuracy — how close credences come to the truth — without any appeal to betting behavior or practical stakes.

Please compare these two arguments in some depth. What exactly does each one establish, and what does each assume that the other does not? (I understand Joyce's proof turns on restricting attention to some class of "legitimate" scoring rules, and I'd like to know why that restriction is needed and how secure it is.) Finally, which of the two do you regard as the more fundamental vindication of probabilism? Please defend your judgment rather than simply cataloguing the considerations on each side.
