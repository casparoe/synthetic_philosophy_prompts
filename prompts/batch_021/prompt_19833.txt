I cover research on rationality and decision-making, and last month I sat in on a decision-theory workshop where, after one of the talks, two audience members got into an argument the moderator eventually had to cut off. The talk's example was a stylized one: an agent choosing among three secondhand bikes, A, B, and C, who prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A. (1) He said this preference pattern is irrational, full stop: a trader could swap the agent from one bike to the next, each time for a small fee and always toward whichever bike the agent currently says it prefers, until the cycle closes and the agent is back where it started — poorer than before, with the whole sequence repeatable without limit. (2) She said this only indicts short-sighted, step-at-a-time choosing: an agent who sees the whole cycle coming and simply refuses the first trade (or fixes on a plan and holds to it) is never pumped at all, so it's myopia that's irrational, not the underlying cyclical ranking. (3) He replied that this "fix" empties the ranking of content: on pain of ruin, the agent must treat every trade consistent with its own stated preferences as permanently off-limits, so a preference that can never safely be acted on isn't doing any work — it's decoration, not a real preference. (4) She answered that this proves too much: Ulysses's desire to steer toward the Sirens was perfectly real, which is exactly why he had himself bound to the mast, and no one thinks the desire was fake just because he made sure never to act on it. Neither budged, and I haven't been able to settle it myself. Could you go through the exchange move by move — who has the better of it at each step, not just who wins overall — and flag any consideration relevant to the disagreement that neither of them raises?
