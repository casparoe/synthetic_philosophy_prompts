Over the past several months I've been slowly falling down a rabbit hole about something that sounds like it should have a simple answer but apparently doesn't: what actually makes two organisms count as members of the same species? It started when I came across a description of the Ensatina salamanders that form a ring of populations around California's Central Valley, where each population can interbreed successfully with its immediate neighbors along the ring, yet the two populations that meet at the southern end, after tracing the ring all the way around, cannot interbreed with one another at all. By the definition I was taught in school - that a species is a group whose members can produce fertile offspring together - it isn't even clear whether this counts as one species or several. That question sent me down a longer trail of reading (mostly encyclopedia entries and popular-science write-ups, since I have no formal training in biology or philosophy), and I discovered that biologists and philosophers of biology have never actually settled on a single definition of "species." I now have a working list of six of the more prominent proposals: the Biological Species Concept, the Phylogenetic Species Concept, the Morphological Species Concept, the Ecological Species Concept, the Cohesion Species Concept, and the Evolutionary Species Concept.

What makes this genuinely puzzling to me, rather than just a matter of picking a convention, is that none of these six concepts seems to survive contact with the full range of hard cases out there. Besides ring species like the Ensatina, there are lineages that reproduce asexually and so never "interbreed" in the first place (bdelloid rotifers, many dandelions), there are bacteria that routinely swap genes across what would otherwise be considered separate lineages through horizontal gene transfer, and there are hybrid species, like the sunflower Helianthus anomalus, that arose from crossing two other existing species rather than by splitting off from a single ancestor. Every concept I've read about seems to handle one or two of these cases gracefully and then fall apart on the rest.
