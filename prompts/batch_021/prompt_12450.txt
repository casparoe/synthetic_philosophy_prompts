I have been turning over an idea about precommitment on my own for a while, and I would like an outside read on it before I take it any further. I have no formal training in philosophy or law, so if I am just reinventing something that already has a name, or walking straight into a hole someone patched decades ago, I would rather find out now.

Start with the source of the term. In the Odyssey, Circe warns Odysseus about the Sirens and gives him a precise method for surviving them: fill his crew's ears with wax, have them lash him to the mast, and tell them in advance that if he begs to be freed once the song starts, they are to bind him tighter rather than obey. Odysseus passes this plan on to his men, but the restraint itself is carried out by a second party who has agreed beforehand to treat his future pleading as noise rather than as a command he is entitled to give. Take away the crew's independent willingness to disregard him, and there is no precommitment at all — only a man tied to a mast who can ask to be let go.

The same structure seems to show up in every "Ulysses contract" I can think of, from locking away a credit card to a recovering addict's commitment device: some second party — a bank, an app, a friend — has to be the one actually withholding. The present self can design the trap; it cannot spring the trap on its own future self, or it would not be a trap.

This made me wonder whether the same gap sits underneath an idea I have seen tossed around a lot, that a constitution is a kind of Ulysses contract a country writes for itself against its own future majorities. Two real clauses make the comparison concrete rather than metaphorical. The U.S. Constitution's amendment article carves out one thing that cannot be changed through the ordinary amendment process without the affected state's own consent: a state's equal vote in the Senate. Germany's Basic Law goes further, placing human dignity and the democratic, federal character of the state permanently beyond the reach of any amendment whatsoever. Both look like a founding generation trying to do what Odysseus did.

But if I have read the myth correctly, the question this raises is not just whether a majority can bind a future majority — it is who, exactly, is playing the crew. For Odysseus the answer is obvious and consensual. For a constitution, the candidates — courts, supermajority voting thresholds, entrenched political norms — are far messier, and each invites the same suspicion: whichever body has the standing to override what a present majority clearly wants, in the name of people long dead, is where the real authority is actually sitting. Calling the arrangement a precommitment does not dissolve that authority; it just relocates it and dresses the relocation up as something the majority did to itself.

One disanalogy worries me more than the others. Odysseus-before-the-mast and Odysseus-under-the-spell are the same continuous person, which is at least part of why it is intelligible to say his later self is bound by his earlier choice. A generation ratifying a constitution and a generation living under it two centuries later share no such continuity; they are not the same people in any sense that ordinarily licenses one person's choice binding another's. So the comparison may fail well before it ever reaches the "who is the crew" problem.

Here is what I would find useful. First, does the "who plays the crew" question actually add something to the existing debate over who gets to enforce a constitution against a sitting majority, or is it the same debate wearing a costume? Second, does the generational disanalogy above sink the comparison outright, or is there a plausible way to repair it? Third, is there enough here to be worth developing into a full essay, or is the core idea too thin once it is stated plainly? And if I have gotten any detail of the Sirens episode or the two constitutional clauses wrong, I would like to know that as well.
