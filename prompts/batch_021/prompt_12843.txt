Long-time lurker on the rationalist-blogosphere side of the internet (LW, ACX, the whole cluster), and for the past year or so I've been one of five volunteer editors on a small collaboratively-run wiki a few of us started to catalog and rate "conspiracy-ish" claims floating around online -- think a scrappy RationalWiki/Snopes hybrid. Our house style has been pretty firmly generalist: every entry opens with a standard box explaining that claims alleging secret coordination among lots of independent, powerful people get a low prior by default, because big conspiracies are supposedly statistically leaky and tend to collapse within a few years if they're real -- there's a paper, I think by a physicist named Grimes, that actually tries to Fermi-estimate this. That default is now splitting the editor team, and I'm the deciding vote, so I want an outside opinion before I commit to one side.

The entry in question (I've fictionalized/composited the specifics on purpose so we're not just relitigating an actual news story) concerns a claim that an agrochemical company's own in-house scientists privately flagged a bestselling pesticide as wiping out local pollinator populations years before regulators caught on, based on a couple of leaked internal emails, and that the company then quietly lobbied to slow-walk the follow-up studies. Two editors want the standard debunking box slapped on top, since the claim has exactly the shape our policy exists to flag (secrecy plus coordination plus a powerful institutional actor). The other two think that's question-begging: Big Tobacco, Purdue Pharma, and the VW emissions scandal all had exactly that shape too, right up until the documents surfaced, so "this resembles a conspiracy theory structurally" can't by itself be evidence the claim is false, or we'd have had to call all three implausible the day before they broke. I think this is basically the generalism-vs-particularism debate philosophers have had about conspiracy theories, but its been years since I actually read any of that and I don't trust my memory enough to break the tie myself. Could you draft the memo I'll circulate to the other editors before we hold the tiebreaker vote? I need a real recommendation on how to classify this specific entry, plus criteria the five of us could reuse next time this comes up, and whichever way you land, give the losing side's strongest argument a fair hearing instead of a paragraph of lip service.
