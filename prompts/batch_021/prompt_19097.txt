My grandmother died two weeks ago, and I have been asked to give her eulogy at the funeral this Saturday. It is turning out to be far harder to write than anything I have produced for the seminar on classical Islamic philosophy I am currently taking, the same seminar in which I first learned to take her seriously as a philosopher and not simply as my grandmother. She spent almost fifty years teaching kalām at a small seminary, and to the very end she held, without a trace of metaphor, the classical Ash'arite occasionalism she built her courses around: following al-Ghazālī's Tahāfut al-Falāsifa (we read the Seventeenth Discussion, on causality, in Marmura's translation two weeks ago, which now feels like an unkind coincidence), she believed that fire does not really burn cotton, that medicine does not really cure, and that a heart does not really fail from strain, because no created thing has any power of its own to produce anything at all — God alone acts, recreating the pattern we mistake for cause and effect at every instant, purely as a matter of habit ('āda) that He remains free to suspend, as He famously did for Abraham in the fire. She used to interrupt us at the dinner table for saying the kettle "boiled the water," and she had apparently been telling my mother for years, half as a joke and half not, that if anyone ever stood up at her funeral and said her body had "finally given out" or that she had "lost her fight," she would come back just to argue about it — the joke being, of course, that on her own view nothing comes back, or fails, or fights, by its own power either.

The funeral will hold two audiences at once: several of her former students and two of her old colleagues, who will notice immediately if I retreat into vague, generic talk about God's plan, and most of the rest of the family, including my father's Catholic in-laws and my younger cousins, who have never heard of occasionalism and just need the eulogy to feel like it makes sense and takes their grief seriously. Could you write the eulogy I should actually give, something like seven or eight minutes long? It has to hold to her specific, technical position on causation rather than smoothing it into something more comfortable, it cannot lean on any of the ordinary causal idioms she spent her life gently refusing, and ideally it should locate whatever genuine comfort her theology has to offer on its own terms, rather than importing comfort from outside it.
