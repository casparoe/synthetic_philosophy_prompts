I had a long argument this past weekend with an old friend who teaches philosophy about whether genuine moral dilemmas -- situations in which an agent is under two conflicting obligations she cannot jointly satisfy, so that whatever she does, she does something wrong -- are even logically possible. I found myself defending something like the Ruth Barcan Marcus and Bernard Williams line: that such conflicts are real, and that the guilt or "moral residue" a conscientious person feels afterward, even having done the best available thing, is itself evidence that the losing obligation did not simply vanish once the choice was made. My friend answered with something closer to Alan Donagan's position: if morality is a consistent system, and "ought" agglomerates -- if you ought to do A and ought to do B, you ought to do both -- then, since ought implies can and you plainly cannot do both, one of the two "oughts" was never genuine to begin with, and the residual guilt is just a psychological artifact rather than evidence of anything. We went back and forth for the better part of an hour without either of us budging.

It turns out this isn't purely academic for me. I sit on the clinical policy committee at the community mental health center where I work, and we have been asked to rewrite our guidance on confidentiality versus the duty to warn or protect an identifiable third party from serious threatened harm. The current one-page policy simply instructs clinicians to "exercise judgment in balancing the two obligations," which in practice means the duty to warn always prevails and the matter is considered closed once the warning is delivered. Some committee members want to formalize exactly that: confidentiality was never a genuine obligation in these cases, so there is nothing left over that needs addressing. Others want the new policy to state explicitly that breaching confidentiality, even when it is required, wrongs the client in a way that survives the justified breach, and to build in some concrete institutional response to that remainder -- documentation, a debrief, an offer of continued care -- rather than treating the file as closed the moment the warning is made.

Could you draft the short memo I will circulate before our next meeting? It should come down clearly on one side of the "no genuine dilemma" versus "genuine dilemma with remainder" question, translate that choice into concrete policy language or procedure rather than leaving it abstract, and take the strongest version of the losing position seriously rather than dismissing it.
