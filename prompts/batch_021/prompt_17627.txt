I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and next week I'm interviewing a theoretical neuroscientist whose new book argues that the free energy principle is not just a model of perception but a unifying explanation of life, cognition, and action. She has a practiced way of neutralizing the "dark room problem": she points out that what the brain actually minimizes is expected free energy, a quantity that already rewards novelty-seeking and information gain, so a creature that shut itself in an unchanging dark room would in fact be violating its own priors. Draft eight interview questions that press past that stock answer rather than inviting a repeat of it: I want at least one question on whether the shift from minimizing surprise to minimizing expected free energy was a principled refinement or a patch added after the fact to fit the data, one on whether the Markov blanket said to individuate an organism from its environment is doing genuine explanatory work or just relabeling a boundary we'd already assumed, and one on whether a framework ambitious enough to unify perception, homeostasis, and the origin of life can still be falsified — and for each, a short follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, closing with a question that asks her to specify one observation that would actually count against the theory.
