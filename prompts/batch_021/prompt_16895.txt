"Wittgenstein discusses the example of a believer who says 'I believe in the Last Judgement,' contrasting this with an ordinary empirical belief such as believing there is a chair in the next room. He also discusses a Father O'Hara, who claimed in a BBC broadcast that Christian doctrines could be supported by the same kind of reasoning used to support scientific claims. (a) Using these two examples, explain what Wittgenstein thinks is distinctive about the 'grammar' of religious belief. (b) The philosopher Kai Nielsen later used the label 'Wittgensteinian Fideism' to criticize the view that religious language games are self-contained systems that cannot be judged by any standard external to them. Explain Nielsen's criticism, and assess whether it is a fair characterization of Wittgenstein's own position, as opposed to that of his later interpreters."

And here is Malik's answer in full:

"(a) For Wittgenstein, an ordinary empirical belief, like the belief that there is a chair in the next room, is the kind of thing you could check by looking, and you would revise or drop the belief if you looked and found no chair there. It is held on the basis of evidence, and further evidence could always come in and overturn it. Belief in the Last Judgement does not work like this: the believer is not treating it as a hypothesis about a future event that could, in principle, be confirmed or disconfirmed the way an empirical prediction can, since nothing that could happen would count as 'checking' it in the way that walking into the room checks whether there is a chair there. Instead, the belief shapes the way the person lives their entire life, so it plays what Wittgenstein calls a regulative role rather than being one more opinion that could simply be swapped out if the evidence changed. This is also why Wittgenstein discusses Father O'Hara's broadcast: O'Hara wanted to put religious doctrines on the same footing as scientific claims, defending them with evidence and probability the way a scientist defends a hypothesis. Since this is exactly the picture of religious belief that Wittgenstein thinks is right, that the rationality of faith should ultimately answer to the same standards of evidence we use elsewhere, he treats O'Hara's broadcast as a useful, if slightly clumsy, model for how a believer ought to talk about their own belief if pressed to defend it.

(b) Nielsen's criticism, which he calls 'Wittgensteinian Fideism,' is that this picture makes religious language into a closed system: each language game (religious, scientific, aesthetic, and so on) has its own internal rules for what counts as a good reason, and it is a kind of category mistake to bring in standards from outside the game to criticize what goes on inside it, in the same way it would be confused to criticize a chess move for breaking the rules of checkers. Nielsen thinks this is Wittgenstein's own settled view, not just something invented by his followers, since it follows directly from applying the general picture of meaning in the *Philosophical Investigations* to the special case of religious language. On this view, a believer's claims cannot be shown irrational by an outsider, since the language game supplies its own criteria of rationality and there is no neutral standpoint from which to judge between games. Nielsen objects that this lets religious belief off far too easily: however strange a belief's content, it could never count as irrational so long as it was consistent with the rest of the practice it belonged to, which amounts to a troubling relativism about truth. I agree with Nielsen here: if believers can always retreat to the claim that outsiders are applying the wrong language game's standards, the view cannot really distinguish reasonable faith from any other consistent but bizarre system of belief, which is a serious cost for a philosophical account of religious belief to have."
