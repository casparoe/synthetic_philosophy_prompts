At my company we run blameless postmortems after every major incident, and a few weeks ago two colleagues of mine, Nate and Farah, ended up in a long argument that started as a disagreement about our postmortem template and turned into something neither of them could resolve. The incident itself was mundane as these things go: a TLS certificate on one of our public-facing services expired and took the service down for about ninety minutes. Digging into the timeline, we found the outage had been "preventable" in at least four different ways: an automated renewal cron job had been silently disabled during an infrastructure migration six months earlier and nobody re-enabled it; a monitoring rule that would have paged someone when the cert hit thirty days to expiry had been muted after a false positive a few months back and never unmuted; the on-call engineer did get paged about two hours before expiry, but the page was bundled with a batch of unrelated noisy alerts and got triaged away under general alert fatigue; and, further back still, a senior engineer had filed a ticket over a year earlier proposing a proper certificate-management system, which the eng manager had deprioritized every quarter since. Our postmortem template has a mandatory "Root Cause" field, and the argument was about what should actually go in it.

The exchange went roughly like this. (1) Nate said the root cause was obviously the disabled cron job: fix that one thing and the rest of the chain never happens, so that's "the" cause. (2) Farah said that can't be right on its own terms, since re-enabling the alert would also have single-handedly prevented the outage, and so would the on-call engineer simply looking more closely at the page — by Nate's own test those are equally "the" cause, and now he has three candidates instead of one. (3) Nate conceded the point but argued it actually shows the test is too permissive: it also technically implicates the manager who kept deprioritizing the certificate-management ticket, and surely nobody wants to say a roadmap decision from some earlier quarter "caused" this week's outage — so some notion of proximate cause must be doing real work here, over and above mere "would have prevented it." (4) Farah replied that "proximate" can't simply mean "closest in time," since the muted alert and the missed page are both much more recent than the cron job change — if he's leaning on temporal proximity, he should be blaming those over the cron job, not the other way around. And if he doesn't mean temporal proximity, she wanted to know what he did mean, because as far as she could tell "proximate cause" was just a label for whichever candidate he already felt like blaming. (5) Nate then proposed a different test: the cron job, the alert, and the on-call miss are all failures of things that were already up and running — established systems that quietly broke — whereas the certificate-management system was never built in the first place, so there was no prior normal state for its absence to count as a deviation from. On this view, several things can count as causes at once; he just wanted a principled way to exclude the deprioritized-ticket case specifically. (6) Farah answered that "an established system that quietly broke" is doing exactly the work "proximate" was doing a minute earlier, just relabeled, because whether something counts as the normal, expected state of affairs is itself a fact about what the organization decided to set up and expect, not a fact about the server. She pushed this with a thought experiment: if the company had never had any renewal automation or alerting at all, and the identical physical chain of events played out — nothing automated, nothing monitored, cert quietly expires — would the outage then have no cause, purely because there was no established system for anything to deviate from? That seemed like the wrong kind of fact to settle a question about certificates and servers. Nate's last move was to suggest that maybe there's a genuine difference between things that actively bring an outcome about (the chain from disabling the cron job to the cert actually expiring) and things that merely fail to catch or reverse it afterward (the alert, the page), and that only the former should really count as causes — though he admitted, on reflection, that he wasn't sure he could make that distinction hold up either.

Neither of them budged, and I honestly couldn't tell you who came out ahead. Could you go through the exchange move by move and evaluate each numbered point on its own merits — who's making the stronger move at each stage, and whether any move is really just an earlier one restated in new terms — and then step back and say whether you think "root cause," as distinct from "contributing factor," is a coherent thing to be looking for in a case like this, or whether Farah's suspicion is basically right that it's blame-attribution wearing a lab coat?
