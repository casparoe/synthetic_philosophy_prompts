Over the past year I've gotten pretty into effective altruism blogs and forums, mostly as a hobby — I don't have any formal background in philosophy or decision theory, I just find this stuff addictive to read late at night. A while back I ran into a concept called "evidential cooperation in large worlds," sometimes discussed alongside "acausal trade," and I haven't been able to stop thinking about it since. As I understand it, the basic idea is this: if the universe (or the multiverse, if you take something like that seriously) is extremely large or infinite, then there are almost certainly other decision-making agents out there, causally sealed off from us forever, who reason in ways similar enough to how we reason that our choices are statistically entangled with theirs, even though nothing either of us does can ever physically affect the other. So if I choose to act cooperatively, generously, or true to some set of values even when it costs me, and even when the beneficiaries of that cooperation aren't "my kind" in the sense of sharing my exact values, that choice is itself evidence that similar agents elsewhere, reasoning the same way, are extending the same courtesy toward values unlike their own, including mine. Multiply this across an unimaginably large population of unreachable agents and you apparently get a case for cooperating with people (or aliens, or successor AIs, or whoever) you will never meet, observe, or verify anything about.

What I'd like from you is a short story that makes this idea felt rather than just argued. I don't want an explainer with a plot bolted on; I want you to pick a character (they don't have to be human) who is confronted with a decision that only really makes sense under this kind of reasoning, and to let us sit inside the strangeness of it: the total absence of feedback, the fact that the "trading partner" is a statistical abstraction rather than someone you can hear from or thank, and the discomfort of compromising toward values that aren't fully your own for the sake of a bet you can never see settled. Maybe that's a researcher on a generation ship who works out the argument alone and has to decide what to do before anyone else finds out, or an AI weighing a commitment toward some hypothetical version of itself it will never get to check in on, or something else entirely — whatever setting feels truest to the idea. I'd especially appreciate it if somewhere in the story someone else, a skeptic, a rival, an earlier version of the protagonist, pushes back and forces the main character to say out loud why this is supposed to be better than just being kind for its own sake. I'm genuinely unsure whether the idea is a profound extension of ethics or an elaborate way of talking yourself into being nice for no reason, and I'd like the story to leave that tension standing rather than resolve it for me.
