I'm drafting a scene for a novel in which a fictionalized Leibniz is handed a modern account of panpsychism and asked what he makes of it. Before I write his lines, I want to know what he'd actually be committed to saying, not just have him invoke "monads" the way an introductory lecture does and move on. Would Leibniz regard the combination problem — the difficulty of explaining how countless tiny units of experience could add up to one unified mind like ours — as a real problem for a view like his, or does his own system dissolve the question before it can even arise? And if it dissolves the question, what does that dissolution cost him: is there some commitment he accepted without much fuss that a modern panpsychist trying to solve the same problem would balk at? Ground the answer in what he actually held about composite bodies, dominant monads, and the difference between a true unity and a mere aggregate.
