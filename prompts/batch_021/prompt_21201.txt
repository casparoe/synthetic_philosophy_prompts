My mother, Diane, died three weeks ago at seventy-eight, after thirty-one years working as a public defender. Sometime in her late fifties, a friend from her book club handed her a photocopied essay by a philosopher named Galen Strawson, built around what he calls the Basic Argument, and it took over the rest of her life. The argument, more or less as she explained it to me across two decades of dinner-table lectures, is that you can only be truly, ultimately responsible for what you do if you're responsible for the kind of person you are, since that's what decided the action in the first place; but you can only be responsible for the kind of person you are if you made yourself that way on purpose, and whatever led you to make that decision was itself just more of the person you already were, before any of your own deciding had happened, so the responsibility keeps sliding backward and never lands anywhere. She was firm that none of this needs determinism to be true, and she liked to put it this way: tossing some randomness into the chain doesn't hand you authorship of yourself, it just turns the last unearned step from a domino into a coin flip. What she took from all this was that nobody — not the men she spent her career defending, and not herself — ultimately deserves credit or blame for who they became, though she never treated that as a reason to stop trying to become better, or to stop asking other people to.

She ran a Thursday-night discussion group on this subject at the public library for the better part of ten years, and a good number of that crowd — retired colleagues, a couple of social workers, and one retired philosophy professor — will be at the memorial and will catch it immediately if the eulogy blurs what she meant. Most of the room, though, will be family, and none of my cousins have ever heard of Galen Strawson. She left a note asking that nobody stand up and say she "earned" the woman she became, or that she "overcame" a hard upbringing through sheer force of will, since both are exactly the kind of self-authorship claim she spent thirty years arguing nobody gets to make. She asked just as firmly that nobody flatten this into "she thought it was all fated and couldn't have done otherwise," because that was never her point and she would have hated hearing it put that way.

I'm not a philosopher, and I've probably gotten some of the finer points above slightly wrong, but I know that particular distinction — between denying someone deserves the credit and denying they could have done otherwise — was the one thing she needed done right. Could you write the eulogy for me? It should run about seven or eight minutes, needs to work for both halves of the room, and can't say anything on her behalf that she would have stood up and corrected.
