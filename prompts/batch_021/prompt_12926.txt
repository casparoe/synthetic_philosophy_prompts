I'm a novelist finishing a book with a historical strand involving Alan Turing, and I've drafted a short afterword for it — a page of nonfiction reflection on a passage of his that never makes it into the popular tellings of his life. I'd value your comments on the draft below, especially on (a) whether I've gotten the philosophical and historical details right, and (b) whether the interpretive claim in the second paragraph is a genuine insight or just a clever-sounding stretch. Here it is:

Tucked into the sixth section of "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," after Turing has made fairly short work of the theological objection, Lady Lovelace's, and several others, comes a paragraph that still unsettles readers who stumble on it. Turing turns solemn about telepathy — one of four phenomena he groups under that heading, alongside clairvoyance, precognition, and psycho-kinesis, though telepathy is the one he treats as best attested. He tells us the statistical case built up from decades of card-guessing trials struck him as overwhelming, and that a properly rigorous version of the imitation game therefore has to reckon with the possibility that a telepathic interrogator could pick out the human respondent by means having nothing to do with what either party actually types. His proposed remedy — running the whole test inside what he calls a "telepathy-proof room" — is offered completely straight-faced, with none of the ironic distance he allows himself elsewhere in the section.

The standard move, on the rare occasions this paragraph gets discussed at all, is to file it under "Turing's one embarrassing lapse," a residue of a Cambridge milieu in which psychical research still had a foothold in respectable science. I want to resist that reading, or at least complicate it. Structurally, the ESP objection isn't an anomaly in section six; it's the same move Turing makes throughout — grant an objection its strongest empirical premise, then ask whether the imitation game still does its job under that assumption. Read that way, the telepathy-proof room isn't credulousness so much as Turing following his own method one step further than anyone else was willing to, and the interesting question stops being "did he really believe this?" and becomes "does the patch actually work, or does shielding the room just relocate the very problem the test was invented to solve — namely, how do you ever know you've blocked every channel of leakage you haven't thought of?"

I'm least sure of two things: whether I'm characterizing Turing's own confidence in the evidence fairly, and whether the telepathy-proof room is best read as a serious methodological proposal rather than a dry joke at the reader's expense. I'd rather have those points challenged now than have a reviewer catch them later, so please don't be gentle if something is off, whether that's a matter of fact or a matter of the argument not actually working.
