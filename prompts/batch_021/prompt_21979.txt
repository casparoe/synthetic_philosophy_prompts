Hey, so I'm on the Frege–Geach chapter of my dissertation (metaethics, general expressivism), and I've hit a snag I keep going back and forth on. My reading group chewed on it last week and now I can't let it go, so I scribbled my current thinking into a page of rough notes and would love comments before I bring it to my advisor on friday — mainly (1) am I representing Blackburn and Gibbard fairly, and (2) does the little test case I build near the end actually show what I think it shows, or am I just missing some bit of machinery that already handles it fine.

Quick recap for context: the problem is usually posed via negation/conditionals — an expressivist has to say what attitude "lying is wrong" contributes when it's just the antecedent of "if lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong," and why the modus ponens using that plus "lying is wrong" comes out valid, given neither premise states a truth-apt fact on the expressivist's own view. Blackburn's move (the tree-of-attitudes apparatus from Spreading the Word) is to say the conditional expresses a higher-order attitude: roughly, disapproval of combining disapproval-of-lying with anything short of disapproval of brother-lying. Validity then isn't classical entailment, it's something like: you can't hold all the premise-attitudes and reject the conclusion-attitude without ending up disapproving of your own actual state. Gibbard's hyperplan version in Thinking How to Live gets the same result differently, over maximally specific plans-for-action rather than trees of pro/con attitudes: an argument's valid when every hyperplan that vindicates the premises (given the relevant non-moral beliefs) also vindicates the conclusion.

Where I get stuck is disjunction. I have a half-memory of Schroeder saying in Being For that disjunction is where this kind of theory earns its keep in a way negation and conjunction don't quite test for (could be misremembering, book's not in front of me), and I think I see why: the negation-and-conjunction machinery seems to assume the agent already has a settled attitude toward each component, but a genuinely disjunctive moral belief usually comes from being unsure which of two things is true, not from a determinate higher-order attitude about a combination of settled ones. So I built a test case: someone asserts "either it was wrong to torture the cat, or it was wrong to lie to mom about it afterward," genuinely unsure which, then learns lying to mom wasn't wrong, and concludes torturing the cat was wrong (a moral disjunctive syllogism). My hunch is Gibbard's hyperplans handle this basically for free, since a hyperplan is just maximally specific and will rule out both, one, or neither act, so ordinary possible-worlds-ish reasoning goes through — while Blackburn's tree approach has to somehow construct, by hand, a single attitude that just IS "disapproving of at least one of these," and it's not obvious there's a non-ad-hoc way to do that starting from mere uncertainty. Does that asymmetry actually hold up, or is there an easy fix for Blackburn I'm not seeing? And honestly, is this even a fresh enough point for a dissertation section, or is it just a repackaging of stuff that's already well-worn in the Being For literature? Tear it apart if you need to, that's kind of the point.
