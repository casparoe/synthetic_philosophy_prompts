Here is the question as it was posed on the problem set:

"State Harsanyi's Social Aggregation Theorem, including the role played by the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms and the Pareto condition. Then answer two further questions: (a) Is it accurate to describe this theorem as an axiomatic derivation of utilitarianism from premises about individual rationality and unanimity alone? Why or why not? (b) Arrow's impossibility theorem tells us that no social welfare function aggregating individual preference orderings can satisfy a small set of independently plausible conditions. Harsanyi's theorem, by contrast, is a possibility result. What exactly licenses Harsanyi's escape from Arrow's negative conclusion?"

And here, verbatim, is the student's answer:

"Harsanyi's theorem (1955) assumes that each of the n individuals in a society has preferences over lotteries satisfying the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms of completeness, transitivity, continuity, and independence, and that 'society' itself has preferences over the same lotteries satisfying those same axioms. This guarantees that each individual i is represented by an expected-utility function u_i, unique up to a positive affine transformation, and that society is represented by some function W. Harsanyi then adds a Pareto condition: whenever every individual is indifferent between two lotteries p and q, so is society. Given these premises, the theorem concludes that W must be a weighted sum of the individual u_i, i.e., W(p) = Σ_i a_i u_i(p) + b for constants a_i and b, with a_i ≥ 0 once the stronger Pareto principle (unanimous strict preference implies social strict preference) is added.

(a) I would say this does give us utilitarianism from remarkably minimal premises. Each individual's rationality reduces to an expected-utility function, the only further assumption is a mild unanimity requirement, and the conclusion is that the social ranking is a straight sum of individual utilities — which is basically utilitarianism, derived without needing a substantive ethical premise like 'everyone's happiness counts equally.' In fact, the theorem hands you interpersonal comparability for free: once each person's utility is pinned down by their own choices over lotteries, summing those utilities is no more arbitrary than summing anything else measured on a shared cardinal scale.

(b) Arrow's theorem is a negative result about aggregating ordinal preference rankings, subject to unrestricted domain, weak Pareto, independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA), and non-dictatorship. Harsanyi reaches a possibility result by effectively weakening IIA rather than by changing the underlying informational assumption: because the social ranking of any two lotteries in Harsanyi's setup is sensitive to how individuals value other, unrelated lotteries through their shared linear utility scale, his framework simply has no real analogue of IIA, and it is this missing condition — not the shift from ordinal to cardinal utility as such — that does the actual work of getting Harsanyi past Arrow's impossibility."
