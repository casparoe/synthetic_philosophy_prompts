I spent my career directing a comparative-cognition laboratory, and only since retiring have I had time to read philosophy properly, which is how I ended up in Descartes' letters to Henry More and to the Marquess of Newcastle rather than just the textbook line about beast-machines. Our lab's founding research subject, a chimpanzee named Josie who spent nearly twenty years learning to communicate with us through a lexigram board, died last month at forty-six, and I have been asked to say a few words at the small memorial the lab is holding for her. Roughly half the room will be old colleagues from a scrupulously anti-anthropomorphic tradition who will bristle at any suggestion that Josie really had something like thought or feeling in the sense Descartes reserved for language-users alone; the other half will be the younger caretakers who raised her and want to hear, plainly, that her inner life was real. I would be grateful if you could write about five minutes of remarks that take the skeptics' caution seriously enough that it does not collapse into the old view of her as clever clockwork, that still give the caretakers what they came for, and that do not quietly pretend to settle a question which, as I now understand, neither the philosophers nor the primatologists have actually settled.
