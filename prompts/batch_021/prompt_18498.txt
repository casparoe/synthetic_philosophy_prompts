A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about whether bolting the two halves of a disassembled bicycle together brings some genuinely new object into existence, and the conversation gradually turned into a dispute about van Inwagen's Special Composition Question. My friend insisted that some moderate answer — composition occurs when the parts are fastened, or bonded, or causally integrated, or whatever the criterion may be — simply has to be right, since that is what ordinary intuition says, and that only philosophers with a taste for revisionary metaphysics would prefer an extreme answer such as universalism or nihilism. I objected that there is supposed to be a serious argument, associated with van Inwagen and Lewis, that rules out every moderate answer on principled grounds: the argument from vagueness. When I tried to reconstruct it for my friend, though, I ran into the obvious objection that vagueness is everywhere in language — "bald," "heap," "tall" — and nobody treats that as a metaphysical emergency, so why should vagueness in composition be any different? Could you explain, at an intuitive level, exactly why the vagueness argument is supposed to be fatal specifically for intermediate answers to the SCQ, rather than just another instance of the harmless vagueness we tolerate everywhere else? I would also be curious whether van Inwagen's own preferred answer — that composition occurs exactly when the result is a living organism — actually escapes the problem, or whether it just relocates the vagueness to the origin of life.
