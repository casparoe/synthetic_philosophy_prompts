I'm a retired physicist (spent about forty years doing low-temperature solid-state work) and since retiring I've been sitting in on a philosophy of religion class at the community college near me, mostly for fun. We just spent two sessions on Gödel's ontological argument, working through the "positive property" axioms and how the whole thing runs in S5 modal logic. Afterward, an old colleague from my physics days send me an article about how back in 2013 a pair of logicians fed the whole proof into an automated theorem prover, and the machine confirmed the argument is logically valid, with no gaps or errors anywhere in the derivation. Thats been rattling around in my head ever since.

Here's my trouble: in my field, if an independent method checks a result and it holds up, that's about as good as it gets - it's the same reason we now treat the Kepler conjecture as settled, now that the proof has been machine-verified line by line. So if arguably the greatest logician of the twentieth century wrote a rigorous proof that God necessarily exists, and decades later a computer went through the whole derivation and found it sound, why isn't that treated as something close to a mathematical resolution of the question, the same way machine-checking settled Kepler? Everyone I've asked in the class just kind of smiles and changes the subject, which honestly reminds me of how certain colleagues used to wave away inconvenient results back in my working days. Am I missing something real here, or is there an actual reason nobody treats this as decisive?
