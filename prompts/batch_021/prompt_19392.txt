She argues that once you require a prior over the smaller envelope's contents with finite expectation, whether switching is favored given the sum observed is simply a fact about that prior's shape, and no mystery remains. He argues that finite expectation isn't itself required by expected-value reasoning, and that a legitimate, properly normalized prior can be specified under which switching is favored no matter what sum appears. This works precisely because that prior has infinite expectation: every conditional expectation favors the swap, even though no unconditional comparison between the envelopes is available. Have them debate whether this is just the St. Petersburg problem in disguise, as she thinks, or a genuine failure of conglomerability that exposes a deeper limit on conditionalization, as he thinks -- each engaging the other's actual argument rather than a caricature of it.
