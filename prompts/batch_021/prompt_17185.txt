Superrationality keeps resurfacing in the rationalist corners of the internet I read, and it came up again recently in a long argument with some friends from a forum I frequent. I first encountered the idea in Hofstadter's "Metamagical Themas" columns, where he argues that two agents facing a genuinely symmetric one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma - each with common knowledge that the other reasons exactly as they do - should both cooperate, since an asymmetric outcome like "I defect while you cooperate" isn't something either of them can coherently choose unilaterally. The idea has had a long afterlife on LessWrong and in the functional/timeless decision theory literature, but almost every retelling I've come across leans on the same crutch: the two parties are stipulated to be exact copies of one another, which is precisely what licenses the symmetry argument in the first place. What I've never seen dramatized well is the harder case just one step removed from that idealization: two agents who are highly correlated but demonstrably not identical, so that each has genuine, irreducible uncertainty about whether the other will actually reach the same conclusion they do.

I'd like you to write a short story, roughly 800 to 1500 words, built around that harder case. Some possible setups, though you should feel free to invent your own: two branches of a single uploaded mind that diverged slightly after a fork years ago and have had no contact since; two members of independently evolved species who exchange exactly one message before each must decide, with no further contact, whether to divert scarce resources toward a colony only one of them can actually reach; or two AI systems trained on similar but not identical objectives who must simultaneously decide whether to disable a shared safeguard. Whichever setting you choose, the characters should have to reckon explicitly with how much shared reasoning is actually required for the superrational move to make sense, and the story should earn its philosophical weight through plot, dialogue, and consequence rather than through one character lecturing another. I'd also ask that you resist the easy outs: don't quietly reveal that the two parties were identical after all, don't give them enough back-and-forth beforehand that the situation stops being a genuine one-shot dilemma without binding commitments, and feel free to leave open whether their choice actually gets vindicated by the outcome rather than tying the philosophical question up with a bow.
