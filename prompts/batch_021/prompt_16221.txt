For more than three decades I worked as a computational physicist specializing in lattice gauge theory, and for the whole of that career my collaborators and I built our work on an assumption we could never actually prove: that pure Yang–Mills theory possesses a mass gap. Our glueball spectra, our confinement calculations, whole grant proposals rested on treating this as settled, even though everyone in the field knew perfectly well that a rigorous existence-and-mass-gap proof remains one of the unsolved Clay Millennium Prize problems, backed by nothing more than numerical, non-rigorous lattice evidence. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy in earnest, and looking back at that practice through this new lens, I keep stumbling over a basic question: what, if anything, did it mean for us to have been so confident about a statement that is either a necessary mathematical truth or a necessary falsehood, given that ordinary probability theory assumes logical omniscience and leaves no real room for an agent to be uncertain about such things?

A former postdoc of mine, who now works in AI alignment, recently sent me the 2016 paper on logical induction by Scott Garrabrant and his coauthors at the Machine Intelligence Research Institute, which seems tailor-made for exactly this predicament. It constructs a market-like procedure in which computable "traders" bet on the truth values of arithmetical sentences, with prices set so that no trader drawn from a class of efficiently computable trading strategies can extract unbounded profit from them, and it shows that the resulting probabilities behave sensibly even for statements nobody has yet proved or refuted. I found this genuinely illuminating, but also somewhat unsatisfying on reflection, since its guarantees are asymptotic and are stated relative to an idealized, countable population of efficient traders, which leaves me wondering how much of this is meant to be normative for a finite, fallible community of physicists reasoning via lattice simulations, analogies to solvable models, and peer review, rather than anything resembling a formal trading strategy defined over a fixed deductive process.

I would be grateful if you could lay out the most important implications of logical induction for a case like this: both for how someone in my position should now think, retrospectively, about the credence my field implicitly placed in the mass gap conjecture, and more generally for how working scientists ought to reason about, and act on, open mathematical conjectures that their theories quietly depend on. In particular, I would like to know whether the framework genuinely dissolves the puzzle of what such a credence could even mean, or whether it mainly relocates that puzzle onto new questions about which trading strategies a finite scientific community can actually be said to approximate.
