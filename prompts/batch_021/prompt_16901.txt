I never had any formal training in philosophy. I only started reading seriously about it a few years ago, after one too many frustrating arguments with my uncle about where he gets his news, and that reading eventually turned into a small podcast I record out of my spare room. Next month I'm interviewing a philosopher who has built her career defending anti-reductionism about testimony: the view that when someone tells us something, we're entitled by default to believe them without first needing independent positive reasons to trust the source, because that entitlement is supposed to be just as basic as the entitlement perception or memory already gives us. I've now heard her give the same tidy two-minute summary of the position — Reid's "principle of credulity" pitted against Hume's insistence that trust in testimony has to be earned through experience — on two other shows, and nobody has pushed her past it.

Could you draft me eight to ten interview questions that don't let her stop there? I'd like at least one question that presses on the tension between calling the entitlement "basic and non-inferential" and still needing some account of why trust, rather than doubt, is the correct default — and since I suspect her go-to reply will be that reductionism is self-defeating (that we could never gather the needed evidence without already relying on testimony for language and background belief in the first place), I want a sharp follow-up asking why "reductionism can't be satisfied" should show that anti-reductionism is true, rather than just that we're stuck with an imperfect trust we can never fully vindicate. I'd also like a question about what work the idea of a "defeater" is really doing in her account — whether having to stay alert for reasons to doubt a speaker is structurally so different from the reliability-tracking that reductionists demand, or just the same requirement repackaged as a background condition — with a follow-up pressing on cases where noticing a defeater depends on background knowledge that was only ever acquired through testimony in the first place.

Finally, I want one question built around a case I keep turning over myself: an isolated community where everyone sincerely and consistently reports something false, because the only people anyone ever cross-checks a claim against are equally in the dark. Does her view really commit her to calling those hearers justified in their false belief, and if she has some way of blocking that verdict, isn't whatever does the blocking just the reductionist's own move in disguise? Close the list with a question asking what specific finding, case, or argument would actually move her off anti-reductionism altogether, rather than just toward a more careful version of it.
