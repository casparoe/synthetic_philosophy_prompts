Three months ago the startup I had worked at for six years was acquired, and the resulting payout means that, for the first time, I am in a position to make a genuinely significant charitable donation. Wanting to reason about the decision carefully rather than simply follow my instincts, I began reading about cause prioritization and quickly ran into the "cluelessness" objection to consequentialism, which has more or less stalled my thinking ever since. Below are my working notes toward a longer piece I may eventually write up properly; before I go further, I would value your comments on whether the argument actually holds together.

The setup: I am weighing (a) funding bednet distribution or cash transfers in low-income countries against (b) funding advocacy for a domestic carbon tax, and I wanted to compare them on broadly consequentialist grounds.

1. The problem is not merely that I lack precise figures for either option's effects; it is that both interventions plausibly have large, unforeseeable downstream effects whose sign I cannot even guess at. Saving lives through bednets changes population size, which changes aggregate consumption, land use, and the number of animals raised in factory farms, in directions I have no way of signing. A carbon tax alters the pace and character of economic growth in ways that could hasten or delay the development of transformative technologies, an effect that plausibly swamps the direct climate benefit in expected-value terms.

2. I want to distinguish this from a more benign kind of uncertainty that I gather Hilary Greaves calls "simple" cluelessness: cases where an action has effects of unknown sign, but where nothing favors one option over another (small, unforeseeable shifts in who ends up being conceived years down the line, because of tiny changes to people's schedules, say). This kind of noise seems safe to set aside, since it attaches more or less symmetrically to every option on the table, including doing nothing.

3. My cases in (1) do not feel like that, which is presumably what she means by "complex" cluelessness instead. In each case I can tell a reasonably specific story for why the unforeseeable effects might be large and might favor one option, and an equally specific story for why they might favor the other, with no way to weigh these stories against each other or against the direct, foreseeable benefits I started with. So the considerations do not wash out to neutrality; I simply do not know how to form a stable credence at all.

4. My attempted way out: even if I cannot rank (a) and (b) all-things-considered, perhaps I can still rank them on a restricted basis — counting only reasonably foreseeable, near-term effects — provided the further-out effects in (1) are symmetric between (a) and (b) in some sense, so that setting them aside is not ignoring a reason to prefer one option, just declining to guess at an irrelevant one. But I suspect this move just smuggles the "simple" cluelessness reasoning from (2) back into a case I have already described, in (3), as not fitting that mold.

I would value your comments on three things: (i) whether (2) and (3) mark a genuine distinction rather than merely redescribing ordinary uncertainty in fancier language; (ii) whether the move in (4) is actually available to me given what I say in (3), or whether it is wishful thinking dressed up as a principled distinction; and (iii) whether there is a more defensible way to salvage some action-guidance here without simply abandoning consequentialism for a rule of thumb or a side-constraint at the first sign of difficulty.
