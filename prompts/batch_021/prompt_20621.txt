Two friends of mine who both did grad school in economics got into it over drinks about the centipede game -- the one where a pot grows every round but whoever "takes" first grabs the bigger share, so backward induction says the first player should just take immediately, even though they'd both do way better by cooperating for a while.

Mara said the immediate-defection result isn't just a rule of thumb, its basically forced: Aumann actually proved that if it's common knowledge both players are rational, backward induction is the unique outcome, full stop. Deniz said that's exactly the weird part -- the proof needs "rational" to cover what a player would do at every node, including ones nobody ever reaches, and the second somebody actually passes past the point where they were "supposed" to take, that's evidence the whole common-knowledge-of-rationality premise has already collapsed, so the argument only really holds together right up until the game actually gets played. Mara replied that off-path stuff is beside the point: before anyone moves, backward induction still tells you the correct first move for a rational player who expects rationality from their opponent, and nothing that happens later changes what that first move should have been. Deniz shot back that this is question-begging -- being rational just means best-responding to your actual beliefs about the other person, and if Deniz has decent reason to think Mara (who's read all the same papers) won't actually defect immediately either, then passing is Deniz's best response regardless of what an idealized backward-induction argument says, so calling that move "irrational" just assumes the very thing that's in dispute.

They went back and forth on this for like 40 minutes and asked me to settle it, which I honestly couldn't.
