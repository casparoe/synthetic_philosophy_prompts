I'm a philosophy professor, and my mom died on Tuesday -- I'm giving her eulogy at the funeral this weekend. She was a diplomatic historian who spent her whole career on the July Crisis of 1914, and her whole thing, going back to grad school, was contingency: decades of publications arguing against "timetable" readings of how WWI started, the A.J.P. Taylor-ish idea that once the mobilization schedules kicked in the war became unstoppable by sheer logistics. She'd point out that the assassination itself only happened because the driver took a wrong turn onto Franz Josef Street, right past where Princip was standing after his first attempt had already failed, that the Kaiser's own marginal notes on the Serbian reply show him looking for a way out days before the declaration, that peace was possible at four or five separate junctures in July even after Sarajevo -- and that anyone who narrates it as inevitable, whether that's the old Fischer "Germany planned this all along" line or the more fatalistic "great powers were bound to collide sooner or later" line, is smuggling in a conclusion the evidence doesn't actually support. She got genuinely annoyed at other people's funerals when eulogies said things like "he was always meant to" or "her time had come," and she'd elbow me in the pew about it. I know for a fact she'd hate hearing any version of that said over her own body.

The problem is the eulogy has to work for a room that's maybe a third historians and old colleagues who'll know exactly what she'd object to, and two thirds family and neighbors who just want to be comforted and have never heard of the July Crisis. I cant stand up there and lean on the "everything happens for a reason" register she spent her whole career arguing against -- that would basically be lying about her at her own funeral -- but I also can't deliver forty minutes on structuralism versus contingency in 1914 historiography to people who came to grieve. Can you write the eulogy for me? I want her actual view of history and causation to be doing real work in it, not just a name-dropped fact about her job, and I want it to land as love for the two-thirds of the room who are there for comfort rather than argument.
