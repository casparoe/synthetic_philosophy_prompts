And here's the student's answer:

"Black's target is the Principle of Identity of Indiscernibles (PII): the claim that if x and y share all their properties, they must be one and the same thing. Ordinary counterexamples to this, like two peas in a pod or two drops of water that look exactly alike, aren't very threatening, because a defender of PII can point out that even if the two objects match on every intrinsic quality (colour, shape, taste), they still differ in their relations to everything else: one pea sits an inch from a particular fold in the pod, the other doesn't; one drop is closer to the tap. As long as PII is read as covering relational properties too, cases like this leave it untouched.

Black's contribution is to strip away the 'rest of the world' that these relational differences trade on. He asks us to imagine a universe with nothing in it but two spheres, exactly alike in size, material, temperature and every other intrinsic feature, sitting some distance apart. Since nothing else exists, any purely qualitative relational description true of one sphere — 'is such-and-such distance from an iron sphere of diameter d' — is automatically true of the other as well. So the spheres agree not only on their monadic properties but on every general relational one too; there's no true qualitative description that picks out one and not the other. Yet there are, Black insists, two spheres and not one, since each has its own location. This is precisely the situation the 'relational properties' defence can't handle, since that defence needs some relation that differs between the objects, and a perfectly symmetric two-object universe supplies none. So even a version of PII strong enough to survive the pea and water-drop cases goes under here.

Black also considers an objection meant to save PII: sphere A has the property of being identical to A, which sphere B lacks (and vice versa), so really the spheres do differ after all. He's right that this move keeps PII from being falsified, but only by trivializing it — letting in a 'property' like that makes PII come out true automatically for any two distinct things whatsoever, so it can't be what a defender of PII really wants. Where I think Black actually comes down, though, is that this is the correct account of what individuates the spheres: since no qualitative fact tells them apart, it has to be their primitive thisness, or haecceity, that makes them two rather than one. So in the end the thought experiment shows that two numerically identical things can still come apart qualitatively, which is why most metaphysicians since have only wanted to accept Leibniz's Law in some restricted, non-trivial form."
