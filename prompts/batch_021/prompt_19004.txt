I've spent the better part of a week down a rabbit hole that started at my local rationalist meetup, where an old argument flared up again: does Gödel's incompleteness theorem show that minds cannot be reduced to formal systems in the way Lucas and, later, Penrose claimed, or is that whole line of reasoning a well-worn dead end that keeps getting reinvented by people who haven't read the responses to it? I went back and reread Lucas's original 1961 paper, the relevant stretches of both of Penrose's books, and an old Scott Aaronson essay on the subject that a friend dug up, and what struck me is how much sharper the actual exchanges in the literature are compared to the one-line dismissals ("Gödel's theorem is about formal systems, not people!") that tend to circulate whenever this comes up on forums.

So here is what I would like your help with. Below are eight distinct objections that have been pressed against the Lucas–Penrose argument at one point or another. I have reconstructed each in my own words and deliberately stripped away the names usually attached to them in the secondary literature, so that whatever ranking you give tracks the actual logical weight of the move itself rather than deference to whoever is credited with having made it.

1. To run the diagonal argument, the reasoner must be entitled to assert that the formal system F capturing their own reasoning is consistent (or sound). But Gödel's second incompleteness theorem says F cannot prove its own consistency from within. So the confident step from "here is G(F)" to "and I can see it is true" seems to smuggle in exactly the kind of self-knowledge the theorem itself rules out.

2. Even granting the argument's logic in full, all it shows is that no one can be handed an explicit formal description of their own reasoning and thereby catch themselves transcending it. That is compatible with each of us being extensionally identical to some Turing machine all along; we would simply never be positioned to know which one, or to run the construction on ourselves. Being a machine and being able to certify which machine you are come apart, and the argument only ever threatens the latter.

3. Actual mathematicians are not sound reasoners: they entertain false lemmas, misremember proofs, and occasionally hold contradictory beliefs for years before noticing. A formal system modeling such a person's output is almost certainly inconsistent, and an inconsistent system trivially proves everything, Gödel sentence included. The interesting gap the argument needs only opens up for a flawless idealization of human reasoning that no working mathematician instantiates.

4. In his second book, Penrose tried to repair the argument by running it off a notion of soundness a reasoner could, in some idealized sense, come to accept about the very system formalizing them, rather than off bare consistency. Commentators replied that this repair just relocates the original gap one level up: accepting that F is sound is not the same as F encoding that acceptance, and the derivation trades on sliding between "F proves p" and "the reasoner, using F as one resource among others, comes to believe p."

5. Mechanism about the mind does not require that whatever implements a person's cognition be a tidy, humanly specifiable axiomatic system that could in principle be written down and handed back to them for inspection. It might instead be an enormous, opaque, non-axiomatized computational object, shaped by evolution and learning, with no compact description anyone could ever produce, let alone diagonalize against. If so, the entire "let F be a formal system capturing your reasoning" setup never gets a foothold on any actual candidate.

6. The Gödel sentence for any formal system rich enough to reproduce human mathematical competence would be a syntactic object of mind-boggling length, nothing like the tidy self-referential sentences that appear in expository treatments, even granting every step of the logic that gets us there. Nothing in the argument explains how a finite, resource-bounded brain is supposed to intuit the truth of such an object, so a fully successful version would establish at most a curiosity about idealized reasoners, with no bearing on any realistic comparison between human and machine intelligence.

7. The argument fixes on a single, static formal system as its target, but on one influential picture, human mathematics is an open-ended, historically extended practice that keeps licensing new axioms and ordinal-logic extensions of exactly the kind Turing himself discussed, rather than a machine grinding out theorems from one fixed base forever. Whether such a self-revising practice can be identified with "a machine" at all is precisely what is at issue, so treating it as a fixed F begs the question against one of the more interesting forms of mechanism.

8. Suppose the logical argument goes through without a hitch. It would still only license the modest conclusion that human mathematical competence outruns any single fixed formal system; it says nothing about consciousness or subjective experience. The further move to a specific noncomputable physical mechanism in the brain is a distinct empirical wager, one that stands or falls on considerations that have nothing to do with Gödel's theorem, and that most parties to the debate treat as a separate matter entirely.

For each of these, I want you to judge how damaging it is to the Lucas–Penrose argument — that is, if the objection is basically correct, how much of the argument's anti-mechanist conclusion is left standing?
