I've been reading Rorty for a novel with a philosopher character, and I'm in a reading group that just finished Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity. At the last meeting, two other members got into an argument the rest of us only watched. Dana argued that Rorty's antirepresentationalism -- since there's no vocabulary-independent moral reality for argument to track, redescription must do the work argument once did -- backs his claim that sentimental narrative, not argument, enlarges people's sense of who counts as one of us; novelists, she concluded, needn't worry about philosophical rigor, only about writing prose particular enough to make a stranger's pain vivid. Mateo objected that this is a strange lesson from someone who spent his career arguing for that anti-theory conclusion: if argument can't move anyone toward solidarity, why argue for the thesis instead of writing a novel? Dana said Rorty's target was philosophers, not strangers: he was arguing them out of chasing rational foundations for human rights -- a meta-level task argument can do even without ever making anyone less cruel toward anyone in particular. Mateo answered that this distinction is too convenient: Rorty clearly hoped his books did more than reshuffle research priorities, and readers who say he changed how they see cruelty and difference are reporting the very effect he insists only narrative produces. Dana replied that's an unproven empirical claim, not a contradiction: intellectuals might really be argument-responsive in a way felt dispositions toward outsiders are not. Could you go through their exchange and say who has the better of it at each step, not just who wins overall, and flag anything you think both of them are missing?
