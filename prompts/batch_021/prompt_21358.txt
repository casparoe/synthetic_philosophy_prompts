I'm a retired analytical chemist -- spent about thirty years in a lab before I hung up the coat -- and somewhere in the last couple of years I fell into reading philosophy of language, of all the random things to pick up in retirement. Started with a secondhand logic textbook my daughter got me as a half-joking gift, and now I'm apparently the guy at dinner parties who wants to argue about conditionals.

Right now I'm stuck on something I ran into while reading about the difference between indicative and counterfactual conditionals. Theres a famous minimal pair I keep seeing quoted (I believe its originally due to a logician named Ernest Adams) that goes roughly like this: (1) If Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, someone else did. (2) If Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have. Sentence (1) strikes me as obviously true -- Kennedy's dead, so if it wasn't Oswald, somebody else must have pulled the trigger. Sentence (2) strikes me as clearly false, or at least a lot shakier, since nobody thinks there was a backup assassin sitting around in case Oswald lost his nerve. What gets me is that (1) and (2) have exactly the same antecedent and consequent -- the only thing that changed is the grammatical mood. I brought this to my Tuesday philosophy meetup (mostly other retired engineers, plus one ex-lawyer who thinks he's cleverer than the rest of us) and we argued in circles for a whole session without landing anywhere. Half the room kept insisting a conditional is a conditional, full stop; the other half insisted mood obviously changes the content, end of story. It bugs me on a professional level too, honestly, since half my old career ran on counterfactual causal claims -- "if we hadn't added the catalyst, the reaction never would have taken off" -- and I'd always just assumed that kind of reasoning sat on the same uniform logical footing as everyday "if...then" claims.

So here's what I'd like from you. Write me a dialogue between two philosophers of language who land on opposite sides of this puzzle. One holds that both indicative and counterfactual conditionals get their truth conditions from comparing the actual world to the closest possible worlds in which the antecedent is true (the possible-worlds camp -- Stalnaker's apparently the name most associated with the unified version of this, and Lewis for the counterfactual side specifically). The other holds that indicative conditionals like (1) were never in the business of describing possible worlds to begin with, and are better understood in terms of how confident you'd be in the consequent once you suppose the antecedent -- meaning their truth-aptness, if they even have any, is a completely different animal from whatever is going on with counterfactuals like (2) (Adams and Edgington, I gather, are the relevant names). I want them to actually argue about the Oswald/Kennedy pair itself -- each pressing on the spot where the other's theory either delivers the wrong verdict or has to swallow something awkward to dodge it -- rather than just each delivering a lecture in the other's general direction. Please make both sides sharp; I don't want a strawman on either end, just two people who genuinely think the other is making a basic mistake, and who each know enough to defend their corner.
