And here is the answer I need to grade, transcribed exactly as the student submitted it:

(a) The gene's-eye view, associated with Dawkins, treats the gene rather than the organism as the fundamental unit of selection, because genes are copied from generation to generation with very high fidelity, whereas organisms are one-off assemblages rebuilt from scratch each generation. Dawkins captured this with the replicator/vehicle distinction: genes are "replicators" because they are the entities whose structure is preserved by the copying process, while organisms are "vehicles" built by replicators to promote their own survival and further replication. This is the background for Hamilton's rule, which says a gene for altruistic behavior will increase in frequency when rb > c, where r is the genetic relatedness between the altruist and the recipient, b is the reproductive benefit to the recipient, and c is the reproductive cost to the altruist. Because relatedness is what lets a copy of the altruist's gene be propagated indirectly through the recipient's offspring, kin selection is usually presented as vindicating the gene's-eye view rather than undermining it.

(b) The Price equation decomposes the total change in a trait's average value into a covariance term, capturing the effect of selection, and a second term capturing any systematic bias in transmission from parent to offspring. When the population is structured into groups, the covariance term can itself be split into a within-group component and a between-group component, so a trait like altruism can be selected against within each group (non-altruists always do at least as well as altruists in the same group) while still being selected for between groups (groups with more altruists produce more offspring overall). This within/between decomposition is exactly the mathematical engine behind Sober and Wilson's trait-group version of multilevel selection theory in Unto Others. They present their theory as a response to Williams's 1966 critique of Hamilton's newly published kin-selection theory, and argue that once selection is allowed to act at the group level as well as the individual level, altruism can spread even in the absence of the genetic relatedness that Hamilton's rule requires.

(c) The equivalence thesis holds that, because Hamilton's rule can itself be derived as a special case of the Price equation's covariance term, gene selectionism and multilevel selection are not rival empirical hypotheses at all but merely two different statistical partitions of one and the same evolutionary process. Asking which level "really" does the selecting is therefore asking a question that the mathematics has already dissolved. I find this fundamentally correct: once two frameworks have been shown to be interchangeable ways of carving up the same causal process, with no difference in what either one predicts, debating which is the "true" unit of selection becomes a merely verbal dispute rather than a further substantive question — in much the same way that it would be pointless to argue over whether a piece of land is "really" three adjoining fields or one large field with two internal fences.
