I teach a graduate seminar on theories of distributive justice, and this term I am also serving on my department's graduate fellowship committee, which administers a small discretionary hardship fund for students facing costs their stipend was never designed to cover. At our last meeting the committee had two requests in front of it and only enough money to fund one in full, and two of my colleagues on the committee, Mara and Devesh, found themselves in a back-and-forth over how to choose between them that I think tracks the "equality of what?" debate far more closely than either of them realized at the time. Elena has a mobility impairment and needs a particular powered wheelchair together with a paid aide to reach her offsite fieldwork site; without the money she can still get there, but only by a much slower and more exhausting route that eats substantially into her research time. Jonah, who grew up with considerably more material comfort than most of his cohort, reports that he genuinely cannot get any dissertation work done in the shared, noisy apartment his stipend affords him, and is requesting a supplement covering the difference between that rent and the rent on a single-occupancy apartment.

Here, as best I can reconstruct it, is how the exchange went. (1) Mara opened by proposing that the committee simply split the money evenly between the two requests, on the grounds that it is not the committee's place to rank whose need is more legitimate than whose. (2) Devesh objected that an even split is not neutral at all: Elena is not asking to be made better off than her peers, only for the extra resources needed to reach the same capability to do fieldwork that everyone else already has for free, so an even split would in fact leave her worse positioned than the rest of the cohort. (3) Mara conceded the point about Elena, but pressed Devesh on where his own principle stops: if the standard is "give people whatever it takes to reach the same real opportunity to do the work," then Jonah's capacity to write is also, on his own testimony, impaired relative to his peers, just for a different kind of reason. (4) Devesh replied that Elena's extra cost is simply a fact about her, not the product of anything she chose or could revise, whereas Jonah's difficulty traces back to a preference formed by his upbringing, one he has at least some capacity to adapt to, and that funding it would mean the fund simply rewards whoever is least willing to adjust. (5) Mara answered that Jonah did not choose his upbringing any more than Elena chose her impairment, so "he could adapt" is carrying the entire weight of Devesh's distinction, and she did not see what entitled the committee to treat one unchosen fact about a person as a need and the other as a mere preference.

Could you work through moves (1) through (5) and say who has the better of the argument at each step, and why? I am especially interested in which existing positions in the equality-of-what literature each move implicitly relies on, since nobody at the actual meeting cited anyone by name. I would also like your own view on the deeper question the exchange raises: is there a principled way to rescue Devesh's distinction in (4) from Mara's challenge in (5), or is the committee genuinely stuck without a non-arbitrary line between the two cases? Please close with a one-line verdict in the form \overall{Mara} or \overall{Devesh}, indicating whose overall position comes closer to being right, even though I don't think either of them gets everything correct.
