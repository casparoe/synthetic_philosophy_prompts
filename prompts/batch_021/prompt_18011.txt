I write about the history of ideas, and I'm drafting a column around a detail that surprises most readers: the same John Stuart Mill remembered for On Liberty spent his final two decades edging toward socialism. The shift is visible in the more sympathetic treatment of cooperative production he wrote into the Principles of Political Economy in 1852, and even more so in the unfinished Chapters on Socialism, published in the Fortnightly Review after his death. What he ultimately favored was not state ownership but a gradual, voluntary move toward worker-owned cooperatives, tested in open competition against ordinary capitalist firms rather than imposed by revolution or government fiat — and he remained wary that a fully collectivized system might, given human character as currently formed, blunt incentive and flatten the individuality he prized. I want to put that late-Mill position to work on something he could not have foreseen: the recent, small wave of driver- and courier-owned cooperative apps built explicitly to compete with Uber, Lyft, and DoorDash. Reasoning from the specific grounds on which he preferred gradual, plural, market-tested change over both revolutionary communism and the capitalism of his own day, tell me how he would likely judge this strategy, and where his own logic would lead him to predict it succeeds or fails against venture-funded incumbents.
