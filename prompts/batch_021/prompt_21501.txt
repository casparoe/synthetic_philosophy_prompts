Been chewing on Pascal's Wager a lot lately -- not the pop version ('just believe, whats the harm') but the actual expected-value logic underneath it: if there's even a tiny chance God exists and rewards belief with infinite bliss, the expected value of believing beats any finite cost, so a rational person should believe. Around the same time I went down a rabbit hole on the St Petersburg paradox, where a coin-flip game has infinite expected value on paper but basically nobody would rationally pay more then like $20-30 to play -- which is apparently why Daniel Bernoulli had to invent this whole idea that money and 'utility' aren't the same thing, just to explain the gap. It hit me that Pascal's wager is running the exact same infinite-expected-value move that the St Petersburg game shows can go totally haywire -- except Pascal died decades before anyone had even written that problem down, let alone solved it. So if you could somehow sit him down and walk him through the paradox and Bernoulli's fix, do you think he'd recognize that his own wager has the same problem? Would he have a way to patch the wager, or would he argue his case is genuinely different somehow? Actually reason it through the way he might, not just hand me the standard list of objections to the wager that I could've looked up myself.
