I got roped into serving on my university's academic integrity board this year (I'm just a staff member, no philosophy background or anything, though I did fall down a blog rabbit hole a while back about this idea called "common knowledge" and it's honestly never really left my head since). We've got a real case in front of us now where I think that idea actually applies, and I need to walk into Thursday's meeting with an actual recommendation, not just a gut feeling.

Here's what happened: our intro stats final, like 300-some students, got compromised when someone posted a photo of the answer key to a Discord server about an hour before the last of three staggered exam sessions closed. Server logs suggest maybe a third of the class saw it, but there's basically no clean way to prove who actually used it versus who saw it and ignored it, sorting that out person by person would take a month of hearings on its own. Meanwhile it's become the worst kept secret on campus -- its all over the group chats, the whole class has basically heard by now, and alot of us on the board privately suspected the scale of it even before the logs confirmed anything. Half the board wants to quietly investigate the handful of provable cases and never put out anything official about how big the leak was, on the theory that "everyone already basically knows, so an official statement doesn't tell anyone anything they don't already believe." The other half thinks that's missing something -- that a rumor everyone privately half-believes isn't the same as the university actually confirming it, and that the confirming part is specifically what would let the class do something collectively, like demand the exam get voided for everyone, instead of every student individually assuming nobody else would back them up. I mentioned this to a friend doing a CS PhD and he said something about "the two generals problem" and how everyone knowing something isn't the same as it being common knowledge, but he lost me before he could explain why that would actually matter here.

Could you write the actual memo I'd bring to the board? I need a real recommendation -- confirm the scale publicly, keep it quiet and case by case, or some third option -- with a real timeline attached, since final grades are due in three weeks. And I need it to actually engage with the "it's not new information so it shouldn't matter" side rather than just brushing past it, because that's what half the room believes and I won't get anywhere if I can't respond to it directly.
