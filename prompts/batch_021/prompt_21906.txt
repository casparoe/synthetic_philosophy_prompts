So I'm taking a seminar on free will and moral responsibility this term, and there's something about "Strawson's" position that's bugging me and that nobody in my study group could clear up either. Early in the course we covered the Basic Argument: the idea that to be truly or ultimately responsible for what you do, you'd have to be truly responsible for the character and motives that produced the act, but you could only be responsible for that character by having shaped it yourself, which requires already having some earlier character to do the shaping with, so you get a vicious regress -- and Strawson's conclusion is that "ultimate" moral responsibility is impossible, full stop, regardless of whether determinism or indeterminism turns out to be true. Pretty bleak, skeptical stuff.

Then a few weeks later we get assigned "Freedom and Resentment," and Strawson's argument there is the opposite mood entirely: moral responsibility is grounded in ordinary reactive attitudes like resentment, gratitude, and indignation, these attitudes don't really depend on whether determinism is true, and demanding some further "external" rational vindication of the whole practice (the way the optimists, like Schlick, wanted to justify it purely through social utility) is basically an intellectualist mistake. That reads to me as a defense of moral responsibility against the skeptic, not a proof that its impossible! I asked two different TAs and got two differnt answers, one said he must've revised his view later in life, the other just shrugged it off. Is there some real tension here that people who work on this have had to sort out, or is one of my summaries just off somewhere? I want to actually understand how the account holds together before I write my term paper on "Strawson on responsibility."
