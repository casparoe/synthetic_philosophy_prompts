I've been working on a piece about why AI systems—old symbolic ones, and arguably today's LLMs too—still trip up on plain old common sense, and I keep running into this term "the frame problem" that seems to mean two pretty different things depending on who's talking. Among the classical-AI crowd it's a fairly narrow, technical headache: in a logic-based planner, how do you specify everything that doesn't change when an action happens, without writing a seperate "frame axiom" for every single fact/action pair? My understanding is that this version is basically considered tamed by now — tricks like STRIPS-style add/delete lists, or Reiter's "successor state axioms" (I think that's the right name?), let you sidestep the combinatorial explosion rather than solve it head-on, but either way nobody seems to lose sleep over it anymore.

Then you get to philosophers like Dennett and Fodor and suddenly "the frame problem" balloons into something way bigger and much less obviously handled: how does any finite mind figure out what's relevant to a given decision or belief update, out of a practically unbounded space of things that could in principle matter, without explicitly checking all of them first? Dennett has that fable about a robot sent to rescue its own battery from a room with a time bomb on a wagon, where each successive redesign either misses an obvious side effect or gets blown up while endlessly deriving harmless side-implications (I want to say the robots were called R1, R1D1 and R2D1 — some Star Wars joke — but don't quote me on that). And I recall Fodor leaning on more or less the same worry to argue that "central," non-modular cognition — the holistic kind where anything could in principle bear on anything else — might never get a proper computational theory, unlike nice encapsulated modules like vision.

What I can't get straight for the piece is how these two "frame problems" actually relate to each other. Is the philosophical version just the logical version rescaled up to open-ended real-world reasoning, so that Reiter et al. only ever solved a toy special case and the actually hard problem was the relevance one all along? Or are they honestly different problems that got stuck with the same name by historical accident, in which case computer scientists cracking the narrow one tells us basically nothing about the broad one? And, for what it's worth given your answer to that — does the fact that LLMs seem to muddle through everyday reasoning without any explicit frame-axiom machinery count as real progress on the deep version of the problem, or is it beside the point?
