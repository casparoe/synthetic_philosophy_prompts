So I'm working on a piece about probability puzzles that make otherwise very rational people go around in circles -- Sleeping Beauty, the two-envelopes thing, that kind of stuff -- and a game theorist I talked to for background kept bringing up an older, less famous cousin of these called the "absent-minded driver" problem, from a Piccione and Rubinstein paper from the 90s. The setup, roughly: a driver is on a highway and needs to take the second of two exits to get home, but the exits look completely identical and, being "absent-minded," she has zero memory of whether she's already driven past the first one whenever she arrives at one that looks like this. If she gets off at the first exit she ends up somewhere actively bad (payoff 0); if she drives right past it and gets off at the second she gets the best outcome (payoff 4); and if she loses track completely and sails past both, she ends up somewhere just mediocre (payoff 1) -- worse than nailing the second exit, but better than stopping too soon. Because the two exits are literally indistinguishable to her in the moment, whatever rule she uses for "keep driving vs. get off" has to be the exact same rule both times -- she can't condition on which one she's at, because she doesn't know.

Apparently this one little example caused a real mess in decision theory -- I think it even spawned a small pile of replies in the same journal issue. One camp works out the "planning-optimal" strategy, i.e. whatever a planner would pre-commit the driver to before she even starts driving, so as to maximize her expected payoff. Another camp says that's not good enough, and that you have to instead reason the way the driver herself does, mid-drive, at the moment she's actually sitting at an intersection -- "action-optimal" reasoning -- and apparently these two ways of being "rational" don't always agree once you drop the assumption that agents remember everything perfectly, which is sort of smuggled into ordinary game theory without anyone flagging it. Underneath all of this is a genuinely weird sub-fight about what probability, if any, the driver should assign to "I'm at the first intersection right now" -- naive symmetry says 1/2, but I've also seen people argue for a different number once you factor in that how often she even reaches the second intersection depends on the very strategy she's using, which is a wrinkle Sleeping Beauty doesn't have (there the coin's bias is just fixed by nature, its not something anyone chose).

I've read a few explainers now and still can't tell if there's an agreed "official" answer here, or if its genuinely contested among people who do this for a living. Could you write me an essay-length answer I can use as background -- weigh the real considerations on each side (planning- vs. action-optimality, and whether/how to assign a probability at the intersection) -- but actually commit to a verdict rather than just laying out "here are two internally coherent positions" and stopping there. A conditional verdict is fine, like "action-optimality is right once you fix the belief rule this way, and the planning-optimal answer only looks different because it's smuggling in a different, less defensible notion of rationality" -- I just need you to actually take a side, the way I'd have to for the "so who's right" paragraph of an actual article.
