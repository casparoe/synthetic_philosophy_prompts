I'm a philosophy PhD student working on philosophy of mind, and this term I'm TAing a graduate seminar on the history and philosophy of AI. Last session we worked through Turing's 1950 "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" line by line, including the objection almost everyone in the room wanted to skip past: the last of the nine "Contrary Views on the Main Question," which Turing labels "(9) The Argument from Extra-Sensory Perception." It isn't obviously a throwaway aside — he calls the statistical case for telepathy "overwhelming" and says the objection itself is "quite a strong one." He then works through a specific example: in the imitation game, a man who is a good telepathic receiver correctly names the suit of a hidden card 130 times out of 400, while a machine guessing at random manages only 104 (chance alone predicts 100), so the interrogator can tell them apart. He adds a further complication — if the machine relies on an internal random-number generator to choose its answers, the interrogator's own psychokinesis might bias that generator too — and proposes to resolve the whole difficulty by housing both competitors in a "telepathy-proof room," on the model, by his own analogy, of an eavesdropper "listening with his ear to the wall" who could simply be walled off.

What derailed our seminar is that the fix looks narrower than the problem Turing sets up for himself. He explicitly takes seriously all four items on his own list — telepathy, clairvoyance, precognition, and psychokinesis — and, in the same breath as the card example, concedes that clairvoyance could let the interrogator identify the machine "without any questioning" and that psychokinesis could reach inside the machine and bias its random-number generator. Neither possibility is obviously blocked by a room that is merely telepathy-proof. Arguably it's worse than that: the parapsychology Turing is leaning on for his "overwhelming" evidence, J. B. Rhine's Duke laboratory, had built part of its case for the reality of psi precisely on the claim that scores held up undiminished across distance and physical screening — which is what was supposed to show psi wasn't just an ordinary, blockable physical signal. So Turing's patch seems to model "thought transference" on something that leaks through walls the way sound does, which is close to the picture his own source evidence was supposed to rule out.

Write an essay addressing whether this is a genuine, damaging gap in Turing's handling of the ESP objection, or whether it can be repaired or explained away on his behalf, and say what, if anything, it shows about the imitation game as he specifies it in this paper. Weigh the best case for reading the "telepathy-proof room" charitably — for instance, as a stipulative barrier against any anomalous channel whatsoever, rather than a literal piece of shielding — against the best case for thinking the patch is question-begging, or worse, given what Turing has just conceded about clairvoyance and psychokinesis a few sentences earlier. Don't settle for "it's complicated": end with an explicit verdict on whether the gap is real and consequential, real but inconsequential, or not really a gap at all, and defend your answer.
