And here is the student's answer:

"Aristotle's discussion of exchange in Nicomachean Ethics V.5 begins from the puzzle of how goods as different as a house and a pair of shoes can be exchanged justly, given that on the face of it they share nothing in common. His solution is that money (nomisma) is introduced as a conventional measure that renders such goods commensurable for the purposes of exchange, even though they are not commensurable 'in truth.' He signals the conventional character of money through its very name, which he connects to nomos (custom or law) rather than physis (nature): money has value because we agree that it does, and that agreement could in principle be revoked. What ultimately grounds the comparison between the house and the shoes, however, is the labor that goes into producing each of them; money simply provides a common, publicly recognized unit in which these different quantities of labor can be expressed and compared, which is exactly the point Marx takes up approvingly in Capital when he credits Aristotle with being the first thinker to see that commodity exchange presupposes some underlying equality between the things exchanged.

This account connects naturally to the Politics, where Aristotle distinguishes household management (oikonomia) from the specialized art of wealth-getting (chrematistike). Since money is a mere token with no natural power to produce anything by itself, Aristotle treats its very introduction into exchange as already a step away from nature: as soon as goods are traded for currency rather than bartered directly for other goods, the transaction belongs to the unnatural art of chrematistike rather than to the household's own natural economy of self-sufficiency. Usury is simply the point at which this same logic is pushed to its extreme. Because the Greek word for interest, tokos, also means 'offspring,' Aristotle's objection trades on the idea that money, unlike livestock, has no business 'breeding': interest is wealth generated from currency itself rather than from a genuine exchange of goods, which is why he calls it the most unnatural mode of acquisition of all."
