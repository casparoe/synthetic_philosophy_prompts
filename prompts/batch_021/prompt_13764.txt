So my company just expanded our interview panel from 3 people to 7, and the reasoning HR gave in the all-hands basically amounted to the Condorcet jury theorem, even though nobody actually called it that: 'more independent opinions in the room, more likely we land on the right candidate.' I've actually been added to the new panel starting next month, so this stopped being some abstract thing for me pretty fast. Two people on my team, Marcus and Elena, argued about whether the logic even holds up through basically an entire happy hour, and I honestly can't tell who won.

(1) Marcus said the math is the math: if each interviewer is even a little better than a coin flip at picking the right candidate, and they're voting independently, the odds of the group getting it right go up as you add people, heading toward certainty. So 7 beats 3.
(2) Elena said that's the idealized version -- our panel sits in the same room, hears the answers in the same order, and everyone anchors on whoever senior speaks first, so the votes aren't actually independent, and correlated mistakes don't cancel out the way independent ones do.
(3) Marcus said even so, more people in the room can't make things worse, its just more scrutiny.
(4) Elena said it can: interviews are famously weak predictors of job performance, and if the average interviewer here is below coin-flip accuracy on this particular judgment, Condorcet's theorem runs in reverse -- a bigger, correlated panel of mediocre judges just ends up more confidently wrong, not less.
(5) Marcus said fine, then have everyone submit ratings independently before any discussion happens -- that fixes the correlation problem, so we're back to 7 beating 3.
(6) Elena said that fixes correlation, maybe, but does nothing for competence, and anyway 'best candidate out of five' isn't the single yes-or-no question the theorem was actually built for in the first place, so leaning on Condorcet here might be a category error regardless.
