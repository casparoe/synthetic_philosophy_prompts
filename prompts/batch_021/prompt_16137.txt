My closest friend from college, Marcus, is getting married in June, and he's asked me to give the toast because I'm "the writer" of the friend group. Problem is, Marcus has been called "the Don Giovanni" by all of us since sophomore year (he was, by his own admission, a genuinely terrible boyfriend for most of his twenties), and it's such an established bit at this point that people are already asking whether it'll make an appearance in the speech. It kind of has to.

Here's my snag. I was reading up on Mozart's opera a while back, actually for a minor character in the novel I'm writing, and got stuck on Bernard Williams's essays on it (from that posthumous collection, On Opera, if im remembering the title right). Kierkegaard's the reading everyone measures themselves against -- Giovanni as pure erotic appetite, an idea rather than a person, incapable of learning anything because there's no inside there to change -- but Williams's angle is a bit different: he's less interested in what Giovanni supposedly represents than in the fact that the opera just won't resolve whether the ending is a real moral order reasserting itself or theatre being theatrical, comedy and damnation sitting right next to each other with no seam. Either way, there's no version where Giovanni reforms. The statue gives him one last chance to repent with actual hellfire on the table, and he refuses, flatly, and gets dragged down exactly as he was at curtain-up. So the obvious toast, "our own Don Giovanni finally got his comeuppance, the good kind, via true love," isn't a story this particular opera knows how to tell.

There will be exactly four or five people at this wedding (Marcus's aunt sang Donna Elvira in some regional production decades ago, and my friend Priya has a whole music degree) who will feel that wrongness immediately if I just wave it away -- and then a room full of everyone else who just wants something warm and funny about their friend. Could you write me the toast, three minutes or so out loud, that gets to use the nickname, doesn't pretend the opera's on board with the redemption story, and still lands as a love letter to Marcus instead of a philosophy tutorial?
