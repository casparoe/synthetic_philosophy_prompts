There's this debate in epistemology about "epistemic akrasia" -- being stuck with something like "P, but my evidence doesn't actually support P." The standard view (the enkratic principle) says that combo is just irrational, basically a doxastic cousin of Moore's paradox. But there's a whole family of cases involving misleading higher-order evidence that make trouble for this: say you carefully work out a logic puzzle and feel great about your answer, then a doctor you have every reason to trust tells you the medication you took this morning wrecks reasoning ability in 9 out of 10 people who take it, and there's no way for you to tell if your the lucky one. It really seems rational to now doubt your answer, even stipulating that your original reasoning was flawless and the pill did nothing. Christensen-ish "conciliationist" views kind of bite the bullet here and say fine, rational belief can look akratic. Others push back hard -- I think Horowitz has a well known paper arguing akratic states are always self-undermining once you trace through the commitments, and Weisberg's "Could've Thought Otherwise" is in a similar anti-"level-splitting" spirit. Write an essay that actually engages with the strongest version of both sides and then defends an actual verdict, not just "reasonable people disagree" -- can epistemic akrasia ever be rational, and if so under what conditions? Something conditional like "only when X" totally counts as an answer, just make sure you justify it.
