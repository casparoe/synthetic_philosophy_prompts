And here's my classmate's answer:

Falsifiability and corroboration aren't enough on their own to give Popper what he wants from a theory of scientific progress. Falsifiability just tells you a theory is testable in principle, and corroboration only tells you how well a theory has survived testing so far -- it's explicitly not meant to be read as evidence of truth, since a well-corroborated theory could still be refuted tomorrow. But Popper still wants to say science progresses, that Einstein's theory is genuinely closer to the truth than Newton's, even though on his own fallibilist principles neither one can ever be verified. Verisimilitude is supposed to cash out "closer to the truth" without smuggling in the verificationism Popper rejects.

The qualitative definition works with a theory's content, the class of statements that logically follow from it, split into truth-content (the true consequences) and falsity-content (the false ones). A theory t2 has more verisimilitude than a rival t1 when t2's truth-content includes t1's and t2's falsity-content is included in t1's, with at least one of those inclusions proper -- so t2 says everything true t1 says, and nothing false that t1 doesn't already say.

Popper proved in 1974 that this only actually discriminates between theories when the more verisimilar one turns out to be true. Take two false theories, t1 and t2, where t2 has "excess content" over t1 (t2 entails everything t1 does, plus more). It turns out t2's falsity-content then exceeds t1's right along with its truth-content -- you cannot pick up the extra truths without also picking up extra falsehoods. So no false theory can ever come out more verisimilar than any other false theory on this definition. That's fatal, since Popper thinks every theory we actually have, including our best current ones, is presumably false, so the one comparison he needed verisimilitude to make -- ranking our false theories against each other -- is exactly the comparison it can't make. It's the same reason he counts a high degree of corroboration as a mark of high verisimilitude: a theory that keeps surviving severe tests is thereby shown to have more truth-content than its less-tested rivals. Popper conceded the formal definition doesn't work but kept insisting some serviceable notion of verisimilitude must be recoverable; Niiniluoto and Oddie later tried to rebuild it using similarity between states of affairs rather than the subset relation on content classes.
