I'm working on a piece about the philosophical afterlife of Kurt Gödel's modal ontological argument, and I keep running into the "modal collapse" objection without finding an explanation that goes beyond simply asserting it. As I understand the argument, Gödel defines a being as "God-like" if it possesses every "positive" property, stipulates that necessary existence is itself one such positive property, and uses the notion of an "essence" — a property that necessarily entails all of a thing's other properties — to bridge the gap from the mere possibility of a God-like being to its necessary existence. Critics, Jordan Howard Sobel foremost among them, argue that this same apparatus proves far more than intended: that every actually true proposition turns out to be a necessary truth, erasing any distinction between the actual and the merely possible. I can follow why that conclusion would be devastating, but not how it actually falls out of the axioms. Could you reconstruct, in intuitive rather than purely symbolic terms, exactly which definitions or moves are doing the work that forces this collapse?
