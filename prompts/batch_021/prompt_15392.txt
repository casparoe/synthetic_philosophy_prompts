For my seminar on the philosophical foundations of probability, we've spent the last two weeks on the reference class problem: any individual case falls under indefinitely many reference classes, and different classes can assign that same case very different probabilities, yet there seems to be no principled, non-arbitrary way of privileging one class over the rest. Even Reichenbach's classic fix — using the narrowest class for which reliable statistics exist — arguably just relocates the arbitrariness rather than removing it, as later critics such as Hájek have argued. Everyone in the seminar keeps reaching for the same two illustrations, actuarial life tables and the Doomsday Argument, so for this week's response paper I'd like to offer something different. Could you construct an original example, from a domain of your choosing other than those two, in which at least two equally well-motivated reference classes yield substantially different probabilities for the same individual case, and explain exactly why no non-arbitrary rule is available for adjudicating between them?
