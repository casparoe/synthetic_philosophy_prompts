For the past year, my co-founder and I ran our startup on nothing more than a verbal agreement to "figure it out together," and we are now, somewhat belatedly, drafting a formal founders' agreement. That process has surfaced a real disagreement between us: I think our earlier handshake already bound us to something, while he insists nothing was binding until there was a signature. A friend finishing a philosophy PhD tells me this is, in essence, the debate that runs through the literature on collective intentionality and joint action, and I have been reading around it ever since. I co-host a small podcast on the concepts that quietly structure everyday cooperation, and next month's guest is a social ontologist who defends a strong "joint commitment" account of shared agency, in the tradition of plural-subject theories: the view that when two or more people jointly commit to some course of action, the commitment itself — with no contract, institution, or antecedent convention required — generates real obligations and entitlements running between the parties, of a kind that gives each of them genuine standing to rebuke the other for backing out.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that push on the substance of this view rather than inviting a restatement of it, sequenced so that the normative claims become progressively harder for her to soften. I would like at least one question that forces her to confront the "obligation from nothing" feature of the theory with an unflattering case — two people who jointly commit to carrying out a theft together seem, on her account, to thereby acquire genuine obligations and entitlements toward one another, so does she accept that consequence, and if so, why is "obligation" still the right word rather than just a description of mutual expectation? I would also like one question on the apparent circularity in explaining joint commitment through each party's open expression of willingness plus common knowledge of that willingness — doesn't this already presuppose the rich shared communicative background the theory is supposed to be explaining from the ground up? — and one that asks her to extend the account to large, loosely structured groups, such as a leaderless protest movement or a nation's citizenry, where nobody plausibly holds a pairwise joint commitment with everybody else, without either trivializing the notion or quietly resorting to fiction. For each of these three, add a sharp follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and close the set with a question asking what — short of a signed contract — could possibly count as evidence that two people who behave exactly as if they were jointly committed in fact are not.
