I'm a retired combustion chemist -- fourty-odd years of my career spent measuring how flames actually propagate through fuels -- so you can imagine my double-take when the reading for the philosophy seminar I've been auditing turned out to be al-Ghazali arguing that fire doesn't, strictly speaking, burn cotton at all. I'm not enrolled for credit, but the instructor lets auditors sit in on her peer-grading exercise: before she marks the midterms herself, each of us gets handed one anonymized classmate's answer to grade cold, and she compares notes with everyone afterward. I got assigned the essay question on al-Ghazali's Seventeenth Discussion, and honestly, after forty years of grading lab reports I'm not at all confident my eye for what counts as rigorous transfers over to this stuff.

Here's the question as she set it: "In the Seventeenth Discussion of the Tahafut al-Falasifa, al-Ghazali attacks the philosophers' claim that fire burns cotton by natural necessity. (a) Reconstruct his argument for why we cannot know a necessary connection between the two. (b) Explain the positive account of causation he offers in its place, and say whether it amounts to full occasionalism or something weaker. (c) Summarize how Ibn Rushd responds to this in the Tahafut al-Tahafut, and assess whether his response succeeds."

And here's the answer I was handed to grade (name stripped off, obviously):

(a) Al-Ghazali is attacking the philosophers' claim, following Aristotle, that when fire touches a suitable piece of cotton, burning follows of necessity, simply because burning is fire's nature. He points out that all we ever actually observe is that burning follows contact -- a constant conjunction of two events -- never a necessary tie binding them. Fire and the cotton's burning are two distinct realities, and neither one's existence or non-existence can be deduced from the other's; if it could, denying one while affirming the other would be a contradiction, and it isn't. So the philosophers have quietly upgraded "this is what always happens" into "this is what must happen," and reason alone can't license that upgrade. The stakes are theological as much as logical: if the tie really were necessary, then something like Ibrahim being unburned in the fire in the Qur'an couldn't be a miracle, it would be a flat impossibility, and Ghazali isn't willing to grant the philosophers that.

(b) In place of necessity, Ghazali's own account is occasionalism, full stop: fire has no causal power of its own at all, and the only genuine agent in the burning is God, who creates the burning in the cotton directly (or delegates it to angels) at the moment the two touch, doing so with enough regularity that we mistake the pattern for a law. What we call "fire causing burning" is really just God's habitual practice of creating burning on that occasion. This is worth dwelling on because it isn't a hedge -- Ghazali isn't offering occasionalism as one option among others he's still open to; he needs the fully strong version, since anything less would leave some real power in the fire, which is exactly what he's argued we have no rational grounds for. It's also worth flagging how close this is to Hume's later argument against necessary connection in the Enquiry -- close enough that it's well established Hume absorbed it indirectly, since Ibn Rushd's rebuttal, which lays out Ghazali's argument in detail before attacking it, had already been circulating in Latin translation.

(c) Ibn Rushd rejects this on essentialist grounds: a thing's causal powers are part of what makes it the kind of thing it is, so a fire that can't burn isn't really fire -- you've defined the nature out of the object while keeping the name. He also presses a more practical point: if there's no necessary link between causes and effects anywhere in nature, demonstrative science becomes impossible, since demonstration requires necessary premises, and no one, Ghazali included, actually lives as though causal regularities might dissolve at any moment. Importantly, Ibn Rushd isn't trying to push God out of the picture; he takes God to be the ultimate cause behind the whole natural order, he just denies that this requires treating the secondary, natural causes in between as illusions. I think this succeeds as a description of what scientific practice presupposes, but it's less clear it actually answers Ghazali, who never denied that nature is regular -- only that regularity, however longstanding, adds up to necessity. Ibn Rushd asserts the natures are really there; he doesn't show how observation could ever tell us that.
