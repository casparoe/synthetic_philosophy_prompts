At work we joke that half of software engineering is "bootstrapping" something out of nothing — a compiler that has to compile itself, a test suite with no independent way of being tested, a monitoring system whose own uptime nobody monitors. A few weeks ago, some idle reading online (Wikipedia, a couple of Stanford Encyclopedia articles on skepticism and justification, a few blog posts) led me to a philosophical puzzle with essentially the same name and structure: the "bootstrapping problem," or "problem of easy knowledge" (I believe the names most associated with it are Stewart Cohen and Jonathan Vogel). As I understand the basic case, if perception counts as a "basic" source of justified belief — meaning it can justify something like "that table is red" without your first needing independent grounds for trusting your eyes — then you seem to be able to reason as follows: each time it looks to you like something has a certain color, you also come to believe it has that color; string enough of these matches together and you can inductively conclude that your color vision is reliable, all without ever once checking your eyes against anything other than your eyes. That struck me as basically the epistemological version of Ken Thompson's "Reflections on Trusting Trust": you can get a self-hosting compiler to compile itself and produce a binary that passes every test you throw at it, but that tells you nothing about whether the compiler is trustworthy if the very thing doing the compiling — and thus shaping what "passing the test" even looks like — is the thing you were unsure about in the first place. A sufficiently compromised compiler can be built to keep hiding its own compromise indefinitely, no matter how many times you rebuild it with itself.

I'd like you to write a dialogue between two epistemologists who take this problem seriously and are equally sharp in defending their side — no strawmen. One should hold that this kind of bootstrapping is actually fine: if the source (perception, or the compiler) really is reliable, then every individual step in the reasoning used only justified beliefs, so nothing illegitimate happened, and the "that's too easy" reaction is just squeamishness about externalist justification in general rather than a specific defect in bootstrapping. The other should hold that some principle like "no independent check, no epistemic gain" is being violated, since the exact same reasoning would go through, and would feel just as compelling from the inside, even if the source were broken in a self-consistent way — a stuck gauge, a compromised compiler, a brain in a vat. Please have them genuinely engage with each other's strongest points rather than talk past one another, and have them try to work out, by the end, whether their disagreement reduces to a more familiar fault line (internalism versus externalism about justification, say) or whether it's something that fault line doesn't fully capture. At some point I'd like one of them to address the trusting-trust compiler case directly, either as support for their view or to explain why it's actually disanalogous to the perceptual case. It's fine if they don't reach agreement, but I'd rather the dialogue show one side gradually gaining or losing ground than have it settle into a polite stalemate.
