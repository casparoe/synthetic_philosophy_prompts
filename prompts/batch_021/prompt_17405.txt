My father died two weeks ago after thirty years teaching the children's class at our Jain temple, where anekantavada -- the idea that reality has many sides, and that no claim about it is simply true without specifying which angle it's true from -- was the thing he talked about most, at the temple and at our kitchen table. He could not stand it when people flattened this into "so nothing is really true, it's just your point of view, who's to judge": he would say that move was itself a flat, one-sided claim dressed up as open-mindedness, and "it depends" was, for him, the start of an argument, never the end of one. I am giving his eulogy at the temple hall next week, to a room that will be half people from his classes, who will notice immediately if I soften the philosophy into that kind of relativism, and half coworkers and in-laws who are hearing the word anekantavada for the first time this week. He was also stubborn, and slower to admit fault in himself than in anyone else, and I do not think he would want that quietly smoothed over just because it is a eulogy -- please write it so that it is honest about him in the many-sided way he actually practiced, comforting to people for whom all of this is brand new, and faithful enough that the people who sat in his classes will not wince.
