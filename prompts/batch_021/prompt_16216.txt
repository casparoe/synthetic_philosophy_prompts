I'm taking a graduate seminar this semester that sits at the intersection of machine learning and philosophy of mind, and this week we covered inverse reinforcement learning as a candidate method for figuring out what a person actually wants by watching what they do rather than by asking them. What's stuck with me isn't the algorithmic side but the identifiability problem underneath it. The toy version is almost too simple once you see it: any fixed sequence of actions is "explained" equally well by infinitely many reward functions, including the trivial one that assigns zero reward to everything, since an agent with no reward at all is trivially indifferent among all its own choices. The more unsettling version is what happens once you try to fix this by assuming the agent is only boundedly rational and jointly inferring a reward function together with a model of its own biases or planning limitations: it turns out you can construct pairs of (reward, bias) that are just as simple and fit the behavioral data just as well, yet disagree about what the agent actually values, sometimes attributing to it almost the opposite of what a more charitable pairing would say. I gather there's an actual impossibility-style result along these lines, I believe due to Armstrong and Mindermann, though I wouldn't swear to the exact citation, showing that no simplicity prior can break this symmetry on its own: you always end up smuggling in some further normative assumption about what a "natural" or "rational" agent looks like.

For an assignment in that same seminar we've been asked to produce a short piece of creative writing engaging with one of the term's technical ideas, and I'd like to use this one. Could you write me a short story that dramatizes this specific problem, rather than the more familiar plot where an AI simply misreads an instruction and disaster follows? I keep picturing something contained: a system trained on years of one person's recorded behavior is asked, in effect, to speak for that person's wishes once they can no longer speak for themselves, and two equally rigorous readings of the very same data support two incompatible pictures of what they wanted, with real consequences riding on which one is believed. That is just the setting I keep gravitating toward, though, so feel free to invent your own if a different scenario lets the underlying idea land more clearly.

I'd like it to run somewhere in the 900- to 1400-word range, and I would rather it not resolve into a clean reveal of which reading was "really" correct, since the whole point, as I understand it, is that the ambiguity is principled rather than a bug that more data or a cleverer model could eventually iron out. A title would be a nice touch as well.
