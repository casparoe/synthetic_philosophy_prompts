So I had this argument with my housemate at dinner last night that neither of us really won, and I keep turning it over. She's ABD in an econ program and was walking me through revealed preference theory - the Samuelson move where you stop asking what people "really" want and just define preference off of what they actually choose under their budget constraint, so "utility" becomes shorthand for whatever ordering rationalizes their choices. Fine, i got that part. But earlier in the night she'd also been defending soda taxes and opt-out retirement enrollment as good policy, and it seems to me like those two positions don't mix: if utility is just defined as whatever a person's choices reveal, then someone buying the soda or opting out of the 401k is, by construction, doing the thing that maximizes their utility, and a nudge that pushes them off that choice is - by the theory's own logic - making them worse off, full stop. She said I was collapsing a distinction that behavioral economists actually care a lot about, but got pulled away to put her kid to bed before she could finish explaining it.

I've read enough "coherent agents behave as if they have a utility function" stuff from the LW-sphere to think I'm at least gesturing at something real, but I want to know if I'm actually onto a live tension here or just failing to steelman her position and confusing two different things that both happen to be called "utility." Is there a principled way to hold revealed preference theory and paternalistic nudge policy at the same time, or does one of them have to go?
