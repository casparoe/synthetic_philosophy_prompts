I sit on my university's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. A neuroscience lab has applied to implant chronic recording electrodes in the vertical lobe of several octopuses to study memory consolidation -- survival surgery, weeks of solitary confinement, only experimental analgesia -- and although federal rules exclude invertebrates from oversight altogether, our own policy already extends review to cephalopods given what's known about their cognitive complexity. Nobody on the committee disputes the relevant behavioral and neurophysiological facts; the dispute is entirely over how much moral weight those facts warrant, since members hold genuinely different views about which theory of moral status is correct, and those theories range from ones granting octopuses near-primate-level protections to ones granting them almost none. One faction wants us to weight the decision by the committee's actual spread of credence across these rival theories and choose whichever disposition of the protocol comes out ahead in expectation; another objects that this lets a few members' modest credence in high-stakes theories about octopus sentience effectively dictate the outcome for everyone else. Draft the memo I will circulate before the vote: recommend a specific disposition of the protocol -- approve, reject, or approve with named modifications -- propose a decision rule the committee should use for similar cases going forward, and engage honestly with the worry that this kind of expected-choiceworthiness reasoning can be hijacked by a minority's credence in extreme theories, rather than simply dismissing it.
