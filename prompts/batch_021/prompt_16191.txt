I've been reporting on AI alignment research on and off for the past year, and at a small workshop on "scalable oversight" last month I watched two of the researchers there, Elena and Marcus, fall into an argument over coffee that I haven't been able to stop turning over since. The subject was "debate" -- the scheme, first floated by AI-safety researchers including Paul Christiano, in which two systems argue opposite sides of a question in front of a human judge who cannot verify the underlying claim herself. Their argument was about whether debate can really be trusted to surface the truth, rather than just whichever side argues better. Neither of them was performing for my benefit -- I don't think either one noticed I was still standing there by the end -- and I genuinely can't tell who came out ahead.

(1) Elena opened with the case for optimism: a judge who can't evaluate a claim directly can still recognize a rebuttal that lands, so a debater who is lying should eventually get cornered by an opponent whose only job is to locate and expose the single weak link in its argument -- meaning the judge can reliably side with the truth without ever assessing the substance herself. (2) Marcus answered with what he called the obfuscated argument problem: a sufficiently capable liar doesn't need one weak link, since the error can be spread across a thicket of individually plausible claims, so that whichever spot the honest debater singles out as "the flaw," the judge has no principled way to distinguish a genuine objection from a plausible-sounding but irrelevant one -- so being correct doesn't guarantee you have any accessible way to prove it within the time a judge can reasonably be expected to spend. (3) Elena replied that this is exactly what recursive cross-examination is meant to fix: if both debaters keep drilling into whichever sub-claim is disputed, any buried flaw eventually gets forced down into a piece small enough for the judge, or some delegate, to check directly -- so obfuscation is a problem the protocol is designed to dissolve, not a hard ceiling on it. (4) Marcus said that only relocates the problem, since the debater doing the decomposing is the very party you don't yet trust, so nothing stops a capable enough liar from decomposing dishonestly -- and he cited recent human-judge debate experiments in which judges' accuracy tracked how persuasive they found a debater almost as closely as it tracked who was actually correct, which he read as evidence that the fix doesn't obviously survive contact with real, non-ideal judges. (5) Elena's comeback was that the very same experiments cut the other way: when debaters were pushed to be more persuasive, judges also got more accurate rather than less, which she took to mean that, at today's gap between debater and judge capability, persuasiveness and truth are nowhere near as decoupled as Marcus's argument requires them to be. At that point someone came around with the coffee pot and the whole thing just dissolved.
