I spent nearly 40 years as a field ecologist before retiring, and these days I fill my time reading philosophy of science for fun. Model selection was a huge part of my old job -- always weighing whether a simpler model with fewer parameters was worth preferring over a messier one that fit the data slightly better -- so I got kind of excited when I found out mathematicians think they actually formalized that whole intuition rigorously, via Kolmogorov complexity and Solomonoff induction. The more I read though, the more suspicious I got. The invariance theorem supposedly shows the choice of universal Turing machine doesn't matter, only up to an additive constant, and people treat that as proof the whole framework gives you an objective, language-independent notion of simplicity. But that constant can apparently be arbitrarily large, way bigger then any pair of real theories we'd ever actually compare, and on top of that Kolmogorov complexity itself isn't even computable to begin with. So I dont see how any of this hands a working scientist (or Solomonoff's idealized induction machine) an actual answer about which of two real hypotheses is simpler, rather than just being a neat asymptotic result dressed up in intimidating math. Could you write me an essay working through whether Solomonoff induction and Kolmogorov complexity really vindicate Occam's razor as an objective principle, or whether they just smuggle the same old arbitrariness back in through the choice of machine? Weigh the strongest arguments on both sides, but don't leave it at "good points on both sides" -- commit to an actual verdict, even a qualified one like "yes, but only asymptotically" or "no, this is the wrong tool for the job."
