Had a bad outage at work last month, and the postmortem review turned into a surprisingly heated argument between two people on my team, Nadia and Chris, about what "explaining" an outage even means. I've got zero philosophy background so I can't referee this myself.

The postmortem doc just narrates the incident: deploy went out at 14:03, that made the cache go cold, the cold cache spiked DB read load, DB connections got exhausted by 14:09, and the API started timing out by 14:11.

1. Chris said the doc doesn't actually explain the outage, just describes it. A real explanation needs some general principal behind it -- like "pools exhaust whenever load crosses X under condition Y" -- otherwise we only know what happened this once, not why, and have no basis for predicting a repeat.

2. Nadia said the narrative itself is the explanation. We nailed the actual causal chain, and thats enough to go fix the real problem. Demanding a "general law" before you'll call something explained, she said, imports a physics standard into a domain -- distributed systems, like history -- that mostly runs on messy regularities, not clean laws.

3. Chris replied that Nadia is smuggling a law in anyway: how did we even decide the cold cache was "the cause" and not, say, that it happened on a Tuesday? Picking it out of every true fact about that afternoon already assumes some generalization like "cache misses drive up DB load."

4. Nadia's comeback: sure, background know-how helps you notice what's salient, but that's not the same as the explanation reducing to a law. She can know the DB spike caused the timeouts without being able to state any actually-true general law about it -- any "law" she wrote down would be so full of exceptions it's basically just a rule of thumb in a lab coat. And if that counts as a law, every explanation is a covering-law explanation, so the objection is empty.
