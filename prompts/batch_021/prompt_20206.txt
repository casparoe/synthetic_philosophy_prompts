I've been rereading Eliezer Yudkowsky's metaethics sequence on LessWrong -- especially "The Meaning of Right" and "Math is Subjunctively Objective" -- after a disagreement at our local rationalist meetup over whether he actually dissolves the realism/anti-realism debate or just relabels one side of it. As I understand the argument, he wants "right" to work the way Kripke and Putnam say natural-kind terms like "water" work: it rigidly designates one specific, staggeringly complicated computation over states of the world -- roughly, whatever our full moral psychology would output on reflection -- with the referent fixed by the actual causal history of how humans came to use the word, rather than by any a priori definition. Because the referent is fixed once and for all, he argues, calling it "arbitrary" is a category error, on a par with calling arithmetic arbitrary because it happens to be carried out by neurons instead of Platonic forms: two plus two would still equal four even if brains had evolved differently. On this basis he claims that moral disagreement is disagreement about the output of one specific, non-negotiable computation rather than mere clashing preference, and that the traditional realist/anti-realist dispute was confused from the outset.

Two people at the meetup found this persuasive; I didn't, but under questioning I couldn't get past hand-waving about him "just moving the arbitrariness back a step." Could you write the strongest available argument for the claim that Yudkowsky's position does not actually escape the realism/anti-realism dilemma, but amounts to a relativism indexed to contingent human psychology, dressed in realist-sounding vocabulary borrowed from a theory of reference designed for natural kinds rather than values? I'd like the argument to engage his rigid-designation move on its own terms rather than simply insisting that morality "obviously" isn't like water, to address whether this is ultimately a restatement of naturalism that reinherits standard problems like the open-question argument, and to say whether his own "Three Worlds Collide" -- in which humans, Babyeaters, and Superhappies each extrapolate to irreconcilably different values -- ends up undercutting his resolution rather than merely illustrating it.
