A friend and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about the passage in the Zhuangzi where Huizi comes to offer condolences after Zhuangzi's wife dies and finds him sitting with his legs sprawled out, drumming on a basin and singing instead of weeping. We've been working through the Outer Chapters together this year, and my friend reads Zhuangzi's explanation — that to wail over it would show he didn't understand fate, since life and death are just successive phases of one transformation, no more worth grieving than one season turning into the next — as genuine equanimity. I think it sits oddly against the other anecdote a little later in the same chapter, where he prods a skull by the roadside with his carriage whip, later dreams that it visits him boasting of the freedoms of being dead, and has it flatly refuse his offer to be restored to life, flesh, and family. Neither of us could settle whether these are the same insight told twice or two attitudes toward death that don't quite square with each other, and by the time the check came we'd more or less agreed to disagree.

I'd like to send my friend something that makes light of the whole debate. Could you write a short joke about Zhuangzi's view of death — a line or two is fine, it doesn't need to be a shaggy-dog story? It should be specific enough that someone who has actually read these passages would recognize what it's riffing on — the recycling of forms, the skull's contentment, the refusal to treat dying as a loss, or the friction between the two scenes above — rather than a generic "inscrutable ancient sage" joke that would work just as well for Confucius or Lao Tzu. And it's fine, maybe even better, if it's dry and deadpan rather than jaunty — that seems truer to the material than anything too cheerful.
