Two Fridays ago my car died on a mountain pass, well after dark, far enough from anywhere that walking out was not a realistic option, and my phone had no signal at all. I sat there for close to three hours before another vehicle appeared, driven by a local rancher who, as he told me afterward, does not normally stop for strangers on that stretch of road at night, having twice been drawn into difficult situations that way before. He was willing to drive me the ninety minutes to the nearest town with a working ATM, but only once I had promised him, on the spot, an amount of money well beyond the going rate for that sort of favor, to cover his risk and his lost evening; he made it fairly clear that he was trying to judge, from how I said it, whether I was the kind of person who would actually hand the money over once I no longer needed him for anything.

I paid him in full outside the bank the next morning, but when I described the evening to a friend of mine who is finishing a PhD in philosophy and works on decision theory, she told me I had reconstructed, almost exactly, a case philosophers call Parfit's hitchhiker, used to spell out an idea Parfit called "rational irrationality": roughly, that it can be rational to want to become someone who will do something irrational later, since it is only that disposition, and not the isolated act of paying once you are safe, that gets you picked up in the first place. She added that the case is meant to pose a serious problem for any theory that judges rationality act by act rather than by policy or character.

That has stayed with me longer than the coincidence alone would explain, and over the past week I have fallen down a bit of a reading rabbit hole trying to understand it properly, which is where terms like "Newcomb's problem" and "resolute choice" below come from, though I do not trust myself yet to have understood how they fit together. Could you set out the most important implications of Parfit's hitchhiker and rational irrationality for a situation like mine? I would especially value your addressing: whether the rancher was, in effect, solving a prediction problem of the sort at stake in Newcomb's problem, and what that implies about whether it was the act of paying that was rational or only my having been disposed to pay; how the case bears on the disagreement between assessing acts and assessing policies, dispositions, or what I have seen called "resolute choice"; and whether any of this genuinely generalizes to smaller promises I make constantly in freelance contracting work, such as offering a subcontractor a bonus for a fast turnaround that I cannot be legally compelled to honor, or whether the life-and-death stakes of the original case are doing too much of the argumentative work for the lesson to travel.
