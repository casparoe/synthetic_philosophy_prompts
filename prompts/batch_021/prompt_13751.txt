A friend of mine teaches an intro philosophy-of-mind course at a local college and roped me into grading a stack of short-answer exams over the weekend, since I'm the closest thing she knows to "someone who understands computers." I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, though, so before I write a grade in the margin I'd like a second, more expert opinion on the following answer.

The question was:

"State the Lucas–Penrose argument against mechanism (the thesis that a human mind is, or could be simulated by, some fixed Turing machine). Explain precisely what work Gödel's incompleteness theorems do in the argument. Then give what you take to be the strongest objection to the argument and assess whether it succeeds."

The student's answer:

"Mechanism holds that human mathematical reasoning could in principle be captured by some fixed, consistent formal system F. Gödel's first incompleteness theorem says that for any such F powerful enough to encode arithmetic, there is a sentence G(F), stating of itself that it is unprovable in F. F cannot prove G(F) without being inconsistent, yet we, reasoning from outside F, can see that G(F) is true, since we've granted F's consistency. Lucas's move is that this can be done to any candidate F, so no fixed formal system can capture everything the mind can do — the mind outruns any machine. Gödel himself drew exactly this conclusion, taking the theorems to prove that minds are not mechanical. Penrose later re-ran the same logical argument with more modern trappings, adding the physical conjecture that non-computable quantum processes in the brain's microtubules are the source of this capacity to outrun formal systems.

The strongest objection is Putnam's: the argument needs me to know that my own reasoning is consistent, but by Gödel's second theorem, no sufficiently strong consistent system can prove its own consistency. So I am in no position to certify my own consistency, which is exactly the epistemic position a machine would be in with respect to itself. Putnam's point therefore blocks Lucas's argument from ever getting off the ground, since the required premise — that I can see F's consistency, where F is my own underlying machine — is not something I am ever entitled to assert about myself."
