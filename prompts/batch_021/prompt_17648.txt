So I've spent the last couple weeks reporting a piece on this corner of experimental philosophy where researchers survey people about whether some guy "knows" the bank will be open Saturday, and they vary how much is riding on the answer — low-stakes version, he's pretty sure, no big deal either way; high-stakes version, same guy, same evidence, but now he'll bounce a huge check if he's wrong. Turns out people get way more reluctant to say he "knows" in the high-stakes version even though his evidence hasn't changed one bit, which is a genuinely interesting result. But when I called up two epistemologists to help me explain why, I got two totally different theories for the exact same finding. One kept saying this is about "contextualism" — that "know" works kind of like "flat" or "empty," a word whose truth conditions flex depending on the standards in play in the conversation. The other insisted no, this is "pragmatic encroachment" (he also used the phrase "subject-sensitive invariantism," which did not help), where the word itself doesn't budge, its the actual facts about whether the guy in the story knows that change, because of what's riding on it for him.

I nodded through both calls like I understood, but if I'm honest I walked away unable to say what really distinguishes these two positions, or why you'd need two competing frameworks when they both seem to accommodate the exact same bank-case intuitions just fine. Could you actually get into the weeds on this for me — not the two-paragraph SEP-summary version, I've basically already read that — but something that lays out where the real theoretical daylight is between contextualism and pragmatic encroachment/SSI, and ideally sketches a case (real or invented) where the two views would actually cash out in different verdicts, rather than just being two competing vocabularies for redescribing the same bank story?
