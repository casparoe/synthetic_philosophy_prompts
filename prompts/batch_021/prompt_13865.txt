I've been working through axiomatic bargaining theory over the past couple of weeks — John Nash's original 1950 solution and then Kalai and Smorodinsky's 1975 alternative to it — partly because I keep running into both solution concepts invoked, in different corners of the rationalist blogosphere, as toy models for how two AI systems with different objectives might split the gains from cooperating instead of competing, or for how one might aggregate credences across moral theories under moral uncertainty. What strikes me, now that I've actually sat with the axioms rather than just the headline formulas, is that the two solutions aren't competing approximations of the same target: they're forced apart by a genuine trade-off. Nash's solution is pinned down by Pareto efficiency, symmetry, scale invariance, and Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (shrink the feasible set without removing the old solution point, and the solution shouldn't move). Kalai and Smorodinsky keep the first three axioms but swap in a monotonicity requirement instead: if the set expands in a way that raises your maximum feasible payoff — the best you could conceivably do — while leaving mine exactly where it was, then my payoff under the solution should not go down, even though none of my own options changed. It's a real theorem that you generally cannot have both properties at once, and there are clean cases where the Nash solution does lower one party's payoff purely because the other party's opportunities improved.

I'd like you to write a dialogue that dramatizes this disagreement at the level of people who actually have to act on it, rather than at the level of slogans. Set it up as two negotiation consultants — one a committed Nash-solution partisan, the other committed to Kalai-Smorodinsky — who have separately been asked to model the same case (say, two startup co-founders who long ago agreed to split any exit 50/50, now negotiating a much larger acquisition offer that is mostly attributable to intellectual property one of them developed afterward) and who end up comparing notes. Have them work out, within the dialogue itself, a small concrete numeric example — a feasible set of possible splits, a specific way it expands, and what each solution concept recommends before and after — so that the disagreement between Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives and monotonicity has real content instead of staying abstract. Then have them actually argue about which axiom failure is more damning, and why, engaging with each other's strongest objections rather than restating their own view.

I'd also like the dialogue to take a position, if it can, on a question I raised with a friend who does mechanism design professionally, who waved the whole debate away as academic on the grounds that no real negotiator computes either solution explicitly: is the Nash/Kalai-Smorodinsky disagreement a substantive dispute about what fairness or rationality in bargaining actually requires, or is it "merely" a disagreement about which idealization is more useful for a given application, with no fact of the matter underneath it? I've gone back and forth on this myself and would like to see the strongest version of each answer.
