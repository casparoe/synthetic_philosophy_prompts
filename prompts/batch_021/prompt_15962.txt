I have no formal background in philosophy, but I've spent the past year working through Sartre on my own — mainly Being and Nothingness, with some secondary reading to help me along — and I like to test his ideas against things I notice in ordinary life. Below is a short essay applying his idea of bad faith to something that's been nagging at me: the way people, myself included, now use attachment-style and trauma language to explain their behavior in relationships. I'd like your comments on it. Mainly I want to know whether I'm using "bad faith" the way Sartre actually meant it or stretching it past its proper use, and whether the objection I try to answer near the end actually gets answered or just gets pushed somewhere else. Please push back on the substance rather than the prose.

Here's the essay:

Everyone I know now has a diagnosis for why they act the way they do with the people they date. She is "anxious attachment," so of course she spirals when he's slow to text back. He is "avoidant," so of course he goes quiet during a fight. Someone else's shutting down mid-argument is "a trauma response," their nervous system doing what nervous systems do. What strikes me about this vocabulary isn't that it's false — these patterns are often real, and worth naming — but how it gets used in the moment: less like a description of a pattern than a verdict that settles what happens next.

This is close to what Sartre calls bad faith, though not quite how people usually invoke him. Bad faith isn't simply lying to yourself about a fact. It's a stranger move: treating yourself, for a moment, as though you were a finished object with fixed properties — like his famous waiter, whose gestures are a shade too precise, too perfectly "waiter-shaped" — rather than what Sartre thinks you actually are: a freedom with no fixed nature to fall back on, always out ahead of any fact about you, having to decide, again, right now, what that fact means. His gambler, who has sincerely sworn off the tables, discovers this the moment he's standing in front of them again: yesterday's resolution doesn't reach into today except by an act he performs all over again. Nothing about what he already is settles it for him.

"I'm anxious attachment, that's why I spiral" does the same quiet work. It isn't false, exactly; there may really be a pattern, laid down by real history, that makes spiraling more likely. But the sentence does more than describe the pattern — it borrows the pattern's realness to make the next text message feel already written, so that noticing the urge and sending it anyway stop looking like two separate moments with a choice sitting between them. The diagnosis becomes an alibi with a clinical seal on it.

It also runs the other way, which is the part of Sartre's account I find most convincing. The same person who claims to be helplessly "wired" one week will, the next, when it's convenient, insist that some clingy message they sent "wasn't really them" — pure unattached freedom, no history attached. Bad faith isn't a settled preference for facticity over freedom, or the reverse; it's a willingness to grab whichever one gets you out of the discomfort of being both at once.

The obvious objection is that this is a cruel thing to say to anyone whose reactions come out of genuine trauma — that dissociating or panicking isn't a choice, and telling someone otherwise is just blame dressed up in clinical language. I think Sartre has an actual answer here, not just a dodge: he never denies that the wave of feeling arrives unbidden — fear, panic, the urge to go blank aren't chosen. What's never simply given, on his account, is what you do with it next and what you decide it says about who you are; that stays open no matter how well the label fits. But I'm not sure that distinction survives contact with cases where the "next thing" — going blank, shutting down — isn't experienced as a doing at all. That's the part I most want pushed on.

So, three things in particular: does the essay get bad faith right, or has it been quietly redefined to fit my example; does the last paragraph actually rescue the argument from the cruelty objection or just relocate the problem; and is there a better real-world case than attachment talk that would make the same point more cleanly?
