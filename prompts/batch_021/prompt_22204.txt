And here's my partner's answer, copied exactly: "The doctrine of double effect (DDE) says it can be ok to bring about a bad effect as a side effect of what you're doing even though it would not be ok to bring about that same bad effect as a means to your goal or as the goal itself. More specifically DDE requires that (1) the act itself isn't wrong independent of its effects, (2) the agent doesn't intend the bad effect as an end or as a means, only foresees it, and (3) the good has to be proportionate to the bad. This explains Switch vs Footbridge: in Switch, the one guy's death is a foreseen side effect, the five get saved because the trolley's path changed, not because he died. In Footbridge the man's body is literally what stops the trolley so his getting hit is the means to saving the five rather than a side effect, which is why it's not allowed even though the numbers are identical - basically DDE here is just cashing out Kant's idea that you can't use someone as a mere means to your ends. Foot's Loop Case pushes on this further: the side track loops back and rejoins the main track before the five, so diverting the trolley there only saves them if something heavy enough is on the loop to stop the trolley before it comes back around, i.e. a guy standing on the loop track. So even though flipping the switch in Loop is the exact same kind of act as in Switch, the guy getting hit plays the same causal role as the fat man's body does in Footbridge: it's a necessary means, not a side effect. I think that means DDE actually has to classify Loop with Footbridge, not with Switch, which is uncomfortable given how similar Loop feels to Switch, and it suggests that the impersonal/hands-on distinction people sometimes appeal to instead of DDE can't be the whole story either."
