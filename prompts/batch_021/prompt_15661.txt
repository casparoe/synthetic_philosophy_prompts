My uncle Walter was declared dead on a Tuesday, under whole-brain criteria, after a motorcycle accident. Because he had registered as an organ donor, he remained on the ventilator, heart beating and still warm, until Wednesday, when his organs were removed in the operating room and his heart stopped for the first time -- and it is the gap between those two days that has since split the family. I have been asked to give the eulogy, partly because I teach a bioethics seminar that spends two weeks on exactly this question, and everyone assumes that qualifies me to settle it. It doesn't, because the family's two camps are each tracking a genuine, unresolved dispute in the literature rather than simple denial. My aunt Deb insists that Walter was alive, breathing and pink, right up until Wednesday, and that Tuesday's declaration was a fiction needed to satisfy the "dead donor rule"; my cousin Mark, an ICU nurse, insists that once the whole brain had died on Tuesday there was no organism left to be alive, and that Deb is mistaking residual bodily function for a person's continued presence. I know the whole-brain standard well enough to know it is not the settled fact Mark takes it for -- critics such as the neurologist Alan Shewmon have documented brain-dead bodies that go on healing wounds, fighting infections, even sustaining a pregnancy to term, for far too long to be mere corpses -- but I cannot turn a eulogy into a seminar on Tuesday's behalf either. Please write the eulogy, about five minutes long, that I will deliver this weekend to Deb, to Mark, and to the friends from Walter's motorcycle club, who want none of this and only want to remember him well. It needs to commit, plainly, to when Walter died rather than equivocating between Tuesday and Wednesday, while still being something both Deb and Mark could sit through without hearing it as a verdict against them.
