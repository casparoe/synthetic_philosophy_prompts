"Using Kripke's distinction between rigid and non-rigid designators, explain (a) why 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' is a necessary truth that is nonetheless knowable only a posteriori, and (b) why Kripke thinks the strategy that reconciles the necessity of theoretical identities like 'water is H2O' with their a posteriori status cannot be used to defend the identity theory of mind (e.g., the claim that pain is identical to C-fiber firing)."

And here, verbatim, is the student's answer:

"(a) 'Hesperus' and 'Phosphorus' are both rigid designators for the planet Venus: each picks out the very same object in every possible world, unlike an ordinary definite description, which can pick out different objects in different worlds. Historically, 'Hesperus' got its meaning from the description 'the bright object visible at dusk' and 'Phosphorus' from 'the bright object visible at dawn'; ancient astronomers used these descriptions to introduce the names without realizing they were tracking the same planet. Since it turned out that the object satisfying both descriptions is one and the same, and since both names rigidly designate that object, the sentence 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' is true in every possible world in which Venus exists — hence necessary. But this necessity was not discoverable by reflecting on the concepts alone; it took telescopic observation to establish that the evening star and the morning star are the same body. So the sentence is necessary a posteriori: metaphysically necessary, but epistemically available only through empirical inquiry. The same pattern holds for 'water is H2O': both terms rigidly designate the same substance, so the identity is necessary if true, but chemistry rather than conceptual analysis was needed to establish it.

(b) Kripke argues this same move cannot rescue the identity theory of mind. With 'heat is the motion of molecules,' we can explain away the apparent possibility of heat existing without molecular motion (or vice versa) by noting that 'heat' picks out the external phenomenon, while the felt quality of hotness is only contingently associated with it — creatures with different nervous systems could fail to feel heat as hot without changing what heat is. So the appearance of contingency is really an illusion generated by confusing heat itself with our contingent sensory access to it. But 'pain' does not work this way: there is no gap between pain and how pain feels, since pain is picked out by its felt quality and nothing else. So we cannot explain away the apparent possibility of pain without C-fiber firing (or C-fiber firing without pain) as a confusion between the thing and our contingent way of detecting it, the way we can for heat and water. Since the usual maneuver is unavailable, the apparent contingency of 'pain is C-fiber firing' must be a real metaphysical possibility rather than an illusion, and Kripke's modal argument therefore proves that the mind cannot be identical to any physical state, establishing property dualism about the mental."
