After forty-odd years running a laser-cooling and atom-trapping lab, I retired a couple of years back and, half by accident, started taking a non-credit metaphysics course online just to keep my brain from turning to mush. A few weeks ago we got to Max Black's old thought experiment about a universe containing nothing but two perfectly identical spheres floating in otherwise empty, symmetric space — the one that's supposed to refute Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles, i.e. the claim that no two genuinely distinct things can share absolutely every property. I brought it up with my old postdoc over coffee (we still meet most Thursdays), and we ended up arguing about wether the thought experiment even adds anything new, because it seems to me physics settled this question for real a long time ago, no hypothetical spheres required.

Here's my reasoning: I spent my whole career around genuinely indistinguishable particles. A Bose-Einstein condensate is literally a cloud of rubidium atoms sitting in the exact same single-particle quantum state — same energy, same everything — and the formalism doesn't even let you write down a label for atom #237 versus atom #412: swapping any two of them isn't just undetectable, it isn't even a mathematically different state. Or take two electrons locked together in a spin-singlet pair: individually, neither one even has a definite spin, yet quantum theory is adamant there are two electrons there, not one. That looks to me like real, empirically confirmed, indiscernible-but-numerically-distinct objects, sitting in a lab rather than a philosopher's armchair, which should mean Leibniz's principle is just false as a matter of established science, and Black's spheres are a nice bit of speculation on a question experimental physics already closed decades ago. So am I missing some reason philosophers of physics still treat this as a genuinely open question, or is this basically just a case of physicists having settled a debate that philosophy hasn't fully caught up to yet?
