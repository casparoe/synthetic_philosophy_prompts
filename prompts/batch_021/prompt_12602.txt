I'm the floor-supervisor rep on my plant's joint labor-management "fitness for duty" panel (we manufacture aerospace fasteners), and we've got a case this cycle that's honestly kept me up at night -- partly because it's close to what happened with my own mom before she had to leave nursing. Deb, 61, has been our senior torque-and-spec inspector for 22 years: she's the final human sign-off before load-bearing fastener lots ship. After a minor stroke about two years ago she was diagnosed with mild cognitive impairment, and HR quietly wants to move her to a non-signing desk role "for everyone's protection." Deb's fighting it: since the stroke she's built an extremely detailed decision-tree app on the tablet she keeps on her at all times on the floor, covering basically every spec exception she's run into across two decades, and she hasn't had a single documented miss since she started leaning on it for every borderline call. Her argument is that this is no different from an inspector trusting a torque wrench over "feel," or a pilot trusting a checklist over memory -- the tablet is just part of how her judgment works now, and pulling her sign-off is discrimination, not safety. One of the younger engineers on the panel, who did a philosophy minor, keeps bringing up some idea he half-remembers about how a notebook (or a phone) can count as literally part of someone's memory rather than just a backup for it, if its reliable enough and consulted constantly and trusted the way you'd trust your own recall. Our safety officer isn't buying it: she says that sounds fine right up until a case comes up that isn't in the decision tree and Deb has to actually exercise judgment in the moment, which is the entire thing her signature is supposed to certify she can still do.

We meet again in twelve days and have to land on something concrete, not another extension. Could you write the panel's decision memo -- keep her sign-off authority as is, pull it, or approve something conditional -- with a specific time frame attached, and reasoning that actually engages Deb's side instead of just falling back on "better safe than sorry"? I especially want it to grapple with whether her reliance on the tablet is relevantly like the torque-wrench and checklist cases she's citing, or whether there's a real difference the panel should be leaning on.
