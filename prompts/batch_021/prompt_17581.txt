I spent my working life as a research chemist, and since retiring I have been working through philosophy for the first time, currently Kant's Groundwork with a small reading group. At our last meeting, two of the more argumentative members got into an exchange that was never really settled, and it has been nagging at me since: old habits die hard, and I keep wanting Kant's "test" to yield a clean pass or fail; this exchange left me doubting that it does.

We had been testing the universal law formula on a case of our own devising: "I will ride the train without paying whenever I am confident the conductor will not check." Marcus argued that this fails exactly as Kant's lying-promise case does: if everyone free-rode under those conditions, fare revenue would collapse and the service would stop running, so one cannot even coherently conceive of universalizing the maxim, making it a contradiction in conception and a strict, perfect-duty violation. Elena objected that promising is different in kind: a lying promise is close to self-defeating by definition, since a promise nobody believes is not functioning as a promise, whereas fare collection is only one contingent way of funding transit among several, so universalizing the maxim shows at most that this particular scheme would fail -- an empirical impossibility rather than a conceptual one, and so at most a contradiction in the will. Marcus replied that Kant's own suicide example rests on an equally substantive, non-logical premise about the natural function of self-love, so if that still counts as a contradiction in conception, Elena's stricter bar is not one Kant himself respects. Elena countered that Marcus's looser standard would erase the perfect/imperfect duty distinction altogether, since almost any refusal to help others or to develop one's talents could be redescribed as undermining "the practice of mutual aid" in just the same way.

Could you go through their exchange move by move, say who has the better of it at each stage, note what you think both of them are missing, and close with your own verdict on whether the fare-dodging maxim actually fails Kant's test?
