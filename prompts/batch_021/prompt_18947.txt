So i've been on a bit of a Leibniz kick lately, mainly his identity of indiscernibles thing (no two things can be exactly alike in every way, because God would have no reason to make a perfect duplicate of anything) and how he used it against Newton's absolute space in those letters with Clarke. Then I fell down a quantum mechanics rabbit hole and learned that "identical particles," like two electrons in the same trap, aren't just similar, they're genuinely indistinguishable in the math itself, no property tells them apart. Apparently some philosophers of science rescue a version of the principle anyway by saying the particles are still "discernible," just through relations to each other (like having opposite spin) rather than through anything either one has on its own, and that this whole thing feeds into structural realism, i think its called, the view that relations are more fundamental than the "things" that supposedly have them. But doesn't that clash pretty hard with Leibniz's own metaphysics? His monads are famously "windowless" and don't really relate to each other at all - relations are supposed to just be appearances riding on top of each monad's own private properties. So if you could actually sit Leibniz down and explain all this to him, would he grab the "saved by relations" escape route anyway, would he say it just proves quantum particles can't be substances/monads in his sense, or would he have to give up identity of indiscernibles altogether? Want your actual best guess at which move fits the rest of his system best, not just "he'd be amazed by modern physics."
