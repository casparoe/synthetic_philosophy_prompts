I'm a retired condensed matter physicist (almost forty years on superconductivity and phase transitions), and since retiring I've fallen into reading philosophy of mind, largely because my granddaughter is doing a masters in it and keeps leaving books at my house when she visits. Last month I got to Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument against non-reductive physicalism, from "Mind in a Physical World," and it stopped me cold. As I understand it: if every physical event with a cause has a sufficient physical cause (the closure of the physical domain), and mental properties merely "supervene on" or are realized by their physical bases rather than being identical to them, then whenever a mental property and its physical realizer both look like they're causing the next physical event, we've got two candidates competing for one job. Nobody wants to say every single mental-to-physical transaction is a case of two independent causes overdetermining the same effect -- that would be a wild coincidence repeated billions of times a second in every brain, not an occassional fluke like a firing squad -- so the mental cause is the one that has to give way. Only the physical realizer does any real causal work; the mental just rides along for free.

Here's what's been bugging me ever since: this has exactly the same shape as a worry physicists have kicked around about our own higher-level properties. Temperature, pressure, the order parameter of a superconductor -- none of these are identical to any one microstate, all of them are realized by enormous numbers of different microphysical configurations, and the microphysics underneath is just as causally closed as Kim needs it to be. Yet nobody in physics loses sleep wondering whether temperature "really" causes a balloon to expand, or feels forced to choose between reducing it away or calling it epiphenomenal -- Phil Anderson's old essay "More Is Different" is practically a manifesto against that kind of thinking. So what I'd like from you is an actual comparison: how does Kim's argument, specifically as an argument about mentality, stack up against this more general pattern of higher-level causation that shows up everywhere in the physical sciences? Is there some extra feature of the mental case -- something about how minds get realized, or the particular flavor of multiple realizability involved -- that makes exclusion actually bite there and not for temperature, or is Kim's argument just the generic overdetermination worry wearing a philosophy-of-mind costume, in which case shouldn't it "prove too much" and threaten macroscopic causal explanation everywhere in science? Don't just recite the standard menu of responses (functionalism, anomalous monism, and so on) -- I want you to actually locate where the disanalogy is supposed to live, if it lives anywhere at all.
