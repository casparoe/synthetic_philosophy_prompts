Question: "In The Querist, Berkeley argues that money is not itself wealth but a device for conveying and recording power over other people's labour and goods. Explain this view, how it motivates his proposed remedy for Ireland's shortage of coin, and discuss whether (and how) this monetary theory is grounded in his immaterialism."

Student answer: "Berkeley's central claim in the Querist is that a nation's wealth lies in the industry of its people -- the goods and labour actually produced and circulated -- not in how much gold and silver it happens to hold. Money itself is best understood as a counter or ticket rather than a commodity valuable in its own right: a device for reckoning and transferring power to command other people's labour. This is why he thinks Ireland can be full of idle hands and unused land and still count as 'poor' for want of circulating tickets, even though nothing of real value is missing. He worked this remedy out from the South Sea Bubble crisis in his 1721 pamphlet An Essay Towards Preventing the Ruin of Great Britain, proposing a national bank that would issue paper credit resting on public trust rather than on how much bullion happened to be in the country. Philosophically this is basically his immaterialism carried over into economics: since Berkeley already denies that sensible objects have any being apart from being perceived, it's a short step to deny that money needs any 'real' backing in precious metal either -- a coin is worth whatever it's agreed to be worth, the same way a cherry is nothing but a bundle of ideas of taste and colour. Commentators who treat his support for paper money as just a pragmatic response to Ireland's trade deficit with England are missing that it's continuous with his lifelong rejection of hidden material substrata."
