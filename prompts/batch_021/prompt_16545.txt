I'm a PhD student in medieval Jewish philosophy, currently at work on a seminar paper about ta'amei ha-mitzvot -- the medieval project of assigning reasons to the commandments -- and I keep getting drawn back to the disagreement between Maimonides and Nahmanides over animal sacrifice, since it seems to compress a much larger methodological dispute into one very concrete case. Maimonides, in Guide of the Perplexed III:32, argues that God could not simply legislate sacrifice out of existence: the Israelites had emerged from a milieu he elsewhere calls Sabian, in which sacrificial worship was essentially the only form of religious service they knew, so -- much as a wet-nurse weans an infant gradually rather than changing its diet overnight -- the commandments redirect an already-existing mode of worship toward the true God instead of abolishing it outright. In III:46 he extends this, explaining specific details of the sacrificial code (which animals, which materials, which prohibitions) as pointed repudiations of particular Sabian rites. Nahmanides, commenting on Leviticus 1:9, rejects this fairly bluntly: he notes that the verse calls the offering a "pleasing odor" to God, which he reads as evidence that sacrifice has positive standing in its own right rather than being a concession to backsliders, and he presses the chronological objection that Abel and then Noah were already offering animal sacrifices well before any Sabian idolatry existed for Israel to be weaned off of. In its place, he sketches a symbolic reading on which the components of the rite -- the laying on of hands, the slaughter, the burning of the flesh -- enact on the animal what the offerer himself actually deserves, so as to move him toward repentance. What strikes me is that this isn't hostile point-scoring -- Nahmanides was later one of Maimonides' more prominent defenders when others wanted his works suppressed -- so it reads much more like a real disagreement between two people who otherwise share a method.

Could you write a dialogue in which Maimonides and Nahmanides actually argue this through with each other, rather than each simply restating his own position from his own text? I'd like Nahmanides to press the textual and chronological objections above, and I'd like Maimonides to be given the strongest reply you think is actually available to him -- whether that means biting the bullet on Abel and Noah, reinterpreting the pre-Sinaitic narratives, or something else -- rather than folding immediately; both of them should come across as being right about something. If it comes up naturally, I'd also be curious whether their exchange reveals a deeper disagreement about whether commandments in general must carry intrinsic, non-instrumental significance, or whether that's reading too much into what is at bottom a fairly narrow exegetical dispute -- but please don't force that thread if it doesn't arise on its own. Something in the region of 700-900 words would be ideal.
