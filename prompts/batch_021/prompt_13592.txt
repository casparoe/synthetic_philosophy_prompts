Question 3: (a) Explain Russell's Theory of Descriptions as applied to "The present King of France is bald," and say why the sentence comes out false rather than meaningless on his view. (b) Explain how the distinction between primary and secondary occurrence is supposed to show that "The present King of France is not bald" does not, after all, violate the Law of Excluded Middle. (c) Briefly situate Russell's proposal relative to Frege's treatment of sentences containing non-denoting singular terms. (d) Briefly explain how the objections raised by Strawson and Donnellan complicate Russell's account.

Student's Answer:

On Russell's view, a definite description like "the present King of France" is not a genuine referring expression but what he calls an incomplete symbol: it contributes no independent meaning of its own and must instead be analyzed away in the context of the whole sentence in which it appears. "The present King of France is bald" is accordingly not a subject-predicate claim about some individual, but a conjunction of three claims: that at least one thing is presently King of France, that at most one thing is, and that whatever is presently King of France is bald. Since the first conjunct is false, the whole sentence is false. This is precisely what lets Russell avoid Meinong's strategy of treating "the present King of France" as denoting some being that merely fails to exist: on Russell's analysis there is no such object for the sentence to be about, and no truth-value gap opens up either — the sentence is simply false, on a par with any other false existential claim.

This machinery also dissolves the apparent threat that "The present King of France is not bald" poses to the Law of Excluded Middle. Since nothing is presently King of France, it can seem as though he belongs to neither the class of bald things nor the class of things that are not bald, so that neither "is bald" nor "is not bald" holds of him. Russell's response is that the negated sentence is scope-ambiguous. Giving the description primary occurrence places it outside the negation: "there is a unique present King of France, and he is not bald" — which is false, for the same reason the original sentence was false. Giving the description secondary occurrence places it inside the negation's scope: "it is not the case that there is a unique present King of France who is bald" — and this is true, precisely because no such King exists. So the English sentence is ambiguous between a false claim and a true one, and it is the true reading — the one on which the description has secondary occurrence — that is the genuine contradictory of the original. Excluded middle is preserved once the ambiguity is resolved; it only looked threatened because ordinary language does not mark which reading is intended.

It is worth situating this alongside Frege's treatment of the same kind of sentence. For Frege, "the present King of France" expresses a sense but, because nothing in the world answers to that sense, it fails to secure a reference; and since the reference of a whole sentence — its truth value — is built up out of the references of its parts, a sentence containing a senseful but referenceless singular term itself fails to have a truth value. So although Russell and Frege part ways over the machinery — quantifiers and scope for Russell, sense and reference for Frege — they converge on the same diagnosis of "the present King of France is bald": a perfectly meaningful sentence that nonetheless comes out neither true nor false, a verdict Russell reaches without needing Frege's apparatus of senses.

Strawson's response in "On Referring" presses on this from a different direction: he argued that Russell had mistaken a presupposition of using the sentence for part of what is asserted by it, so that when the presupposition fails, the utterance is neither true nor false rather than false, since the question of truth or falsity simply fails to arise. Strawson developed this further by distinguishing two uses of definite descriptions: a referential use, in which the description is merely a tool for drawing an audience's attention to some particular individual the speaker has in mind, and an attributive use, in which the speaker asserts something of whoever or whatever uniquely fits the description. On Strawson's view, failure of the existence presupposition is most damaging on the attributive use, since there the entire content of what is said depends on something's answering to the description, whereas on the referential use a speaker may succeed in communicating something determinate about their intended referent even if nothing strictly satisfies the description given.
