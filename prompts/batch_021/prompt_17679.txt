I've been down a bit of a rabbit hole this week for a piece on why party politics feels so broken lately, and I ran across this short, oddly ferocious memo Simone Weil wrote for de Gualle's people in London in 1943, arguing France should just abolish political parties outright once the war was over. I went in assuming it'd be the usual "partisanship makes people tribal" complaint, but her actual argument is weirder than that — she seems to be saying that basically any party, whatever it officially stands for, is structurally organized around whipping up collective passion, pressuring its own members into conformity, and pursuing its own unlimited growth as basically an end in itself, underneath whatever platform it claims to have. Even a party founded by perfectly sincere, reasonable people would apparently curdle into this shape on her view, which is a much stranger and more specific claim than "polarization bad."

What I can't quite reconstruct on my own is why she thinks this is baked into the very form of a party rather than something that only happens to corrupt or extremist ones, and how its supposed to crowd out individual judgment about truth and justice specifically, not just civility or open-mindedness. Could you walk me through the actual mechanics of that argument? I'd also love a quick contrast with the more familiar Madison/Federalist No. 10 line that factions are inevitable and the fix is to design institutions that balance them against each other rather than get rid of them — is Weil disagreeing with Madison about human psychology, or is it more that she just thinks abolition is politically feasible in a way he doesn't? And if you happen to know, does any of this connect to that whole "collectives as idols" thing she's known for elsewhere, or would I be overreading that onto this one specific essay?
