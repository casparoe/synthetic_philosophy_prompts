So I'm a retired biologist (spent thirty-odd years working on marine invertebrates), and about a year ago I fell down a philosophy rabbit hole in retirement -- started with a few Stanford Encyclopedia articles on personal identity and parts-and-wholes stuff, and now I can't stop clicking. Something that happened at a dinner party last week has been rattling around in my head ever since, because it feels exactly like the kind of thing philosophers argue about, just with jellyfish-adjacent animals instead of ships or statues.

Two old friends of mine, Renata and Colm, got into a real argument over dessert about the Portuguese man o' war -- the thing that looks like a single balloon trailing tentacles on the surface of the ocean. Renata pointed out that, as a matter of straightforward biology, it isn't one animal in any simple sense: its actually a colony of genetically identical but physically distinct little units called zooids, each doing one specialized job -- one is just the gas-filled float, some are stinging tentacles, some digest the food, some handle reproduction -- and calling the whole colony "an animal" is the same mistake as calling a piece of coral "an animal" just because the polyps happen to be fused together. Colm shot back that this misses what actually matters: the man o' war moves, hunts, and dies as a single unit, and if you cut one of the zooids loose it doesn't go wandering off to live its own life the way a worker ant can carry on foraging alone for a while after leaving the nest -- it just dies, because it was never equipped to live on its own. Renata answered that "can't survive without the others" can't be the real test, because a lichen is literally a fungus and an alga (or cyanobacterium) fused into a single structure, and biologists have argued for decades over whether a lichen counts as one organism or a partnership of two -- so dependence for survival obviously doesn't settle whether something counts as one thing. Colm's comeback was that the lichen partners are two different species with two seperate ancestries, whereas every zooid on a man o' war budded off the very same fertilized egg and is genetically one individual -- so it's still "one" in a sense no lichen ever is. Renata's reply was that identical twins also come from a single fertilized egg that happened to split into two genetically identical halves, and nobody thinks that makes the twins "one person wearing two bodies" -- so shared origin doesn't settle it either. Colm didn't really have an answer to that, but he didn't concede anything either, and by then they'd moved on to arguing about who was paying the check.

I've turned this over a bunch since then, and I honestly don't think either of them was simply wrong, which is what's bugging me. Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who actually had the better argument at each step, not just who won overall? I'd love to know if there's some principled cutoff both of them were groping for but never quite landing on, or whether -- now that I've got a little philosophy under my belt -- "one thing versus many things stuck together" just isn't something biology by itself can ever settle, though I'm not totally sure I'm even framing that right.
