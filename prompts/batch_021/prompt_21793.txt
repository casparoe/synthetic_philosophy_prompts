My father was just told by his neurology team that after years of drug-resistant seizures, a complete corpus callosotomy — severing the corpus callosum, the band of fibers connecting the brain's two hemispheres, all the way through rather than partially — is now the most promising remaining option for controlling them. In trying to understand what his day-to-day life might look like afterward, I went from reading about the surgery itself into the classic "split-brain" experiments of the 1960s through '80s, and from there into a philosophical debate I hadn't expected: a genuine, still-unresolved dispute over whether patients like him remain a single person with one stream of experience after the surgery, or end up as two separate centers of consciousness sharing one body and brain.

What struck me is how symmetrical the dispute is once you get past the pop-science framing. One camp takes seriously the mental-duality (or "two minds") thesis associated with philosophers like Roland Puccetti: that a completed callosotomy produces two independent subjects of experience, each with its own perceptual access, which were simply coordinated closely enough before surgery to look like a single mind. The other camp grants that unity is strained or intermittently disrupted, but denies this amounts to a second subject — pointing out, among other things, that outside specially rigged lab conditions these patients act, speak, and plan as single, fluent agents. My brother is confident the two-minds reading is dualism dressed up in neuroscience; I'm genuinely not sure he's right, which is why I'd like to see the strongest version of each side argued out rather than the headline version of either.

Could you write me a dialogue between two philosophers of mind holding these opposing positions, in which each has to reckon with the other's actual evidence rather than deliver a parallel monologue? I'd want them to get into specifics: what the chimeric-faces test does and doesn't establish about simultaneous, mutually inaccessible perceptions in the two hemispheres; whether phenomena like cross-cuing undercut any appeal to the patients' apparent day-to-day coordination as evidence of genuine unity; and what to make of Gazzaniga's left-hemisphere "interpreter," which confabulates reasons for actions it didn't actually initiate or witness. Please keep both sides equally sharp, so that each is forced to answer the other's best objection rather than simply restate their own view, and don't feel obliged to resolve it neatly at the end — that would be more than I've managed on my own.
