So last night my friend Priya and I got into this long argument that started from the Boeing 737 Max disasters and the Wells Fargo fake-accounts scandal and somehow spiraled into something way more abstract by the end. She kept insisting the real explanation for stuff like that is structural — "the culture" or "the incentive system" pushed people into cutting corners, and that pinning it on individuals just lets the institution off the hook. I kept arguing back that only individuals actually make decisions, so "the culture did it" is just a fuzzy way of describing a bunch of individually explicable choices, and treating it like some separate causal force hovering above the people involved is kind of confused. It got a little personal too becuase I brought up her old marketing job, where she was always blaming "the culture" for stuff that seemed to me like it was pretty clearly just two specific bad managers. Two hours later neither of us had budged, and its honestly been bugging me at work today too, watching people explain away our whole team's dysfunction as "just how this place is."

Afterward I realized this is basically the methodological individualism vs. holism debate that I only half-remember from one social theory elective years ago — something about Popper and Watkins on one side, Durkheim-style "social facts" people on the other, and a vague memory of "supervenience" being relevant somehow too (don't quote me on that part). Could you lay out the most important implications of actually taking methodological individualism seriously, as opposed to some holist view where structures/cultures/institutions have explanatory status that doesn't fully reduce to the individuals in them, for how we ought to assign responsibility or explain outcomes in cases like this? I don't want the cop-out "honestly it's probably a bit of both" answer — if that's genuinely where the strongest arguments lead, fine, but walk me through why, and what's actually at stake practically for how we should think or act differently depending on which view is closer to right.
