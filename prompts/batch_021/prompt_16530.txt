I have no formal training in economics or mathematics, but a couple of years of following online debates about ranked-choice and approval voting has left me suspicious of how often the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem gets thrown around as a conversation-ending trump card against any hope of an unmanipulable election. I'd like to see it actually argued out rather than just asserted. Write a dialogue between an electoral-reform advocate who believes a cleverly designed voting rule can still make strategic voting a non-issue in practice, and a social-choice theorist who knows the theorem's fine print. Have the theorist open by naming exactly which of the theorem's conditions -- three or more alternatives, an unrestricted domain, a single deterministic winner -- are doing the real work, and why the result is sometimes called Arrow's theorem wearing different clothes. Then have the reformer press two genuine escape routes in turn: restricting preferences to be single-peaked, and leaning on results showing that manipulating certain voting rules is computationally intractable even when it's theoretically possible. Have the theorist state plainly what each concession actually costs, and end with the reformer conceding exactly what the theorem forces, and nothing more.
