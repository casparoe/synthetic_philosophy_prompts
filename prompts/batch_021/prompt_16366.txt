The absent-minded driver problem, introduced by Piccione and Rubinstein in their 1997 paper on decision problems with imperfect recall, concerns a driver who must take the second of two consecutive, visually identical highway exits in order to reach her destination. If she takes the first exit she receives a payoff of 0; if she continues past it and takes the second exit she receives a payoff of 4; if she continues past both exits she receives a payoff of 1. Because the two exits look alike and she has no memory of whether she has already passed one, the driver cannot condition her action on which exit she currently faces -- she can only commit, before setting off, to some fixed probability α of continuing at whichever exit she meets. Maximizing her expected payoff over choices of α gives α = 2/3, for an expected payoff of 4/3; this is generally called the "planning-optimal" solution, since it is the policy she would settle on in advance, before the trip begins.

This came up in a graduate seminar on decision theory, and the part that has stayed with me is the following complication, which is where the real controversy lies. Suppose the driver actually arrives at an exit and has to decide, in that moment, what to do, not knowing whether it is the first exit or the second. It seems natural for her to form some credence that this is the first exit rather than the second, and to choose whatever action maximizes expected payoff given that credence, much as she would in any other decision under uncertainty -- call this the "action-optimal" approach. The trouble is that the obvious procedure for generating that credence already presupposes a value of α, so it is unclear whether the in-the-moment calculation is well-defined independently of the very policy it is supposed to evaluate, and unclear what the driver should do if it is well-defined and recommends deviating from the planning-optimal α anyway. Some hold that this shows ordinary expected-utility maximization tacitly assumes perfect recall and needs to be rethought -- perhaps as a coordination problem among the driver's successive "selves" -- once that assumption fails; others maintain that the planning-optimal solution is simply correct and that the appearance of a distinct, competing calculation rests on a mistake. The dispute has an evident kinship with the halfer/thirder debate over the Sleeping Beauty problem, though it is not obvious that the two should be resolved in the same way.

Write an essay that reconstructs this controversy in some detail, presents the strongest version of each side, and then reaches -- and explicitly defends -- your own verdict. Your conclusion should take a genuine stand: for instance, on whether the driver's in-the-moment credence about which exit she faces is well-defined at all, on whether it should be allowed to override the planning-optimal policy on occasions where the two seem to conflict, and on what, if anything, the problem shows about the scope of standard expected-utility theory once perfect recall is given up. A verdict along the lines of "the two notions coincide whenever such-and-such holds, and the planning-optimal solution should win out when they diverge" would count as taking a real stand, and so would a more unequivocal defense of one side; simply concluding that reasonable people disagree would not.
