I'm writing about the philosophical prehistory of debates over machine minds, and I would like to move past the standard gloss on Descartes -- that animals and machines are mere automata without souls -- to the actual argument he gives in Part V of the Discourse on the Method. He offers two reasons a mechanism could never pass for a genuinely thinking human. First, it could never rearrange words flexibly enough to reply aptly to whatever is said in its presence, as even a dull-witted person can. Second, though it might excel at particular tasks, it would fail at others, since reason is a single universal instrument fit for any situation, while mechanical organs are each suited only to a narrow range of actions. These are the same criteria by which he judges that animals, for all their evident skill, do not merely reason less than we do but do not reason at all. Now suppose he were shown a contemporary large language model, fluently producing contextually apt replies across almost any topic put to it. Working strictly from his own two tests rather than from his known conclusion: would he say it clears the first hurdle, and does the second still succeed in withholding a mind from it -- or has he quietly lost the argument he thought he had? Answer by reconstructing the reasoning Descartes himself would need to give, not by restating the standard verdict about animals and machines.
