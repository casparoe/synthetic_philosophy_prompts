I'm drafting a chapter for a historical novel about Leibniz's last years in Hanover, and I've written myself into a corner with a scene I don't yet know how to finish honestly. The chapter is deliberately anachronistic: an imagined conversation, suspended outside ordinary chronology, in which Leibniz is confronted by a modern interlocutor who lays out the fossil record for him — hundreds of millions of years of predation, starvation, and extinction, entire orders of creatures wiped out long before any human existed, driven by a blind mechanism of variation and death that would not even be glimpsed until more than a century after his own death. She presses him on whether this can still be the best of all possible worlds, given that none of this suffering appears to educate, redeem, or morally improve any rational creature, and no sin of any free agent explains it. I've read the Theodicy itself and a fair amount of the secondary literature, but I'm a novelist rather than a philosopher, and I don't want Leibniz's reply to be the cardboard optimism that Voltaire already mocked in Candide. Before I put words in his mouth, I want to be sure I actually understand what his own commitments imply.

So: reasoning strictly within Leibniz's own framework, how would he have to answer her? I'm especially interested in how his three-way distinction among metaphysical, physical, and moral evil, together with his separation of God's antecedent will from his consequent will, would apply to a form of physical evil that precedes and outlasts moral agency altogether, rather than resulting from any creature's sin or serving any creature's education. Could he still treat such suffering as an unavoidable concomitant of choosing the best compossible set of essences — the richest variety of phenomena compatible with the simplest laws — even though, on this occasion, no individual sinner or spectator benefits from it? And does the sheer, wasteful turnover that the fossil record implies sit in real tension with his own commitment to plenitude and the continuity of forms, or is there a way, on his own terms, to fold that turnover back into the same principle rather than against it? I'd rather you reconstruct the strongest, most textually grounded version of his position than default to the Panglossian caricature.
