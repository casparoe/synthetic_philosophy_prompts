Was doing interviews for a piece on people who treat betting as a kind of applied epistemics—pro gamblers, forecasters, that whole scene—and two of my sources ended up arguing with each other for like forty minutes instead of answering my questions. Recorded the whole thing and can't let it go, so I'm bringing it to you instead of my editor.

Renata, who used to bet sports for a living and now sizes positions at a crypto fund, was making the case for something she calls "geometric rationality," which she said she picked up off a rationalist forum post. Her point: Kelly betting (maximize the expected log of your wealth), the Nash bargaining solution (maximize the product of what each side gains, same as maximizing the sum of their logs), and scoring your own forecasts with a log score (maximize the expected log-probability you put on what actually happened) are all secretly the same move—logarithms instead of plain averages—and she thinks that's suspicious in a good way. Her line was that treating arithmetic expected value as "the" definition of rational only looks obvious because nobody notices it's the wrong tool the second something compounds, since a person living through one actual sequence of bets tracks something a lot closer to the geometric average then the ensemble-wide arithmetic one.

Gabe, who does philosophy of science with a decision-theory bent, had three objections. One, this is just expected utility with log utility, nothing new—Samuelson was already attacking Kelly bettors for this decades ago, in a paper so contemptuous it's written entirely in one-syllable words. Two, bargaining isn't a repeated or compounding process at all, so whatever "ergodicity" story justifies swapping in logs for betting doesn't obviously travel to bargaining—finding the same bit of algebra in two unrelated proofs doesn't mean the proofs are secretly about the same thing. Three, taken seriously as a general rule rather than a trick for one class of problems, "always be geometrically rational" apparently forces you to reject the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms outright, which is a huge price for what might just be a cute mnemonic.

Renata's comeback was that the pattern turning up in bargaining and forecasting too, neither of which is a literal repeated gamble, is exactly what makes it interesting rather than coincidental—calling it "log utility in a costume" just redescribes the pattern, it doesn't explain why the costume fits. Gabe said "fits everywhere" is doing a lot of work for three examples somebody went looking for, and pointed out that even restricted to pure betting, full Kelly comes with roughly a coinflip's chance of your bankroll dipping to half its starting size at some point, so calling growth-rate maximizing "the" rational choice quietly assumes a risk tolerance most people don't have, ergodicity story or not.

Neither budged. Could you go through it move by move and say who's got the better of each point, and whether there's something both of them are missing that would've actually moved things forward?
