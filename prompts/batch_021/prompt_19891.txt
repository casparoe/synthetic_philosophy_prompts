In a 1955 Fortune essay titled "Can We Survive Technology?", published while he was serving as a member of the Atomic Energy Commission, John von Neumann examined three then-emerging technologies — nuclear weapons and power, automation, and weather and climate control — and argued that all three shared a common trait: each was, in his words, "neutral all through, providing only means of control applicable to any purpose, indifferent to all," so that no clean line could be drawn between a technology's beneficial and dangerous uses. On this basis, he dismissed banning or inhibiting particular dangerous technologies as a mere "pseudosolution." His reasons were several: science and its technological applications are too interconnected to isolate a dangerous branch without halting progress generally; any ban effective enough to matter would have to be enforced worldwide, which would require an international authority so powerful that creating it would amount to having already solved the problem of great-power conflict rather than providing a means of solving it; and prohibition runs against the basic ethos of industrial civilization, which only a society already disillusioned with that civilization would accept. In place of a ban or a technical fix, his proposed safeguard was simply a continuous sequence of small, correct day-to-day decisions.

Von Neumann died in 1957, well before anything resembling modern machine learning existed. Using his specific arguments above — not his general reputation as a technological optimist — assess how he would most likely have judged today's debates over frontier AI: proposals for a moratorium on large training runs, export controls on advanced chips, and a prospective international treaty constraining the most capable models. Which of his objections to prohibition transfer straightforwardly to this case, which would need serious qualification given features of AI that have no clear analogue among his three 1955 examples, and what would he most likely propose in their place?
