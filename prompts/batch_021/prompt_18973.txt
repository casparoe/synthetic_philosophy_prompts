I've spent years following debates on LessWrong and adjacent blogs, and one thread that keeps resurfacing under different names is the claim that "geometric" reasoning—maximizing expected log-wealth, as in the Kelly criterion—deserves a privileged status over ordinary expected-value reasoning, rather than being just one more preference among many. The strongest version of this claim, due to Ole Peters' "ergodicity economics," holds that ordinary expected-value maximization implicitly averages over an ensemble of parallel copies of the bettor, whereas any actual bettor only ever experiences the time average of a multiplicative wealth process; since the two averages diverge for non-ergodic processes, Peters argues that Kelly-style, risk-averse behavior can be derived from the mathematics of the wealth process itself, with no need to posit a subjective utility function at all. Economists in the Samuelson tradition reply that this is sleight of hand: choosing to care about your own time-average trajectory rather than the ensemble of possible outcomes you might have faced is itself a substantive preference, no less optional than simply assuming logarithmic utility in the first place. Write an essay laying out the strongest form of both sides and defend a conclusion: does the ergodicity argument actually derive Kelly betting from something more basic than a mere preference, or does it just relocate the same old choice of utility function under a more technical-sounding name?
