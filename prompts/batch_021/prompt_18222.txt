I'm a philosophy PhD student working on moral responsibility. After a joint session on negligence and the metaphysics of causation, two people in my reading group -- one a metaphysician, the other a law student -- fell into a running disagreement about causation by omission that neither would drop.

(1) The metaphysician argued that omissions are never genuine causes, only what some call 'quasi-causes.' Her example: suppose you ask a neighbor to water your plants while you're on vacation, they forget, and the plants die. It's equally true that the mayor didn't water them, and had the mayor done so, the plants would have lived; since we don't want to call the mayor's failure a cause, and the only difference between the mayor and the neighbor is a normative fact about whose job it was, the 'causal' verdict is really just tracking duty, not any independent causal fact.

(2) The law student objected that this proves too much, since ordinary overdetermined causation faces an identical selection problem: several factors each satisfy the counterfactual test, yet we call all of them causes and let context pick out the one worth mentioning. So needing a selection principle can't, by itself, show that omissions aren't causes.

(3) The metaphysician replied that the cases aren't parallel: overdetermination involves two or more processes actually occurring, which gives context something to range over, whereas the mayor and the neighbor both did nothing at all, so there is only a bare counterfactual to begin with -- invoking salience just relocates the puzzle rather than solving it.

(4) The law student countered that requiring an actual process is a costly commitment, since negligence law routinely treats a 'but-for' omission -- a conductor's failure to brake, for instance -- as a genuine cause of the resulting harm without any talk of processes; so either large parts of tort doctrine rest on a mistake, or 'cause' means something different in law than it does in metaphysics.

Neither budged, and each seemed to me to have a point. Could you adjudicate this move by move?
