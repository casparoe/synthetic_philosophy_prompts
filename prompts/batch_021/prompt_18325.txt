Hey - I sit on my hospital's ethics committee (they like having one philosopher in the room so everyone feels better about not knowing what to do) and we've got a genuinely hard one for Thursday. Eight years ago a patient with bipolar I disorder signed a psychiatric advanced directive with a Ulysses clause in it: if she's ever manic and refuses lithium, staff are authorized to medicate her over objection anyway - that was the whole point of writing it while well, so a future manic self couldn't undo it. She's manic now, refusing the lithium, and she's not incoherent about it - she says the drug flattens her out and she'd rather ride out six unmedicated months and keep painting than lose the work, and she's said versions of this during past episodes too without ever revoking the thing. Her siblings are split: one thinks the clause should just be honored, that's literally what a Ulysses contract is for; the other thinks the woman in front of us has as much claim to speak for herself right now as the one who signed the form eight years ago, and that calling her "manic" is doing a lot of unexamined work in the argument. Part of me keeps wanting to reach for the analogy to constitutions binding future majorities against their own later objections, but I don't need a poli-sci lecture - I need an actual memo for Thursday: a recommendation (invoke the clause and medicate over objection, or don't) with a timeframe attached, and one that takes the losing side seriously instead of just asserting that precommitments bind. Can you draft it?
