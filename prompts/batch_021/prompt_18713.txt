The question she got was: "Explain what Wittgenstein means by a 'private language' and reconstruct his main argument against the possibility of one, using either the diary/'S' example or the beetle-in-the-box example (or both). Then briefly say whether you think the argument succeeds."

And here's her answer, word for word:

"Wittgenstein's target in this part of the Investigations is what he calls a 'private language' - basically a language that only one person could ever speak, like a made-up code that nobody else happens to know, where the words stand for that person's own inner sensations rather than for anything public. He uses the example of someone who decides to keep a diary of a certain sensation by writing 'S' every time it occurs. The problem is that there's nothing to check the diarist's use of 'S' against: he has no independent way to tell if he's using it correctly today versus yesterday, because 'correct' would just mean 'seems right to me,' and if whatever seems right to me is automatically right, there's no room left for the idea of being right or wrong at all. So 'S' can't really be functioning as the name of a recurring sensation, since naming requires the possibility of misapplying the name, and this setup rules that out.

He makes a similar point later with the beetle-in-a-box example: if everyone has a box only they can look into, and everyone calls whatever's in their own box a 'beetle,' the word 'beetle' can't be doing the job of naming a particular kind of thing, because the actual contents of the box never enter into how anyone uses or checks the word - it could even be empty and nothing about the practice would change. This is basically Wittgenstein anticipating Kripke's rule-following point: since there's no other person around to correct the lone diarist, there's no community there to hold him to a standard, and without a community looking over your shoulder you can never really be following a rule rather than just doing whatever occurs to you. That's why a solitary language-user, someone like Robinson Crusoe alone on an island with no one to talk to, could never really mean anything determinate by his own words.

Overall I think the argument works: meaning has to be a public, checkable thing, or the whole idea of 'correct use' collapses."
