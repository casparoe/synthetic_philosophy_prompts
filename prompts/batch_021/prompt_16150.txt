I don't have any background in philosophy — I work in IT support — but I got interested in epistemology a while back after reading about what it actually takes for someone to "know" something, and I've been working through the topic on my own time since. Two ideas keep getting presented as rivals for explaining the same puzzling phenomenon: that people seem more or less willing to say someone "knows" a thing depending on how much is riding on being right. One is epistemic contextualism; the other goes by "pragmatic encroachment," which I gather is closely related to, maybe even the same thing as, something called subject-sensitive invariantism. I raised this over dinner with a couple of friends recently, and we agreed the two views sound similar when described casually, but none of us could pin down a real case where they would actually deliver different verdicts, rather than just using different vocabulary to say the same thing.

So I built a test case of my own, and I'd like you to use it to settle the argument. Priya is driving to the airport with hours to spare, glances at a fuel gauge reading half full, and tells the friend in the passenger seat, "I know we've got enough gas to get there." In version A, her flight is a routine one; missing it just means rebooking. In version B, everything about her evidence, her surroundings, and what she says out loud is identical, but unbeknownst to her, this is the one flight that will get her to the closing on a house purchase before a deadline that forfeits a large deposit if she's late. Weeks later, a mutual friend who was never told which version happened, and has no reason to suspect anything unusual about the stakes, hears the story secondhand and asks, "So did Priya know she had enough gas?"

My guess is that contextualism and pragmatic encroachment have to disagree about this pair of cases rather than just redescribing the same verdict in different words, because as I understand it, one of them ties the truth of "Priya knows" to facts about Priya's own situation, while the other ties it to the standards operating in whatever conversation the claim actually gets made in — and I've deliberately arranged things so that Priya's situation changes between A and B while the secondhand friend's conversational situation stays exactly the same. Could you go through what each theory actually predicts for versions A and B, being explicit about which feature of the case is doing the work for each one, and tell me whether cases built like this are a genuine, recognized problem for one of the theories rather than the other? I'd also like to know whether I've set the case up in a way that quietly favors one side — I can't tell whether I've stumbled onto a real asymmetry or whether there's some way for the disadvantaged theory to wriggle out of it that I'm just not seeing.
