I host a small podcast on political philosophy, and next week's guest is a political theorist whose forthcoming book defends epistocracy — the view that political power should be apportioned at least partly according to competence rather than distributed equally under the principle of one person, one vote. Rather than simply arguing for restricted suffrage, she defends a specific institutional design: an epistocratic council with suspensive veto power over legislation, whose members are seated on the strength of a competence-certification exam open to any citizen. I don't want the episode to retread the case already made in her book's introduction (the analogy to not letting random passengers fly the plane, Plato's ship of state, and so on); I'd rather spend our forty minutes on air pressing the parts of the view that are hardest to defend.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions for this conversation, each substantive enough that it couldn't be answered by simply restating the thesis. I want at least one question that presses the demographic objection — that any competence test, however neutrally worded, will predictably track existing advantages of race, class, and gender, so that epistocracy in practice just re-entrenches whoever already holds power — and that asks her directly whether she would still endorse the council if her own exam turned out to replicate exactly that pattern. I want at least one question built on David Estlund's proceduralist worry that picking out superior political judgment always presupposes exactly the substantive political disagreements the procedure is supposed to settle, making a genuinely neutral qualifying exam impossible in principle. And I want at least one question that takes seriously the empirical literature on systematic voter bias — of the kind Bryan Caplan catalogues — and asks why a credentialing exam would filter out bias rather than simply reward whichever biases the exam's designers happen to share. For each question, add a short follow-up keyed to the deflection or counter-move she is most likely to make in response.
