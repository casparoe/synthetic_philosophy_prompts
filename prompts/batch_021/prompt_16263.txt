I've been mulling over a disagreement in modal metaphysics that I ran into while reading about possible worlds on my own. When we say something like "I could have taken the other job" or "she might never have been born," there are two quite different accounts of what makes that true. Kripke, and more systematically Plantinga, defend transworld identity: the view that one and the same individual can genuinely exist in more than one possible world, so a world where you took the other job really does contain you, just living differently in it. David Lewis's counterpart theory denies this outright: on his view, nothing exists in more than one world, and that other world instead contains a distinct but merely similar individual, a "counterpart" — what makes the sentence true is facts about that counterpart, not any literal identity with you. Kripke's own objection to counterpart theory, built around the case of a politician named Humphrey who lost an election, is that this looks like a bait-and-switch: if all that happens over there is that some resembling stranger wins, it's hard to see why that should count as something that could have happened to Humphrey himself, rather than to a mere stranger he has no special stake in.

I'd like a short story that makes this disagreement feel like it carries real stakes rather than being a technical curiosity. Could you write one centered on a character who is somehow confronted with one or more "counterparts" of themselves from other possible worlds, and who has to work through, with genuine emotional weight rather than as a mere thought experiment, whether what happens to those other people is happening to them in any sense worth caring about, or whether Kripke's worry is right and it is really no more their business than a stranger's life would be. I'd rather the philosophical point emerge from what the character goes through than have anyone explain the theory out loud.
