I've spent a long time reading LessWrong and the Alignment Forum, and one thing that has always struck me as slightly too neat is how casually the orthogonality thesis gets deployed there — "intelligence and final goals are independent, full stop" — as though it were self-evident once stated. Going back to Bostrom's original paper "The Superintelligent Will" and the corresponding chapter of Superintelligence, I noticed he is actually more careful than the folk version suggests: he explicitly relates the orthogonality thesis to the Humean theory of motivation, the view that beliefs alone can never move an agent to act and that reason's only proper role is picking out means to ends that some independent desire has already supplied — while insisting that the orthogonality thesis does not strictly presuppose Humeanism and could in principle survive even if that picture of motivation turned out to be false. That, together with the fact that Yudkowsky's much earlier "No Universally Compelling Arguments" post seems to be making essentially the same move, started an argument in my reading group about whether the orthogonality thesis is really a distinctive piece of philosophy at all, or just Hume's line that "reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions" translated into the vocabulary of utility functions and optimization power so that it sounds novel to people who haven't read the Treatise.

I'd like you to help settle this. Please write a comparison of the orthogonality thesis with the Humean theory of motivation, understood generally as the instrumentalist picture on which practical reason is only ever about means and never about ultimate ends. Lay out what the two views share, then state, as precisely as you can, in which ways — if any — the orthogonality thesis is a distinct and stronger claim than generic Humeanism about practical reason, rather than a restatement of it in decision-theoretic dress; if your answer is "it depends," say exactly what it depends on. As part of your answer, also take a position on whether Bostrom's companion instrumental convergence thesis — the claim that agents with almost any final goal will still converge on pursuing certain intermediate goals, such as self-preservation and resource acquisition — sits comfortably with strict Humean instrumentalism, or whether it quietly smuggles in a thicker, more rationalist conception of practical reason that a consistent Humean ought to be suspicious of.
