This term I am teaching a graduate seminar on Rawls, cross-listed with the religion department, and a dispute I raised almost as an aside has taken over the last two sessions, so I would like a rigorous treatment of it to bring back to the class next week. In "The Idea of Public Reason Revisited," Rawls tries to make room for religious citizens through what he calls the proviso: comprehensive doctrines, religious or secular, may be introduced into public political advocacy at any time, provided that, in due course, proper political reasons -- reasons that do not simply restate the comprehensive doctrine -- are given that are sufficient to support whatever conclusion the doctrine was invoked to support. Several of my students, channeling an objection associated with Nicholas Wolterstorff, have been pressing the complaint that this arrangement fails the very criterion of reciprocity Rawls invokes to define what counts as reasonable in the first place: a citizen whose operative reasons are already broadly secular and liberal is never asked to translate or supplement anything, while a religious citizen is saddled with extra argumentative work for no reason other than that her premises are religious. One student summarized the complaint more bluntly: the proviso asks the religious citizen to demonstrate that she can also reason like everyone else, which seems an odd thing to advertise as even-handedness.

Write me an essay that actually adjudicates this dispute rather than presenting it as a permanent stalemate between two reasonable-sounding intuitions. Reconstruct the asymmetry objection in its strongest form, taking care to engage with how it bears on Rawls's distinction between comprehensive doctrines and merely political values, and with the "wide view of public political culture" that is supposed to make the proviso less exclusionary than the position often attributed to the original edition of Political Liberalism. Then reconstruct the best reply available on Rawls's behalf -- his own, if you think it succeeds, or a later commentator's, if you think it improves on his -- and tell me which side wins and why. I would rather you defend a qualified verdict, something on the order of "reciprocal with respect to X but not Y," or "reciprocal only once assumption Z is dropped," than retreat into both-sides hedging, so long as you argue directly against the strongest version of the objection rather than a weaker one that is easier to dispatch.
