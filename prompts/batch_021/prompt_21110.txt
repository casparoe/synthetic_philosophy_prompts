I spent thirty-five years as a geologist, mostly in the field, before retiring, and for close to thirty years now, the last dozen as chair, I have sat on the small collections committee of a regional natural history museum whose mineral and gem hall I helped build from almost nothing. Since retiring in earnest I have taken up auditing philosophy courses at the local university, including one this past year on rational choice, and it has left me looking at our committee's own record with a more critical eye than I once did.

Over that span, the main way we have improved the mineral hall has been a standing relationship with a single trusted dealer, who every few years offers to take a specimen or two off our hands, plus a cash difference, for something the committee judges better by whichever criterion feels most pressing at the time. In the late 1990s we traded a large but rather plain Brazilian amethyst geode, plus a modest sum, for a smaller but far more striking smoky-quartz gwindel from an Alpine fissure, because the committee then cared most about crystal form and display appeal. About a decade later, now prioritizing scientific documentation, we traded the gwindel, plus a somewhat larger sum, for a well-provenanced vivianite specimen from the tin mines of Llallagua, Bolivia. Three years ago, building a new wing meant to showcase size and colour, we traded the vivianite, plus a larger sum still, for another Brazilian amethyst geode from the same district as the one we started with, of comparable size and quality. A newer committee member recently built a spreadsheet of the whole sequence and pointed out, not unkindly, that across three trades, each approved without dissent as a genuine improvement, we have paid out a considerable total sum to arrive more or less back where we began.

The dealer has just offered us a fourth such trade on similar terms, and our next meeting is in three weeks. The committee is now split. One faction sees nothing to answer for: every trade was sound by the museum's own stated priorities at the time, the market for that Brazilian locality has genuinely tightened since the 1990s, and judging past decisions by where they happened to end up is not the same as judging whether they were reasonable when made. The other faction thinks the pattern itself is damning, whatever the merits of each step in isolation, and wants a binding rule adopted immediately, even at some cost to our flexibility on future deals with reputable dealers. Could you draft the memo I will circulate before that meeting? I would like a recommendation on the pending fourth offer and on what standing policy, if any, we should adopt going forward, with a timeframe attached, and I would like it to engage the first faction's objection seriously rather than simply treating the spreadsheet as self-explanatory.
