I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mathematics currently auditing a graduate set theory course cross-listed with the math department, and a disagreement that broke out after one of our sessions has been nagging at me ever since. The lecture had just used Zorn's Lemma to show that every vector space has a basis, and over coffee afterward two people in the course, Nate and Lena, ended up in a running argument about how worried we should actually be about the axiom of choice. Nate opened by saying he's increasingly inclined to hold any theorem to a lower standard of trust if its proof genuinely needs full AC rather than just ZF or ZF plus dependent choice (DC), on the grounds that full AC's track record includes things like the Banach-Tarski decomposition of a ball, and that a principle with consequences that outlandish shouldn't be treated as epistemically costless (1). Lena pushed back that Banach-Tarski isn't really a distinctive mark against AC: once you already have Vitali's non-measurable set, built by choosing one representative from each of the uncountably many cosets of Q in R, you already know that "volume" can't be a finitely additive, isometry-invariant function defined on every subset of R^3, and Banach-Tarski is just that same fact re-dressed with rotations instead of plain non-measurability, so anyone already resigned to Vitali sets has no principled extra complaint about Banach-Tarski specifically (2). Nate then pivoted to the well-ordering theorem, which is equivalent to AC over ZF: he finds it telling that nobody has ever written down a well-ordering of R, and that AC, the well-ordering theorem, and Zorn's Lemma provoke wildly different gut reactions from working mathematicians despite being interderivable, which he takes as a sign that AC has outrun whatever intuitive notion of "choosing an element" was supposed to motivate it in the first place (3).

Lena's response was that this divergence in intuitive plausibility across statements that are, by hypothesis, logically equivalent, is itself evidence that our intuitions simply aren't tracking anything reliable in this neighborhood, which undercuts trusting the intuition against AC every bit as much as it undercuts trusting the intuition for it; she added that being unable to write down a witness is true of a great many garden-variety ZF-provable existence claims that bother nobody, so the objection needs to isolate something distinctively wrong with choice rather than with non-constructivity as such (4). Nate's next move was to retreat to what he called a principled middle position: keep dependent choice (DC), which already subsumes countable choice, since ordinary analysis leans on it constantly (countable unions of countable sets, the Baire category theorem, the equivalence between the sequential and epsilon-delta definitions of continuity), but refuse the further increment of strength that gets you full AC, since that increment is exactly what is doing the work in Banach-Tarski, in the well-ordering of R, and in guaranteeing that arbitrary vector spaces have bases (5). Lena's closing shot was that this concedes less than Nate thinks: Solovay showed, in 1970, that ZF plus DC is consistent with every set of reals being Lebesgue measurable and with R having no well-ordering whatsoever, not merely none exhibited, and since the guarantee that every vector space has a basis is itself known, by a theorem of Blass's, to require the full strength of choice, that same world has to give up bases for some vector spaces too. So living inside DC-but-not-AC isn't obviously tamer, on her view, just differently strange, and the only thing separating Nate's acceptable losses from his unacceptable ones is which specific consequences he happened to have already heard of before this conversation (6).
