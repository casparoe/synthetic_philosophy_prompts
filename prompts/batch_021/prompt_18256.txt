Over the last few months I've gotten pretty absorbed in the decision-theory corner of AI-alignment writing (Yudkowsky, Wei Dai, the LessWrong/Alignment Forum crowd), mostly because it scratches the same itch as tracking down a gnarly bug: you're handed a weird edge case and have to figure out exactly which invariant is being violated. I have no formal philosophy training, so my way of checking whether I actually understand something is to write it up cold, in my own words, and see if it survives contact with someone who knows the field better than I do. None of my coworkers are any help here, and Googling just recycles the same three or four posts I've already read. Below is my attempt to explain "updateless decision theory" (UDT) through the counterfactual-mugging scenario, plus a conclusion about it that I'm genuinely unsure I trust.

Here's the setup as I understand it: Omega, a perfect predictor, flips a fair coin. If it comes up heads, Omega shows up and asks you for $100, no strings attached. If it comes up tails, Omega would hand you $10,000 — but only in the possible world where, had the coin come up heads instead, you'd have been willing to pay the $100. Say the coin actually lands heads and Omega duly asks for the money. A causal decision theorist reasons that the tails-world is now purely counterfactual — nothing you do can reach back and change how the coin landed — so paying is a dead loss, and refusing is strictly better. UDT says you should pay anyway, because the right question isn't "what maximizes value given what I now know," it's "which policy, mapping possible observations onto actions, would I have wanted to be implementing before I found out how the coin landed" — and the pay-on-heads policy beats the refuse-on-heads policy in expectation ($4,950 versus $0), even though, conditional on already being in the heads-branch, handing over the money feels locally pointless. My own gloss on this is that UDT amounts to the claim that rationality should be graded at the level of a whole policy or disposition rather than a single act evaluated in isolation, and that once you grant that, one-boxing, paying the toll to the mugger, and similar moves stop looking paradoxical and start looking like the obviously correct policy.

Here's the part I can't quite pin down. My instinct is that all of this is really just old-fashioned precommitment wearing new vocabulary — Ulysses tying himself to the mast, with Omega standing in for the rope — and that it only looks like a new theory of rationality because the predictor is flawless rather than physical. But I've also come across the claim, made in passing in a couple of the threads I read, that this can't be the whole story, because the UDT-style argument is supposed to still go through for an agent that never had any earlier stage in which to strike a bargain with itself: one that's instantiated for the first time already inside the heads-branch, with no history of "deciding in advance" anything at all. The distinction being drawn there — between committing earlier in time versus committing "logically prior" regardless of when you actually come into existence — is exactly where I lose my footing, and I can't tell whether it's a real, load-bearing distinction or just the same idea restated in more technical language to sound deeper than it is. Relatedly, I've seen it suggested that treating "more willing to commit in advance" as straightforwardly more rational stops working once several such agents start bargaining with one another, since each then has an incentive to lock in favorable commitments as early and as irreversibly as possible, before it or anyone else has had time to think things through — which sits uneasily next to the idea that updatelessness is simply the smarter policy, though I've only ever seen this raised as an aside, never argued through. Could you go through all of this point by point and tell me where the reasoning holds up and where it doesn't, focusing on accuracy over style? In particular: is my counterfactual-mugging write-up correct as far as it goes; is "it's basically just precommitment" a fair verdict or does it miss something real; and is the closing worry about competing early commitments an actual tension in the theory, or am I conflating two separate issues?
