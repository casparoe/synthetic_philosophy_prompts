"Arrow's Impossibility Theorem, proved by Kenneth Arrow in 1951, shows that when there are at least three options to rank and at least two voters, there is no way to aggregate individual rank-order preferences into a single group ranking that satisfies four reasonable-sounding conditions simultaneously: unrestricted domain (the method must work for any possible set of individual preferences), non-dictatorship (no single voter's preferences automatically determine the outcome regardless of everyone else), independence of irrelevant alternatives (whether the group prefers A to B shouldn't depend on how voters rank some third option, C), and the Pareto principle -- meaning the outcome has to be Pareto efficient, so the result can't be such that some voter could be made better off without another voter being made worse off.

The theorem generalizes a worry that goes back to Condorcet, who noticed in the eighteenth century that majority-rule voting between pairs of candidates can cycle (A beats B, B beats C, and C beats A), so that there is no consistent 'will of the majority' even though every individual voter has perfectly transitive preferences. Arrow's contribution was to show that this isn't a quirk of majority rule specifically -- it's a structural problem facing any ranked voting rule whatsoever, majoritarian or not.

A natural example is ranked-choice voting, which is still vulnerable to IIA violations: adding or removing a losing candidate can change who wins between two other candidates, even though no voter has changed their relative ranking of those two. Because the theorem is so general, it tells us that the search for a perfectly fair voting system is simply hopeless -- any system we might design, including approval voting or giving each voter a score from 1 to 10 to assign to each candidate, is condemned by Arrow's result to violate at least one of these basic fairness conditions."
