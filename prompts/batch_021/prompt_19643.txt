Ok this is going to sound super nerdy but it's been bugging me all week. I'm on the 3-person architectural review committee for my condo, and we just hit the weirdest snag voting on a neighbor's renovation request. Our bylaws say you can only approve a change if (a) it doesn't touch the load-bearing wall, and (b) it keeps the facade within the historic guidelines - both have to hold.

We each voted on the two conditions seperately, then gave a final approve/reject:
- Me: wall ok, facade ok -> approve
- Other homeowner rep: wall ok, facade NOT ok -> reject
- Engineer on the committee: wall NOT ok, facade ok -> reject

Here's what's been bugging me: majority vote on "wall ok" comes out 2-1 yes, and majority vote on "facade ok" also comes out 2-1 yes - so by our own bylaws it should've been approved. But our actual bottom-line tally was 2-1 to reject, since we voted approve/reject directly instead of issue-by-issue. We went with the bottom-line reject, and it's felt logically off to me ever since.

Turns out this exact setup has a name in social choice/legal theory - the "discursive dilemma," aka the "doctrinal paradox." So here's my ask: can you actually make the strongest case that we should've gone with the issue-by-issue (premise-based) tally instead and approved the renovation? Don't give me a wishy-washy both-sides thing - actually commit to that position and argue it, and deal with the obvious objection that this just lets us override the fact that 2 of 3 of us wanted to reject it, purely because of how the question got carved into two separate issues.
