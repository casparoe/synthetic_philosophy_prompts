I am drafting a novel whose secondary cast includes a character modeled closely on the elder Goethe -- Weimar privy councillor, Olympian sage, uneasy witness to an age of revolutions -- and I want his political instincts to read as the expression of a coherent worldview rather than a mixture of vanity and careerism. Please argue for a specific reading of him: that Goethe's abiding suspicion of political rupture -- his satirical treatment of the Jacobins, his admiration for Napoleon as a bringer of order rather than a liberator, his conspicuous distance from the nationalist fervor of the Wars of Liberation, and even his reported remark in 1830 that the dispute between Cuvier and Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire over the unity of animal structure mattered more to him than the July Revolution unfolding in Paris -- follows from the same organicist commitments that structure his morphology, in which development is always gradual, continuous, and driven from within, never a sudden leap. Then tell me candidly where this argument strains hardest, and whether temperament or courtly self-interest accounts for his politics better than the science does.
