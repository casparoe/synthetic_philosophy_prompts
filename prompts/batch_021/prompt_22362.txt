I'm a retired analytical chemist who's gotten pretty into philosophy since I stopped working, and these days I volunteer with our local Lifelong Learning Institute -- one of those programs where retirees teach short non-credit courses to other retirees -- and I sit on the small committee that has to sign off on course descriptions before they go into the catalog. Last month I actually sat down and read Turing's 1950 "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" straight through, instead of just knowing the imitation-game bit secondhand, and about spilled my coffee at objection nine, "The Argument from Extra-Sensory Perception." Its not a throwaway line for him -- he says outright that the statistical evidence for telepathy is "overwhelming," and seriously proposes running the imitation game inside a "telepathy-proof room" so that a telepathic human contestant's abilities couldn't tip the interrogator off. And it turns out he wasn't off on his own out there either -- apparently Cambridge philosophy had a long, genuinely serious relationship with psychical research going back to the 1880s: there was a whole Society for Psychical Research founded by Cambridge people, and one fairly prominent Cambridge philosopher, C. D. Broad, spent decades writing seriously about telepathy, precognition, and ghosts. So this argument came out of a real intellectual milieu, not out of nowhere.

Here's my actual problem, though. A fellow committee member, a retired classics teacher, has proposed a three-session course called "Turing and the Mind," and her whole third session is built around this ESP argument -- her pitch is that you can't honestly explain what Turing thought the imitation game actually established unless you're willing to sit with the fact that he treated this as a real objection, not a joke. Two other members want that session cut entirely, or shrunk to a passing mention, because they're worried it'll read as the Institute using Turing's good name to lend credibility to psychic phenomena in front of a room of retirees -- some of whom, no joke, already believe in this stuff -- and that it's a gift to anyone at the local paper looking to make fun of us. I keep flip-flopping on this myself, and I definately don't want us to just punt with a vague "add a disclaimer" fix. Could you write the actual memo the committee would circulate before we vote at next month's meeting -- a real recommendation (approve as proposed, reject, or approve with specific changes), the reasoning behind it, a timeframe for acting on it, and a genuine engagement with the two worried members' concern, since I think they've got a real point even if I'm not sure I agree with where they land?
