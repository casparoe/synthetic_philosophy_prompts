I recently started as a research assistant at a foundation that funds work on global catastrophic risk. At a team retreat, two senior colleagues had a long argument about whether an idea called "evidential cooperation in large worlds" (ECL) should shape our grant recommendations, and I've been asked to summarize the dispute before our next meeting.

1. Ines opened by arguing that if the universe (or multiverse) contains many causally isolated agents who reason as we do, then funding causes we personally rate low -- wild-animal welfare, say -- isn't charity but disguised self-interest: cooperating with value systems unlike our own is evidence that agents elsewhere who share our values are simultaneously cooperating with theirs, so every cause ends up better funded in expectation than if each of us funded only what we individually believe in.

2. Marcus objected that this only works if our choice is genuine evidence about what some distant stranger does, and we have no track record and no way to check it: unlike Newcomb's predictor, nothing here has ever been tested, so calling this "cooperation" just dresses up a leap of faith as decision theory.

3. Ines replied that the same complaint would undermine cooperating with an exact psychological twin in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, which most decision theorists accept is rational once evidence needn't come from a causal channel; the twin case and the multiverse case differ only in degree of similarity, not in kind, and ECL only needs the correlation to be nonzero, not verified.

4. Marcus answered that degree of similarity is exactly the problem: a psychological twin is a near-certain match, whereas ECL's whole point is to secure cooperation with agents who hold different values from ours, so the more the argument stretches to cover value-strangers, the weaker any reason to think their decision procedure tracks ours on this specific question.

5. Ines said proponents don't need all value-strangers, only the subset sophisticated enough to have derived and accepted the same argument -- a self-selecting reference class, exactly parallel to restricting the twin case to "whoever reasons like me" rather than "whoever resembles me."

6. Marcus said that move makes the framework unfalsifiable: you define the reference class as "whoever would accept this reasoning," then use membership in that class to justify accepting the reasoning, with no independent way to tell whether the class is large, small, or empty -- so the entire expected-value case rests on an assumption nobody can audit.

Neither of them budged, and I don't know the literature well enough to referee it myself. Could you go through the six moves and say, for each one, who has the stronger position -- not just who wins overall -- and flag whether either of them is at some point leaning on a premise that decision theorists would generally reject? I'd also welcome any consideration that neither of them raised but that bears on who's right.
