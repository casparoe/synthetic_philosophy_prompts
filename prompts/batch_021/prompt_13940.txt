In my graduate seminar on Gödel's philosophical writings, we recently moved past the incompleteness theorems into the unpublished material collected in the later volumes of the Collected Works, particularly the correspondence with his mother, Marianne, where Gödel sets out a surprisingly earnest rationalist case for life after death. As I understand it, the core move isn't really theological: because the world, and the development of a rational mind within it, isn't senseless or arbitrary, it would be absurd for that development simply to be cut off at bodily death, so something of the person has to persist and keep developing. Our professor mentioned, almost in passing, that the argument only really makes sense against the backdrop of Gödel's lifelong identification with Leibniz -- he apparently saw himself as continuing Leibniz's project, and even suspected that some of Leibniz's more radical manuscripts had been suppressed -- but we ran out of time before connecting the two properly and moved straight on to the ontological proof.

I'd like to work out that connection myself before the next class discussion. Could you compare Gödel's argument for an afterlife with Leibniz's case for the natural immortality of the soul in the Monadology and related texts, in particular the claims that simple substances cannot be destroyed by decomposition, since they have no parts to decompose, and that what we call death is really only a kind of enfolding or diminution of the soul's body rather than genuine annihilation? I'm especially interested in whether Gödel's argument is best read as a real descendant of Leibniz's rationalism, in the sense that both ultimately lean on some version of the principle of sufficient reason, or whether it's a substantially looser, more informal argument that only superficially resembles Leibniz's tightly reasoned metaphysics of substance. Relatedly, which of the two seems more vulnerable to the charge that it simply assumes what it needs to prove -- namely, that the world, or its creator, is thoroughly rational and therefore incapable of the kind of waste that a mind's abrupt extinction would represent?
