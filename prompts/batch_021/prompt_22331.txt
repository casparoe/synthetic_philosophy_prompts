I'm a retired geologist, and after fourty years of thinking about rocks I've been filling my time going to a "great ideas" discussion group that meets at the community college — mostly other retirees, but a couple of actual math and philosophy people show up too. Last month somebody brought up the Axiom of Choice and it turned into a real argument between two of the regulars, and I haven't been able to stop turning it over since.

Frank, who spent his career as an electrical engineer, said the axiom should just be thrown out because of Banach-Tarski: if an axiom lets you prove that you can cut a solid ball into a handful of pieces and, with no stretching, reassemble them into two balls each the same size as the original, that's a reductio, plain and simple. Denise, who taught set theory before she retired, said that's a bad reading of the theorem — the pieces you get are non-measurable, so "volume" doesn't even apply to them, and nothing about actual physical balls made of actual matter is implied, since you can't cut anything along a non-measurable set to begin with. Frank came back that this is just relabeling the absurdity, not dissolving it: whatever you call the pieces, you've still broken the intuition that rearranging finitely many rigid parts can't change how much "stuff" you have, and he doesn't see why a name should make that okay. Denise's reply was that he doesn't even get to keep that objection cheaply, because you don't need the full axiom to get Banach-Tarski in the first place — something considerably weaker, in the neighborhood of what you need to prove the Hahn-Banach theorem in functional analysis, is already enough — so tossing full AC to dodge the paradox doesn't even work, and he'd also lose ordinary results like every vector space having a basis, or two infinite sets always being comparable in size, or a countable union of countable sets being countable. Frank granted all that but said needing an axiom to keep doing convenient mathematics is a pragmatic reason, not a reason to think the axiom is *true*, and he'd rather be an honest instrumentalist about set theory than call something true because giving it up is inconvenient. Denise's last word was that this retreat comes suspiciously late: Zermelo and most everyone else who first met the axiom ("given any collection of nonempty sets, you can pick one element from each") found it flatly obvious, not just useful, so Frank only becoming an instrumentalist right after Banach-Tarski shows up looks like the paradox is doing his philosophical work for him, not some independantly-held view about what axioms are even for.

Nobody at the table could settle it, and honestly neither can I. Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who's got the better of it at each stage, not just who comes out ahead overall — and point out anything you think both of them are quietly assuming that doesn't actually hold up?
