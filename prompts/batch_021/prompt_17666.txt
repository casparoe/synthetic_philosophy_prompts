Here's the question as it appeared on the exam: "Explain Molina's doctrine of middle knowledge and how it's meant to secure both divine providence and creaturely libertarian freedom. Your answer should (a) lay out the three moments of divine knowledge -- natural, middle, and free -- and how they are logically ordered relative to the divine creative decree; (b) explain what counterfactuals of creaturely freedom are and why Molinists deny that God's willing or decreeing is what makes them true; and (c) present one serious objection to the overall picture, and sketch how a Molinist might try to answer it."

And here's the answer, transcribed exactly as the student wrote it: "Molina held that God's knowledge unfolds in three logical moments. First, in his natural knowledge, God knows every necessary truth and every possibility open to him, including what any possible free creature could do in any possible circumstance. Second, in his middle knowledge, God knows, for every possible free creature and possible circumstance, what that creature would freely do if placed there -- these are the famous counterfactuals of freedom, and Molinists insist they are necessary truths that hold prior to and independently of anything God decides, which is exactly why God's knowledge of them doesn't compromise the creature's freedom. On the basis of this middle knowledge, God then decrees to create a particular world, and in his free knowledge he knows, as a result of that decree, everything that will actually happen. Because middle knowledge is logically (not temporally) prior to the decree, God can pick out, from all the worlds feasible for him, the one whose history he wants, without ever forcing anyone's hand: he sets up the circumstances, and the creature does the rest. The most famous objection to this picture is the grounding objection, first pressed by Alvin Plantinga, which asks what could possibly make these counterfactuals true if not God's will and not yet any actual choice by the creature -- there seems to be no truthmaker for them at all. Molinists tend to respond either by denying that every truth needs a truthmaker in the relevant sense, or by insisting that facts about what a free creature would do are simply true in their own right, in a way that's no more mysterious than the fact that any subjunctive about a free agent has to be either true or false."
