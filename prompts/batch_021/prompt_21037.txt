In AP Physics, we learned that one of the classic pieces of evidence for general relativity is its explanation of the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion, a discrepancy astronomers had already measured decades before Einstein proposed the theory. This led me to read further about how philosophers of science analyze confirmation, where I encountered the "problem of old evidence" in Bayesian epistemology: if I already know some evidence E with certainty, so that my probability for E is already 1, then conditionalizing on E cannot raise my credence in any hypothesis, which seems to imply that E can never confirm a theory proposed only afterward to explain it. This seems to clash with the strong intuition that the Mercury case really did confirm general relativity, yet the very same reasoning also seems to fit my own science-fair project from last year, where I built my hypothesis only after noticing a pattern in data I already had, which is close to what researchers sometimes call HARKing. Could you explain what you take to be the most important implications of the problem of old evidence for how I should actually weigh cases like these? In particular, I would like to know whether the standard philosophical responses to the problem give me any principled way to treat the general-relativity case as genuinely strong confirmation while still remaining suspicious of my own after-the-fact hypothesis, rather than concluding that both stand on equally shaky ground.
