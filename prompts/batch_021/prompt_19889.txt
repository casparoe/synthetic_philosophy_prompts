In my physics elective this term, we spent a class period on the history of ideas about space and motion, and my teacher used Newton's rotating bucket to introduce the substantivalism/relationism debate. As I understood the setup: Newton pointed to the concave shape of the spinning water as proof that rotation is absolute — relative to space itself — since the concavity doesn't track the water's rotation relative to the bucket (right after the bucket starts spinning, the water is still flat even though it's now moving fast relative to the bucket walls; only later, once the water has sped up, does it curve). Leibniz's side held that space is nothing more than the relations among bodies and has no independent existence, and Mach pushed this further, suggesting that the water climbs the sides of the bucket because it is rotating relative to all the other matter in the universe — the "fixed stars" — rather than relative to some mysterious empty container called absolute space.

My teacher then told us that Einstein was directly inspired by Mach's idea, coined the term "Mach's principle," and built general relativity partly hoping to formalize it, and that one of the main achievements of relativity was getting rid of Newton's absolute space for good, since GR has no preferred rest frame and every motion is defined only relative to other things. I came away assuming that this basically closed the case in favor of Leibniz and Mach. But when I went looking for more on this over the weekend, I kept finding philosophers of physics who still call themselves "substantivalists" about spacetime, discuss something called the "hole argument" as a live problem, and treat gravitational waves as things that carry energy through spacetime itself, as though spacetime were a genuine object and not just shorthand for relations between material things. That last part especially confused me, since it sounds like exactly the kind of claim relativity was supposed to have made obsolete.

I asked my teacher about this after class, but her answer was quick and I don't think I fully absorbed it. So I wanted to ask: if general relativity already vindicated the relationist side of the bucket argument by getting rid of absolute space, why do serious philosophers and physicists still defend substantivalism today, and what would even be left for a substantivalist to argue for once Newton's absolute space is gone?
