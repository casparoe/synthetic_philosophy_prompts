I've been lurking on rationalist blogs and forums since the old LessWrong glory days (SSC/ACX now I guess), and a couple years back somebody's book review of L.A. Paul's *Transformative Experience* — the one with the vampire thought experiment — got stuck in my head in a way that never really left. Her basic point, as I understood it, is that for certain big decisions the normal expected-utility approach breaks down, not because you lack information about the outcome, but because the self who'd have to assign value to that outcome doesn't exist yet, so theres no stable standpoint from which to do the calculation honestly.

I sit on my hospital's neuroethics committee, and we've got a case this week that feels like that exact problem wearing a lab coat. We have a patient with a decade of treatment-resistant depression who's an excellent candidate for deep brain stimulation under our research protocol. She's given careful, fully informed consent. But there's a known, unsettling pattern in the psychiatric DBS literature: a meaningful minority of patients who improve on every objective measure nonetheless report afterward that they feel like "a different person," or that the improvement doesn't feel like *them* — and some ask to have the stimulator turned off despite great mood scores, while other families report the mirror complaint (they preferred the old personality, even though the patient herself is delighted). I mentioned this to a friend who does clinical ethics and she said "that's just autonomy vs. beneficence," but that feels like it's dodging the actual question, which is *whose* autonomy — the person who consented, or the different person the treatment might produce — should bind the other if they end up disagreeing.

Could you draft the short memo I'd bring to Thursday's committee meeting? I need it to land on an actual recommendation (something concrete — a staged/reversible protocol, an advance-directive-style mechanism, mandatory follow-up intervals, whatever you think is defensible) rather than just concluding "this is hard," and it should take seriously the strongest argument for just deferring to whatever the post-treatment patient says, not only the identity-change worry.
