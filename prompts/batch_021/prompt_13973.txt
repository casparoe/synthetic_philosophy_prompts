So I'm elbow-deep in a novel with a pretty vile bigoted character (1950s Alabama), and something's been nagging at me that feels more like a philosophy of language problem than a craft one. Even when my narrator only reports that he spat out a slur, never printing the actual word, or when another character brings the word up later just to say how ugly it was, the sentence still reads as contaminated in a way that reporting a garden variety insult doesn't. Some theories of slurs put the nastiness directly into the word's meaning, so it stays toxic no matter how it's embedded (quoted, mentioned, negated, whatever); other theories treat the nastiness as basically the speaker's attitude at the moment of use, which you'd expect to evaporate once your only mentioning the word instead of actually using it to insult somebody. Which family of theory actually explains what i'm running into, and why does the "attitude" kind fail to predict that mentioning should fully launder the word?
