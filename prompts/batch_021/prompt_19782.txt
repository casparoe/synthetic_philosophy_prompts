So this happened in my friend group over the last couple weeks and I honestly can't stop turning it over in my head. My friend Marcus has flaked on plans a bunch this year, and last week he snapped pretty hard at another friend, Liv, over text, just because she asked twice if he was still coming to her birthday thing. Afterwards he explained he's had diagnosed anxiety for years, and that when he's spiraling he gets snappy "out of nowhere" and isn't really himself in those moments. That set off a whole argument between Liv and another friend, Dan, at dinner a few days later, and I basically just sat there eating my fries while they went back and forth. I couldn't tell who was winning.

Dan's first move was that Liv should just let the whole thing go — if the anxiety is what made Marcus snap, being mad at him for it is like being mad at someone for flinching, there's no real choice there to hold against him. Liv said sure, but if you take that all the way then literally nothing Marcus does is ever his fault, he could pin any bad behavior on the anxiety, and also it feels kind of off to basically downgrade your friend into a patient instead of someone you can be normal with. Dan came back with a tweak: you can seperate the actual snap, which does sound involuntary, like a tic, from the pattern of flaking, which involves stuff he could plausibly change, like texting people earlier — so excusing the one thing doesn't mean excusing everything he does. Liv's comeback was that this line looks kind of made up: the anxiety plausibly touches the flaking too, so either the whole territory gets excused or none of it does, and picking which parts "count" as the illness and which count as him just seems convenient. Dan's last word, before we got interrupted by the check, was that it's not arbitrary — you can ask whether Marcus, once he's calm, treats something as a problem he owns and tries to manage (he does take his meds, and he does apologize after) versus something he just describes as happening to him with no sense he could've acted differently.
