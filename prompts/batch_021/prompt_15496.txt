I recently co-taught a graduate seminar that paired philosophy-of-mind students with machine learning researchers, and one disagreement kept resurfacing throughout the term: whether talk of "representations" inside a neural network should be taken at face value or treated as a mere manner of speaking. The test case we kept returning to was one of the cleaner successes of mechanistic interpretability: the discovery of so-called induction heads in transformer language models. As I understand the mechanism, these are pairs of attention heads, composed across layers, that jointly implement something like the algorithm "find an earlier occurrence of the current token in the context, then predict whatever token immediately followed it" — a simple copying routine that turns out to explain a surprising amount of in-context learning. My machine-learning colleagues were inclined to say that successfully reverse-engineering this algorithm vindicates a full-blooded realism about interpretability: the head doesn't merely correlate with the repeated token, it represents it, in whatever sense cognitive scientists have traditionally meant by that term.

I remain unconvinced, and I would like your help sharpening the worry rather than simply asserting it. Every naturalistic theory of mental content I know of that earns the label "realist" — Dretske's informational semantics, Millikan's teleosemantics, Fodor's asymmetric-dependence account — builds in some version of a misrepresentation condition: for a state to genuinely represent some content, rather than merely correlate with it, there must be a fact about what the state is supposed to track, such that it could in principle get this wrong, and that fact cannot simply be stipulated by whoever happens to be doing the interpreting. Dennett's "real patterns" account, by contrast, is happy to call something a representation whenever attributing that content is the most efficient way of compressing and predicting the system's behavior, and it explicitly declines to demand any further, interpreter-independent fact about correctness.

Please argue that the induction-head case (or, if you find it cleaner to work with, the closely related case of the Fourier-style algorithm that networks are found to implement when trained on modular addition) supports only Dennett's deflationary verdict, and not the realism about interpretability that my colleagues favored, on the grounds that no non-stipulative story is available for what it would be for an induction head to misrepresent. Give me the strongest version of this argument you can construct, and take seriously the most natural realist rejoinder before you try to dispatch it: namely, that training a network by gradient descent on a well-defined loss is exactly the sort of history that fixes a function in the way teleosemantics requires, so that failures on held-out or adversarial inputs count as genuine misrepresentations rather than mere quirks of description.
