I'm a senior in high school, and this semester our discrete math elective spent a couple of weeks on Gödel's incompleteness theorems, which sent me down a rabbit hole of reading about related results. That's how I found Löb's theorem and, through some old MIRI papers on what they call "tiling agents," what people in AI safety circles refer to as the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents. As I understand it, Löb's theorem says that if a formal system can prove the general statement "if I can prove P, then P is true," that alone forces the system to be able to prove P outright, so a system never really gets that kind of self-trust for free except in the trivial case where it could already prove P anyway. People working on self-modifying AI point out that this becomes a genuine practical wall: an agent whose entire method for acting is "only do what I can formally prove is good" can never fully prove, in that same rigorous sense, that a future, upgraded version of itself running the same policy will only do things that are actually good, because proving that kind of general reflection principle runs straight into Löb's theorem. So a perfectly consistent, rational agent still ends up stuck either trusting each new version of itself on faith or refusing to ever improve itself at all.

For English class, we've been asked to write a short story that uses a real philosophical or scientific problem as the actual engine of its plot — the way Asimov built stories around the Three Laws, or the way Greg Egan built Permutation City around ideas of consciousness and computation — rather than using science fiction as mere set dressing. I want to build mine around the Löbian obstacle, but I'm struggling to turn something this abstract into a story that isn't just a dialogue-shaped lecture. Could you write a short story of around 1,000 words centered on the moment when a research team, or perhaps the system itself, has to decide whether to activate a self-modified successor it cannot fully vouch for, knowing that the very same rigor that makes the current system trustworthy is exactly what makes that final proof of trust impossible? I'd rather the ending sit honestly with that tension than resolve it neatly.
