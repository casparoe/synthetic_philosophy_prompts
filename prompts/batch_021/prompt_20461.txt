I've spent the past week or so chasing a citation trail that started on LessWrong, in a thread about the "binding problem" in cognitive science — the puzzle of how a mind manages to represent that *this* redness and *that* roundness belong to a single apple, rather than floating free as separate, unconnected features. A commenter pointed out that philosophers had already been wrestling with a structurally similar puzzle back in 1893, in F.H. Bradley's *Appearance and Reality*, under what's now generally called "Bradley's regress." I've since read the Wikipedia and SEP summaries, and they confidently tell me *that* a vicious regress is supposed to arise here, but not how, mechanically, it actually falls out of Bradley's own sentences — and I'd rather work that out from the primary text than from someone else's gloss on it. Here is the passage I'm stuck on (Book I, Chapter III, "Relation and Quality," usually cited in the secondary literature as AR 27–28), which comes right after Bradley has argued that a relation can't be "nothing" to the qualities it relates without those qualities simply ceasing to be related at all:

> But how the relation can stand to the qualities is, on the other side, unintelligible. If it is nothing to the qualities, then they are not related at all; and, if so, as we saw, they have ceased to be qualities, and their relation is a nonentity. But if it is to be something to them, then clearly we now shall require a new connecting relation. For the relation hardly can be the mere adjective of one or both of its terms; or, at least, as such it seems indefensible.[1] And, being something itself, if it does not itself bear a relation to the terms, in what intelligible way will it succeed in being anything to them? But here again we are hurried off into the eddy of a hopeless process, since we are forced to go on finding new relations without end. The links are united by a link, and this bond of union is a link which also has two ends; and these require each a fresh link to connect them with the old. The problem is to find how the relation can stand to its qualities; and this problem is insoluble. If you take the connection as a solid thing, you have got to show, and you cannot show, how the other solids are joined to it. And, if you take it as a kind of medium or unsubstantial atmosphere, it is a connection no longer. You find, in this case, that the whole question of the relation of the qualities (for they certainly in some way are related) arises now outside it, in precisely the same form as before. The original relation, in short, has become a nonentity, but, in becoming this, it has removed no element of the problem.
>
> [1] The relation is not the adjective of one term, for, if so, it does not relate. Nor for the same reason is it the adjective of each term taken apart, for then again there is no relation between them. Nor is the relation their common property, for then what keeps them apart? They are now not two terms at all, because not separate. And within this new whole, in any case, the problem of inherence would break out in an aggravated form.

Two things are tripping me up. First, Bradley seems to treat the mere non-termination of the process — the fact that we're "forced to go on finding new relations without end" — as already sufficient to show the whole relational scheme is unintelligible. But an infinite series isn't automatically vicious; nobody loses sleep over the fact that "p," "it is true that p," "it is true that it is true that p," and so on, goes on forever. So what exactly, in this specific stretch of text, is supposed to make Bradley's regress vicious rather than benign in that way? Is he claiming that each further connecting relation would actually have to exist as a distinct item in order for the previous connection to obtain (a regress of being), or that each connection has to be understood via the next one before the previous connection counts as intelligible (a regress of explanation)? I can't tell whether Bradley is equivocating between these two readings in this passage, or whether there's some reason they collapse into the same requirement for him.

Second, the footnote rules out treating the connecting relation as an "adjective" via three sub-cases — adjective of one term, adjective of each term taken separately, and common property of both. Could you walk through why Bradley thinks each of these three fails, and then tell me whether, in ruling them out, the footnote also quietly forecloses the moves that later philosophers actually made in response to him (Russell's insistence that relating is simply the primitive job of an external, "independent" relation; or Armstrong's later idea that states of affairs unify their constituents through a non-mereological form of composition) — or whether the footnote is really aimed at a narrower target than that, leaving those later escape routes untouched by anything argued here.
