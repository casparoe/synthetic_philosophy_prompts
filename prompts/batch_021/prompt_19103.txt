I'm at work on a novel in which a preventable tragedy occurs because a whole succession of minor characters fail, each in some small and separate way, to intervene—no single failure sufficient on its own, yet the disaster would not have happened had any one of them acted differently. Before deciding how the narrative should apportion blame among them, I want to get clearer on the underlying philosophical terrain. Could you compare how counterfactual theories of causation (in the tradition of David Lewis) and physical-process or transference theories of causation (of the sort developed by Wesley Salmon and Phil Dowe) handle omissions of this kind? Does each recognize a mere "failure to act" as a genuine cause of the outcome, and if so, at what cost? My own sense is that counterfactual approaches risk multiplying causes absurdly, since anyone who could have helped and didn't will satisfy the relevant counterfactual, while process theories avoid that proliferation only by denying that omissions are ever really causes at all. If that's roughly right, I'd like your assessment of whether the law's device of a prior "duty to act," as discussed in Hart and Honoré's work on causation, actually resolves this dilemma at the level of metaphysics, or merely smuggles in a moral judgment to do the theory's work for it.
