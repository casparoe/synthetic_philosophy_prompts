Retired physicist here (condensed matter, mostly) -- spent the last year or so falling down the philosophy-of-physics rabbit hole, starting with Sean Carroll's entropy book and working my way into the primary lit from there. One thing I can't shake is the "Past Hypothesis" business: the idea that the universe (or at least our branch of it) started out in a wildly low-entropy state, and some people -- Albert and Loewer, with their "Mentaculus" picture, if I'm remembering the name right -- want to actually promote that boundary condition to a fundamental LAW of nature, on Humean best-system grounds. Other people think that's basically a category error: your dressing up a one-off initial condition as a law just because doing so buys a ton of explanatory power, when really it either needs some further (cosmological?) explanation, or should just be left alone as a brute contingent fact.

Could you write me a dialogue between two philosophers of physics on opposite sides of this? Make them equally sharp -- have the "it's a real law" side actually press the best-system case hard, and have the other side push back just as hard, e.g. on the arbitrariness of privileging the initial boundary over the final one, or on whether "probability" even makes sense applied to a one-off cosmic initial condition. I want genuine back-and-forth, each one responding to the other's actual best move rather than two monologues talking past each other, and it's totally fine if it doesn't resolve neatly at the end.
