Spent about 35 years doing statistical mechanics before I retired (entropy, the thermodynamic arrow of time, that whole business), and for the last year or so I've fallen down a philosophy rabbit hole reading about theories of causation. Wrote up some rough notes trying to sort out the difference between David Lewis's counterfactual theory and James Woodward's interventionist theory (which is basically dressed-up Judea Pearl, the causal graphs / do-calculus stuff I actually used a bunch in my own work). Can you look over what I've got, tell me if I'm actually getting the two theories right, and whether my worry at the end holds any water?

Here's the gist as I understand it. Lewis says C causes E just when E counterfactually depends on C (chained together to handle preemption) - cashed out via similarity between possible worlds. The famous headache is backtracking: naively, counterfactual dependence seems to run both ways in time, so why does causation only run forward? Lewis's fix, in his 1979 "time's arrow" paper, is a weighting system for similarity between worlds: a world that matches the actual past perfectly and diverges via one small, localized "miracle" right before the antecedent counts as closer than a world that changes the past to accomodate the antecedent, because changing the past means erasing every trace that event left scattered forward through time - one big, coordinated miracle instead of one small one. So causation's arrow ends up riding piggyback on a contingent "asymmetry of overdetermination" - roughly, the fact that traces of past events proliferate forward in time way more than traces of future events reach backward, which always struck me as entropy wearing a philosopher's hat.

Woodward and Pearl instead go structural: X causes Y if some hypothetical surgical intervention on X - one that only touches X, cuts its other incoming arrows, and doesn't sneak around through some other route to Y - would change Y. No possible-world similarity metric needed, which is presumably why it's so much more usable for actual science.

My worry: doesn't the interventionist story just quietly help itself to the very asymmetry Lewis was sweating to earn? An "intervention" by construction only reaches forward, and you need the graph's upstream/downstream structure laid out correctly before you can even define one in the first place. So it looks like Woodward is presupposing the causal (and temporal) order as a primitive, while Lewis is at least attempting to derive it from something further down - even if his derivation is famously janky. Is that an actual, recognized problem with interventionism, or is there an obvious response I'm missing? Mainly just want to know if I've mangled either theory, so don't sugarcoat it.
