I'm a PhD student in history and philosophy of science, currently drafting a dissertation chapter on Thomas Hobbes's philosophy of mathematics and how it bears on his broader theory of demonstrative knowledge. This past semester I've been working through the long and rather embarrassing dispute between Hobbes and John Wallis, which was sparked by the purported quadrature of the circle that Hobbes appended to De Corpore in 1655, promptly demolished by Wallis in his Elenchus Geometriae Hobbianae, and which nonetheless dragged on for over two decades as Hobbes kept returning with new "solutions," right up until his death in 1679. What comes through clearly in the secondary literature on the affair (Jesseph's history of the dispute especially) is that this was never simply a matter of Hobbes making a careless slip: his confidence in his constructions was tied to substantive philosophical commitments, among them his insistence that geometrical objects are ultimately bodies and motions rather than abstract or "indivisible" quantities, his suspicion of the increasingly algebraic and symbolic methods that Wallis and his contemporaries were importing into geometry, and his broader conviction that geometry is uniquely certain among the sciences precisely because we ourselves construct its objects out of definitions we lay down.

All of which has left me wondering, half-seriously, how Hobbes would have responded had he somehow lived to see Ferdinand von Lindemann's 1882 proof that π is transcendental -- a result which, building on Hermite's earlier proof of the transcendence of e, finally settled that no straightedge-and-compass construction can square the circle. Would Hobbes, judged by his own epistemological standards, have had to accept this as a genuine demonstration and concede final defeat, or do his specific and rather idiosyncratic views -- about what geometrical objects are, about which methods count as properly demonstrative, and about the relation between algebra and geometry -- give him principled resources for refusing to recognize Lindemann's argument as a geometrical refutation at all, as opposed to simply digging in out of stubbornness, as he arguably did against Wallis? I would like your own considered assessment of this question, engaging as closely as you can with the specifics of Hobbes's actual views on mathematical method and demonstration, rather than a generic statement about wounded pride in a defeated seventeenth-century mathematician.
