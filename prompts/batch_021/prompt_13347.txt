I'm working on a feature about eternal-inflation cosmology, and before I write it up I want to work through its strangest internal dispute properly rather than just describe it: the Boltzmann brain problem. If a region of spacetime settles permanently into a de Sitter-like vacuum, rare thermal and quantum fluctuations will, over infinite time, spontaneously assemble far more isolated, momentary brains with false memories of a coherent past than the finite number of ordinary observers produced by the universe's actual history of star and galaxy formation -- so a cosmology with an eternal de Sitter future seems to predict that any observer, including us, is overwhelmingly more likely to be a freak fluctuation than a genuine product of cosmic evolution. Physicists such as Don Page and Andreas Albrecht treat this as a genuine defect that such theories must somehow escape, whether through vacuum decay, a different choice of measure, or rejecting the model outright; others invoke the measure problem in eternal inflation -- central to work by Alan Guth, Raphael Bousso, and their collaborators -- to argue that any verdict on whether Boltzmann brains actually dominate is measure-dependent and therefore inconclusive. Write an essay that presents the strongest form of both positions and defends a clear verdict: does Boltzmann-brain domination give physicists genuine grounds to constrain or reject a cosmological model, or is this probability reasoning pushed past the point where it still yields a meaningful conclusion?
