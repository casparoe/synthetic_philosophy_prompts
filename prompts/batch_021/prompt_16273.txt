In my philosophy of law seminar we just worked through the Hart–Fuller exchange in the 1958 Harvard Law Review: Hart's separability thesis, defended via the grudge-informer case, against Fuller's argument that a system flouting the internal morality of law (his desiderata of generality, promulgation, prospectivity, clarity, non-contradiction, possibility of compliance, constancy, and congruence between declared and administered rules) forfeits its claim to be law regardless of pedigree. I'm curious how far their disagreement actually extends into a case neither of them could have anticipated. In June 2016, an attacker exploited a bug in the smart-contract code governing "The DAO," a pooled investment vehicle built on the Ethereum blockchain, to siphon out a substantial share of its holdings through a sequence of transactions that followed the literal code exactly, even though they defeated every purpose the code's authors and investors understood it to serve. The Ethereum community responded with a deeply contested retroactive hard fork that reversed the theft, a move a minority rejected on the principle that the code's execution should be the final word, splitting off to preserve the original chain as Ethereum Classic. Reconstructing only the actual arguments Hart and Fuller made against each other, how do you think each of them would have judged (a) whether the attacker's transaction was legally "valid" under the DAO's own rules, and (b) whether the hard fork was a legitimate correction, or instead exactly the kind of covert retroactivity that, on Hart's own view, should only ever be undertaken openly? Finally, say which of the two reconstructed positions strikes you as more defensible once the "law" in question is self-executing code rather than a judicially administered statute.
