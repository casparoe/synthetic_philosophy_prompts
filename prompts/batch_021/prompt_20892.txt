"Question 4 (15 points). In 1961, Daniel Ellsberg described a decision scenario, now known as the Ellsberg paradox, that is usually taken as evidence against the claim that a rational person's degrees of belief can always be represented by a single, sharp probability function.
(a) [5 pts] Describe the scenario and the pattern of preferences that generates the puzzle.
(b) [4 pts] Explain precisely why this pattern of preferences is difficult to reconcile with orthodox subjective expected utility theory -- the framework associated with Savage and de Finetti.
(c) [3 pts] 'Imprecise probability' theories respond to the puzzle by representing a rational agent's belief state with a set of probability functions (a 'credal set') rather than a single one. Explain, in your own words, how this move is supposed to make room for the preferences described in (a).
(d) [3 pts] Give one substantive objection to this imprecise-probability response, and say briefly whether you find it persuasive."

And here is the answer I've been asked to grade:

"(a) Ellsberg's scenario involves two urns. Urn 1 contains 50 red balls and 50 black balls, so the odds there are exactly even and known. Urn 2 also contains 100 red and black balls, but you aren't told the proportion -- it could be anything from all red to all black. You pick a color and an urn, and win a prize if you draw that color. Most people, asked to bet on red, would rather draw from Urn 1 than Urn 2. Strikingly, most people also prefer Urn 1 over Urn 2 when betting on black. So the same urn is being avoided regardless of which color is being bet on, which is the heart of the puzzle: it looks like people aren't simply forming an estimate of the odds in Urn 2 and betting accordingly.

(b) This is a problem for orthodox Bayesian decision theory because the theory is never given any stated odds for Urn 2 to work with -- since no probability for red versus black in Urn 2 is handed to the subject in advance, expected-utility maximization has nothing to compute with, and so the theory cannot generate any prediction about which urn a rational person should prefer. What Ellsberg's experiment really reveals, then, is an empirical fact about ordinary people's psychology -- that they dislike unknown odds -- rather than a genuine logical difficulty internal to the theory itself.

(c) The imprecise-probability response says we should stop insisting that the agent's belief about Urn 2 be squeezed into one number. Instead, the agent's credence is represented by a whole range of candidate probabilities for red, say anywhere from 0 to 1. Once you have given up a single number like this, the only decision rule that does not quietly smuggle back exactly the kind of unmotivated precise probability the theory was trying to avoid in the first place is to judge each bet by its worst-case expected value across the whole range: betting on red from Urn 2 then has a worst-case value of zero (if the balls turn out to be all black), and betting on black from Urn 2 also has a worst-case value of zero (if they turn out to be all red). So both bets can reasonably come out worse than the guaranteed fifty-fifty odds of Urn 1, without ever needing a single number for the probability of red in Urn 2 and a complementary number for black that add up to one in the usual way.

(d) One serious objection is that judging every bet purely by its worst-case value throws away almost everything else in the credal set. Two agents could have very different overall beliefs about Urn 2 -- one who thinks literally any mix from all-red to all-black is on the table, another who is fairly confident the true mix is close to fifty-fifty but still won't completely rule out either extreme -- and as long as their worst-case values agree, this decision rule treats the two agents as making identical choices. That seems like a real cost, since it makes an agent's decisions insensitive to differences in belief that plainly matter. I find this fairly persuasive, though I can see why someone who wants a genuinely robust, worst-case-proof procedure might be willing to pay that price."
