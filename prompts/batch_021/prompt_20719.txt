I spent my career as an astrophysicist, and now that I am retired I have been working my way through the philosophy of time. The argument that has really unsettled me is the Rietdijk–Putnam reasoning, in the vivid form Penrose gives it as the "Andromeda paradox": because simultaneity is relative, two people merely strolling past one another on a street can have planes of simultaneity that disagree — by hours or days, depending on their walking speed — over whether some distant civilization's fateful decision has already been made. That seems to leave the presentist without any coherent, frame-independent set of events to call uniquely real. A philosopher friend insists this is too quick, however, and that serious presentists have specific replies rather than a shrug. Could you write a dialogue, set just after a colloquium on relativity of simultaneity, in which a presentist and an eternalist work through this together? The presentist should mount one considered strategy in real detail — a neo-Lorentzian appeal to a privileged but undetectable frame, say, or Howard Stein's proposal that objective becoming is only definable pointwise, never across a whole simultaneity surface — rather than simply insisting that time still feels like it passes; the eternalist should press the actual costs of whichever strategy gets chosen. I would like several rounds of genuine back-and-forth, with both sides equally rigorous and no artificially tidy resolution at the end.
