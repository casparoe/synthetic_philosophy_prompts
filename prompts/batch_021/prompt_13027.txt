A friend of mine, who is midway through a PhD in decision theory, and I spent most of last night arguing about the centipede game, and neither of us managed to convince the other. We agreed on the setup and on the standard backward-induction argument for why fully rational players should defect immediately: the mover at the very last node has no future left to protect, so she should take; anticipating this, the mover at the second-to-last node knows that passing will only let her opponent claim the larger share at the next step, so she should take too, rather than pass and end up worse off than if she had taken herself; and this reasoning unravels all the way back to the first move, so that full, common rationality apparently requires defecting immediately, even though mutual cooperation to the end would leave both players much better off. Where we disagreed was over whether this argument is genuinely coherent, and not merely a poor predictor of how people behave in experiments.

My friend pressed what she called the "paradox" reading of the game, tracing it to an argument associated with Pettit and Sugden that Aumann's later, formal treatment of common knowledge of rationality was meant to head off. The worry is this: the backward-induction argument only tells you to defect at some node k if you can still rely on common belief in your opponent's rationality holding at k and afterward. But if the players really were commonly believed to be rational all along, a "late" node reached after several rounds of mutual cooperation should never have come about in the first place. So either reaching node k already counts as evidence that common belief in rationality has broken down, in which case you lose your grounds for expecting the other player to defect from k onward and the whole rationale for defecting at k collapses; or else that belief is read dispositionally, as a fact about what each player would do at every node whether it is reached or not, robust to any apparent counter-evidence, in which case it becomes unclear why a player should defect early purely out of fear of a future defection that, on this reading, no observed deviation could ever make more or less likely. I tried to sketch a way of using Aumann's framework to escape this dilemma, but I could not do so convincingly on the spot, and my friend remained unpersuaded.

I would like your help finishing the argument I could not make. Please argue, as rigorously as you can, for the "paradox" side of this dispute: that no notion of common knowledge, or common belief, of rationality can both (i) be strong enough to generate the full backward-induction unraveling from the very first move of the centipede game, and (ii) remain stable under conditioning on the "off-path" information that some later node has actually been reached, without the resulting argument either begging the question or losing its normative force. Please engage directly with the dispositional, "rational-at-every-node" move that is meant to rescue backward induction from exactly this kind of objection, rather than resting your case on the purely empirical observation that real players tend not to defect immediately.
