I've spent the past few weeks reporting on how economists and philosophers justify the discount rates built into climate models, pension design, and long-term infrastructure planning, and one piece of the underlying logic keeps resisting a clean explanation for my readers. The standard "Ramsey rule" splits the social discount rate into two ingredients: one term reflecting the fact that future generations will likely be richer, so an additional unit of consumption matters less to them, and a separate "pure" rate of time preference, which discounts future well-being for no reason other than its being future — that is, even holding fixed how large the benefit is, how certain it is to occur, and how much it would matter to whoever experiences it. What I find striking is that Frank Ramsey, the mathematician after whom the rule is named, treated this second ingredient as almost an embarrassment: in his 1928 paper on saving he called discounting later enjoyment against earlier enjoyment "ethically indefensible," and argued that a rational planner should set it at or near zero. Henry Sidgwick had made a related argument decades earlier, insisting that the mere fact that a moment of happiness lies further off in time is not, by itself, a reason to weigh it less heavily than one closer at hand. Almost every philosopher I've interviewed for this piece treats a positive rate of pure time preference the way Ramsey did: as defensible only as a failure of imagination, or as a convenient fudge for avoiding the very demanding sacrifices that zero discounting would impose on the present generation.

For balance, I'd like to give readers the most rigorous version of the opposing case, and so far every source I've asked to defend a positive rate ends up quietly smuggling in something else instead — the chance that civilization doesn't survive long enough to collect the benefit, say, or the fact that future people may not exist at all — rather than defending pure time preference on its own terms. Could you construct the strongest argument you can that discounting future well-being simply because it is future, independent of any such uncertainty about whether or how much it will occur, is nonetheless rationally or ethically defensible? I'd be especially interested in whether the argument can also explain why the same reasoning would not equally license discounting the well-being of people who are merely distant in space rather than in time, since that's an objection my sources keep raising against each other without ever quite settling it.
