I'm reporting a feature on how families navigate a parent's last wishes, and while interviewing two brothers for it, I ended up as an unwilling referee for an argument they haven't dropped. Their father, before he died, had made both of them promise to scatter his ashes off a cliff in Donegal where he'd once proposed to their mother. He died that spring, and by the time the family could make the trip, a storm-damaged coastal road and a new grandchild's arrival had made it impractical, so the younger brother, Felix, suggested a local reservoir instead, "since at least it's done." His older brother, Owen, said that isn't keeping the promise at all, and the two of them have been going back and forth about it ever since.

The argument, which happened right in front of me, went roughly like this. Owen said a promise binds you the moment you give your word, and their father's death doesn't cancel that — if anything it locks the obligation in place, since he's no longer around to renegotiate it or release them from it. Felix said that gets the structure of obligation backwards: a duty has to be owed to somebody, and a dead man isn't a subject who can hold a claim against the living anymore, so finishing the errand is now an act of devotion rather than the discharge of a debt — you can't wrong someone who no longer exists to be wronged. Owen said that can't be the whole story, since we obviously do think you can wrong the dead in some sense: executors are bound by a will, and betraying a secret someone told you in life still feels like a betrayal of them after they've died, not a victimless act now that they're gone. Felix said both of those are really obligations to the living in disguise — wills are enforced for the heirs' sake and the state's interest in orderly succession, and secrets matter for what exposing them would do to the living person's reputation and relationships; strip away every living stakeholder, he said, and there's nothing left that could be wronged.

Neither brother has budged, and I'd like a more careful referee than I managed to be in the moment. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right about what — where each brother's reasoning rests on a genuinely defensible view about why promises bind, and where it overreaches or helps itself to something it hasn't earned? I'm especially curious whether "an obligation needs a living party who can hold the claim" is actually true of promises in general, or only seems true because most promises happen to be made to people who stick around to collect on them — and, either way, what, if anything, both brothers are missing about what made this particular promise binding in the first place.
