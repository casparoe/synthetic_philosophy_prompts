A couple of weekends ago, a few friends and I got into what turned into a much bigger argument than I expected, all because of a betting game one of them, Callum, had read about somewhere and wanted to try out at a gathering. The rules were simple: two players alternate turns, the pot starts at two dollars, and on each turn the player to move can either "take" -- ending the game immediately and pocketing the larger share of whatever is currently in the pot, leaving the smaller share for the other player -- or "pass," which doubles the pot and hands the decision to the other player, up to a cap of six turns. Callum played it against our friend Sasha and took the money on his very first turn, ending the game for two dollars total, even though if they had both kept passing all the way to the sixth turn they would have split a pot worth well over a hundred dollars. Sasha thought this was a ridiculous way to play and said so, and the two of them ended up going back and forth about it for the rest of the night. I looked the game up afterward and it turns out to be a real thing that game theorists study, called the centipede game, and the specific assumption Callum and Sasha were fighting about even has its own name: common knowledge of rationality.

Here is roughly how the exchange went. (1) Callum said taking immediately was simply the correct move: reasoning backward from the last possible turn, the player facing that turn should obviously take rather than pass, since there is no future turn left in which passing could pay off; and if both players can see that coming, the player one turn earlier should take too, rather than pass into a situation where the other player will simply take everything next; and this logic unravels all the way back to turn one, so two players who are rational, and who each know the other is rational, and know that they know it, and so on, should never let the pot grow at all. (2) Sasha replied that a chain of reasoning which concludes two rational people should walk away with two dollars each, when ten more seconds of cooperation were available to both of them, is a strange thing to call rational, and that the argument undermines itself besides: it depends on each player being certain the other will reason the same way all the way down, yet the theory itself says a rational player facing a rational opponent should never pass -- so if the game ever did reach a later turn, that would already prove the certainty false, and it is not obvious what the theory is supposed to tell either player to do from that point on. (3) Callum answered that plenty of useful models lean on idealizations that do not hold exactly, in the same way economists keep using rational-agent models despite knowing real people are not perfectly rational, and that the backward-induction logic still identifies the uniquely rational move at any given turn, regardless of what happened on earlier turns. (4) Sasha was not satisfied: she said that doing what the argument says regardless of what you have just watched happen is a strange definition of rational, and that if a friend calmly passing on turn three is supposed to be evidence of something, a theory that tells you to disregard it anyway has gotten the idea of rationality backward.

Neither of them budged, and when I mentioned it to a third friend a few days later, she told me this exact disagreement has apparently kept real game theorists and philosophers busy for decades without a clean resolution, which did not make me feel any better about being asked to referee it.
