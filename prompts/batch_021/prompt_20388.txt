I spent about thirty-five years doing insect systematics before I retired — describing new weevil species, endless arguments with colleagues about lumping versus splitting — and the whole time I basically treated "species" the way a chemist treats "element": a category you sort specimens into becuase they share the right defining traits. Since retiring I've been sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group at the local university, and a couple weeks ago we worked through some Ghiselin and Hull, including that stapled photocopy everyone in the group seems to already own of Hull's "A Matter of Individuality." Their claim that species are actually individuals rather than natural kinds or classes really threw me. I get the surface analogy — I'm a member of the Kowalski family by descent, not because I satisfy some "Kowalski-ness" property — but I couldnt tell in the meeting whether this is a genuinely load-bearing philosophical claim or just a relabeling exercise that happens to sound profound.

Could you help me actually understand what work the individuality thesis is supposed to do? Two things are nagging me in particular. First, if a species is an individual made up of organisms as its "parts," the way an organism is made of cells, what stops literally any ancestrally-connected chunk of the tree of life — a genus, a whole clade, a population that only lasted one breeding season — from also qualifying as an "individual" in exactly the same sense? Something has to be doing the work of drawing the boundary around just the right chunk, and I don't see what it is. Second, does accepting the thesis actually change anything downstream, or is it basically inert — does it, say, have anything to do with why evolutionary biology doesn't seem to have strict, exceptionless "laws" the way physics does, or with whether a name like "Panthera leo" functions more like a proper name than like a definition-based kind term? I've poked at Sober's old textbook chapter on this but I still feel like I'm missing the punchline everyone else in the group seemed to already get.
