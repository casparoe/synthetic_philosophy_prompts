So for the past few weeks I've been going down this whole AI safety rabbit hole (started as a distraction from studying for my calc test, zero regrets) and I keep running into this thing people call the "Löbian obstacle." From what I can piece together: there's a math result, Löb's theorem, that says if a formal system like Peano arithmetic can prove the general statement "if I can prove P, then P is actually true," then it turns out the system could've just proved P outright — you don't get free trust in your own provability, it collapses into you actually having the proof already. AI safety people use this to argue that an AI can't fully trust a future version of itself, or a successor agent it builds, using a rule like "whatever future-me / my successor proves is safe, actually is safe," because getting that rule to hold in general is basically as hard as just solving safety directly, and if you bolt it on as an extra axiom instead you risk contradictions. It gets treated as a real obstacle to self-modifying AI ever being able to verify its own safety.

Here's my actual question though. This sounds suspiciously like way older problems in epistemology about not being able to vouch for your own reasoning by using that same reasoning — like Hume's point that you can't justify induction without already using induction (which is circular), or the "problem of the criterion" thing about needing a standard to check your beliefs against while also needing already-checked beliefs to get the standard in the first place. Can you compare the Löbian obstacle specifically with Hume's problem of induction (bring in the problem of the criterion or the Cartesian circle too if you think it's actually useful, not just for padding)? I want to know two things: where does the Löbian obstacle basically boil down to that same old self-trust regress just wearing a math costume, and where is it genuinely doing something new because it's about formal provability rather than perception or induction? And — this is the part my friend and I keep arguing about and can't settle — is the Löb's theorem version actually more airtight and harder to escape than the informal philosophical versions, or does it just look scarier because it's dressed up in logic notation?
