So I'm teaching a seminar this term on 19th-century social philosophy, and we just spent a session on Kierkegaard's *Two Ages* — specifically the part that usually gets excerpted on its own as "The Present Age" (I'm using the Hong translation, for what it's worth). My students latch onto the soundbite version immediately — Kierkegaard hated newspapers, modern life is "leveled" and shallow, cue easy comparisons to Twitter, everyone nods, we move on to Marx. Which is fine as far as it goes, but it means nobody actually engages with the moving parts of the argument, and I'd like to nail those down more precisely myself before I teach this again next year.

Here's specifically what I want unpacked. Kierkegaard treats "the public" as a genuinly novel kind of collective entity, and he's pretty insistent that it's categorically different from a people, a generation, an association, or any other concrete grouping people belong to — and, more strongly, that the press doesn't just report on or serve this public, but actually conjures it into existence in the first place. So mechanically, how's that supposed to work? What is it about print, anonymity, and the kind of "opinion without commitment" the press enables that turns a bunch of scattered individuals into "a public" rather than, say, a community or a movement? And where does "leveling" (Nivellering, I think, though don't quote me on the Danish) fit into that story — I don't think he means it in the simple sense of "everyone becoming more equal," there's something more dialectical going on there, maybe even something that could cut in a positive direction later in his authorship, and my students always flatten that nuance right out. If you've got room, I'd also genuinely love to know whether this is aimed at the same target as the "the crowd is untruth" line from elsewhere in his work, or a distinct idea that just rhymes with it. One thing I don't want, though, is the Corsair-affair biographical explanation — yes, I know about the cartoons mocking his trouser legs, I don't need that angle again — I want the conceptual machinery, not the grudge.
