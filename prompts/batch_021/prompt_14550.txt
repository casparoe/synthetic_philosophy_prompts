So I've been chipping away at a longform piece on the dark matter vs. MOND fight in cosmology, and a couple weeks ago I sat in on a "Philosophy Meets Physics" panel that went a bit sideways during Q&A. The panel had an astrophysicist, Dana, whose spent her career on galaxy rotation curves, and a philosopher of science, Milo, who was there for big-picture commentary. Someone in the audience asked why the field hasn't just settled the dark matter/MOND question after 40-odd years, and the two of them fell into a genuinely heated back-and-forth I've been chewing on ever sense.

Dana went first: every time a new observation threatens either camp, its defenders bolt on some new patch — dark matter gets a tweaked particle profile or halo shape, MOND gets a relativistic extension or an extra free function for clusters — so neither side has technically been refuted. Doesn't that just mean, she asked, that the choice between them is underdetermined by the evidence, full stop, and everything past that point is sociology and taste? Milo pushed back: real Duhem-Quine underdetermination requires the rivals to be empirically equivalent, and these two aren't — cold dark matter made independent predictions (the relative heights of the CMB's acoustic peaks, how fast structure has to grow to look like the cosmic web we actually see) that were later confirmed, while MOND's cluster-scale fixes have mostly been reactive rescues, not predictions. That's why the field lopsidedly favors dark matter even though MOND hasn't been logically killed off. Dana shot back that "independent prediction" vs. "reactive patch" is exactly the kind of call Duhem himself said only "good sense" could make, not something read straight off the data — so Milo had just relocated the underdetermination one level up, to which patches count as principled. Milo granted theory choice always involves that kind of judgment, but said Duhem's actual thesis was narrower than people assume — limited to physics, and resolvable (he thought) by that same good sense — and that the "evidence basically never decides" reading is really Quine's later, more radical spin, often pinned on Duhem by people who haven't read him closely. Dana's comeback: from inside a live controversy, working scientists usually can't tell a principled patch from an ad hoc one until well after the fact, once one side has racked up a track record — so the distinction Milo's leaning on isn't actually available at the moment it would need to do any work.

Neither of them budged, and I didn't want to referee it on the spot. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who had the better of each point, rather than just handing one of them the overall win? And is there a real underdetermination worry tangled up in this whole debate that neither of them quite managed to put their finger on?
