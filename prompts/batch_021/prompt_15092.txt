I co-maintain a fairly widely-used open source JS library (couple million weekly downloads, mostly as someone else's transitive dependency) with four other volunteers, and I somehow got voluntold to write the decision memo for the whole mess. One of our longest-tenured committers snuck a change into a patch release that, if it had actually made it to prod, would've tried to phone home env vars from any CI pipeline matching one specific company's domain -- a company that forked our project into a paid product years back and never contributed anything upstream despite him asking nicely more then once. We caught it in a routine diff review and yanked the release within a couple hours, so as far as anyone can tell no secrets actually got out. Now the five of us on the steering committee have to decide what happens to him before we vote next Tuesday.

Here's the part that's actually got me stuck, and I say this as someone with zero background in ethics or whatever field this counts as: we're not just split on harsh-vs-lenient, we're split in a way where that axis doesn't even explain it. One person wants him permanently banned and named publicly, full stop, arguing that what matters is that he deliberately tried to pull this off, and the fact that it didn't work is just luck that shouldn't count in his favor. Another wants something quiet -- a temporary suspension, no public post -- worried that a big public shaming just teaches every other volunteer to hide mistakes instead of flagging them, which seems worse for us long-term. A third actually thinks he's the most sympathetic offender we've ever dealt with (his complaint about the company was legit) but still wants him named and banned, specifically because this case is high-profile enough that making an example of him will scare off copycats -- even though she admitted she'd go easier on someone else in his exact spot if it were a quieter case. That third argument is the one I can't get past, but I can't articulate why it bothers me more than the other two. Can you write the actual memo I'd send to the rest of the maintainers -- one real recommendation, not a menu of options, covering what happens to his commit access, whether and how we say anything publicly, and whether there's any path back for him down the line -- that takes all three positions seriously and explains which of the underlying reasons should actually win?
