My hospital just made everyone redo mandatory implicit bias training (there was A Whole Thing after a complaint last year), and the facilitator showed us that classic shoot/don't-shoot study — the one where people playing a fast reaction game are more trigger-happy with unarmed Black men than unarmed white men, and slower to shoot armed white men than armed Black guys. Fine, I already believed bias is real, that's not the part that got me. It's that he then said the reason these trainings don't really "stick" is that the bias isn't happening in your judgement at all, its happening in perception itself — like your visual system has already tagged the guy as more threatening before any conscious decision-making even starts, so you can't will your way out of it any more than you can will your way out of an optical illusion. He used that to argue we should stop trying to "check our biases" in the moment (too late by then) and just redesign the environment so snap judgements matter less.

This has been rattling around in my head all week because I make fast calls on who "looks" like they're in serious pain constantly, and if it's really perceptual and not judgement, training feels almost like theater. I brought it up with a friend doing her PhD in cognitive psych and she just made a face and said "that's... not exactly settled" before getting pulled into a meeting. So: is it actually established that this kind of bias lives in perception itself rather than in the split-second judgment calls that happen right after perceiving, and does the answer even change whether training could help?
