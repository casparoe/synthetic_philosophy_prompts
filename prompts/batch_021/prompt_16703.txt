Over dinner last night, a friend and I fell into a long argument about junk DNA that neither of us was willing to let go of. She was defending the ENCODE consortium's headline claim that something like eighty percent of the human genome is "functional," since so much of it shows measurable biochemical activity: transcription, transcription-factor binding, distinctive chromatin marks, and the like. I pushed back with the rebuttal from evolutionary biologists -- I want to say it was Graur and colleagues, in a paper that memorably compares the genome to a television set -- that this conflates having some current causal effect inside the cell with being there because it has that effect, and that on the latter, selection-based reading, most of the genome really is the inert or parasitic junk it looks like. She accused me of just stipulating my preferred definition of "function" and declaring victory. Having slept on it, I am no longer sure who had the better of the argument. Could you work through how Larry Wright's etiological account of function and Robert Cummins's causal-role account would each actually rule on the ENCODE case, and then tell me whether my friend and I were having a real disagreement about the genome or merely talking past each other with two incompatible notions of "function" -- and, if it is the latter, which notion strikes you as the more defensible account of biological function in general?
