I recently joined a small forecasting startup where users trade on conditional questions such as "If the central bank raises rates this quarter, will the housing price index fall by more than five percent?"; the market-clearing price is meant to track the true conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent. At a product review, one of our academic advisors invoked David Lewis's triviality results to argue that this whole premise is confused: since Lewis proved that no proposition can be assigned a probability matching the corresponding conditional probability except in degenerate cases, she concluded that there is no stable quantity for a market like ours to be discovering in the first place. I've since skimmed the 1976 paper myself, but I'm not a logician, and I can't tell whether that is a fair gloss of what Lewis actually established or a significant overreach. Which is it, and what exactly do the triviality results rule in and out?
