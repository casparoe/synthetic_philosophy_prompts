And here's the student's answer: "For Ames and Rosemont, Confucian role ethics is best understood as virtue ethics adapted to a relational picture of the person, rather than a totally seperate species of theory. Where Aristotle locates virtue in an individual soul with a fixed function aimed at eudaimonia, Confucius locates it in the relations themselves: a person just is their roles as son, father, elder brother, or minister, and to be good is to perform these roles well. Ren (仁), usually rendered 'benevolence,' isn't a trait a self has in isolation -- it's achieved only through relations with others, which is why Confucius says that filial piety and fraternal submission are 'the root of ren' (Analects 1.2). Relationships in the wulun are meant to be correlative rather than one-directional -- a father has to be loving for a son's filiality to mean anything -- and li (ritual propriety) gives these relations their concrete, everyday form. Because there's no self prior to or apart from the roles it occupies, I find the relational picture convincing, and I'd say it mainly amounts to a more sophisticated, relationally grounded version of virtue ethics rather than a wholly new category of theory."
