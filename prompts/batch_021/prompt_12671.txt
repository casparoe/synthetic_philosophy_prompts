Ok, so I'm working on a piece about scientific progress and fell down a rabbit hole on Popper's old idea of "verisimilitude" that I can't climb back out of by myself. Popper wanted a rigorous way of saying a false theory (Newtonian mechanics, say) can be closer to the truth than another false theory (Kepler's laws), even though both are technically false. His formal definition cashed this out as roughly "truth content minus falsity content" -- but then Tichy and Miller independently proved in 1974 that on his own definition, you can never actually find two false theories where one comes out more verisimilar than the other, which was kind of the whole point of the exercise. Since then people have tried "closeness to the truth" fixes instead (Niiniluoto, Oddie, etc), built around distance between possible worlds, but critics say this just relocates the problem, since how similar two theories are seems to depend on which langauge or set of properties you use to carve up logical space -- a weird thing for a supposedly objective notion to depend on.

So: is verisimilitude a coherent, rescuable idea, or a dead end? Write me an essay that actually engages with the technical objections on both sides, not a highschool-level "what is truth" survey, and lands on a real verdict -- either defend some specific account of truthlikeness and explain how it dodges the problems above, or argue the whole project is hopeless and say what should replace it in our picture of scientific progress. Don't hedge your way into a "both sides have a point" ending.
