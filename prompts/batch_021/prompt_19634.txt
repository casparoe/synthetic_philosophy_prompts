I've spent enough time on LessWrong and its offshoots that I thought I had the reductionist picture pretty well internalized: talk of beliefs, intentions, and reasons is just a higher-level, compressed way of describing patterns that are ultimately implemented in physics, and that's not supposed to be a threat to the reality of those higher-level things. Yudkowsky's old posts on reductionism convinced me of that years ago. But a personal situation has recently reopened the question in a way I haven't been able to argue myself out of.

I've been in treatment for depression for about a year, combining an SSRI with fairly standard CBT-style talk therapy. My psychiatrist, half-jokingly, summed up the division of labor like this: your mood is downstream of serotonin and a couple of other neurotransmitter systems, full stop; therapy "works," to the extent it does, only because restructuring your thoughts happens to shift that same underlying chemistry too. That comment has bothered me more than it probably should. If my mood, and any change in it, is fully caused by some physical state of my brain, and any mental-level event I might point to — "I noticed the belief that I'm a burden was distorted, and revised it" — is itself nothing over and above some further brain state, then the physical story already seems to supply a complete, sufficient chain of causation from one brain state to the next. Bringing in the "reasons" story as a second cause of the same mood shift looks like it either duplicates a cause that was already sufficient on its own, or gets crowded out by it. So is my noticing and revising anything more than a description running alongside changes in brain chemistry that were going to happen regardless — a story with no independent causal traction of its own?

I put this to a friend from my in-person rationalist meetup, and we argued about it for an hour without either of us being able to say exactly which premise was doing the illegitimate work. I have a strong suspicion this is a recognized problem in philosophy of mind, with its own name and literature, rather than a puzzle I stumbled into over dinner — and that whatever's wrong with the argument is probably wrong in an instructive way. Where does the reasoning actually break down, and what does that literature suggest about whether the "reasons" story is doing any real causal work?
