I'm taking a seminar on consequentialist ethics this term, and I also sit on the five-member committee that allocates our university's Student Sustainability Fund. Two finalists remain for this cycle's $30,000: a rooftop solar retrofit with an audited estimate of near-term CO2 reductions, and a student campaign lobbying the board of trustees for fossil-fuel divestment, whose case for impact depends on a chain of further steps — whether divestment moves capital, whether that pressures the industry, whether that eventually changes emissions — that none of us can quantify with any confidence. Two committee members, invoking what our seminar has been calling the "cluelessness objection" to consequentialism, want to fund only the retrofit, since the divestment campaign's impact is unknowable past a certain point and so, they argue, isn't commensurable with an audited figure. A third member calls this arbitrary: the retrofit's own downstream effects — on the campus energy budget, on the projects it forecloses, on the grid it feeds into decades out — are just as unknown, and she argues that the real question is not whether either proposal's long-run effects are unknowable but whether anyone has grounds for assuming those unknowns cancel out rather than tilt systematically in one direction, which no one on the committee has actually argued. I need to circulate the committee's decision memo before next week's vote. Please draft it: recommend a specific allocation between the two proposals — not necessarily an even split — and engage the third member's point directly, rather than simply asserting that the two cases are, or aren't, alike.
