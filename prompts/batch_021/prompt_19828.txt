Here's the question: "Solomonoff induction is often described as a way of making Occam's razor mathematically precise. Explain how it favors simpler hypotheses over more complex ones, and then respond to the following objection: since Kolmogorov complexity is only defined relative to a choice of universal Turing machine, and two different machines can rank the very same pair of hypotheses differently, isn't the whole scheme arbitrary rather than an objective fact about simplicity?"

And here's the student's answer: "Solomonoff induction puts a prior over every hypothesis that could be generating the data, where a hypothesis is just a program, and it weights each one by 2^-K(h), K(h) being the hypothesis's Kolmogorov complexity, i.e. the length of the shortest program that produces it. Since that weight shrinks exponentially as K(h) grows, shorter programs get way more prior mass than longer ones, so out of all the hypotheses that fit the data equally well, the shorter one automatically wins out. That's the sense in which the razor gets made precise: simple theories aren't assumed to be true, they're assumed to be more probable up front, and then you update on evidence with Bayes' rule as usual.

The objection is fair as far as it goes - K(x) really isn't an intrinsic property of a string, it's relative to whatever universal machine defines it, and the same string can be cheap to describe in one machine's language and expensive in another's. But this is precisely what the invariance theorem takes care of: for any two universal machines, the complexities they assign to a given string can never differ by more than some fixed constant, basically the cost of one machine simulating the other. So as you move to more and more complex hypotheses, that fixed constant becomes negligible next to the size of the complexities you're comparing, which means any two universal machines will end up agreeing on which of two complicated hypotheses is simpler. So the arbitrariness really only bites for short, toy-example strings; for the kind of rich hypotheses actual scientific theories are, the ranking becomes essentially machine-independent, and the razor comes out objective after all, just objective 'in the limit' rather than exactly."
