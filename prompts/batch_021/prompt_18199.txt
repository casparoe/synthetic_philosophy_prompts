Our physics class touched on quantum entanglement and the EPR paradox this year, and the lesson left me curious enough to look further into the topic on my own. Over the past few weeks, a friend from my physics club and I have been working through material on Bell's theorem, mostly the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's entry on it, and we keep running into the same point of confusion. Most popular explanations describe Bell's "locality" assumption as though it were a single condition, but the more careful treatments note that it actually splits into two logically independent conditions that philosophers of physics call "parameter independence" and "outcome independence." As far as we can tell, the standard view is that quantum mechanics respects parameter independence but violates outcome independence, and that this exact combination is what allows entangled particles to violate Bell's inequality without allowing anyone to send a signal faster than light.

What neither of us has been able to get an intuitive grasp on is why the two conditions have to divide up that way, rather than the reverse. We can follow the derivation showing that Bell's condition factors into these two pieces, but we don't have a real feel for what each piece actually says about the underlying physical situation, or why entanglement so naturally falls on the side of violating outcome independence while preserving parameter independence. Could you explain, in an intuitive way rather than through formal probability notation, what really distinguishes these two kinds of non-locality, and why this particular split is exactly what allows quantum correlations to be non-local without contradicting relativity?
