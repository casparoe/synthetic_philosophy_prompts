Two of my coworkers got into an argument during a code review retro a few weeks back, and I haven't been able to stop thinking about it since. We're a smallish startup, maybe 20 engineers, and Sana, one of our most senior people, was trying to explain why she catches way more bugs than everyone else. She said something like 'honestly at this point I don't read the diff line by line anymore, the bad line just visually pops out at me before I've consciously looked for anything, it's not really a conclusion I reach, it's closer to just seeing it.' Dave, another senior engineer, pushed back hard: he said that's not a different kind of seeing, it's just pattern-matching that's gotten so fast and automatic it feels immediate, and she's mistaking a really quick judgment for an actual perceptual thing. Everyone else just watched it turn into a whole side-argument, and I wrote down the back and forth afterward because honestly it was more interesting than the retro itself.

(1) Sana said it has to be perceptual rather than inferential, because it happens before she's even aware of looking for anything -- same as how you can't help but see a color, you don't "conclude" red. (2) Dave said feeling instant doesn't prove anything's perceptual: tons of fast, automatic, non-conscious stuff, like recognizing a word while reading, is still higher-level processing that just feels immediate, so immediacy alone doesn't settle it. (3) Sana said that when people watch her scan a diff, her attention jumps straight to the bad line before she could've consciously reasoned anything out -- everyone's looking at the same pixels, but she's clearly not having the same experience of the screen a junior would. She added that if literally any evidence you could ever point to just gets waved away as 'that's attention, not perception,' then the whole distinction is unfalsifiable and maybe not even a real claim. (4) Dave said attention snapping there faster is exactly what you'd expect from expertise even if the raw visual experience is identical for both of them, so it doesn't prove her point -- but he said the claim isn't unfalsifiable at all: pin her gaze somewhere neutral with an eye tracker, or just flash the diff for a split second, and see if she still flags the bug better than a novice using only peripheral vision, with attention held fixed. If she does, that's a real, testable difference in early processing, not just faster attention. (5) Sana said fine, but that experiment, even if it worked, would only show her visual system got retrained after years of staring at code -- not that her beliefs about THIS diff, right now, are reaching down and changing what she currently sees. And she admitted she wasn't sure anymore whether that difference, permanently retrained perception versus perception being actively shaped in the moment by belief, was even the thing they'd been arguing about the whole time.

I mentioned all this to a friend doing a cog sci PhD, half expecting her to say we were both talking nonsense, and instead she said this is an actual, unresolved debate in philosophy of mind called 'cognitive penetration of perception' that I'd never even heard of. Could you go through Sana and Dave's exchange move by move and say whose got the better argument at each step, not just who wins overall? And I'm specifically stuck on move 5 -- did Sana basically hand Dave the win there by conceding it might just be retrained perception, or is she actually onto a separate question that neither of them had been debating up to that point?
