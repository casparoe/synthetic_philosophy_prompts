And here is my classmate's answer, unedited:

"(a) Putnam's argument starts from the observation that a mental state like pain can apparently be shared by creatures with radically different physical makeups. He asks us to imagine that pain in humans is identical to some specific neural state, such as C-fiber firing, as the identity theorist claims. But octopuses, whose nervous systems are organized nothing like ours, and hypothetical Martians or silicon-based beings, who might have no neurons at all, seem perfectly capable of being in pain too. Since none of these creatures could possibly have C-fibers, the identity theorist is stuck saying either that they cannot really be in pain, which is hard to believe, or that 'pain' names a different physical state in each species, which defeats the point of a single psychophysical law. Putnam concludes that pain cannot be identical to any one physical-state type, which refutes token identity theory, the claim that a mental state is nothing over and above whatever physical state realizes it on a given occasion.

(b) Functionalism is designed to survive exactly this problem. Instead of defining a mental state by its physical composition, functionalism defines it by its causal role: the pattern of connections it has to sensory inputs, behavioral outputs, and other mental states. Pain, on this view, is whatever state is typically caused by tissue damage, tends to cause winces and avoidance behavior, and tends to cause a desire that it stop. Because this causal profile can in principle be realized by C-fibers, by whatever an octopus has instead, or by a silicon microchip, functionalism can count all three creatures as being in pain without needing them to share any physical substrate, turning Putnam's problem case into a predicted feature of the theory rather than a threat to it.

(c) The dilemma Block raises trades on the fact that a functional specification can be written at different grains. Write it narrowly, tying the relevant inputs and outputs closely to human specifics, and the theory becomes chauvinist, denying minds to creatures like Putnam's Martians that plainly ought to count. Write it broadly enough to include them, and the theory risks becoming liberal, awarding minds to systems that obviously lack them. Block makes the liberal horn vivid with the Chinese Room: if the entire population of China were handed walkie-talkies and assigned to play the role of individual neurons, collectively instantiating the functional organization of a mind, functionalism seems committed to saying the resulting system is in pain when the network is stimulated appropriately -- a conclusion Block thinks we should resist."
