Question: In Word and Object, Quine uses the "gavagai" scenario to introduce the thesis of the indeterminacy of translation. (a) Explain the scenario, and give at least two rival translations of "gavagai" that Quine says are equally compatible with all the evidence available to the linguist. (b) Explain in what sense the indeterminacy Quine has in mind is supposed to be more radical than the ordinary underdetermination of scientific theories by evidence. (c) Is Quine's argument for (b) convincing? Briefly say why or why not.

Student's answer:

(a) Quine's scenario is "radical translation": a linguist tries to translate a language with no bilinguals and no dictionaries to help, working only from the natives' behavior. A rabbit runs past and a native utters "Gavagai"; the linguist starts out translating this as "Rabbit," or "Lo, a rabbit." Quine's point is that exactly the same pattern of assent and dissent -- the same stimulus meaning -- would result if "Gavagai" were translated instead as "undetached rabbit part" or as "rabbit-stage" (a momentary temporal slice of a rabbit), since a whole rabbit is never present without its undetached parts, or without some temporal stage of it, also being present. Since the only evidence available to the linguist is the natives' dispositions to assent or dissent given a stimulation, and all these candidate translations fit that evidence equally well, no amount of further evidence of the same kind could settle which one is right.

(b) Quine thinks this is really just a vivid instance of a more general phenomenon he'd already argued for in philosophy of science: the underdetermination of theory by evidence. Just as two total physical theories can both fit every possible observation and yet diverge elsewhere, two translation manuals can both fit every possible pattern of assent and dissent and yet disagree about what a given native sentence says. What makes the translation case feel more dramatic is just that we aren't used to thinking of meaning as theoretical. Once you accept that linguistics is continuous with the rest of science, translating "Gavagai" is underdetermined in exactly the way any hypothesis about unobservables is underdetermined by data -- no more and no less.

(c) I find the argument convincing given Quine's behaviorism about meaning. If meaning is nothing but dispositions to verbal behavior, then whatever isn't fixed by those dispositions isn't fixed at all, and gavagai shows reference isn't fixed by them. The natural way out is to deny the behaviorism -- to say the native's mental states settle what she means even when her behavior doesn't reveal it -- but Quine would say we have no independent handle on such states except through behavior anyway, so that's less a way out than a refusal to play.
