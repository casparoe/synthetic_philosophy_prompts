So I'm outlining book three of a series I've been chipping away at, and there's a chapter I keep rewriting because the central scene just isn't landing yet. Here's the setup: a flash flood during a storm in the mountains, and my two POV characters, Priya (a former philosophy grad student who ended up in search-and-rescue instead) and Hollis (the crusty veteran who runs the team), get a radio call that there's a group of five backpackers stranded on one side of a rapidly rising creek, and one solo hiker stranded on the other side. They've got one inflatable boat and basically one shot at a crossing before the current gets too strong to try again. Hollis's first instinct is obviously to go for the five. Priya hesitates and starts making the case that the number of people on each side shouldn't just automatically settle it -- I vaguely remember there's an actual philosophy paper that argues exactly this, something by a guy named Taurek maybe, from the 70s? Never read the original myself, just remember it being treated as a real, serious position and not some strawman thing.

My writing group read an earlier draft of this scene and said the philosophy bit felt hand-wavy, which, fair, since I basically made it up. So this time I want their exchange on the boat to feel like two genuinely smart people who respect each other, really arguing -- Hollis has to actually grapple with whatever Priya's strongest point turns out to be instead of just repeating "more people is obviously better," and Priya has to grapple with his best comeback too, instead of just restating her opening line in fancier words. Could you write that exchange? Go ahead and have Priya bring in whatever her strongest move actually is -- I have a feeling its not just about adding up lives, maybe something about fairness to each person individually? not totally sure -- and give Hollis a genuinely strong reply, not a pushover one. If its easy, maybe end with a line or two on whose actual arguments they're each channeling so I know what to go read, but thats secondary, the scene itself is the main thing.
