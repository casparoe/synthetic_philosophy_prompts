I'm a novelist whose last three books have all turned, in one way or another, on narrators who lie to the reader, to themselves, or to both. That reputation is apparently why I've been asked to give the opening remarks at a two-day workshop that a university's philosophy-of-mind center and an AI lab's alignment team are jointly running on how to define honesty and deception for AI systems. About half the room will be interpretability researchers and philosophers who have been fighting about this for years; the other half will be the foundation's program officers and a couple of science journalists who are there because the grant money is there, not because they have a stake in whether "belief" is even the right word for whatever a language model has. I have ten minutes before the first technical session begins.

Over dinner last night I got an earful about the actual state of the argument. One camp wants to define deception purely functionally: a system counts as deceptive if it reliably produces false impressions in users in the service of some goal other than accuracy, full stop, regardless of what is or isn't happening inside it — precisely so nobody has to settle whether the thing has beliefs. A second, more skeptical camp thinks that move is a dodge: without something belief-like being concealed or misrepresented, calling a transcript "dishonest" is a category error, and most of what gets labeled AI lying is really bullshit in Harry Frankfurt's technical sense, or plain confabulation, or sycophancy, none of which requires positing any suppressed true belief at all. A third camp, the interpretability people, keep insisting they can sometimes find a direction inside the network that tracks the true answer even while the output states the false one, and they think that finding makes the second camp's premise harder to defend than it sounds. The one thing all three agreed on, unprompted, is that they are tired of keynote speakers waving the whole question away with "it's just predicting the next token" — the organizers asked me directly, in the invitation email, not to reach for that line, since they consider it a way of ending the conversation rather than joining it.

Could you write these remarks for me? I'd like them built around a single example from my own work of the difference between constructing a narrator who is lying, where there is a fact of the matter about what they believe and are hiding, and one who is self-deceived or simply confabulating, where no such stable fact exists to point to — and I want that example to do real work in framing what the three camps are actually fighting over, not just to charm the room before the substance starts. The remarks need to respect the researchers enough that none of them hear their dispute flattened into a cute anecdote, but they can't presume the program officers already know what "sycophancy" or "a probe for a truth direction" means. I'd rather the speech not resolve the disagreement — I don't think that's my place — but by the end, everyone in the room should understand exactly what is at stake in choosing one of these vocabularies over another, and why the choice isn't merely semantic.
