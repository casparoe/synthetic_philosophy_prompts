A few of us on my team started an informal lunchtime reading group after a coworker linked a philosophy podcast's show notes, and this week's selection was Max Black's 1952 dialogue on the identity of indiscernibles. The setup, if I'm remembering it correctly, has two speakers debating whether it's coherent to imagine a universe containing nothing but two qualitatively identical iron spheres, a couple of miles apart, with every property duplicated between them. Black's point is that since there is no qualitative difference to appeal to, and yet there are clearly two spheres rather than one, the principle that no two distinct objects can have exactly the same properties must be false.

In the discussion afterward, a coworker who minored in philosophy raised a rebuttal to Black that I hadn't come across before, which she called "weak discernibility": the claim that the spheres are not, in fact, fully indiscernible, because each stands in an irreflexive relation to the other — "is exactly two miles from," say — that neither sphere bears to itself. Since this relation numerically distinguishes the spheres without appealing to any purely monadic (one-place) property, the idea is that Black's thought experiment only refutes an implausibly strong, monadic-only version of the identity of indiscernibles, while a weaker, relation-permitting version survives untouched. Something about this struck me as slippery — a bit like a code review where an elegant-looking fix turns out to quietly assume the very thing it was supposed to establish — but I couldn't pin down exactly what was wrong with it, especially since ordinary relational facts (e.g., "Alice is taller than Bob") obviously distinguish things all the time without anyone calling that circular.

Could you construct the strongest available argument for the position that weak discernibility does not actually rescue the identity of indiscernibles from Black's counterexample — that is, that even the relational formulation of the principle, and not merely the monadic one, is refuted by the two-sphere scenario? I would especially like the argument to identify what, precisely, is illegitimate about invoking "is two miles from" to individuate the spheres in a way that does not equally indict mundane relational discernment like the Alice-and-Bob case; an argument that can't draw that line isn't really targeting weak discernibility in particular. You can set aside the unrelated rebuttal (which I gather also exists) that reinterprets Black's universe as non-Euclidean and therefore as containing only one sphere after all — I want to stay focused on the weak-discernibility response specifically.
