I've seen the following argument made a few times on rationalist and EA-adjacent blogs, and I haven't been able to find the flaw in it. Von Neumann and Morgenstern proved that a rational agent's preferences over lotteries pin down a cardinal utility function for that agent, unique only up to a positive affine transformation. Harsanyi then proved that any social ranking of those same lotteries that respects unanimity (the Pareto principle) and itself obeys the VNM axioms must take the form of a weighted sum of the individuals' cardinal utilities. Put together, this seems to dissolve the old objection that utilitarianism needs some hopelessly subjective way of comparing one person's happiness to another's: just elicit each person's cardinal utility function from their choices under risk, feed the numbers into Harsanyi's formula, and both the interpersonal comparison and the correct weights drop out of the mathematics. So is the "problem of interpersonal comparison of utility" that moral philosophers keep citing actually still a live problem, or has decision theory quietly solved it?
