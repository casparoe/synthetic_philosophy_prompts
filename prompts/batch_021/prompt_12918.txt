A friend and I have spent much of this semester disagreeing about the prospects of a joint investment, and last week she deployed what she clearly intended as a conversation-ending move. Since we each know the other is not a fool, and we have each been fully candid about our current probability estimates, she argued, our continued disagreement is itself a sign of irrationality: two Bayesians who share a common prior and have common knowledge of each other's posteriors on some event cannot rationally agree to disagree, and that, she pointed out, is just Aumann's theorem. I wasn't able to articulate on the spot why this seemed to be proving far more than it should, so I'd like to work out the objection properly.

Please argue for the position that Aumann's agreement theorem, correctly understood, gives us little reason to think that two people who persist in disagreement after a long exchange are thereby being irrational. I'd like you to engage with the actual moving parts of the theorem rather than simply asserting that real people aren't perfect Bayesians. In particular, please build a case around: (a) what work the common prior assumption is doing inside the proof, and why treating it as a costless idealization rather than a substantive and often unmet constraint comes uncomfortably close to assuming the conclusion; (b) the gap between one person merely knowing the other's current posterior and the full, infinitely-iterated common-knowledge condition the theorem actually requires, and how easily that condition breaks down once there is even slight uncertainty about the other party's rationality or candor; and (c) whether the more dynamic "we can't disagree forever" results, in which iterated exchange of posteriors converges to agreement, genuinely rescue the theorem's everyday relevance or merely relocate the same idealizations into the communication protocol itself. If you think there's a serious rejoinder available here — for instance, something in the spirit of Robin Hanson's attempts to defend a common prior via a "pre-rationality" condition — please raise it, but explain why you still find it insufficient to license the practical conclusion my friend wanted to draw.
