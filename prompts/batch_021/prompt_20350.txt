I was on assignment at a small, closed-door workshop on AI alignment last month, covering a session on what the organizers kept calling "coherence arguments" — the idea, built on expected-utility theory and Dutch-book/money-pump reasoning, that any sufficiently capable agent whose preferences can't be represented by a single utility function is exploitable, and that this is why we should expect advanced AI systems to end up behaving like stable expected-utility maximizers rather than agents with vague, shifting goals. During the coffee break, two attendees kept arguing about this well after everyone else had wandered back in, and I ended up transcribing most of it rather than joining in, since I couldn't tell who was actually getting the better of the exchange. One, a decision theorist I'll call Elena, works on the formal side of this; the other, an AI-safety researcher I'll call Marcus, spends most of his time evaluating actual models rather than proving theorems about idealized agents.

(1) Elena opened with the standard argument: if an agent's preferences over some set of options are intransitive or incomplete, you can in principle construct a sequence of trades that leaves it strictly worse off by its own lights, and any agent that noticed this happening would want to avoid it — so pressure toward coherence is pressure toward eventually being representable as an expected-utility maximizer. (2) Marcus objected that this shows less than it seems to: an agent with merely incomplete preferences can dodge the whole problem by adopting the simple policy of never accepting a trade unless it's a clear improvement, which blocks the money pump without forcing the agent into a complete ordering over everything. (3) Elena replied that the dodge isn't free — a policy of refusing every ambiguous trade also forfeits trades that would have been strictly beneficial, and a sufficiently reflective agent should eventually notice it is leaving value on the table, which pushes it toward completeness anyway, just more slowly. (4) Marcus shifted ground: even granting full coherence, he said, "expected-utility maximizer" is close to an empty description, since any observed behavior whatsoever can be rationalized after the fact by some utility function — including a degenerate one that simply ranks whatever the agent actually did as best — so coherence alone can't establish that a system has a stable goal it will pursue across new situations, which was the whole point of bringing these arguments into a conversation about AI risk. (5) Elena's comeback was that the degenerate-utility-function trick only works for a single, already-fixed trajectory considered in isolation; the coherence pressure she cares about operates prospectively, across an agent's choices among options it hasn't yet settled, and there it still favors preferences that are stable and transferable across situations over ones invented after the fact to fit whatever happened to occur.

Neither budged, and the conversation only ended because the next talk started. I'd like your help sorting out who actually had the better of it. Could you go through moves (1) through (5) in order and, for each, say who comes out ahead and why, rather than just handing down an overall winner? I'd also be grateful for a note on whether either of them is quietly leaning on a premise the other should have challenged more directly.
