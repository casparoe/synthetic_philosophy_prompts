Hey, I'm working on a piece about where Kripke's ideas about names actually came from, and I fell down a rabbit hole into John Stuart Mill's "A System of Logic" (1843), since apparently he's the guy Kripke credits for the idea that names don't have "sense," they just point at stuff. Here's the bit thats been bugging me, from the chapter "Of Names":

"Proper names are not connotative: they denote the individuals who are called by them; but they do not indicate or imply any attributes as belonging to those individuals. ... the name, once given, is independent of the reason. A man may have been named John, because that was the name of his father; a town may have been named Dartmouth, because it is situated at the mouth of the Dart. But it is no part of the signification of the word John, that the father of the person so called bore the same name; nor even of the word Dartmouth, to be situated at the mouth of the Dart. If sand should choke up the mouth of the river, or an earthquake change its course, and remove it to a distance from the town, the name of the town would not necessarily be changed. ... Proper names are attached to the objects themselves, and are not dependent on the continuance of any attribute of the object."

I think I follow the basic claim - "Dartmouth" doesn't mean "town at the mouth of the Dart," it just labels the place, so the label sticks even once the geography that inspired it is gone. What I can't quite work out is how much of Kripke's whole rigid designator / necessary a posteriori apparatus is already lurking in here versus stuff Mill definately didn't have. Is this basically Mill stumbling onto rigid designation over a century early, or is there some crucial ingredient missing from his picture, one that means it couldn't, on its own, get you all the way to something like "Hesperus is Phosphorus" being necessarily true yet only discoverable through observation? Would love a breakdown of what's actually doing the extra work here that Mill's text doesn't spell out.
