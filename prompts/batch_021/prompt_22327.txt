A friend of mine, Talia, who is far more traditionally observant than I am and who has spent the last several months going through the Guide for the Perplexed with me a chapter at a time, and I had a long, unresolved argument last night about Maimonides' chapter on the rationale for animal sacrifice (Guide III:32). Talia's reading is the strong one: that Maimonides is quietly conceding that sacrifice has no religious value in itself, that it was purely a concession to a people newly weaned off Egyptian and Sabian idol-worship, and that a hypothetical nation that hadn't grown up sacrificing to idols would never have been commanded to sacrifice at all—so that there is, on his view, nothing to mourn in the practice's disappearance. I think that reading proves too much: Maimonides never says anything this deflationary about prayer, and he visibly strains, in his handling of Jeremiah, to keep the text from saying outright that sacrifice was never really commanded. We went back and forth for an hour and got nowhere. Here is the stretch we kept returning to, in Friedländer's translation:

"Many precepts in our Law are the result of a similar course adopted by the same Supreme Being. It is, namely, impossible to go suddenly from one extreme to the other: it is therefore according to the nature of man impossible for him suddenly to discontinue everything to which he has been accustomed. [...] For this reason God allowed these kinds of service to continue; He transferred to His service that which had formerly served as a worship of created beings, and of things imaginary and unreal, and commanded us to serve Him in the same manner [...]. By this Divine plan it was effected that the traces of idolatry were blotted out, and the truly great principle of our faith, the Existence and Unity of God, was firmly established [...]. As the sacrificial service is not the primary object [of the commandments about sacrifice], whilst supplications, prayers, and similar kinds of worship are nearer to the primary object, and indispensable for obtaining it, a great difference was made in the Law between these two kinds of service. [...] Because of this principle which I explained to you, the Prophets in their books are frequently found to rebuke their fellow-men for being over-zealous and exerting themselves too much in bringing sacrifices [...]. Jeremiah declared: 'For I spake not unto your fathers, nor commanded them in the day that I brought them out of the land of Egypt, concerning burnt-offering or sacrifices. But this thing commanded I them, saying, Obey my voice, and I will be your God, and ye shall be my people' (Jer. vii. 22, 23). This passage has been found difficult in the opinion of all those whose words I read or heard; they ask, How can Jeremiah say that God did not command us about burnt-offering and sacrifice, seeing so many precepts refer to sacrifice? The sense of the passage agrees with what I explained to you [...]. You must not see any difficulty in the Passover which was commanded in Egypt; there was a particular and evident reason for that [...]. The first commandment after the departure from Egypt was given at Marah [...]. According to the true traditional explanation, Sabbath and civil laws were revealed at Marah [...]. We have thus proved that the first laws do not refer to burnt-offering and sacrifice, which are of secondary importance."

Could you help me actually work through the argument here, rather than just referee our impressions of it? Two things in particular. First, does the "not the primary object" language commit Maimonides to Talia's strong claim—sacrifice as a mere historical accident with no positive standing of its own, more or less the reading Nachmanides later attacked him for—or does a primary/secondary ranking leave room for a softer view on which sacrifice is genuinely subordinate to prayer without being thereby worthless? Second, is the maneuver against Jeremiah doing real argumentative work, or is Maimonides simply narrowing "commanded... in the day that I brought them out of Egypt" just enough—Sabbath and civil law given at Marah, with the Passover in Egypt fenced off as a special case—that the verse can no longer say what it plainly looks like it's saying? I'd like to go back to Talia with an actual account of how the argument is structured, rather than just my side of it argued more loudly.
