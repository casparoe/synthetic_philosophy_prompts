For an independent project in my school's philosophy elective, I just read Rebecca Goldstein's short biography of Kurt Gödel, and one part of it has stuck with me for weeks. Everyone learns Gödel for the incompleteness theorems, but apparently he also spent real private effort on a rationalist argument for life after death -- distinct from his more famous, more purely logical ontological proof of God. The gist, as I understood it from letters he wrote to his mother decades before his own death, was a kind of dichotomy: either the world is fundamentally rational, in which case it would be absurd for it to grow a mind capable of open-ended development and then simply extinguish it right as that mind is getting started, so some kind of continuation has to follow; or the world isn't rational in that way, in which case we have no grounds for trusting reason at all, including the reasoning behind that very worry, so the worry cancels itself out. What makes this almost unbearable to sit with is how he actually died. Increasingly convinced people were trying to poison him, he stopped eating once his wife Adele -- who had spent years tasting his meals first to prove they were safe -- was herself hospitalized and could no longer do it for him; he starved to death in 1978, reportedly weighing around thirty kilograms, with a death certificate said to list the cause as something like "malnutrition and inanition caused by personality disturbance."

I'd like you to write a short story, roughly 1,000 to 1,500 words, built around that exact friction: a man capable of reasoning his way to something like a proof that minds don't simply switch off, dying of a fear that his own was about to be switched off on purpose. I don't need strict biographical accuracy, and you don't even have to use his real name if a lightly fictionalized version serves the story better, but please keep both real pieces intact -- a rationalist's genuinely worked-out case for why some kind of continuation must follow death, and a slow, physical death driven by a fear that argument never touched. Feel free to narrate from inside his head, or from someone closer to the ground, like his wife or a nurse, whichever carries the tension better. The one thing I'd ask you to avoid is a tidy ending that quietly comes down on one side of the question -- I'd rather it stay exactly as unresolved on the page as it apparently was for him. I'm hoping to submit whatever comes out of this to my school's literary magazine, so I'd rather it read as a real short story than as a dressed-up biography.
