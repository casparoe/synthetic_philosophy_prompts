I've been rereading the sections of Hegel's *Philosophy of Right* dealing with sovereignty and the state's relation to other states, partly prompted by some discussions on LessWrong and Astral Codex Ten about the "long peace" and the strategic logic of nuclear deterrence. What strikes me is that Hegel's position on war is much more specific, and much more strongly held, than the popular caricature that he simply thought conflict was "dialectically necessary." In the Remark to §324, he explicitly denies that war is an absolute evil or a merely contingent misfortune with contingent causes, and argues instead that a prolonged, let alone perpetual, peace breeds a kind of corruption in a people — he compares it to the way a becalmed sea stagnates — while periodic war restores individuals' sense that their private and particular ends (property, family, even their own lives) are ultimately negatable in service of the state's ethical substance. This is also, I take it, why he has so little patience for Kant's proposal of a federation of states to secure perpetual peace: absent some authority standing over sovereign states the way a court stands over quarreling private persons, he thinks the relation between states remains, in effect, a state of nature in which the ultimate arbiter of a dispute, once negotiation fails, is war.

Given that background, I'd like your best reconstruction, working from Hegel's own premises rather than reaching for a generic verdict of "he would have hated this" or "he would have loved this," of what he would say about two features of contemporary warfare he never had to reckon with. First, nuclear deterrence has produced, among the great powers, almost exactly the kind of prolonged peace his argument suggests should be corrupting — except that any war capable of "restoring" a people's ethical health among nuclear-armed states would now be closer to a civilization-ending catastrophe than a bounded contest. Does his own logic commit him to regarding this long peace with alarm, or does the changed character of what such a war would now mean give him independent grounds to retract the argument rather than extend it? Second, insofar as war still occurs, it is increasingly fought by drones and progressively autonomous systems that let the stronger side risk none of its own personnel. Since Hegel ties the ethical significance of war to individuals demonstrating, by risking their lives, that they are not simply attached to their private existence, what becomes of that argument once one side, or eventually both, has nothing personally at stake? I am less interested in a verdict on whether Hegel was right than in a careful account of whether these are developments his specific argument can absorb on its own terms, or whether they expose an assumption it was quietly relying on.
