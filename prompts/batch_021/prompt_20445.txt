So my friend Priya and I basically spent our whole dinner last night arguing about Wittgenstein's On Certainty (we've got this little two-person reading group going, don't judge). It started because I said hinge propositions are supposed to be the fixed bedrock of a belief system, like they just don't move, and she said that's kind of a misreading and that Wittgenstein is pretty clear elsewhere that hinges can shift around over time and even swap places with ordinary empirical claims, so "fixed" is the wrong word entirely. Neither of us could fully win the argument so we ended up just reading bits of the actual text out loud at each other, which is honestly where I got more confused rather than less.

Here's the passage that started it, the classic "hinge" bit (On Certainty §341-343):

"That is to say, the questions that we raise and our doubts depend on the fact that some propositions are exempt from doubt, are as it were like hinges on which those turn. That is to say, it belongs to the logic of our scientific investigations that certain things are indeed not doubted. But it isn't that the situation is like this: we just can't investigate everything, and for that reason we are forced to rest content with assumption. If I want the door to turn, the hinges must stay put."

And here's the one Priya kept throwing back at me, the river-bed passage from a bit earlier (§96-99):

"It might be imagined that some propositions, of the form of empirical propositions, were hardened and functioned as channels for such empirical propositions as were not hardened but fluid; and that this relation altered with time, in that fluid propositions hardened, and hard ones became fluid. The mythology may change back into a state of flux, the river-bed of thoughts may shift. But I distinguish between the movement of the waters on the river-bed and the shift of the bed itself; though there is not a sharp division of the one from the other. And the bank of that river consists partly of hard rock, subject to no alteration or only to an imperceptible one, partly of sand, which now in one place now in another gets washed away, or deposited."

What I can't figure out is whether these two bits are actually compatible or whether Wittgenstein's just kind of helping himself to two different pictures without reconciling them. The door/hinge image makes it sound like the point is that something has to stay rigid or the whole system seizes up, full stop, but it doesn't obviously say any particular hinge is eternal. The river-bed image seems to go further and say specific propositions genuinely migrate between being "hinge" and being ordinary tested belief over historical time. So is "the hinges must stay put" compatible with individual hinges being swappable like that, as long as some hinge or other is always in place? Or is Priya right that the river-bed passage is basically Wittgenstein loosening up the more rigid-sounding hinge metaphor (I know 96-99 actually comes before 341 in the numbering, not after, which confuses me too, tbh, since it reads like the more "advanced" version of the idea). Also curious what "mythology" is doing in that second passage, definately not a throwaway word for him given the Frazer stuff he'd written about years earlier — is he gesturing at something specific there or am I just reading too much into one word? Would really appreciate an actual close reading here rather than the two-sentence SEP-article summary of hinge propositions, since that's pretty much what Priya kept lobbing at me over dinner.
