So this happened at my rationalist-adjacent reading group (the kind where half the room has read way too much LessWrong), the week we covered Parfit's hitchhiker. Recap in case the exact version matters: you're dying in the desert, and a driver will only take you to town if she judges you'll pay her back once you're safe. The catch is that once you're actually in town, paying does nothing for you causally, so a straightforwardly causal reasoner won't pay -- she predicts that in advance and just leaves you stranded, while someone with the "irrational" disposition to pay anyway gets picked up, pays, and ends up better off than dead.

Marcus and Talia got into a real back-and-forth about what this actually shows, and I honestly think they're both onto something, so I want a proper referee instead of me just picking a side.

1. Marcus: this refutes causal decision theory outright. A CDT agent, once safe in town, has nothing left to causally affect by paying, so it won't -- and a theory that predictably gets you killed in a dilemma you saw coming is just wrong.

2. Talia: that only nails naive CDT. A sophisticated CDT agent forecasts the dilemma before ever sticking out a thumb and precommits to paying; CDT itself endorses adopting that policy in advance, so the case argues for commitment devices, not against CDT as a theory of rational choice.

3. Marcus: a commitment only works if it's backed by something with teeth -- a contract, a device, whatever. With nothing but an earlier resolution and no enforcement, a genuine causal reasoner recalculates from scratch at the moment of truth, finds paying still does nothing, and reneges anyway. Sophistication without hardware behind it doesn't rescue CDT -- the driver reads character instead of demanding a signature precisely because there's nothing to sign.

4. Talia: that's what resolute choice is for. McClennen's whole point is that a rational planner can just carry out a plan formed earlier as such, not because its re-derived as locally optimal all over again, but because treating your own plans as binding is itself the rational policy for an agent who cares about more than the current instant.

5. Marcus: except that IS the extra machinery. The second you say the right unit of evaluation is the whole policy rather than the isolated act, you've conceded the premise behind updateless/functional decision theory. "Resolute CDT" is just FDT wearing a CDT nametag, and Talia can't defend CDT while quietly changing what CDT evaluates.

6. Talia: or that's basically Parfit's own point in raising "rational irrationality" to begin with -- the case was never meant to crown one theory of moment-to-moment choice, it was meant to show that being disposed to act, later, in a way that looks locally irrational, can itself be the rational thing to bring about in yourself. That's a claim about which dispositions are worth having, and its compatible with more than one theory of the underlying acts.

Neither of them budged and it didn't feel like a clean sweep either way to me. Can you go through all six moves and adjudicate each on its own merits, not just who "wins" overall?
