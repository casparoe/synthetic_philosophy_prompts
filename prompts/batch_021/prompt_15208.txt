So for debate this year there's a decent chance we get assigned a round on "resolved: political power should be apportioned according to political competence," which is basically the epistocracy vs. democracy debate (epistocracy = the idea that people who actually know about politics should get more say than people who don't, instead of strict one-person-one-vote). Two of my teammates, Marcus and Naomi, were supposed to be prepping both sides together in the library during practice, but they ended up just arguing their actual opinions for almost the whole hour instead of writing cards, and now they both keep asking me who "won" since I was sitting right there the whole time. I don't think either of them is obviously wrong so I wanted an outside opinion.

Here's roughly how it went. Marcus opened by saying we already let competence decide who's in charge in every other high-stakes situation — you don't let a random person fly a plane or perform surgery just because they want to — so it's weird that politics, which affects way more people than either of those, is the one area where we insist total ignorance should count the same as expertise. He also brought up that John Stuart Mill, of all people, a guy who was pro-democracy and pro women's suffrage back when that was a genuinely radical take, still argued for giving extra votes to more educated citizens because he didn't trust an uninformed majority. Naomi said the surgeon comparison doesn't hold up: theres an objectively correct way to take out an appendix that you can check against outcomes, but "what's the best immigration policy" is literally the thing people disagree about, so any "competence test" for politics has to smuggle in somebody's answer to that exact dispute and then call it the neutral baseline. Marcus adjusted his position: fine, don't test opinions, just test dry factual civics knowledge, like naming the three branches of government or explaining what a tariff actually does — that's not opinion, that's just knowledge. Naomi answered that this has already been tried and it didn't stay neutral: literacy tests in the Jim Crow South were "purely factual" on paper too, and were still used to gut Black voter turnout, because whoever designs and grades the test controls who counts as informed enough to matter, and that person answers to no one. Marcus's comeback was that a policy being abused historically doesn't prove the policy is flawed in principle — we didn't get rid of juries just because juries have been abused — and besides, modern democracies already hand huge, basically unaccountable power to unelected people constantly, like central bankers or judges, and nobody thinks that makes the whole system illegitimate.

That's where it fizzled out because the bell rang, and now I honestly can't tell who was actually ahead by the end. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who had the stronger point at each specific step, not just an overall winner, since I think they were each right about something? And if there's some bigger consideration about epistocracy that neither of them thought to bring up, I'd love to know so I can casually drop it next practice and look smart.
