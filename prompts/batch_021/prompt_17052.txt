I spent 35 years as a condensed-matter physicist before retiring, and lately I've fallen into a philosophy-of-mind rabbit hole, currently chewing through Jaegwon Kim's "Physicalism, or Something Near Enough." His causal exclusion argument keeps grabbing me because it rhymes with arguments I used to have with colleagues about whether things like temperature or a superconducting gap "really" cause anything over and above the underlying particle motions, or whether that's just a convenient higher-level story we tell. Kim's version, as I understand it: if a mental event M causes some behavior P, and M is realized by (or supervenes on) a physical event P*, and P* is already nomologically sufficient for P thanks to the causal closure of the physical, then M looks like a redundant extra cause -- and systematic overdetermination of every single mental-to-physical case is a pretty unattractive bullet to bite.

A few of us from my old department have a little reading group going now (mostly retired physicists plus one very patient ex-biologist, meetings run long), and we've spent two sessions arguing about which of the standard replies to this actually rescues mental causation versus just relabels the problem. The candidates we've collected so far: reductive/type-identity theory, Davidsons anomalous monism, the Shoemaker/Wilson "subset of causal powers" realization view, Yablo's determinate/determinable move, List and Menzies' interventionist difference-making account, just biting the overdetermination bullet, strong emergentism with downward causation, and denying the causal closure of the physical outright (interactionist dualism). Nobody can agree on an order, so I said I'd get an outside opinion before next month's meeting.
