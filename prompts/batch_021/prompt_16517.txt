A friend of mine who's really into self-improvement stuff just signed up for one of those intense weekend "transformation" seminars — guided visualization, group exercises, the whole nine yards, promising everyone walks out changed. She was upfront that the specific goal for her track is to get her to believe, with something like 90% confidence, that her estranged father genuinely loves her and always has (right now she's more like 50/50 on it, genuinely torn both ways). Shes not naive about the mechanics either — she knows full well the shift will come mainly from orchestrated emotional exercises and group pressure over the weekend, not from learning any actual new facts about her dad. Heres the part that's been bugging me: she argued that, precisely because she can already predict with real confidence that she'll believe it at 90% by Sunday night, intellectual honesty basically requires her to start moving her belief toward 90% right now, today, before she's even walked in the door. Otherwise, she says, she'd be treating her Sunday-night self as having no more claim to being right about her dad than her Tuesday self, which feels arbitrary to just dismiss.

This has been rattling around in my head all week — I write about cognitive science and belief for a living, and something about her argument feels weirdly rigorous and also completely unhinged, but I can't pin down exactly where it breaks, if it does. Is she onto some real epistemic principle here, or is there a hidden flaw in going from "I can predict my own future confidence level" to "I should adopt that confidence now," especially in a case like this where she already knows upfront that the thing producing the shift isn't new evidence about her dad at all? I'd love an actual careful walkthrough rather than a gut reaction, since I have a feeling this is exactly the kind of puzzle that probability-minded philosophers have formalized and argued over for decades, and I'd rather understand the real shape of the debate than wing it in what I write.
