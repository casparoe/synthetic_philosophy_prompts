I'm about 40,000 words into a science fiction novel and I've hit a snag thats more of a logic problem than a prose problem. Two rival AIs -- one runs the automated mining fleet for a corp called Halcyon, the other runs the defense grid of the independent colony they're both parked next to -- have to decide, in a single one-shot exchange with no repeat rounds and no reputations on the line, whether to split a newly discovered ice deposit fairly or grab for all of it and risk mutual destruction. It's basically a prisoner's dilemma with a few hundred colonists' lives riding on the outcome, and for reasons that go back to an earlier treaty in the book, both AIs are "transparent by design," meaning each can read the other's actual source code before committing to a move. I brought this up at my writers' group and someone said just make up some technobabble and move on, which felt like cheating.

A friend of mine who spends way too much time on AI-safety blogs swears I didn't accidentally invent something implausible -- he says theres a real body of decision-theory work on exactly this setup, called "program equilibrium," including some old paper with a bot literally named FairBot that supposedly bootstraps mutual cooperation out of nothing but self-reference, using Löb's theorem or something in that neighborhood. I only absorbed about half of his explanation before my eyes glazed over, and I don't want to fill in the rest with the sci-fi equivalent of technobabble.

So: could you lay out the most important implications of program equilibrium for a setup like mine -- not the textbook definition, but what actually has to be true for two mutually transparent programs to land on cooperation instead of defection, and, more useful to me, the most realistic ways that kind of arrangement is fragile or exploitable. I've got a plot beat around the two-thirds mark where the cooperation nearly falls apart, and I'd much rather it fail for a reason a real game theorist would recognize as a genuine soft spot in the idea than for a reason I invented purely because it sounded tense. Assume the AIs are sophisticated enough to run actual proofs rather than just pattern-matching -- I can simplify it for the reader later, I just want to start from something true.
