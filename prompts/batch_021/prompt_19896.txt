okay so I picked up Roger Penrose's "The Emperor's New Mind" over break (mostly skimmed it tbh) and I think I finally get why strong AI has to be impossible as a matter of pure logic, not just "the tech isn't there yet." The argument as I understand it: any AI's reasoning is ultimately implemented by some fixed formal system of rules. Gödel's theorem says that for any consistent formal system, there's a sentence (the "Gödel sentence") that's true but which the system can never prove from inside itself. A human looking at the system from outside, though, can see that the sentence is true, just by reasoning about the system rather than inside it. So whatever formal system you point to as "the AI's brain," a human can always find a truth that system misses — meaning human mathematical understanding can't be fully captured by any single algorithm, no matter how sophisticated. I know Gödel's SECOND theorem says a system can't prove its own consistency from the inside, but that shouldn't block this argument, since its US proving things about the machine from outside, not the machine trying to certify itself... right?

My roommate (doing a PhD in theoretical CS) rolled her eyes at me and said "that's literally just the Lucas-Penrose argument, pretty much nobody in logic thinks it actually works," but when I pushed her on why, all she gave me was some mumbling about consistency being "circular" that didn't really land for me. So is this actually a solid proof that minds can't be machines and her whole field is somehow in denial about it, or am I missing something here?
