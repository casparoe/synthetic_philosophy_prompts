I joined my hospital's clinical ethics committee three months ago, partly because my younger sister, who has bipolar I disorder, asked me last year to be her healthcare proxy while she drafts her own psychiatric advance directive during what has been a long stable stretch for her. Watching her try to write instructions that her future, unwell self would not be allowed to overrule got me quietly obsessed with what she and her psychiatrist call a "Ulysses contract," and I've been reading around the topic on my own ever since. So it felt like more than coincidence when, at my very first substantive meeting, I was handed a case file that puts the whole idea to a real test and asked to draft the discussion memo.

The patient is a woman in her thirties with a well-documented history of severe manic episodes. Eight months ago, while two independent psychiatrists agreed she had full decision-making capacity, she executed a detailed directive naming her mother as proxy, specifying a medication regimen, and including an explicit Ulysses clause instructing clinicians to hospitalize and treat her over her objection during any future episode, "even if I seem lucid and insist at the time that I have changed my mind." She arrived at the emergency department yesterday manic — pressured, grandiose, but coherent — and is now adamant that she is revoking the directive on the spot. The attending's capacity assessment is genuinely split along the standard prongs: she can understand the relevant information and communicate a choice, but it's contestable whether she can appreciate her situation or reason about it without her judgment being warped by the grandiosity itself. Making things harder, our state's directive statute, like many, lets a patient revoke at any time unless she has already been adjudicated incapacitated — which she has not — so on paper her objection today seems to simply cancel the very clause that was supposed to survive it.

Could you draft the short memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting? I need it to recommend one concrete course of action for this admission, including who decides and on what timeline, rather than just surveying considerations. One committee member keeps describing the directive as being like a constitution's entrenched clauses binding a future legislature, and I'd like the memo to say plainly whether that comparison actually supports treating her past wishes as authoritative or instead helps explain why they can't be — while still taking seriously the strongest argument for respecting what she is telling us right now she wants.
