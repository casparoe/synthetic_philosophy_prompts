I've recently been appointed to my state's judicial council committee that drafts model jury instructions for wrongful-death and survival actions, and I'd like your help with a memo before we vote next month. Current instructions already let a decedent's estate recover for pain and suffering the decedent consciously experienced before dying; the reform on the table is whether to add a further category compensating the estate for the future life itself that the death cut short -- what's sometimes called "hedonic" or "loss-of-life" damages, distinct from whatever survivors themselves lost in income, companionship, or funeral costs.

A retired appellate judge on the committee has circulated her own memo arguing hard against the addition. She quotes Epicurus's line that death "is nothing to us," runs through Lucretius's argument that the eons after we die are, in point of fact, no different in kind from the eons before we were born, and concludes that since the decedent no longer exists to enjoy or to miss anything, there is simply no one left for the new category to compensate: an award for "loss of life" would be answering a loss that was never actually suffered by anybody. Several other members consider this a rhetorical trick dressed up in dead languages, since it seems to entail that killing a healthy twenty-year-old outright is, once her funeral is paid for, no worse for her than merely maiming her; they want the category adopted, and want it to carry real weight in the damages figure juries reach.

I happen to be teaching a graduate seminar on the philosophy of death this term, which is presumably the only reason I was asked onto the committee in the first place, and my instinct is that the judge's argument, whatever its pedigree, is not actually decisive. But I don't want to send my colleagues a memo that simply asserts this without meeting her argument on its own ground -- in particular, the question of who is supposed to be harmed by a death, and when, if not a person who by then no longer exists. Could you draft the two-to-three-page memo I'll circulate before the vote? It should come down clearly on one of adopting the new category, rejecting it, or adopting some conditional version of it (say, scaled to how vividly the decedent anticipated the loss before dying); it should put the reasoning in terms the committee's judges and lawyers can actually use when drafting instruction language; and it should give the judge's Epicurean argument a substantive answer rather than waving it away as clever but beside the point.
