In a graduate game theory seminar cross-listed with the economics department, we recently spent a session on the centipede game, and I found that the discussion stopped just short of the issue I actually find puzzling. Everyone was happy to note that backward induction recommends defecting at every node, that this conflicts with the cooperative play routinely observed in experiments, and that this tension is often billed as "the centipede paradox" -- but that framing makes the puzzle sound like a merely empirical embarrassment for the theory. I understand there is a separate, purely theoretical version of the paradox that has nothing to do with experimental data and that arises already from taking seriously the epistemic assumptions behind backward induction itself. My professor mentioned Aumann's result that common knowledge of rationality is sufficient to yield the backward-induction outcome, and gestured at Binmore's objection that this is somehow the wrong moral to draw, but we ran out of time before actually reconstructing the argument.

Could you walk me through that deeper version of the paradox? I'm especially interested in the worry -- which I believe shows up in various forms in Binmore's, Reny's, Bicchieri's, and Stalnaker's writing on the topic -- that common knowledge of rationality is, in some sense, unstable or self-undermining once you ask what a player is entitled to infer upon reaching a node that backward induction says should never be reached: how can it be common knowledge from the very first move that every player will follow the backward-induction strategy all the way down, given that reaching almost any node in the tree is only possible because someone has already failed to do so? I already have a solid grip on backward induction itself and don't need that part re-derived; what I'm after is an intuitive but precise account of why the epistemic premises behind the result are supposed to be so hard to maintain consistently throughout the tree, and how, if at all, Aumann's formal theorem is meant to survive that worry.
