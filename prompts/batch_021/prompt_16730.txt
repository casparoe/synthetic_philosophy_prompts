I'm about a third of the way into a short story that's basically my own riff on that old "Appointment in Samarra" fable -- you know the one, a guy sees Death in the marketplace, Death gives him a funny look, guy bolts to another city thinking he's dodged fate, and of course that's exactly where Death was headed all along. My version swaps the marketplace for a trading floor and swaps Death for a very literal actuary who has never once been wrong about when someone's going to die. I mentioned the plot to my writing group and one guy, an ex math major, said "oh, that's just Death in Damascus" like it was obvious, and told me it's apparently an actual named problem in decision theory. So now I've fallen down a research hole instead of writing.

From what I can piece together (I think this traces back to a Gibbard and Harper paper from the 70s), philosophers use this exact setup to make trouble for causal decision theory, and apparently it's a different, worse kind of trouble than Newcomb's problem causes -- which is the one part of this whole area I already feel okay about. Everything I'm reading keeps saying the Damascus case shows there's no "ratifiable" act available at all, and treats that as a much bigger deal than "CDT just gives the answer that feels wrong," which is the usual complaint about two-boxing. I dont have a real feel for what ratifiability actually means, why Newcomb's problem manages to dodge this particular issue while Damascus doesn't, or what it even looks like for a decision to "flip-flop" the way some of these writeups describe.

Two smaller things I'd love folded in if you have room: I've seen it claimed that letting the agent randomize -- flipping a coin instead of just picking a city outright -- rescues ratifiability even though no straight choice does, and I can't work out why that would make a difference. Separately, for the story I'd actually prefer my actuary to be very reliable but not literally infallible -- does the paradox survive if he's right, say, 90% of the time instead of 100%, or does the whole thing specifically need certainty to get off the ground?
