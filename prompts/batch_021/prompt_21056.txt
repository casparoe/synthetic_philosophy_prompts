I'm on the small committee at the Christian college where I teach that reviews our institutional statement of faith every five or six years (a mix of a couple trustees, the provost, and a few of us faculty). The current line on the atonement says something like "Christ bore in Himself the full penalty due for our sins, dying in our place to satisfy the demands of divine justice" -- pretty standard penal substitution language. One of the philosophy professors on the committee brought up an old short paper by David Lewis (yes, the metaphysician) where he pokes at the analogy everyone reaches for -- that Christ paying our "penalty" is like a friend paying off your debt, so it doesn't matter who actually pays as long as the amount gets covered. Lewis's worry, as she explained it to me, is that punishment doesn't seem to work like debt in that way: what makes a punishment just is supposed to be that the guilty party is the one who bears it, not just that "enough suffering" occurs somewhere in the ledger -- and if you give that up and treat the penalty as fungible like a debt after all, it gets hard to say why a benevolent God couldn't have just forgiven the debt outright, the way a generous creditor can, without requiring anyone, even Christ, to suffer at all.

She wants us to either loosen the wording so it doesn't hang everything on one specific mechanism of substitution, or add some kind of clarifing note acknowledging the difficulty. Two of the trustees are pushing back, and not just about donors and optics -- their real point is that a confessional statement is supposed to be a stable thing people subscribe to across decades, not a document you reopen every time a clever philosophy paper surfaces a wrinkle, and this exact clause happens to be the one our denomination's regional body checks new faculty hires against, so touching it isn't a small, contained edit. I'm the committee secretary and need to circulate a short memo before we vote at next month's meeting laying out my actual recommendation -- keep the wording, revise it, or something in between -- with reasons I could defend in the room. Can you draft that memo for me? It should take a real position instead of punting to "needs more discussion," and it has to actually engage with the Lewis-style worry rather than just asserting that the doctrine is traditional so it's fine.
