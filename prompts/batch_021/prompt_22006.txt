Dutch Book arguments are invoked constantly across the rationalist blogosphere to explain why credences must obey the probability axioms, but the popular version glosses over a live dispute about what the argument actually shows. Write a dialogue between two committed Bayesians -- the traditionalist and the deflationist -- who agree that incoherent credences are defective but disagree about why. The traditionalist holds that vulnerability to a guaranteed loss is itself the irrationality, and wants the same style of argument run diachronically, so that refusing to conditionalize comes out irrational too. The deflationist treats the betting apparatus as dramatic packaging rather than the substance of the argument. On this view, a sufficiently forethoughtful agent could simply decline the last bet in the book once the pattern becomes evident, and the diachronic version additionally requires the bookie to know the agent's update policy in advance -- so the deflationist wants to keep only the structural residue: that an incoherent agent prices one and the same gamble differently depending on how it is described, treating the talk of money and exploitation as inessential dramatization. Have the two of them engage directly with each other's strongest moves rather than talk past one another, and push toward some kind of verdict on whether that structural residue can really carry the weight the pragmatic story was supposed to carry, especially when it comes to justifying conditionalization rather than mere synchronic coherence.
