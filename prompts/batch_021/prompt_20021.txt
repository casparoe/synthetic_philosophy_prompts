A while back I was trying to work out my own personal risk of developing a hereditary condition that runs in my family, and I noticed something strange: depending on whether I looked at statistics for "men over 40," "men over 40 with this specific family history," "men over 40 with this family history who also don't smoke," or some even narrower group, I got quite different numbers, without any obvious place to stop and settle on the one group that should count as "the" right one for a statement about my own individual risk. A friend who studies philosophy mentioned that this is a known issue called the reference class problem, and pointed me toward some further reading. From what I gathered, it's usually introduced as a problem specifically for frequentism, since if you hold that probabilities just are (or are defined by) long-run frequencies within some class of similar cases, you need a principled way of picking that class, and there generally isn't one, because any single case belongs to indefinitely many classes that would each hand you a different frequency.

What really threw me was coming across a paper by a philosopher named Alan Hájek arguing that this isn't merely a frequentist embarrassment that other theories of probability can shrug off. As best I can tell, he argues that essentially the same problem resurfaces for propensity theories, where the "objective chance" of an outcome is still only defined relative to some specification of the chance-generating setup, and even for Bayesians, to the extent that they want their personal credences to be constrained by, or calibrated to, some external notion of objective chance (via something like Lewis's Principal Principle). I can vaguely picture how a reference-class-style choice might sneak back in through the way you describe a "setup" or a "trial," but I don't have the technical background to judge whether this generalization actually holds up or is more of a rhetorical stretch dressed up as a parallel problem. Could you walk me through, as intuitively and non-technically as you can, why the reference class problem is supposed to survive outside of frequentism, ideally with a concrete example of where exactly, in a propensity theorist's or a Bayesian's setup, an implicit "which class do I use" choice is hiding?
