The following is an exam question from a graduate seminar on practical reason, together with a student's written answer to it.

Question: Explain Bernard Williams's distinction between internal and external reasons for action, including how he understands the agent's "subjective motivational set" and why he thinks external reason statements are problematic. Illustrate the internalist analysis with one of his own examples, and state precisely what conclusion he draws about the status of external reasons.

Student answer: "For Williams, a reason statement can be read in two ways. An internal reason statement is true only relative to the agent's 'subjective motivational set,' or S: A has an internal reason to φ just in case φ-ing is an effective means of satisfying some desire A already has within S, and the statement is false if no such desire is present or if φ-ing would not actually serve it. An external reason statement, by contrast, is supposed to hold of A regardless of what is in her S — the way Kant's categorical imperative is meant to bind any rational agent whatever she happens to want.

Williams argues that the external notion cannot really be made sense of. A consideration counts as a reason for someone only if it could, through some sound piece of practical reasoning, come to explain or motivate her action; a consideration with no foothold anywhere in her existing motivations could not move her in that way, and could at best produce a change in her non-rationally — through conversion, or a shift in sensibility — rather than through deliberation that reveals the consideration to already be a reason for her. He makes the point with the example of a man who treats his wife inconsiderately: an onlooker who insists that the man 'has a reason' to treat her better is speaking loosely unless something already in the man's S — some vestige of concern for her that sound deliberation could get a grip on — can anchor that reason. If nothing of the kind is present, Williams says, the truth is only that the man should treat her better, not that he has a reason to; the onlooker has mistaken an external evaluation of him for an internal reason. On this basis, Williams concludes that external reason statements are without exception false and that internal reasons are the only genuine kind, a conclusion he puts to work against the Kantian idea that moral requirements bind agents independently of their desires."
