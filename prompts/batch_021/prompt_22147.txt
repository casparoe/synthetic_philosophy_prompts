A friend and I spent most of a long train ride arguing about suffering-focused ethics after we'd both attended a talk on existential and "s-risk" (suffering risk) scenarios, and I still can't tell whether she won the argument or just wore me down. Her central move was what I gather is a fairly old objection to negative utilitarianism: if the only thing that ultimately matters, morally speaking, is the minimization of suffering, then the single most effective and impartially "best" action available to anyone in a position to do so would be to painlessly and instantaneously annihilate all sentient life, since a universe empty of sentience trivially has zero suffering in it. She pointed out that this isn't a strawman she invented on the spot but a classic challenge to the view, one that surfaced in the philosophical literature not long after Popper first floated the idea that public policy should aim at reducing misery rather than maximizing happiness. From there she concluded that because every negative utilitarian she's ever read explicitly recoils from this "benevolent world-exploder" conclusion, and because none of them actually goes around trying to end the world, the position must simply be incoherent as stated: either you accept an implication you find monstrous, or you quietly abandon the very priority on suffering that was supposed to distinguish the theory in the first place. On her view, this is basically why no serious contemporary philosopher can hold suffering-focused ethics without some self-deception.

I found this more persuasive in the moment than I now think it deserves, but I couldn't pin down exactly where the argument goes wrong, if it does. Does this "world-destruction" argument actually refute negative utilitarianism and suffering-focused views as a class, or does it really only succeed against one particular, fairly extreme formulation of the view, leaving other suffering-focused positions untouched? And even setting that aside, is there a gap between granting some purely axiological ranking of hypothetical worlds and being rationally committed, as a matter of practical recommendation, to actually pursuing world-destruction? I'd like to understand whether my friend's argument is the decisive objection she takes it to be, or whether it trades on collapsing distinctions that suffering-focused ethicists have good independent reasons to draw.
