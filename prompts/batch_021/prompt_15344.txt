I'm a junior at my high school, and I run the interview segment for our philosophy club's podcast — a fairly modest production, just a laptop mic and a couple dozen regular listeners. Next month's guest is a philosophy PhD student whose dissertation defends Galen Strawson's "Basic Argument" against moral responsibility: roughly, the claim that nobody can be truly responsible for anything they do, since that would require being responsible for the way they are, which would in turn require having chosen to be that way on the basis of some earlier self one would also have to be responsible for, and so on without end. I first ran into the argument through a blog post of hers that walks through this regress step by step, and I'm worried that if I don't plan carefully, the interview will just be her giving that same walk-through again in slightly different words.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that push past a restatement of the argument? I'd like one question, with a follow-up keyed to her likeliest answer, on why Strawson insists the argument is neutral between determinism and indeterminism — why he thinks letting genuine randomness into the causal story doesn't rescue responsibility either, since a random act seems no more "hers" than a fully determined one would be. I'd also like one question, with a follow-up, that raises the fact that Strawson traces part of the argument's pedigree to Nietzsche's mockery, in Beyond Good and Evil, of the wish to be causa sui — something about hauling yourself into existence by your own hair, though I might be misremembering the exact image — and asks whether that pedigree makes the Basic Argument look like a genuine discovery or more like an old intuition that keeps recurring without ever managing to convince most working philosophers.

Finally, I'd like one question, with a follow-up anticipating the likeliest way she'll try to sidestep it, built around the irony that Galen Strawson's own father, P.F. Strawson, wrote "Freedom and Resentment" to argue that reactive attitudes like resentment and gratitude don't need metaphysical backing and can't really be argued away regardless of what turns out to be true about ultimate responsibility. What I want the follow-up to press is whether the Basic Argument is supposed to change how we actually live, or only what we're rationally entitled to believe. Please close the list with a question asking what kind of consideration, if any, would count as evidence against the Basic Argument for her, so the segment doesn't end on pure exposition.
