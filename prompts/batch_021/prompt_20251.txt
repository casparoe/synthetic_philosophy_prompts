I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years, and one habit that has rubbed off on me is trying to locate the actual structure underneath a dispute before reaching for a verdict about who is right. I can't quite manage that trick for a low-stakes but oddly persistent conflict at work, so I wanted to think it through with you.

My company merged with a similarly sized firm about six months ago, and the two engineering teams now have to coordinate daily, which has surfaced a specific and stubborn source of friction. On my team, a Slack direct message implies same-day urgency, and people are expected to at least acknowledge one within an hour or two during working hours; this wasn't always the norm here — I'm told that a few years ago a couple of unusually fast-responding engineers joined, others started matching their pace so as not to look unresponsive by comparison, and within a year the quick turnaround had become the assumed default for everyone, newcomers included. The team from the other company settled into the opposite habit: a DM is treated as a mild imposition, reserved for things that genuinely couldn't be handled in a channel, and it's entirely unremarkable to leave one unanswered for a day or two. Since the merger, my former colleagues describe the other team as checked-out, while I gather, secondhand, that they describe us as unable to respect a colleague's time. Neither description strikes me as dishonest, exactly, and in isolation neither habit seems any more defensible than the other on the merits — yet the disagreement keeps generating real indignation on both sides rather than a shrug.

I've been asked to help draft a joint communication policy, and one of our senior engineers wants to shortcut the whole discussion by simply mandating whichever habit belongs to the larger of the two teams, on the theory that numbers should settle it. That may well be the pragmatic way to end the dispute, but it sidesteps the question I actually want an answer to: is either side correct here in any real sense, or is "correct" simply the wrong category to apply to a case like this? And if it's the latter, why does the disagreement still feel so morally charged rather than like a shrug-worthy difference in taste, and does the answer bear on whether we should pick one existing habit, engineer some blend of the two, or just let the merged team settle into a new default on its own, the way the original habit apparently formed the first time? I'd rather understand the actual shape of the problem than be handed a generic "communication is important, here's a compromise" answer.
