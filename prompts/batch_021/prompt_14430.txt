Two weeks ago I found out that I hadn't made our school's varsity soccer team, after playing JV for the past two years and spending the whole summer training for tryouts. When my best friend asked how I was doing right after the roster went up, I told her, quite seriously, "I didn't make the team, but I don't believe it." She laughed and said that was just a contradiction and didn't really mean anything, but I pushed back, because it didn't feel like nonsense to me -- it felt like the single most accurate description of what was actually going on in my head at that moment. We ended up arguing about it for a good twenty minutes without either of us backing down: her position was that the two halves of the sentence can't both be true of the same person at the same time, and mine was that they obviously can, since people fail to believe things that are happening to them all the time.

What's been nagging at me since then is something I only noticed afterward. If I'd said the same kind of thing about my teammate who also got cut ("she didn't make it, but she doesn't believe it"), or if I said it now, a few weeks later, about that exact moment ("for a second after I saw the list, I didn't believe it"), neither version would sound odd to anyone. It's only the version where I'm reporting my own disbelief about my own life, right as it's happening, that seems to cause a problem. I don't know enough philosophy to tell whether my friend was right that I said something empty or confused, or whether I was actually onto something real that an ordinary true-or-false sentence just isn't built to capture cleanly. Could you help me work out what's really going on here -- whether there's a genuine inconsistency in what I said, and why the exact same words stop sounding strange the moment they're about someone else, or about a moment that's safely in the past?
