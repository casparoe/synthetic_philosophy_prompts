I am putting together a session for my graduate seminar on formal approaches to distributive ethics, and I keep coming back to the question of how much argumentative weight Harsanyi's aggregation theorem can really bear. The result is often presented as though it delivers utilitarianism almost for free: if each individual and a social evaluator have preferences over lotteries that satisfy the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms, and the social preference respects a minimal Pareto condition on individual indifference, then the social utility function must be an affine, weighted sum of the individual utility functions. Harsanyi treated this as close to an axiomatic vindication of utilitarianism, and later writers such as John Broome have developed related aggregation results in much the same spirit.

The theorem has also attracted serious objections. Amartya Sen argued that it trades on an equivocation between "utility" as a mere numerical index of an individual's attitude toward risk and "utility" as an interpersonally comparable measure of welfare, so that the additive structure the theorem delivers may be an artifact of the von Neumann-Morgenstern representation rather than a genuine discovery about the shape of the good. Peter Diamond, in his 1967 comment on Harsanyi, described a case in which an indivisible benefit has to go to one of two equally situated people, and argued that Harsanyi's axioms commit us to being indifferent between simply assigning it to one of them and settling the matter with a fair coin toss whenever the two options yield the same expected utility -- a verdict that seems to miss something real about the value of a fair procedure. A colleague of mine in decision theory maintains that these objections are ultimately answerable and that the theorem still earns its utilitarian conclusion; I am not convinced, and would like a considered second opinion before I commit myself in front of the class.

Please write an essay -- the sort of thing I could circulate to the seminar as a model answer -- on the following question: does Harsanyi's aggregation theorem give us good reason to accept some form of utilitarianism as the correct procedure for aggregating welfare across individuals, or do the Sen- and Diamond-style objections show that its premises are either question-begging or too normatively thin to support that conclusion? Set out the strongest version of the argument on each side, but do not end on a non-committal note: tell me where, on balance, the argument succeeds or fails, and say explicitly whether the tension between ex ante and ex post ways of honoring the Pareto principle can be resolved or simply has to be lived with.
