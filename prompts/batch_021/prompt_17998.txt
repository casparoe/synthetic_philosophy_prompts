After retiring a few years ago from a career as an experimental physicist working mostly on low-temperature thermodynamics, I have finally had the leisure to read philosophy properly, and I have lately fallen down a rabbit hole on money-pump arguments in decision theory. The basic argument struck me at once as a cousin of something I spent decades taking for granted: just as a device that returns to its exact original state at the end of every cycle while still yielding a net output of useful work, with nothing else put in, is impossible on pain of being a perpetual-motion machine, an agent with cyclic preferences (she strictly prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A) can be walked through a cycle of trades that returns her to her original holding while relieving her of a small fee at every step -- a sort of perpetual-motion machine that runs on her money instead of on heat. This is offered as a practical vindication of the requirement that a rational agent's preferences be transitive, and I found it about as convincing as the thermodynamic argument it echoes.

What has since complicated things for me is the literature on "resolute choice," of the kind developed by the decision theorist Edward McClennen. As I understand the proposal, an agent can keep her cyclic preferences and still never actually be pumped, provided that at the very first trade she commits to a complete policy for the whole sequence and then sticks to it even at the later points where, taken on its own, the next trade would look like a further improvement. This is presented as an exercise of specifically diachronic rationality, belonging to plans rather than to momentary acts, and not simply hostage to the synchronic coherence of preference at an instant. I cannot decide whether this is a genuine insight about the proper unit of rational assessment, or whether it merely relocates the transitivity requirement to a higher level -- she now needs a consistent ranking over entire policies, which looks suspiciously like the very thing transitivity was supposed to supply in the first place -- nor can I tell whether resoluteness is doing any work that a merely "sophisticated" chooser -- one who simply uses backward induction to avoid ever entering the first bad trade -- does not already do more cheaply.

I would be grateful if you could write me a dialogue between two decision theorists of equal standing and sophistication, staged beside the agent at the very moment she is offered the first trade in the cycle: one who defends the money-pump argument and denies that resolute choice offers any genuine escape from it, and one who defends resolute choice as a legitimate exercise of diachronic rationality that a purely synchronic analysis of preference is simply blind to. I would like the two of them to engage directly with each other's strongest moves rather than talk past one another -- in particular, I want the money-pump defender to press both the "you have only pushed transitivity up a level" objection and the "sophisticated choice already achieves this without any resoluteness" objection, and I want the resolute-choice defender to have genuine, considered replies to each rather than simply restating the position.
