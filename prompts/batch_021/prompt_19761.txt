I have spent a good deal of my free time over the past year reading Thomas Hobbes on my own, and one thing has stuck with me more than his political theory. In his later years Hobbes was locked in a long, bitter dispute with the mathematician John Wallis over a supposed solution to squaring the circle, never conceding even after Wallis kept exposing the flaws in his proofs. Digging into the background, I found what looks like the deeper root of that stubbornness: because Hobbes held that nothing exists except body, he could not accept a truly dimensionless point or a perfectly breadthless line, and instead treated a point as a body whose size we simply agree to disregard, and a line as an extremely thin body rather than a width-less abstraction. At the same time, he singled geometry out as the one science of his day capable of complete certainty, apparently because we construct its objects ourselves rather than discovering them ready-made in nature. I'd like help working out whether these two commitments are genuinely connected: is his materialist reworking of points and lines actually doing some of the work behind the claim that geometry alone can be fully certain, or are they just two separate Hobbes doctrines sitting side by side? And if there is a real connection, does the same reasoning explain why he hoped his own political philosophy could eventually be made just as rigorous as geometry?
