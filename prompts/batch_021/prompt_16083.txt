QUESTION:
Judy Benjamin is dropped into a region divided, by two crosscutting boundaries, into Red or Blue territory and, independently, into a Headquarters area or a Second Company area. Before she learns anything, her credence is spread uniformly over the four possibilities -- Red-HQ, Red-Second-Company, Blue-HQ, Blue-Second-Company -- 1/4 each. She radios her own side and receives exactly one piece of information before the radio dies: "If you're in Red territory, the odds are 3:1 that you're in the Headquarters area rather than the Second Company area." Nothing is said about Blue territory, and nothing compares the overall likelihood of Red to Blue.

Now suppose we update Judy's credences using the following rule: among all probability assignments over the four possibilities that are consistent with the stated 3:1 constraint, pick the one that minimizes cross-entropy (relative information) against her prior.

(a) Compute Judy's new probabilities for the four possibilities under this rule, showing your work.
(b) Report her new probability of being in Red territory, compare it to the prior probability of 1/2, and say whether the comparison strikes you as surprising given what the message actually said.
(c) In roughly 150-250 words, explain what this example is generally taken to establish about extending ordinary belief-updating to evidence that comes as a conditional probability, and note any qualifications or alternative responses you think are worth mentioning.

(b) Judy's new probability of Red territory is 0.351 + 0.117 β‰ˆ 0.468, down from the prior 0.5; correspondingly, Blue rises to about 0.532. This is genuinely surprising: the message was entirely "internal" to Red, and never compared Red to Blue, so you'd expect P(Red) to stay at 0.5 while only the internal 3:1 split moved. Instead, minimizing relative information quietly pushes some probability toward Blue as a side effect. Interestingly, the 50/50 split within Blue itself is untouched (0.266 = 0.266) -- it's only the overall Red/Blue balance that shifts, not the internal Blue ratio.

(c) This example is one of the classic counterexamples to Jeffrey conditionalization -- Jeffrey actually devised the Judy Benjamin case himself, as a kind of stress test for his own machinery of probability kinematics, and it's usually read as showing that his rule for updating on soft evidence breaks down whenever what you learn is a conditional rather than a categorical claim: the rule moves credences around in places the evidence never touched, which isn't the "minimal" or conservative change we want from an updating rule. Given that, I think the natural and basically correct response is to stop leaning on relative-information minimization, or on Jeffrey's rule generally, for this kind of case, and instead fall back on plain conditionalization: read "if Red, then most likely Headquarters" as equivalent to the material conditional not-(Red and Second Company), and condition on that in the ordinary way. Ordinary conditionalization only rescales probabilities by how well each possibility fits what was actually learned, so it can't produce the kind of unmotivated spillover into Blue that shows up above -- which is exactly the feature that makes relative-information minimization look suspect as a general updating rule in the first place.
