So a random bit of context: I started a new job a few months back at a big fulfillment center (scanning, quotas, the whole deal), and its honestly kind of soul-crushing, not because anyone's mean to me but because I feel like a replaceable part in a machine, like nothing I actually do belongs to me in any real sense. I was venting about this to my roommate over dinner, who's currently in a Marx reading group, and she immediately went "oh that's alienation, textbook." But then when I added "also they clearly don't pay us anywhere near what we're worth to the company," she said no, that's a completely different concept, exploitation, and that people mix the two up constantly. She also mentioned, kind of offhand, that there's apparently a whole scholarly fight about whether the "young" Marx who wrote about alienation and the "mature" Marx of Capital who wrote about exploitation were even doing the same project anymore — like some people think he basically ditched the alienation stuff as too Hegelian and unscientific once he got older, while others think it's the same critique the whole way through just dressed differently.

Now I can't stop turning this over in my head. Are exploitation and alienation actually just two names for the same underlying problem with capitalism, one pitched more economically and one more psychologically/existentially, or are they genuinely separate diagnoses that could in principle come apart (like, could you imagine a worker who's exploited in the technical surplus-value sense but not alienated, or the reverse)? And if you had to say one of the two concepts is doing more of the real philosophical work in Marx's critique, or is more defensible as a standalone critique of capitalism today, which would it be and why? I'd love an answer that actually engages with the interpretive debate rather than just handing me dictionary definitions of both terms, since I already sort of get those on their own.
