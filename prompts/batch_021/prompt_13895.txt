I'm a PhD student in economics, and a few of us in the decision theory reading group run a small podcast on formal approaches to rational choice under uncertainty. Next month's guest -- who trained originally as a physicist, which shows in how she argues -- has built much of her career defending a position she calls "geometric rationality," loosely aligned with the "ergodicity economics" research program: the claim that an agent facing repeated multiplicative gambles (investment returns, repeated favorable bets, anything that compounds) should evaluate strategies by their long-run time-average growth rate rather than by expected value, and that this recovers the Kelly criterion not as one utility function among many but as something close to a forced move, once you notice that wealth trajectories are non-ergodic and that "expected value" quietly substitutes an average over a probability ensemble for the average a single agent actually lives through in time. She's a practiced debater who tends to fold every objection back into a restatement of that ergodicity point, so I'd like the questions to make that harder to do.

Could you draft nine or ten interview questions along those lines? I want at least one question that presses on whether geometric rationality is really a rival to expected utility theory or just isoelastic utility with a relative risk aversion coefficient of exactly one -- log utility -- dressed up in the vocabulary of ergodic theory, given that standard Bayesian decision theory has never claimed to dictate which utility function is correct and has always been able to accommodate log utility as one admissible choice among others. I want one on the Samuelson-style objection that Kelly's optimality is asymptotic and almost sure -- a claim about who wins "in the long run" with probability one -- which doesn't obviously tell an agent facing a single decision, or a life of finite length, to wager the full Kelly fraction rather than some smaller one; include a follow-up for the case where she answers by pointing to bankroll size or number of repetitions, since practitioners sympathetic to her view still routinely recommend "half Kelly" or less. I want one that questions the choice of wealth itself as the privileged variable, given that Kelly's own 1956 result was derived for a gambler with a specific informational edge over a horse race and framed as a fact about information rate rather than offered as a general theory of practical reason -- so why should growth of wealth, rather than growth of some other quantity, be the thing rationality is defined over? And I want one that goes directly at that origin: does the framework's mathematics inherit any genuine normative force from its descent from Shannon's theorem, or is the information-theoretic ancestry just packaging that makes a particular utility choice sound more inevitable than it is? For each of these, add a short follow-up question tailored to the answer she's likely to give. Close with a question asking her to name one concrete decision problem, if one exists, where geometric rationality and orthodox subjective expected utility maximization actually recommend different actions to a real agent -- so that listeners can judge whether this is a substantive dispute about rational choice or a disagreement about vocabulary.
