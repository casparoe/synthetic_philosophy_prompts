In a philosophy seminar on social norms this term, we spent several weeks on David Lewis's account of convention, and a disagreement I overheard between two friends last week seems like a good test case for it. One friend was complaining about the tip-prompt screens now appearing at self-checkout kiosks and coffee-shop registers: tipping there, he argued, is a purely manufactured expectation -- there's no server, no personalized effort, nothing resembling the service tips were originally meant to reward -- so declining to tip in these settings isn't a moral failing but simply the correct response. The other disagreed: once enough customers tip in a given setting, she said, workers and employers start planning around that income, so declining stops being a neutral choice and starts imposing a real cost on people who now depend on it, meaning the practice becomes something one ought to go along with, whatever its arbitrary origin. He replied that this proves too much: by that logic, any expectation a company successfully manufactures and gets enough customers to comply with becomes binding on everyone else, turning successful manipulation into genuine obligation. She conceded there should be some difference between a norm that genuinely serves everyone who follows it and one a company simply imposes top-down, but admitted she couldn't say exactly where that line falls. Neither of them could settle it, and neither could I. Could you go through their exchange move by move, saying who has the better of it at each step, and then note anything important that both of them are missing?
