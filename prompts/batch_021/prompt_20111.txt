So in my medieval philosophy seminar we just spent two whole classes on Avicenna's "flying man" argument right before jumping into Descartes, and honestly it's been rattling around in my head since. For anyone who doesn't know it: Avicenna asks you to imagine a person created fully-formed in a single instant, suspended in empty air with his limbs spread out so they can't even touch each other, and totally cut off from every sense — no sight, no sound, no touch of anything, including his own body. His claim is that even in that state, this person would still affirm his own existence, his "self," without affirming the existence of a single limb or organ. Avicenna thinks this shows that self-awareness doesn't depend on awareness of the body at all.

My professor mentioned in passing that there's actual disagreement among Avicenna scholars about what this is even supposed to prove — like some people (I think she said Marmura, or someone with a name like that) read it as basically a modest epistemological point about self-awareness being immediate and not inferred from body-awareness, while other people treat it as a full-blown proof that the soul is a separate immaterial substance from the body, kind of like a proto-Descartes move. That made me wonder if it even survives the classic objection thrown at Descartes's similar argument in the 6th Meditation: that just because you can conceive of/doubt your body while being certain of your "self," that doesnt actually mean the self COULD exist without the body, so the argument confuses an epistemic gap with a real metaphysical distinction.
