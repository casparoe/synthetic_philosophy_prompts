I'm taking a graduate seminar this term that's basically half formal epistemology, half decision theory, and this past week we covered the Sleeping Beauty problem and the absent-minded driver problem back to back, which felt like a funny coincidence since I've spent years quietly losing track of which exit I'm at on my own commute (there are two identical-looking exits off my highway and I've definately missed the right one more than once). Now that I've actually sat down with the driver problem's math though, I think I've noticed something and I want someone to sanity-check me because my study group is split on it.

In the driver problem, exiting at the first of the two intersections pays 0, sailing past both pays 1, and nailing the second one pays the jackpot of 4. If you actually optimize over this, it turns out the best strategy is to exit with probability 1/3 every time you find yourself at an intersection (so keep driving with probability 2/3). And in Sleeping Beauty, the "thirder" answer says her credence that the coin landed heads, on any given waking, should also come out to exactly 1/3. Two puzzles that are both fundamentally about not being able to tell which "instance" of a situation you're currently in, and they both bottom out at the same number — that seems like way more than a coincidence to me. I've been going around telling people that the absent-minded driver problem is basically just Sleeping Beauty dressed up as a decision problem, and that accepting the standard 1/3 solution to the driver problem commits you to being a thirder. One friend in the seminar backs me up, but another keeps saying I'm "smashing together two questions that aren't actually the same kind of question" without ever explaining what she means. Is she right, and if so, what exactly am I collapsing together that shouldn't be collapsed?
