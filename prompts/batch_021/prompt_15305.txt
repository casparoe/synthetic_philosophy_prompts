I work in formal verification, writing system specifications in TLA+ and Coq, and until recently I simply assumed that a sufficiently precise first-order specification pins down the mathematical structure it is meant to describe. A few weeks ago, while preparing an internal talk on the foundations of our own tools, I finally worked through Skolem's paradox: the fact that, if ZFC is consistent, it has a countable model, even though ZFC itself proves, via Cantor's theorem, that uncountable sets exist. I follow the textbook resolution — that countability turns out to be relative to the model rather than an absolute property of a set — but it has left me genuinely unsure what our specifications actually determine, if anything, and whether 'the' natural numbers or 'the' continuum are determinate objects at all. Could you lay out the most important implications of Skolem's paradox, both for how practitioners should regard the ambitions of first-order axiomatization and for the wider debate over mathematical realism? I would especially value your judgment on which of these worries are philosophically substantive and which merely reflect a limitation of first-order logic that a categorical second-order formulation would dissolve.
