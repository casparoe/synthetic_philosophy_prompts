Now, while grading final exams, I have come across a student's answer to almost exactly the question we were arguing about, and I find I can no longer tell whether it is a genuinely strong answer or an articulate version of the very conflation my friend was pressing on me.

The question, worth 20 points, reads: "In the Pensées, Pascal observes that human beings, unable to make what is just strong, have instead made what is strong just, and concludes that laws should be obeyed because they are laws, not because their justice has ever been demonstrated -- indeed, he warns that tracing an established law back to its true foundation is itself dangerous. Reconstruct Pascal's argument for this position, explain what work it does within the larger structure of the Pensées, and assess whether it is best read as a version of Hobbes's justification of sovereign authority or as resting on different premises. Support your answer with reference to Pascal's treatment of custom and of reason elsewhere in the text."

Here is the student's answer in full: "Pascal's argument turns on the claim that human reason cannot agree on, or demonstrate, what justice actually requires, so no state could survive if its laws had to wait on a demonstration of that kind. States found their laws on force instead, and only afterward dress that force up as justice, so that it comes to be obeyed willingly rather than merely feared. This is why Pascal insists that laws are to be obeyed simply because they are laws and not because their justice has been shown, and why he warns that tracing an established law back to its true foundation is dangerous: the foundation turns out to be custom and force, and exposing this openly would unsettle an order that depends on not being questioned. This line of thought is close to Montaigne's discussion of custom in the 'Apology for Raymond Sebond,' which Pascal is clearly drawing on, though he pushes it toward a more explicitly religious conclusion than Montaigne does. It also serves the larger argument of the Pensées, in which Pascal repeatedly shows that unaided reason cannot settle the questions that matter most -- about justice, but also about the soul and about God -- so that we come to feel the need for something besides reason, which for Pascal is ultimately faith. Since reason cannot ground justice, and looking for its foundation only exposes custom and force, Pascal's real conclusion is that there is no such thing as justice in an objective sense: only the various customs that different times and places happen to enforce as if they were justice.

Because of this, I think Pascal's position lines up much more closely with Hobbes's justification of sovereign authority than it might first appear, and not only in the shared observation that justice unbacked by force is empty -- Hobbes's own point that covenants without the sword are 'but words, and of no strength to secure a man at all' is essentially the political-theory version of what Pascal is describing. The two converge most in their shared conclusion that what actually holds a society together is superior force rather than moral truth discovered by reason. From there, the positions look like versions of the same theory: Pascal, like Hobbes, is in effect telling us that submitting to the existing custom is the rational choice, since the only alternative to accepting whatever force currently underwrites the law is the worse chaos of contesting it. The disagreement between them is mostly one of temperament -- Pascal moralizes about human vanity where Hobbes stays clinically neutral -- but the underlying political theory, that authority is simply force that has succeeded in presenting itself as justice, is the same in both."
