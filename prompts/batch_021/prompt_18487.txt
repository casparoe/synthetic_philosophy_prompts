ok so in my world religions elective we're doing a daoism unit and today we read two zhuangzi bits — the one where his wife dies and instead of crying he's sitting on the floor banging on a wash basin and singing, and the one near the end where he's dying himself and tells his students not to bother burying him properly because heaven and earth are already his coffin and the sun and moon his jewelry, and when they say they're scared crows will eat him he says something like "above ground the crows get me, below ground the worms do, so what's the difference." our small group got stuck arguing about what he's actually claiming and it turned into this whole back-and-forth between mia and dev that never got resolved.

mia's take: zhuangzi thinks death just isn't bad, period, it's a natural transformation like winter turning into spring, so grieving over it is an actual mistake, not just an overreaction. dev disagreed — his read is that zhuangzi's real point is we can't know whether death is bad, since we're stuck inside life with no way to check what's on the other side, so the move is "stop assuming you know it's bad," not "it's actually fine," which is a much weaker claim.

mia came back with the line where zhuangzi says that if he kept wailing over his wife it would mean he "didn't understand fate" — which sounds like he thinks grief is just straight up false, not merely unjustified given what we don't know. dev's counter was that the text also says zhuangzi did grieve for a bit at first, before he thought it through and stopped, which fits his reading better: its not that grief is automatically wrong, its that staying in it once you realize you have no actual grounds for treating death as uniquely terrible is wrong. then mia pointed at the deathbed scene and said the crow/worm line isn't agnostic AT ALL — that's someone cracking jokes because he's already decided it doesn't matter, not someone suspending judgement.
