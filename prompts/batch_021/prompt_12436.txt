I spent about thirty-five years as an experimental condensed-matter physicist -- low-temperature stuff, specific heats, phase transitions -- and used entropy everyday without ever once wondering where its arrow actually comes from. Now that I'm retired I've landed in a little book club with two other retired scientists, and we've been chewing through some philosophy of physics: Sean Carroll's "From Eternity to Here," then David Albert's "Time and Chance." Albert's move is to just posit a "Past Hypothesis" -- the universe started out in an extraordinarily low-entropy macrostate, full stop, no further explanation on offer -- and then let ordinary statistical mechanics do the rest. My first reaction was that this is a fudge dressed up as a postulate: Penrose has that famous estimate that the initial state occupied something like one part in 10^(10^123) of the available phase space, and Albert's basically asking us to accept that and move on.

What's been nagging at me since is a counter I found in Carroll's book -- one of my book club mates has really dug his heels into it -- namely, that the only real alternative to swallowing that fine-tuning is to treat our whole observed pocket of order as a rare thermal fluctuation out of an eternally boring equilibrium background. That view has a nasty consequence though: on it, observers like us should overwhelmingly turn out to be short-lived "Boltzmann brain" flukes with entirely false memories, rather than creatures with real records of a genuinely ordered past. My friend thinks this forces you straight back to the fine-tuned Past Hypothesis, on pain of not being able to trust any of your evidence at all -- including whatever evidence got you doing physics in the first place. I'm not convinced it's that clean a knockdown, but I cant quite pin down where it goes wrong.

So I'd like you to make the strongest case you can that the Boltzmann-brain worry does not actually force us to prefer the fine-tuned Past Hypothesis over the fluctuation picture -- that one can take the fluctuation story seriously without thereby losing the right to trust memory and evidence the way my friend thinks. Don't just wave the problem away, and be honest at the end about what you think is the weakest link in your own case.
