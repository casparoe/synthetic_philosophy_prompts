So I've been knee deep in research for a novel that's partly set in abolitionist circles in the 1850s (nothing dramatic yet, just trying to get the texture right before I invent anybody), and I brought a chunk of David Blight's Douglass biography to book club because I couldn't stop talking about it. It kicked off a real argument between two of the members, Marcus and Nadia, that's been rattling around in my head ever since, and I honestly can't referee it myself. Marcus's opening move was that Douglass's famous flip -- from agreeing with Garrison that the Constitution was a pro-slavery pact not even worth honoring (Garrison literally burned a copy once, calling it a covenant with death) to later insisting that read properly it was "a glorious liberty document" -- was basically motivated reasoning dressed up as legal argument. Whatever clever case Douglass and guys like Lysander Spooner built, he said, the delegates who wrote the fugitive slave clause and the three-fifths clause obviously meant to protect slavery, and no reinterpretation changes what people actually meant at the time.

Nadia pushed back that it's not nothing that the framers went out of their way to keep the words "slave" and "slavery" out of the document itself, using phrases like "all other Persons" and "held to Service or Labour" instead -- and if the Constitution only has authority because "we the people" consented to its actual words, that deliberate silence ought to count for something, especially since most of what we know about the framers' real intentions only came out decades later, through Madison's private convention notes. Marcus wasn't having it: nobody in 1787 was fooled by "other persons," he said, so calling it ambiguous is really just a lawyer's trick, convenient for a guy who needed a legal hook to keep fighting slavery through electoral politics rather than accept Garrison's bleaker but more honest answer of disunion. Nadia said that's the actual crux of it -- Marcus wants the text governed by what certain framers secretly understood, while Douglass and Spooner's whole point was that a document's binding meaning has to come from public words ordinary people (and enslaved people, who consented to none of it) could actually read, and that construing euphemistic, ambiguous grants of power narrowly isn't a trick, its just a normal move in reading law. Also, she added, Douglass having political motives doesn't automatically make his reading wrong -- plenty of people land on good arguments for mixed reasons.

Neither of them budged before we got dragged into a separate fight about the cheese plate, so it never got resolved.
