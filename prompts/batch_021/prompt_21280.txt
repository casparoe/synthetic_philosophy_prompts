I'm a few weeks into reporting a longer feature on the "mechanistic interpretability" crowd -- the Anthropic/DeepMind types who go poking around inside a model's weights looking for "features" and "circuits" -- and I keep running into a conceptual snag none of my interviews have really resolved for me. A lot of these researchers talk the way Chris Olah did in that old "Zoom In" essay, where features are kind of like cells and circuits are like the organelles they're built from, and finding them is basically a biological-style discovery of structure that was already sitting there, just waiting to be dissected out. Then I described the "Golden Gate Claude" stunt to a philosopher friend at a dinner party, and she said the whole thing sounded to her less like discovering organelles and more like Dennett's old "real patterns" idea -- a pattern counts as real if some stance on the system lets you compress or predict it better, full stop, no further fact needed about whether its a literal, separable part of the machine.

I can't tell which framing is doing more work once you throw in stuff like superposition and polysemanticity -- a single direction in activation space apparently doing several unrelated jobs depending on context, which sounds a lot more Dennett-pattern than Olah-organelle to me. So could you actually compare the two views for me -- the "features and circuits are natural kinds we're literally discovering" realist picture, versus Dennett's more deflationary "real patterns" picture -- and say which one the empirical mess coming out of interpretability research (superposition, polysemantic neurons, features that shift depending on what basis you're looking at the activations in) actually fits better?

Don't just tell me "it depends" -- I need an actual verdict with the reasoning spelled out, since my editor keeps kicking this section back asking me to "pick a side already."
