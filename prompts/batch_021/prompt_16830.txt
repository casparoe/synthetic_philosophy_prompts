We both grant Kant's classic move against Anselm/Descartes: existence isn't a "real predicate," it doesn't add anything to a concept -- hence the hundred-thalers bit (a hundred real thalers contain no more, conceptually, than a hundred merely possible ones). Where we split is whether that move actually touches Gödel's modal version. Gödel never puts bare "existence" into God's definition -- he defines God as whatever has all "positive properties," then seperately adds necessary existence as one of those positive properties (via the essence / NE(x) setup) and runs the whole thing through S5 to get that a God-like being necessarily exists in every possible world, this one included. My friend thinks Kant's objection just doesnt reach that far, since "necessary existence" isn't the same target as plain existence. I think Kant's got more ammo, because in the Postulates of Empirical Thought he already argues modal notions generally (possibility, actuality, necessity) add nothing to a concept's content either -- they just express its relation to our cognitive faculties, not a further determination of the object itself.
