For my seminar on the philosophy of artificial intelligence, I've been working through Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," and I'm stuck on his response to what he calls the argument from extrasensory perception. Turing takes the statistical evidence for telepathy surprisingly seriously, worrying that a telepathically gifted human contestant could let the interrogator distinguish human from machine in the imitation game for reasons that have nothing to do with either party's capacity to think; his proposed fix is to run the test in a "telepathy-proof room." This seems like a strange move: rather than treating the mere possibility of ESP as evidence against the imitation game's validity as a criterion for thought, he treats it as a contingent nuisance to be engineered around. Could you explain why Turing thought isolating the participants this way was an adequate response, and whether this concession exposes a deeper problem with using conversational indistinguishability as an operational criterion for thinking?
