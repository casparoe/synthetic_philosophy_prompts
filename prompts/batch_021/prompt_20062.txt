For the past two years I have served on my university's Academic Integrity Board, and because I also teach the department's graduate seminar in formal epistemology, I keep noticing a theoretical wrinkle underneath what looks like a purely administrative problem. When an instructor flags a paper as possibly AI-written, the detection software we license does not return a verdict; it returns a number, something like "82% likely AI-generated," and neither the software nor the hearing that follows ever gives us certainty about the underlying fact. Several members of the Board want to treat any score above a fixed threshold as effectively decisive, on the reasoning that the number has already absorbed whatever uncertainty there is, and that any further hedging on our part is epistemically unmotivated. That policy amounts, as I see it, to conditionalizing on the proposition "this essay is AI-generated" as though we had learned it with certainty, when what we actually have is closer to the case Richard Jeffrey introduced probability kinematics, or "Jeffrey conditionalization," to handle: a shift in credence produced by a fallible instrument, not a new certainty available to condition on.

The complication is that real cases never arrive as a single number. A typical file accumulates the detector's score, then a stylometric comparison with the student's earlier, unflagged work, then whatever the student says at the hearing -- three pieces of soft evidence delivered at three separate meetings. Having now sat through enough of these hearings, I have an uncomfortable suspicion, which a colleague's informal tally of our closed cases seems to bear out, that the Board's eventual confidence depends on the order in which this evidence gets considered, in a way that plain conditionalization on certain evidence would not permit but that Jeffrey updating, absent some further constraint, does not obviously rule out. I do not think this makes my colleagues who favor a bright-line threshold unreasonable: they are managing several hundred cases a year on volunteer faculty time, and a rule that requires reconstructing a posterior from first principles at every hearing is not one an all-volunteer board can actually run.

I would like your help turning this into a document the Board can vote on. Please draft the short policy memo I will circulate before our meeting in two weeks, recommending a specific, usable protocol -- with a date by which it would take effect -- for how detector scores and other uncertain evidence arriving in stages should be combined into a misconduct finding, including whether a score should ever be allowed to settle a case on its own, and what, if anything, should be done about the order-sensitivity I have described. I want an actual recommendation, defended against the strongest version of the threshold-rule position, not an even-handed survey of considerations.
