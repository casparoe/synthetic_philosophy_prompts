Since moving to Jerusalem last year, I've begun attending a study group affiliated with an organization that trains Kohanim and drafts halakhic protocols for resuming Temple service, which has pushed me to read Maimonides on sacrifice directly rather than rely on secondhand summaries. In the Guide (III:32), he treats the sacrificial cult as a concession to the idolatrous practices the Israelites had absorbed from their surroundings, tolerated and redirected toward God only because weaning them off it all at once would have been psychologically impossible, with prayer implicitly cast as the more advanced end-point of that trajectory. Yet in the Mishneh Torah he codifies the sacrificial laws in exhaustive, permanently binding detail, and separately rules that the Temple Mount's original consecration sanctified it for all time rather than only for as long as the Temple stood — a ruling some of the group's more activist members cite as grounds for resuming offerings like the Paschal lamb even before the Temple itself is rebuilt. Given this tension between his historicizing rationale for sacrifice and his treatment of its laws as eternally binding, how do you think Maimonides himself would react to such a movement: would he see it as fulfilling a law he took such pains to legislate, or does his own account of why sacrifice was instituted in the first place give him grounds to feel more conflicted about the project than its present-day advocates do?
