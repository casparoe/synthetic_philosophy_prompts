A friend of mine who is finishing a PhD in computational neuroscience and I spent most of a long train journey arguing about Integrated Information Theory (IIT), Giulio Tononi's account on which a system's degree of consciousness is measured by its integrated information, Φ ("phi"), while the character of that experience is given by the shape of the maximally irreducible cause-effect structure the system specifies over itself. We ended up rehashing Scott Aaronson's well-known blog critique of the theory, in which he pointed out that IIT's own formalism assigns enormous Φ to certain simple, static systems -- large, regular grids of logic gates and similarly densely interconnected circuits of the sort (expander graphs, in effect) that would be needed to implement something as mundane as an error-correcting code -- apparently far more Φ than any serious estimate assigns to an actual human brain. Pressed on this, Tononi did not retreat or patch the theory; he bit the bullet, maintaining that if such a system really instantiated that cause-effect structure, it would indeed have a rich, if utterly alien, form of experience, and that the intuition that a static logic circuit obviously cannot be conscious is exactly the sort of confident but unsupported prejudice a serious science of consciousness needs to be willing to overrule.

My friend thinks this response gives the game away: a theory that can absorb any counterexample simply by declaring its consequence acceptable has stopped constraining anything, and is well on its way to being unfalsifiable -- essentially the same complaint that a large number of consciousness researchers pressed publicly against IIT a few years ago. I found myself defending Tononi instead, on the grounds that plenty of good theories have earned their keep precisely by overriding confident intuitions (relativity did this to simultaneity, quantum mechanics to locality), and that whether Tononi's move counts as principled or merely ad hoc turns on whether IIT's axioms actually entail the disputed verdict, not on how unwelcome that verdict feels. Neither of us managed to convince the other before our stop.

I'd like you to make the strongest possible case for my side of that argument: that Tononi's bullet-biting in the face of Aaronson-style counterexamples is an intellectually legitimate way of defending IIT, rather than a symptom of the theory's failure or vacuity. Please engage with IIT's actual axioms and postulates, not just the headline Φ value, in explaining what would have to be true of the theory for this kind of bullet-biting to count as principled rather than ad hoc, and address head-on whether the analogy to cases like relativity and quantum mechanics really holds up, rather than simply asserting that intuitions are generally unreliable.
