Question (20 pts total): In "Minds, Brains, and Programs" (1980), Searle introduces the Chinese Room thought experiment to argue against what he calls "Strong AI." (a) [6 pts] Reconstruct the thought experiment and state Searle's main conclusion in your own words. (b) [8 pts] Explain the Systems Reply to Searle, and explain how Searle himself responds to it. (c) [6 pts] Does Searle's response to the Systems Reply succeed? Defend your view in a short paragraph.

Student answer:
(a) Searle imagines a man who doesn't know any Chinese locked in a room with a big rulebook (written in English) that tells him how to match strings of Chinese symbols coming in through a slot with other strings of Chinese symbols to send back out. To the people outside, who are feeding in questions in Chinese and getting sensible answers back, it looks like whoever is in the room understands Chinese. But the man is just doing syntactic symbol-shuffling -- matching shapes, not meanings -- so he never understands a word of it. Since the man is doing exactly what a computer running a natural-language program would do, Searle concludes that no matter how good a program is at producing the right outputs, running it can never by itself give a computer understanding, because syntax (form) isn't sufficient for semantics (meaning). This refutes "Strong AI," the view that a suitably programmed computer literally has a mind, and shows that computers can never really think.

(b) The Systems Reply grants that the man himself doesn't understand Chinese, but says Searle's looking at the wrong unit: the man is just one part of a bigger system that also includes the rulebook, the paper, the room, etc, and its the whole system, not the man, that understands Chinese -- similar to how no single neuron in your brain understands English but the whole brain does. Searle's response is that this doesn't actually solve anything, because the system still has no way to connect the Chinese symbols to anything in the real world -- its stuck manipulating shapes with no sensory access to whatever those symbols are supposed to refer to. Real understanding requires being causally hooked up to the world through perception and action, and a room full of paper rules has no such hookup, so bundling the parts together into a "system" doesn't give you semantics any more than the man alone had.

(c) I think Searle's response works. The Systems Reply basically just relocates the problem instead of solving it: you can ask the exact same question of the system that you asked of the man -- how does it ever get from shapes to meanings? -- and the answer is still that it can't, because its still just doing syntax. Since meaning seems to require some kind of real-world grounding that pure symbol-manipulation doesn't have, I think Searle is right that Strong AI is false and that no digital computer, however cleverly programmed, will ever actually think.
