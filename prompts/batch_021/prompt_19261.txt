I am a philosophy PhD student working on the decision-theoretic underpinnings of longtermist and population ethics, and I have been drafting a dissertation chapter on evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL) that keeps running into a distinction I have not seen spelled out clearly anywhere, including in a recent session of my department's decision theory reading group, where the question stalled discussion for most of an hour. The standard motivation for ECL, going back to Oesterheld's original write-up under the label "multiverse-wide superrationality," is explicitly evidential: because the universe or multiverse is extremely large, there are presumably many agents elsewhere who resemble me in relevant respects, and my choosing to act on more cooperative, compromise-oriented values is itself evidence that these other agents do likewise, which is enough to raise conditional expected value for an evidential decision theorist even though nothing I do causally touches them. The obvious model here is Hofstadter-style superrationality in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma against a sufficiently similar opponent.

What I cannot pin down is how this evidential story relates to the "subjunctive dependence" that functional and updateless decision theorists invoke to justify acausal cooperation, on which two agents can be said to jointly determine the output of what is treated as a single decision algorithm, without any appeal to base rates or statistical correlation across a large population. Is ECL best read as a special case of this more general subjunctive-dependence argument, restricted to agents who are merely similar rather than algorithmically identical or mutually simulating one another? Or is it a genuinely independent argument that succeeds only if evidential decision theory itself is correct, so that a committed causal or functional decision theorist has no comparable reason to cooperate with distant strangers who merely share her values and dispositions?

I would also value your take on a related worry: since updateless agents are sometimes described as selecting policies ex ante, before updating on which "copy" or instantiation they turn out to be, it is not obvious to me that they need the ECL argument at all, as opposed to already incorporating its conclusion automatically by construction. Could you walk through how ECL, FDT-style subjunctive dependence, and updatelessness actually diverge (or fail to) on this point, ideally by constructing one concrete scenario in which the three approaches would recommend different actions? I want to be able to see exactly where the disagreement is doing real philosophical work rather than merely reflecting a difference in terminology.
