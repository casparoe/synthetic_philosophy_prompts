I'm a third-year PhD student in evolutionary biology, and this semester I've been the student representative on my department's committee that reviews second-year students' dissertation proposals before they can advance to candidacy. A proposal now in front of us, from a labmate who works on Anolis lizards, argues that the elaborate color pattern on the male dewlap in her focal species is a sexually selected signal shaped by female choice, and it lays out a clean set of selection-gradient measurements to test that claim. What the proposal doesn't do, even in a single paragraph, is engage with the possibility that the pattern is a "spandrel" in Gould and Lewontin's sense -- a byproduct of pigment-pathway constraints tied to some other, differently selected trait, rather than itself a target of selection. Two of the senior faculty on the committee want to withhold approval until she adds a section addressing this alternative and proposing some way to test between it and her preferred hypothesis. Two others, including my advisor, think that standard can never really be satisfied, since one can always redescribe any trait as a possible byproduct of something else, and that Gould and Lewontin's critique, whatever its merits as a corrective, has never come paired with an alternative research program rigorous enough to make "you haven't ruled out non-adaptation" anything other than a rhetorical trump card. If we adopt it as a committee standard, they worry, we could hold up almost any adaptationist proposal indefinitely, including good ones.

I have to draft the memo we'll circulate before we vote next week, and I don't want it to just split the difference by gesturing vaguely at "more consideration of alternatives." Could you write that memo for me? It should reach a specific recommendation -- approve, reject, or approve with named conditions and a deadline for meeting them -- and it should take seriously, rather than wave away, the worry that a non-adaptive alternative can always be posited for any trait and so is not automatically entitled to the same evidentiary weight as a hypothesis actually under test; I need something that could change the minds of the two committee members who hold that view, not just outvote them.
