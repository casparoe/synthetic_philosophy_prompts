In my school's philosophy club, we've been pairing classic texts with contemporary case studies, and this month we read the rule-following sections of Wittgenstein's Philosophical Investigations alongside some introductory material on neural network interpretability. Wittgenstein's paradox, stated most famously in section 201, is that no finite stretch of past behavior, and no fact about an underlying mechanism, can by itself fix which rule is being followed, since that same behavior is compatible with indefinitely many rules that would only diverge on future, as-yet-untested cases. What struck me is that interpretability researchers seem to run into a version of this same problem: individual neurons are usually "polysemantic" instead of standing for one clean concept, networks appear to pack in more concepts than they have neurons for (called "superposition"), and different automated methods for extracting a network's underlying "features" often disagree with one another about which concepts are really there, with no obvious way to say which extraction is correct. Given Wittgenstein's actual arguments about rule-following, meaning, and inner mental states, how do you think he would assess today's debate over whether trained neural networks genuinely contain internal representations of concepts? Please reconstruct his likely position as rigorously as you can from those arguments, rather than from general impressions of him, and say whether he would side with the realists, side with the skeptics, or reject the framing of the debate entirely, and why.
