I spent about thirty-five years running a fly genetics lab before I retired, and now that I've got time on my hands I've finally been reading the philosophy I never got round to as a student. Lately its been causation -- I worked through Woodward's *Making Things Happen* over the summer and then fell down a rabbit hole into the Pearl/do-calculus stuff and the older Lewis counterfactual theory, partly because it slowly dawned on me that "knock out gene X and see if the phenotype still shows up" is basically the textbook example of what these philosophers call an "intervention," and I have opinions about whether that whole approach actually beats the counterfactual theory or is just a more careful notation for the same idea. Anyway, I sit in on a philosophy-of-science discussion group at the university near me now (nice of them to let a retired biologist audit), and next month's guest is a philosopher who has spent her career defending the interventionist theory of causation against the older counterfactual approach. Somehow I got handed the job of writing the interview questions, probably because I'm the only one in the group who's actually run an experiment.

Could you draft eight to ten questions for me that don't just let her restate the view? I'd like at least one question that presses on the circularity worry -- that "intervention" gets defined using causal vocabulary in the first place (a cause of X that doesn't affect anything else), so maybe the theory explains causation partly in terms of itself rather than really reducing it the way Lewis was trying to; one on cases where no intervention, not even an idealized one, seems available -- things like the causal role of someone's sex or species, or a one-off event like the origin of the universe -- and whether the view just has to call those causal claims meaningless or confused; and one that asks whether interventionism is even a genuine rival to the counterfactual theory at all, given that Pearl's own formalism defines the relevant counterfactuals by doing "surgery" on structural equations -- so maybe it's the same theory in fancier clothes. For each of these, add a follow-up question keyed to whatever deflection she's most likely to reach for first. If a question on preemption or overdetermination cases fits naturally, throw it in as an eleventh.
