"Kavka's puzzle asks us to imagine an eccentric billionaire who will pay you a million dollars tonight if, by midnight, you've formed a genuine intention to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow at noon -- nothing lethal, just a very bad day. Crucially, he doesn't need you to actually drink it to get paid; he has some reliable way of telling whether you really intend to, and pays out the moment he's convinced. The puzzle is that once midnight has passed and the money is already yours, you have zero reason to go through with it, and if you know that now, it looks like you can't really form the intention in the first place -- you can wish you were the kind of person who'd drink it, but a sincere intention has to be backed by an actual reason to act, and you know in advance you won't have one. So the perfectly rational thing to want (the free million) turns out to be something only a less-than-fully-rational person could actually pull off, since only they could form the intention convincingly.

I think this is basically the same structure as Parfit's hitchhiker, where you promise to pay the driver once you're safely in town even though you'll have no reason to pay once you're already there -- in both cases you're being asked to keep a promise after the benefit of making it has already been secured, and the real question is whether a rational agent can bind themselves that way. Kavka's answer, echoing later work on resolute choice, is that a genuinely rational agent can adopt policies rather than just maximizing act by act, which is what lets them form intentions like this and actually stick to them."
