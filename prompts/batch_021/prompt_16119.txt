Below is a short essay on Thomson's lamp and the exchange between Thomson and Benacerraf about what it shows. I'd like comments on it before I develop it further.

James Thomson's lamp is supposed to show that completing an infinite sequence of discrete tasks in a finite time — a "supertask" — is not merely physically but conceptually impossible. Imagine a lamp, initially off, wired to a button that toggles it. Press the button at t = 0 (the lamp goes on), again at t = 1 minute (off), again at t = 1.5 minutes (on), again at t = 1.75 minutes (off), and so on, halving each successive interval. Since 1 + 1/2 + 1/4 + ... = 2, infinitely many presses will have occurred by t = 2 minutes. Thomson's question: at t = 2, is the lamp on or off?

His argument is that it cannot be on, since every press that turned it on was followed, before the two minutes were up, by a later press that turned it off; symmetrically, it cannot be off. Since the lamp must be in one state or the other, and neither can be secured by appeal to the history of presses, Thomson concludes that the history itself — the supertask — cannot have been completed. Nothing could count as having pressed the button infinitely many times in this way, because there is no coherent answer to what state doing so would leave the lamp in.

Paul Benacerraf's 1962 reply is that this trades on an unearned assumption: that the lamp's state at t = 2 must be entailed by its states at all earlier times. Nothing forces that assumption. The setup specifies the lamp's state at each member of a sequence of instants converging to, but never reaching, 2; that sequence of states (on, off, on, off, ...) simply has no limit, so no particular value at t = 2 is forced on us by what came before. That there is no answer to "on or off?" that follows from the stipulated history is not a contradiction — it is a further fact about the lamp that the description never settled, and that we are free to stipulate however we like. On Benacerraf's diagnosis, Thomson refutes nothing about supertasks; he only shows that his own description of this one was incomplete.

I think Benacerraf is right that there is no formal contradiction here, but I want to suggest that his reply settles less than it is usually credited with. Granting that the lamp's terminal state is a further stipulation, rather than a logical consequence of the sequence, does nothing to show that any physical switch could execute that sequence in the first place. An operation performed at t = 1, again at t = 1.5, again at t = 1.75, and so on, with the gap between successive operations shrinking without bound as t approaches 2, seems to require a triggering mechanism capable of operating at unboundedly high frequency in that interval. If nothing answering to our concept of a physical switch could do that, then the question Benacerraf's diagnosis leaves untouched — and the one I suspect is doing most of the intuitive work in the original puzzle — is not "what, logically, is the state at t = 2?" but "could anything satisfying the physical description ever get that far to begin with?"

I would appreciate comments on three things: whether the reconstructions of Thomson's argument and of Benacerraf's reply are faithful to the originals; whether the closing argument is valid, or whether "unboundedly high frequency" is doing some illicit work that a defender of Benacerraf could reasonably reject; and whether this way of framing what is really at stake in the lamp case is worth pursuing, or is simply a restatement of a point made more carefully elsewhere in the supertask literature.
