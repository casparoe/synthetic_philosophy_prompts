This semester I am taking a philosophy course on rational choice and strategic interaction, and a situation from my own life has been bothering me because it seems to have exactly the structure of the abstract cases we discuss in class, where what is rational for you to do depends on correctly anticipating, several steps in advance, how someone else will respond.

My roommate and I have an informal arrangement: whenever one of us is short on rent at the end of the month, the other covers the difference, and the following month the person who was helped pays back a little more than they received, rather than the exact amount, partly as thanks and partly to build up a cushion for next time. In March, for example, I gave her two hundred dollars; in April, needing help herself, she gave me back two hundred and thirty; and so on, with the amount creeping up a little each time and the roles alternating depending on who is short that month, for most of the past year. The complication is that we both know exactly when this must end: her visa expires in six months, after which she is moving back home for good. When I described the arrangement to a friend from my seminar, she argued that it is secretly irrational. Her reasoning went like this: in the sixth month, whoever is due to be helped has no reason to pay anything back afterward, since there is no later month in which reciprocating could benefit them, so whoever would be due to help in the sixth month should simply refuse, anticipating this. But then the fifth month becomes, in effect, the last month that matters, so exactly the same reasoning applies to it, and so on, back through every month, all the way to the one we are in right now. On her view, two people who were fully rational, self-interested, and aware of all this could never have started such an arrangement to begin with.

I find this conclusion very hard to accept, since the arrangement obviously benefits us both and neither of us shows the slightest inclination to back out early. Yet when I try to identify the exact point at which her chain of reasoning breaks down, I cannot quite locate it. Is there a genuine flaw in this argument, or does it simply rest on assumptions about rationality that do not hold for real people? I would appreciate help thinking through where, if anywhere, the reasoning goes wrong, and whether philosophers have already worked out a standard way of handling this sort of puzzle.
