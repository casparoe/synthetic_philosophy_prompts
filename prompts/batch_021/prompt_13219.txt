Condorcet's 1785 essay on the probability of majority decisions gave us both the Jury Theorem — majority voting among competent, independent voters converges reliably on the truth — and the cyclical-majority paradox that bears his name. Two centuries later, legal and social-choice theorists (Kornhauser and Sager in the 1980s, then List and Pettit) identified a structurally related but distinct problem: aggregating not preferences but judgments over logically connected propositions. In their standard example, a three-judge panel must rule on liability, which by law requires both a valid contract and a breach of it; a majority affirms each of the two premises taken separately, yet a majority also denies that the defendant is liable overall, so voting premise-by-premise and voting directly on the conclusion yield opposite verdicts even though every judge reasoned perfectly consistently. List and Pettit showed that this "discursive dilemma" is an instance of a general impossibility theorem: once the propositions at stake are sufficiently interconnected, no procedure for aggregating judgments can satisfy even a small set of seemingly minimal conditions of fairness and logical consistency all at once.

Given Condorcet's own rationalist project of using mathematics to certify which collective decision procedures deserve our trust, how do you think he would have reacted to this result? Would he have treated it as a cousin of his own paradox, fixable by the same kind of institutional engineering he favored for voting rules, would he have seen it as undermining the deeper ambition of grounding political authority in the epistemic reliability of majorities, or would his reaction have been something else entirely? Ground your answer in the specifics of his actual views on collective reasoning and institutional design, rather than generic Enlightenment optimism about reason.
