I'm reporting a piece on the "right not to know" in genetic testing — the well-documented fact that most people at risk for Huntington's, for instance, choose not to take the test even when it's free, accurate, and takes about an afternoon. While I was talking it through with a friend of mine who's an actual decision theorist (not just a rationalist-blog dabbler), he waved the whole phenomenon away by invoking Good's theorem on the value of information: a rational Bayesian who's about to make a decision should never turn down a costless chance to learn more first, since doing so can only weakly help, never hurt, in expectation. In his view the decliners aren't making some defensible values-based call, they're just being irrational, and the theorem isn't ambiguous about it.

That answer bugged me, but "ignorance is bliss" obviously wasn't gonna cut it as a comeback against someone quoting an actual theorem at me, so I want to come back better armed. Can you build the most rigorous case you can for the position that Good's theorem does NOT actually convict the decliners of irrationality — that you can accept the theorem completely and still think declining is the rational choice for plenty of these people? Don't give me the hand-wavy version, get into the actual guts of the proof: what its assuming about the decision problem and the action set staying fixed before and after you look, what "costless" is quietly doing there, and whether the classic "you can always just ignore the new evidence and act as you would have anyway" defense of the theorem survives once you allow that simply knowing something (dread, an altered sense of your own future, whatever) can itself be part of what a person values, apart from any action it changes. This is for a sidebar aimed at readers who already know some decision theory, so no need to dumb it down for my sake.
