Random one, but it's been nagging at me all week, and I think I want to steal it for the book I'm drafting, so I want to get the philosophy of it straight first. My nephew Sam got a week of lunch detentions for talking back to his teacher — totally deserved, nobody disputes that part. What's weird is that his friend Theo, who had absolutely nothing to do with it, went up to her afterward and asked if he could just serve the detentions himself instead, so Sam could go to some club thing he didn't want to miss. She said sure, why not, probably because it saved her the hassle of chasing Sam down all week, we figure. So Theo sat alone in that classroom at lunch for five days straight, and Sam never apologized, never missed a single lunch period, nothing went on his record, its like the whole incident just evaporated for him.

My sister (Sam's mom) thinks this is basically fine, even a little noble of Theo, and her argument is that nobody forced him into it and the detentions technically still happened, so the school's actual rule got satisfied either way, end of story. I can't shake the sense that something's genuinely wrong here though, and not in a vague "that's not fair" way — if the person who did the thing never experiences one bit of consequence for it, in what sense did anyone actually get punished for talking back at all? I'm working a version of this into a subplot for the novel (higher stakes than a school detention, but same basic shape), and I want the character who objects to it to have a real argument, not just an aggrieved shrug. So: is there an actual case for why this kind of stand-in punishment doesn't hold up, philosophically speaking, or is my discomfort just squeamishness about something that's genuinely fine once you look at it straight on?
