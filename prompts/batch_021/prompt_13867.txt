Last weekend, over dinner with a friend from my old graduate cohort, I found myself in a two-hour argument about trolley problems and the doctrine of double effect. She thinks the whole apparatus — intending an effect rather than merely foreseeing it, treating a death as a "side effect" rather than a "means" — is basically a way of redescribing an act until our intuitions come out right, and she pressed the point with Philippa Foot's old contrast between craniotomy and hysterectomy: if crushing the skull of a child stuck in the birth canal is forbidden because the death would be the means to saving the mother, why is removing a cancerous, pregnant uterus permitted, when the fetus dies there too and everyone knew it would? I didn't have a fully satisfying answer, and now I need one for real, because a version of exactly this problem has landed on my hospital's ethics committee.

I serve on the clinical ethics committee at a Catholic-sponsored hospital that has adopted the USCCB's Ethical and Religious Directives. Our standing protocol for tubal ectopic pregnancy permits salpingectomy — removing the affected fallopian tube — on the reasoning that the embryo's death is a foreseen side effect of excising diseased, potentially rupturing tissue, not a means to any good end. The OB-GYN department now wants standing approval to offer methotrexate as a first-line, non-surgical alternative in stable, unruptured cases, since it spares patients surgery and preserves fertility better; several committee members insist this would be a direct abortion, since methotrexate works simply by halting the growth of the trophoblast, with no separate "diseased organ" to point to; others respond that this is a distinction without a difference, since we could redescribe methotrexate the same way we redescribe salpingectomy if we wanted the doctrine to come out the other way. Could you draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before our meeting next Tuesday? It should recommend one concrete path forward — approving the protocol change, rejecting it, or approving it with specific conditions — and it should actually work through the means/side-effect reasoning on its own terms, rather than just citing the directive, taking seriously whatever tells against the option you end up recommending.
