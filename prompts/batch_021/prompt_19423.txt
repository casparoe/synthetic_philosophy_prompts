I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, but I spend a fair amount of time in AI-safety and effective-altruism circles online, where the idea of "strong longtermism" comes up constantly. As philosophers like Will MacAskill and Hilary Greaves have argued, because the number of people who could exist in the far future vastly outnumbers everyone alive today, the expected value of almost any action we take is dominated by its effects on that future, which is why proponents treat existential-risk reduction as close to the overriding moral priority of our era. For entirely unrelated reasons, I've also been reading Edmund Burke's Reflections on the Revolution in France, where he describes the state as a partnership stretching across the dead, the living, and the unborn, and reserves real contempt for revolutionaries willing to discard inherited custom and sentiment in favor of abstract calculation -- his complaint that the age of chivalry had given way to an age of sophisters, economists, and calculators reads like an attack on precisely the sort of expected-value reasoning that generates longtermist conclusions in the first place. So I'm curious: presented with the actual case for strong longtermism, would Burke recognize a kindred spirit in its concern for generations not yet born, or would he file it alongside the calculating rationalism he became famous for warning against? Walk me through how his own commitments pull in both directions, and tell me which pull you think should win.
