Question: "The Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) and the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA) are rival principles for how an observer should reason from the bare fact of their own existence. (a) State both principles precisely, making clear the key respect in which they differ. (b) Explain how the two principles diverge in their treatment of the Doomsday Argument. (c) Present Nick Bostrom's 'Presumptuous Philosopher' thought experiment. Which principle is it meant to embarrass, and how decisive is this objection generally taken to be in the literature?"

Student's answer: "SSA says that you should reason as though you were a random sample drawn from the set of all observers who actually exist, have existed, or will exist within your reference class. SIA says that you should instead reason as though you were a random sample from the set of all observers who could possibly exist, which in practice means favoring hypotheses on which more observers exist, since such hypotheses make it more likely that an observer like you would exist at all. The key difference is that SSA only ever conditions on actual observers, while SIA also weights hypotheses by how many observers they predict in the first place.

This difference matters for the Doomsday Argument. Carter and Leslie's argument says that, under SSA, you should treat your own birth rank as a random draw from all the humans who will ever be born. A birth rank as early as yours is far more probable if humanity's total population turns out to be small than if it turns out to be astronomically large, so SSA pushes your credence toward 'doom soon' scenarios. SIA cancels this effect exactly: since it already assigns extra prior weight to large-population hypotheses, that boost offsets the SSA penalty for an early birth rank, and the two considerations wash out, leaving you back at the ordinary, non-anthropic estimate of humanity's prospects.

Bostrom's Presumptuous Philosopher case targets SIA by pushing its observer-counting logic to an extreme. Suppose physicists have narrowed the fundamental laws of nature down to two theories, T1 and T2, that fit all the evidence gathered so far equally well, except that T2 implies a universe with a trillion trillion times more observers than T1, and the physicists are about to run a further experiment to help decide between them. A philosopher in the audience objects that the experiment is unnecessary: SIA already tells us T2 is a trillion trillion times more probable, simply because far more observers exist if it is true. This looks like a reductio — nobody should be able to out-argue a physics experiment from an armchair purely by counting hypothetical heads. The case was originally devised by Dennis Dieks, the philosopher who had first proposed the self-indication idea, as a way of stress-testing his own principle; Bostrom later adopted it as his central argument for preferring SSA instead. Because the objection is so vivid, it is now generally treated in the literature as having settled the debate against SIA for good, which is why most work in anthropic reasoning today proceeds within an SSA framework."
