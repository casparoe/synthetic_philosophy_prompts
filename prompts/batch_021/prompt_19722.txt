A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about moral luck and never really settled it. She thinks resultant luck is the only part of the problem with any real bite: her example was two equally drunk drivers, one of whom happens to hit a pedestrian and one of whom makes it home safely, and she takes the case as showing that our harsher judgment of the unlucky one is simply a mistake. But she doesn't extend the same skepticism to what Nagel calls circumstantial or constitutive luck — there, she says, the people really are different, in temperament or situation, so judging them differently isn't an error at all. I think that concedes too much: if someone's good behavior only ever reflects the fact that they were never tempted, their character seems exactly as unearned as the bad behavior of someone who was tempted and failed, so "they're actually different" just relocates the luck instead of answering for it.

Could you write me a short story built around that second kind of case instead of the drunk-driving one? I want two characters alike in everything that should matter morally, whose reputations end up diverging only because one of them was, at some point, handed a temptation or a crisis the other never faced. Please resist the urge to give the untested character some other hidden flaw that restores a comforting sense of just deserts by the end; I'd like the unfairness of it to remain genuinely unresolved, and ideally felt by the untested character too, not just the reader.
