I'm currently pregnant and haven't had a drop of alcohol in months, but I still edit the tasting-notes column in my wine club's monthly newsletter. Each month a bottle gets nominated, half a dozen members taste it at our meeting and post their reactions in our group chat, and I distill the highlights into a short published note — something like 'a bright, mineral-driven Chablis with real length on the finish' — that goes out to the roughly fifty people on our mailing list, despite my not having tasted the wine myself. Two friends in the club recently found out that this is how the column actually gets made and had a surprisingly heated argument about it after last month's tasting. I wasn't really party to the dispute, just its subject, so I mostly listened and kept quiet.

(1) Callum's opening claim was that there's nothing dishonest going on: he takes his doctor's word on diagnoses he can't independently verify, and buys bottles all the time on the strength of a critic's score without tasting them first, so relaying the table's consensus about a wine should be no different in principle. (2) Naomi, who minored in philosophy and said this is basically a version of what's called the problem of 'aesthetic testimony,' disagreed: a tasting note reports a perceptual response someone actually had in the moment, she said, and there's no fact left over for testimony to transmit once you subtract everybody's actual palate. (3) Callum replied that this proves too much, since professional wine writers fold a room's consensus into one authoritative note all the time, and every subscriber to a fifty-person newsletter already understands that a monthly column is compiled rather than a solo first-hand report. (4) Naomi answered that writers who do this attribute it explicitly, whereas the column runs in a flat, unhedged first person; she added that whoever wrote 'real length on the finish' can't field a single follow-up question about why, and a claim that can't survive one follow-up looks more like a lucky guess wearing a verdict's clothes than like knowledge passed on by testimony. (5) Callum's last move was to grant that a full descriptive note might need an actual palate behind it, but to insist that a bare bottom-line recommendation like 'worth buying this month' doesn't, since that's structurally just an aggregated score, and nobody thinks a wine's average critic rating is dishonest merely because no single taster produced that number.

Neither of them budged, and since the column is something I actually produce every month, the argument has stuck with me more than I expected.
