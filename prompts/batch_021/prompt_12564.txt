So this happened in my department's evo-psych reading group and it's kind of taken over every lunch for the past month, alot more than I expected. I'm a PhD student in cognitive science (working on music cognition, of all things), and a few weeks back we read the old Gould & Lewontin "Spandrels of San Marco" piece alongside a chapter on the evolutionary origins of music. It set off an argument between two of the postdocs, Dana and Marcus, that neither of them can seem to close out.

Here's roughly how its gone. (1) Dana's position is that music is basically a spandrel: a byproduct of adaptations for language, auditory scene analysis, and motor sequencing, and that we're just pattern-detecting machines getting pleasurably hijacked by patterned sound -- Pinker's "auditory cheescake" line, essentially. (2) Marcus says that's too quick, because music actually has the marks people normally accept as evidence of adaptation: it's a human universal, it emerges on a reliable developmental timetable without formal teaching, it recruits dedicated reward circuitry, and it tracks things like perceived mate quality the way Geoffrey Miller's sexual-selection account would predict -- so waving it away as "just" a spandrel is special pleading. (3) Dana comes back that none of that actually distinguishes an adaptation from a mere exploitation of one: universality and easy development are exactly what you'd expect if music is free-riding on older systems, and "engages reward circuitry" just restates that patterned sound is pleasant, not that it was built by selection for that job -- per Gould & Vrba, the burden is on the adaptationist to show current utility was the actual target of past selection, and for a behavior like this there's no fossil record of Pleistocene mate choice to check that against. (4) Marcus fires back that this burden-of-proof standard is a trap: there's no literal fossil record for language or pair-bonding either, and Dana accepts those as adaptations without blinking, so if "no direct fossil evidence" sinks music-as-adaptation it should sink those too -- the actual test has to be convergent circumstantial evidence (universality, canalized development, neural specialization, fitness correlations), and by that standard music clears the bar. (5) Dana's response is that this is exactly Gould and Lewontin's original complaint: adaptationist stories are built so that almost any pattern of evidence can be narrated as confirming them after the fact, and "cultural universality" is just as compatible with a byproduct that every culture stumbles onto -- the way everyone independently discovers cooking or fire-making -- as with a genuine sexually-selected ornament.

Neither of them has budged and honestly I flip back and forth on it every time I replay the argument in my head. Could you go through this move by move, 1 through 5, and say who's got the stronger case at each step rather than just handing out an overall winner -- and then flag anything you think both of them are quietly getting wrong, like whether "spandrel," "exaptation," and "adaptation" are even doing clean enough conceptual work here to settle a case like this one way or the other?
