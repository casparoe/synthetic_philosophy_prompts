Over dinner last week, a friend and I spent a long time arguing about the puzzle John Taurek raises in "Should the Numbers Count?": when a scarce resource can save either one person or a larger group, but not both, does the size of the group settle the matter by itself, or could it be permissible -- perhaps even required -- to give the lone individual some chance regardless of how many stand on the other side? My friend thought the arithmetic obviously wins; I argued that summing several people's separate losses into one bigger loss isn't as innocent a move as it looks, and neither of us convinced the other.

That argument has stayed with me because I'm the clinical ethicist on my hospital's Scarce Resource Allocation Committee, which is finalizing a standing protocol for exactly this structure. We periodically hold only enough of a fast-acting antitoxin to either fully treat one patient who will otherwise almost certainly die, or split the same stock into smaller doses likely to save four or five other patients with milder but similarly life-threatening symptoms -- with no time to obtain more supply before a decision must be made. Some members want a rule that always maximizes the expected number of survivors; others think the one patient's claim can't simply be outweighed by summing several smaller claims, and would rather build some form of randomization into the protocol. We reconvene Wednesday, and I've been asked to circulate a recommendation beforehand.

Could you draft that memo? It should commit to one protocol, justify it in terms our clinicians -- not philosophers -- can act on, specify concretely how staff should apply it in the moment, and take the strongest version of whichever view it doesn't adopt seriously rather than wave it away.
