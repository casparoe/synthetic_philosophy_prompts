In my school's economics club, our advisor ran a live "dollar auction" as a demonstration of escalation of commitment: whoever bids highest wins a one-dollar bill, but the second-highest bidder also has to pay their final bid and gets nothing. Bidding blew past a dollar and didn't stop until it hit eleven, and by then two members, Ana and Jonah, had gotten into an argument about it that's still going.

Ana says that once you're the second-highest bidder above the value of the prize, continuing to bid isn't irrational at all: you're going to lose your last bid anyway if you drop out, so raising by one more increment for a shot at winning some of your money back is rational damage control, not escalation of commitment.

Jonah says that's exactly the reasoning that turns two rational people into the auction's victims: if both bidders apply that logic every round, the bidding is guaranteed to blow past any sensible number, so whatever you call each individual step, the overall pattern is the textbook case of escalation of commitment, not an exception to it.

Ana replies that Jonah is confusing the individual decision with the aggregate outcome: a move can be the rational best response at every single step even though it predictably leads somewhere bad overall, and "escalation of commitment" is supposed to name a bias in individual reasoning, not just a bad group result.

Jonah answers that if "rational" gets stretched to cover any best response no matter how foreseeably ruinous, the word stops doing real work, and that the actual irrational moment came earlier, when each of them chose to enter an auction whose structure made the eventual runner-up's trap obvious from the start.

They've asked me to settle it, but I'd rather hear an outside view first. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each point, not just who wins overall? Please also flag anything you think both of them are missing about how the dollar auction is usually analyzed in game theory, and close with a one-line verdict on who had the stronger overall position.
