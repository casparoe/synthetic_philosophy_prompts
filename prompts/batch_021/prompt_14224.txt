At work I've been trying to design a single scoring function that aggregates satisfaction ratings from many different users into one ranking objective, and a colleague pointed me to Harsanyi's aggregation theorem as justification for making it a weighted sum of everyone's individual utility functions. As I understand the result, it proves that this additive, linear form is the only aggregate objective consistent with each user and the aggregator itself acting like a rational expected-utility maximizer, together with a minimal unanimity condition — which seems to mean that any rule that deliberately gives extra weight to worse-off or lower-scoring users, the way prioritarian ethical theories recommend, isn't just a different value judgment but is actually mathematically inconsistent. If that's correct, I don't understand why philosophers still treat the dispute between utilitarian and prioritarian approaches to aggregation as an open normative question rather than as something Harsanyi's theorem already closed on purely logical grounds. Is there a gap in this reasoning?
