At a risk meeting last month, our head trader refused to price a structured note on a newly launched index whose volatility we simply can't pin down with any confidence, even after the desk's quants showed her that our best-guess volatility estimate for the new index matched, almost to the decimal, our estimate for a long-established index she trades without hesitation. Pressed on the inconsistency, she just shrugged and said "ambiguity aversion," which sent me down a rabbit hole from the Knightian risk/uncertainty distinction to Ellsberg's three-color urn — the original thirty-red, sixty-black-or-yellow single-urn version, not the simplified two-urn story most popular accounts lead with. What I found there is that the preference pattern in Ellsberg's experiment can't be rationalized by any single subjective probability distribution, since it violates Savage's sure-thing principle — but I can follow that formal result without yet having an intuitive, gut-level sense of why the violation is supposed to be so damning, rather than a harmless quirk of preference. Could you explain, intuitively, what the sure-thing principle is actually doing within Savage's framework, and why Ellsberg's choices defeat it? I'd then like you to assess whether the leading formal responses to ambiguity aversion — Choquet expected utility, maxmin (multiple-priors) expected utility, and the smooth ambiguity model — genuinely show that an instinct like my trader's is rational, or whether they merely redescribe the same bias in more sophisticated mathematical terms.
