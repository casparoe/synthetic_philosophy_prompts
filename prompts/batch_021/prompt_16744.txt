A few years back I turned down an eighteen-month research posting in Antarctica. What actually stopped me wasn't the cold or the isolation, but a strange thought I couldn't argue my way out of: I had no way of knowing, from where I stood, what that life would actually feel like from the inside, and I suspected that whoever came home afterward would want different things out of life than I did on the day I said no. That made it oddly hard to even ask myself whether turning it down was the right call. I've since read that philosophers have a name for this kind of choice, a transformative experience, and that it splits into an epistemic half (you can't learn what an experience is like except by having it) and a personal half (living through it can remake your values so completely that your earlier self isn't in a position to judge your later self's verdict). Together these are supposed to break the usual approach of weighing a big decision by its expected payoff, since you can't put a value on an outcome you have no way of conceiving from where you currently stand. Write me a short story built around a character facing a decision with exactly that structure, but please avoid the two examples I keep running into everywhere — becoming a parent and the philosopher's go-to case of turning into a vampire — and let the impossibility of the choice come through in how the character reasons and acts rather than being explained to the reader.
