I spent the better part of forty years as a field ecologist, mostly on invasive species and trophic cascades, and if there's one thing that got hammered into me over and over it's that even a well-meaning intervention in a complex system can snowball into consequences nobody saw coming, sometimes decades later (the cane toad business in Australia, or how bringing mongooses into Hawaii to deal with rats mostly just wiped out the ground-nesting birds instead). Since I retired I've been sitting in on a little philosophy reading group at the university, and a while back we did a session on something called "consequentialist cluelessness" that struck a real nerve with me. Apparently Hilary Greaves distinguishes between "simple" cluelessness, where the unforeseeable knock-on effects of an act more or less cancel out and shouldn't worry us, and "complex" cluelessness, where we have some lopsided reasons to think the unforeseen effects skew good or bad but no principled way to weigh them against the stuff we can actually foresee. And I think it's Lenman, though don't quote me on wich paper, who pushes the further point that this isn't just a practical limitation -- literally any act, even something as trivial as stopping to chat with a neighbour, alters the timing of events enough that within a few generations it will have changed who exists, so we can't even get the sign of the long-run consequences right, let alone there magnitude, even in principle.

I sit on the small grants panel of a local land trust, and every round we're choosing between things like funding a species reintroduction, buying up a parcel of habitat outright, or just redirecting the money to some well-evidenced global health charity of the GiveWell variety. Could you lay out the most important implications of this cluelessness debate for how a panel like ours should actually reason about those choices? I don't need a tour of the literature -- I'm more interested in whether taking it seriously should change what we favour in practice (does it quietly push us toward "legible," short-feedback-loop projects and away from anything with long tendrils, and is that a bias we should resist or lean into?), whether it means downgrading how much weight we put on consequentialist reasoning versus other considerations when we make the call, and whether any of the proposed fixes floating around out there (bracketing certain effects, focusing on robustness, that sort of thing) would actually give us anything beyond the ordinary humility about unintended consequences that any ecologist already has drilled into them.
