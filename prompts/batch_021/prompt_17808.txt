This term I am teaching a graduate seminar on the ethics of influence. Our session on manipulation versus legitimate persuasion, in which we contrasted accounts of manipulation as "bypassing someone's rational agency" with weaker, intention-based accounts, has produced a running argument between two of my strongest students that has now outlasted the class by a week, conducted mostly by email with me cc'd as an increasingly baffled referee. The case that set them off was a hypothetical I posed in section: a national health service is considering switching organ-donor registration from an opt-in system to an opt-out one, on the grounds that most people say, when asked directly, that they support donation, yet comparatively few ever get around to registering under the opt-in default. Nadia maintains that the switch would be a clear case of manipulation; Ben maintains it would be legitimate, even rather unremarkable, institutional design. Both have held their ground across several rounds, and I no longer trust my own judgment about who, if anyone, is winning.

Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it. (1) Nadia opened by arguing that the switch is manipulative because its entire predicted effect comes from exploiting status-quo bias -- people simply staying with whatever is pre-selected -- rather than from giving anyone a new reason to donate; a policy whose success depends on people not stopping to think is manipulating them, not persuading them. (2) Ben replied that this cannot be the right test, since the existing opt-in default relies on exactly the same inertia to keep registration low, and nobody derived that default from careful deliberation either. If merely depending on status-quo bias is enough to make a default manipulative, then every default is manipulative, including the one Nadia isn't complaining about, so inertia alone cannot be doing the moral work she needs it to do. (3) Nadia conceded that no default is free of inertia, but relocated her objection: what makes the proposed default manipulative is that deregistering would be a discrete, faintly stigmatized act -- actively removing yourself from a registry of people who help save lives -- whereas failing to register under the old system carried no comparable social cost. Choosing the default that is harder to walk back, not merely having some default or other, is the manipulative move. (4) Ben granted that asymmetric reversal costs are morally relevant, but argued this only condemns badly implemented opt-out systems, not the switch itself: make deregistering a single anonymous click, exactly as easy as registering used to be, and Nadia's objection disappears -- yet her complaint was supposed to indict the switch independently of implementation, so she owes either a further reason or a narrower claim. (5) Nadia answered that even a frictionless opt-out still works only because its designers can count on most people never revisiting the choice at all, and that a policy engineered around people's predictable failure to reconsider is manipulative even when reconsidering would cost nothing -- the way an auto-renewing free trial is manipulative even when cancellation is one click, because it is designed around people forgetting, not around any cost of cancelling. (6) Ben's closing reply was that relying on predictable non-reconsideration is only objectionable if reconsidering would actually change what most people want; since large majorities already say they support donation, the policy is not exploiting any gap between behavior and preference, only economizing on people's limited attention for a choice most of them have, in effect, already made -- which struck him as closer to a form that pre-fills your correct address than to anything we would ordinarily call manipulation.
