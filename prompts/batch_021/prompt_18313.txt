This is what I've been calling in lecture the "profligacy problem" for counterfactual accounts of omissive causation — basically everyone who didn't intervene turns into a cause. (a) Explain the problem clearly, in your own words. (b) Present and evaluate ONE strategy for blocking the result that the Queen caused Rosa's death while still allowing that the gardener did. Here's the answer, transcribed exactly as submitted:

"Both the gardener's not-watering and the Queen's not-watering pass the simple counterfactual test: had either of them watered Rosa, Rosa would have lived. So a purely counterfactual theory of causation seems committed to saying both of them caused Rosa's death, which is absurd — we don't think random people who didn't intervene are causes of every bad thing that happens. This is the profligacy problem.

The most influential response, defended by David Lewis, restricts which omissions count as causes by appeal to a normative baseline: an omission is a cause only when the agent who omitted was under some kind of duty or role-based expectation to perform the relevant action. The gardener has a job to water Rosa, so her not-watering deviates from what was supposed to happen; the Queen has no such role, so her not-watering isn't a deviation from anything and doesn't count as a cause. On this view, whether c is a genuine cause of e, when c is an omission, is not settled by counterfactual dependence alone — there also has to be a fact about what the relevant norm or default was.

This is a nice fix because it matches our intuitions about the two cases exactly, and explains why we can say "the gardener caused Rosa's death" while denying "the Queen caused Rosa's death" even though the same counterfactual holds for both. And since the normative solution shows that the Queen's failure to water isn't a cause of Rosa's death at all, it follows that the Queen bears no moral responsibility whatsoever for what happens to Rosa, whereas the gardener, having genuinely caused the death, is blameworthy for it.

The cost of the view is that it makes causation itself partly normative, at least for omissions — whether c causes e can turn on facts about duties and roles that have nothing to do with physics. McGrath has pointed out that this is uncomfortable for anyone who wants causation to be a purely natural, mind-independent relation, and argues we're stuck choosing between accepting that normative element or biting the bullet on profligacy."
