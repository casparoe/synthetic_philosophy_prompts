A friend of mine, Mark, quit a secure government job last year to paint full-time, with no savings cushion and no gallery representation yet, calling it "betting on myself." At dinner recently, two other friends, Elena and Diego, ended up arguing about whether this counts as a costly signal in the sense biologists use for a peacock's tail or a gazelle stotting in front of a predator. Here's the exchange as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Elena said it obviously qualifies: taking on real financial risk would be irrational for someone without genuine talent, so being willing to do it is honest evidence of skill, just as only a fit peacock can afford to grow and carry an enormous tail.

(2) Diego objected that a genuine handicap signal needs a receiver whose behavior the display is meant to change -- peahens choosing mates, predators picking easier targets -- and it isn't clear who is supposed to be reading Mark's sacrifice.

(3) Elena said the receivers are simply whichever galleries or buyers eventually decide whether to back him; they update on the sacrifice the way peahens update on the tail.

(4) Diego granted that, but said the theory's real requirement isn't merely that the signal be costly -- the cost has to be lower for the genuinely talented, or an untalented painter could send the identical signal at the identical price, since Mark's lost salary doesn't depend on whether he can actually paint.

(5) Elena replied that the cost is still lower for a talented painter in expectation, since he's more likely to succeed and recoup it, so the required asymmetry survives, just further out rather than upfront. Diego answered that this quietly assumes what it needs to prove: if we already have to believe someone is probably talented before we can call his expected cost low, the "signal" isn't doing independent work -- it's a symptom of a belief we'd need other grounds for anyway, which makes it a correlate of confidence rather than a genuine handicap. And confident but untalented people exist, and would produce exactly the same display.

Neither of them budged. Could you go through moves (1) through (5) and say who has the better of it at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also like to know whether Diego's distinction between a genuine handicap and a mere correlate of belief is doing real work here, or is the technicality Elena takes it to be, and what, if anything, this exchange is missing about how costly signaling is actually supposed to work.
