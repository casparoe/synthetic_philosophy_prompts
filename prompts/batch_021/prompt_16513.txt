I'm a software engineer, and I got pulled into reading about pragmatism a few months ago after a colleague described our team's engineering culture as basically "applied pragmatism" -- if a design keeps working reliably under real load, that's what makes it good, full stop. That comment sent me down a rabbit hole that started with William James's 1907 Pragmatism lectures and led back to Charles Sanders Peirce's earlier work, since James was always careful to credit Peirce as the originator of the whole approach -- there's a well-known 1898 address James gave at Berkeley, "Philosophical Conceptions and Practical Results," in which he traces the notion directly back to Peirce's 1878 paper "How to Make Our Ideas Clear." What surprised me is how sour this apparently turned: by 1905 Peirce was renaming his own position "pragmaticism," a term he reportedly chose partly because it was too ugly for anyone to want to steal it, specifically to distance himself from what he felt James and others had turned his idea into. From the summaries I've found, I honestly can't tell whether this was a genuine disagreement about what makes a belief true, or whether Peirce, a logician trying to state a narrow rule for when a concept is clear, and James, a psychologist turning that rule into a full theory of truth, were mostly just talking about two different things while remaining personal allies.

Could you write a dialogue between Peirce and James -- imagine the two of them sitting down together not long after James's book appeared -- in which each tries to show the other exactly where his account of truth breaks down? I'd like Peirce to insist on the difference between the pragmatic maxim as a method for clarifying what a concept means and a full definition of truth, and to defend truth as the opinion the community of inquirers would converge on in the long run, regardless of what any individual currently finds satisfying. Have James push back that a "final opinion" nobody will ever actually reach can't do the work Peirce wants it to do, and have him defend his own picture of truth as something that happens to an idea through its verification in lived experience, while resisting the charge that this just reduces truth to whatever a person prefers to believe. They were close enough personally, and sharp enough philosophically, that I don't want either one flattened into a strawman here -- I'd like them genuinely responding to each other's best points, move by move, and ending with some explicit verdict, even a qualified one, on whether they are actually disagreeing about the nature of truth or mainly emphasizing different halves of a single view.
