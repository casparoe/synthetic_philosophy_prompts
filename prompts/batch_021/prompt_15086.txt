So this happened at a dinner thing a few of us do every month, and it's been rattling around in my head ever since. My friend Reema and her boyfriend Chris have this arrangement where she went full vegetarian, even though she's told me straight up she doesn't think factory farming is that big a moral deal, and in exchange Chris stopped buying from a couple of fast fashion brands he used to love, even though he's said the sweatshop labor side of it doesn't really bother him either. Basically, Chris cares a lot about animal welfare and Reema doesn't, and Reema cares a lot about garment worker conditions and Chris doesn't, so they each agreed to do the other's "big thing" instead of their own. They explained this to the table like it was some genius life hack, and two of my other friends who had nothing to do with the arrangement, Lily and Dev, ended up in this back and forth about whether it even makes sense, and neither one backed down.

Dev opened with: the whole setup is kind of fake, because if Reema genuinely doesn't think factory farmed meat is a problem, her going vegetarian isn't fixing anything from where she stands, she's just doing Chris a favor and dressing it up as ethics. Lily said who cares what's going on in Reema's head, less factory farmed meat is getting eaten and less money is going to sweatshops, which is what the two of them each say they want more of in the world, so by both of their own standards things just got better, full stop. Dev came back with, ok, but there's something weird about a "moral" setup that only holds together as long as both people keep seperately caring about their own side of it -- the second Chris stops caring about factory farms, theres nothing left inside Reema pulling her toward the tofu, so really this is a contract, not a moral commitment, which makes it bribery with better PR. Lily's answer was that basically all our moral commitments are conditional on stuff too (she brought up only visiting a difficult uncle as long as he behaves himself), so being conditional can't disqualify it on its own, and that bribery specifically means paying someone to do something wrong, whereas this is paying someone to do something that, by their own admission, is fine or even good, just not their priority. Dev wasn't done though -- he said fine, not bribery exactly, but it still bugs him that neither of them has actually changed their mind about anything, they've just bought themselves some local peace and quiet instead of working out who's actually right, and that seems like a bad substitute for real moral progress. Lily's last word was that maybe that's backwards: given that neither of them is all that sure they've got ethics figured out anyway, doing a bit of what the other person is convinced really matters, in exchange for them doing the same, might actually be a more honest response to that uncertainty than each one sitting around waiting to be argued into agreement.
