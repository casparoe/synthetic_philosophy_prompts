For my final project in AP Seminar, I've been researching the Doomsday Argument, a topic I originally fell into after a friend from our school's Effective Altruism club sent me down a rabbit hole of old forum threads on anthropic reasoning, some of which trace back to a philosopher named Nick Bostrom. I think I have a decent handle on the basic argument itself: if you treat yourself as a random sample drawn from all the humans who will ever live, your own birth rank gives you a reason to update toward a smaller total population, meaning "doom" arrives sooner than you'd otherwise think. What's been tripping me up is the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA), which almost everything I've read describes as the accepted fix for this.

From what I can tell, SIA says that when you're comparing hypotheses about how many observers exist in total, you should weight each hypothesis by the number of observers it predicts, and once that's built into the math, the Doomsday shift cancels out exactly, leaving you with the same probabilities you would have assigned before ever thinking about your birth rank. If that's right, I don't understand why anthropic reasoning still gets treated as such a contested, unresolved topic -- it sounds like SIA is a correction that any careful Bayesian should just get for free. The one objection I keep running into is something called the "presumptuous philosopher," but every version of it I've found relies on bizarre setups involving infinitely large or infinitely many universes, which seems like a pretty marginal edge case that wouldn't come up in any realistic piece of scientific reasoning. So is it fair to say that, among people who have actually worked through the math, SIA has basically settled the Doomsday Argument, and the presumptuous philosopher is mostly a curiosity for people who like fussing over infinities?
