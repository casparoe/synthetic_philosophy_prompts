(b) Cases of late preemption (e.g., two rocks thrown at a bottle, where rock A shatters it a split second before rock B would have arrived) are harder for the stepwise account. Explain why, and describe the fix that's proposed to handle it. (5 pts)

And here's the answer I need graded:

"(a) For early preemption (assassin A shoots and kills the victim while backup assassin B stands by ready to shoot but never gets the chance), Lewis's fix is to stop asking whether the effect depends on the cause in one step, and instead look for a chain of events c, d1, d2, ..., e, each depending on the one before it. The victim's death doesn't counterfactually depend on A's firing taken by itself, since B would've fired if A hadn't — but there IS a chain of intermediate events (bullet leaves gun, travels, hits victim) that does depend, step by step, on A's firing, and no such chain runs through B, whose process got cut off before producing anything. Causation is the transitive closure ('ancestral') of this stepwise dependence, so the analysis correctly picks out A and not B.

(b) Late preemption is harder because B's process (the second rock) is still in flight when the bottle shatters, so it's not obviously true that no chain of dependence runs through B either — there's no clean intermediate event where B's chain fails the way there is in early preemption. This is actually the case that pushed Woodward to move from plain counterfactual dependence to 'causation as influence': instead of just asking whether e depends on c, you ask whether there's a whole pattern of dependence between whether/when/how c occurs and whether/when/how e occurs. Even though the bare fact of shattering doesn't depend on rock B, the manner and timing of the shattering would've been different if B's trajectory had varied, and that's enough of a dependence pattern to make A the cause and B causally irrelevant.

(c) Trumping preemption is supposed to survive even the influence fix. In Schaffer's case, a major and a sergeant simultaneously order the troops to advance; by convention the troops obey the higher-ranking officer whenever orders conflict, so intuitively it's the major's order that causes the advance, not the sergeant's. But neither order passes even the fine-grained dependence test: the advance doesn't depend on the major's order, since the sergeant's order alone would've produced the exact same advance in the exact same manner and at the exact same time (the orders are qualitatively identical), and by symmetry it doesn't depend on the sergeant's order either. So the counterfactual/influence test is symmetric between major and sergeant, but our causal judgment isn't — the asymmetry is coming from the chain of command, which counterfactual dependence has no way to see.

(d) I think Woodward's account actually does better here, because interventionism lets you build the chain of command directly into the causal model instead of reading causation off brute similarity between worlds. Concretely: intervene on the major's order (do(Major = advance) vs. do(Major = silent)) while holding the sergeant's order fixed at 'advance', and the outcome changes, because once the chain of command is represented as a variable in the graph, setting the major's variable is what fixes the 'order obeyed' node that's upstream of the troops' behavior. Since a Woodward-style intervention is a purely formal, idealized operation on the graph rather than a metaphysical claim about which world is most similar, it can encode exactly the normative/conventional fact (rank trumps) that the case turns on — which is why interventionism succeeds where Lewis's theory doesn't."
