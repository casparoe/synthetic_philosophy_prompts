In my Philosophy of Religion seminar this semester, right after we'd covered Gödel's modal ontological argument, two classmates of mine got into an argument that carried on well past the end of class, and I still can't tell who came out ahead.

(1) Mara argued that the proof collapses under its own weight: once you accept Gödel's axioms, you can derive that every actually true proposition is necessarily true, so there is no genuine contingency left in the world. Since we plainly do live in a contingent world, she said, something in the axioms has to go, and that alone is reason enough to set the argument aside.

(2) Devon replied that this "modal collapse" result is an unwelcome consequence, not a formal contradiction, and unwelcome consequences are costs, not refutations. He pointed out that necessitarianism isn't unheard of in the rationalist tradition -- Spinoza more or less embraced it, and there's a live scholarly dispute over whether Leibniz's system secretly does too -- so biting that particular bullet isn't as disqualifying as Mara made it sound.

(3) Mara answered that the comparison cuts the wrong way. A committed necessitarian who already denies libertarian free will has nothing extra to lose from collapse, but most people drawn to a proof of God's existence are traditional theists who also want God's creation of the world to be a free act that could have been otherwise. For them, she said, collapse isn't a strange but tolerable side effect -- it quietly commits them to exactly the necessitated universe their own theology was supposed to rule out.

(4) Devon granted that this lands for anyone hoping the proof would underwrite that fairly orthodox picture, but said it only shows the argument doesn't hand theists everything they might want, not that the inference to a necessarily existing being is worthless. He added that there are revised versions of Gödel's proof, built specifically to block collapse while still reaching a necessarily existing God-like being, and if any of those work, the lesson is that Gödel's own definitions of necessary existence and essence were careless, not that the ontological argument is beyond repair.

Neither of them budged, and I don't think either was right about everything. Could you go through moves (1) through (4) and say who has the better of it at each step, note anything important that both of them are missing, and finish with your overall verdict on whether modal collapse is fatal to Gödel's argument or just a repairable defect in his particular formulation?
