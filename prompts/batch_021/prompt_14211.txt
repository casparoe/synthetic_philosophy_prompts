Spent about 35 years as a field entomologist before I retired, and now I've been getting into philosophy in my spare time, mostly philosophy of science and biology. Something that always bugged me on the job: ask five different taxonomists whether two beetle populations count as "really" seperate species and you'll get five different answers, depending on whether they go by morphology, interbreeding, or genetic distance. I just read a piece calling this "the species problem," and the gist of it was that since biologists cant even agree on one definition, species aren't an objective feature of nature at all — just a bookkeeping convention we impose on a continuous evolutionary process, no more real than a category like "weed" or "shrub." Honestly that tracks with what I saw in the field for decades, so I'd assumed this is basically settled and that's the mainstream view among philosophers of biology now. Is that a fair summary of where the field has landed, or am I missing something?
