ok this is a side project thing, i got nerd-sniped. i'm a phil PhD student doing formal/social epistemology (aumann's agreeing-to-disagree stuff, common knowledge, all that) and fell down a rabbit hole into the finance version of the same result: the milgrom-stokey no-trade theorem. rough idea - if traders are bayesian rational and share a common prior, the mere fact that someone's willing to trade with you on their private info should already move your beliefs enough that the trade stops looking good, so purely speculative trade "shouldn't" happen. obviously real markets trade a ridiculous amount, so people have proposed a handfull of standard escapes:

1. heterogenous priors (traders just don't share the same prior to begin with, so they can "agree to disagree")
2. liquidity/hedging trades - reasons to trade that have nothing to do with info (rebalancing, life events, etc)
3. risk-sharing from differences in risk aversion or endowments
4. overconfidence - traders overrate the precision of their own signal
5. bounded rationality - traders don't run the full "ok but if they're willing to trade, what does that tell me" regress
