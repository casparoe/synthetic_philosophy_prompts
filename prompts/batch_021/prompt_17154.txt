QUESTION (15 pts): Using the classic pair of low-stakes/high-stakes bank cases from DeRose (Hannah's evidence about the bank's Saturday hours is held fixed across both cases), answer the following: (a) [8 pts] How does an epistemic contextualist explain why "I know the bank's open tomorrow" seems true of Hannah in the low-stakes case, and "I don't know the bank's open tomorrow" seems true of her in the high-stakes case? (b) [4 pts] How does a proponent of pragmatic encroachment / subject-sensitive invariantism explain the very same shift differently — be explicit about what each view holds fixed and what it lets vary. (c) [3 pts] In 2-3 sentences, say whether you think these are ultimately two descriptions of the same phenomenon or genuinely rival theories, and why.

STUDENT ANSWER:

(a) The contextualist says "knows" is context-sensitive, similar to a word like "flat": it picks out different epistemic standards depending on the conversation it's used in, even though Hannah's own evidence about the bank's hours never changes between the two cases. In the low-stakes case no error-possibility has been made salient, so the modest evidence she has (she was there on a Saturday a few weeks ago) meets the low standard in play, and "I know" comes out true. Once the stakes go up, the possibility that the bank changed its hours becomes salient, the standard for "knows" rises, and that same evidence no longer meets it, so "I don't know" comes out true instead. This is a claim about the semantics of "knows" as used by speakers, not a claim about any change in Hannah's underlying epistemic position, which stays fixed across the two cases.

(b) DeRose's pragmatic encroachment view rejects the idea that the word "knows" itself is shifting meaning. Instead "knows" picks out one fixed relation in every context, but whether Hannah stands in that relation to the proposition depends partly on her own practical situation, not just her evidence — since the cost of being wrong is so much higher in case two, more evidence is required for her to actually count as knowing, even though "know" hasn't changed meaning at all. So where the contextualist lets the truth conditions of "knows" shift with context while holding Hannah's epistemic position fixed, this view holds the meaning of "knows" fixed and instead lets whether Hannah satisfies it shift along with her stakes.

(c) Since the two views end up agreeing about which of Hannah's utterances are true in each case, I'd say they're best read as two descriptions of the same underlying phenomenon in different vocabularies rather than genuine rivals — whichever framework you pick, you get identical verdicts about when knowledge-talk is appropriate, so the disagreement is mostly terminological.
