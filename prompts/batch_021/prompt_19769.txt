Since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have been auditing a philosophy of mathematics seminar at a nearby university, mainly to get a proper grip on what Gödel's theorems do and do not establish. The instructor, who knows I have the technical background for it, occasionally has me look over a problem set as an additional check before finalizing grades. I would like an independent read on the item below before I send her my own comments.

The question set to students was: "Gödel's second incompleteness theorem is often glossed as showing that 'no sufficiently strong, consistent, recursively axiomatized theory can prove its own consistency.' (a) State the theorem more precisely, including what the sentence Con(T) is standardly taken to express, and briefly indicate how the diagonal lemma enters into its proof. (b) Evaluate this claim: 'Given Gödel's theorem, we can never have any rigorous mathematical grounds for trusting that Peano Arithmetic is consistent — at best, its consistency is an article of faith.' Your evaluation should engage with at least one actual attempt to establish Con(PA)." (10 points)

Here is the answer one student submitted:

"Gödel's second incompleteness theorem says that if a theory T is consistent, strong enough to represent basic facts about the natural numbers, and its axioms can be listed by an algorithm, then T cannot prove the sentence Con(T) that formally expresses T's own consistency. The proof uses the diagonal lemma to build, inside T, a sentence that in effect says of itself 'I am not provable in T'; formalizing the reasoning behind the first incompleteness theorem then shows that if T could prove Con(T), it could also prove this sentence, which contradicts the first theorem.

The claim that PA's consistency has to be taken purely on faith is too strong, though. In 1936 Gödel proved that Con(PA) does follow after all, provided one accepts one further principle that goes beyond PA: transfinite induction up to the ordinal ε₀. Since that extra principle can be justified by entirely concrete, checkable reasoning about the notation for ordinals below ε₀, the resulting proof is finitary in exactly Hilbert's original sense, even though it rests on a principle PA itself cannot prove. So for elementary arithmetic, at least, Hilbert's program succeeds after all — it is only for stronger theories, such as full set theory, that the second theorem forces us to give up on a finitary consistency proof."
