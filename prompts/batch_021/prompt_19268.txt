A friend and I spent most of dinner last week arguing about a claim G. E. Moore makes in Principia Ethica: that personal affection and the appreciation of what is beautiful, combined into a single state, are the most valuable things we know — and that the appreciation only counts for much if it responds to qualities the object actually has, rather than qualities we merely credit it with. She thought this makes the theory sound less like love and more like an audit; I argued it's the one part of Moore still worth defending, since it's exactly what separates real regard from sentimentality. I don't think she ever quite conceded, though I'd like to believe I won.

I now have to make good on that argument. I'm toasting my friends Priya and Jonah at their wedding this weekend, in front of roughly eighty guests — mostly family and coworkers who have never read Moore and don't need to, plus three or four friends from my old philosophy program who will know exactly what I'm doing, and will notice if I do it badly. Priya spent a year personally inspecting every joint of the first bridge she designed before she would let her own parents drive across it; Jonah still writes notes in the margins of books he lends out, on the assumption he'll get them back. Please write the toast, about three minutes long, that lets Moore's actual requirements — correct appreciation of real qualities, and his claim that such states are organic unities rather than sums of separable goods — genuinely shape what gets said about this particular couple, rather than serving as decoration, and that gives some honest answer, even an implicit one, to the question his own isolation test raises: if a state like this doesn't strictly require witnesses to have its value, what is a room full of them actually for?
