I'm a junior at my high school and one of the student representatives on our Honor Council, the body that hears academic integrity cases and recommends sanctions to the dean. Our bylaws set up two tiers: a first or minor offense, like an uncited paraphrase, gets a warning and a chance to redo the work, while anything serious or repeated gets referred to the dean with a recommendation of suspension or expulsion. Both tiers currently use the same evidentiary standard: a majority of the nine of us (six students, three faculty) must find it "more likely than not," based on whatever testimony is presented, that the student actually did it. There's rarely a confession or camera footage involved, so most cases really come down to a teacher's account weighed against the student's.

Two weeks ago we heard a case that split the council: a senior accused of fabricating data in a lab report, an offense serious enough under our bylaws to send it straight to the expulsion track, based on nothing more than a single teacher's testimony. I'd just covered pragmatic encroachment in my philosophy elective—we've been doing a unit on contemporary epistemology—and it seemed to fit exactly: the same uncorroborated testimony that would easily support a warning in a minor case felt obviously too thin to justify something this serious, which suggests that what it takes for us to know, or be justified in believing, that a student is responsible should rise with what's riding on the finding, even though the evidence itself hasn't changed at all. A friend on the council, who leans toward epistemic contextualism instead, thinks this gets the phenomenon right but frames it wrong: on her view, what shifts isn't how much justification it takes to believe something as the stakes rise, but what a phrase like "sufficient evidence" in our bylaws even means as used in a low-stakes versus a high-stakes hearing—and she thinks that distinction actually matters for how the bylaws should be worded. A third member, though, wants none of it: he thinks whether the student did it is a plain fact that shouldn't shift around depending on the punishment, and that if one teacher's word isn't good enough to justify expulsion, it shouldn't count as sufficient for a warning either—keep one standard for the finding itself, and let the stakes affect only the sanction that follows from it. I'm also genuinely unsure whether it matters that, unlike the classic cases I've read about where the person doing the believing also bears the stakes, here it's the council doing the believing while the student bears essentially all of it.

I have to circulate a short discussion memo before next Wednesday's meeting, and I'd like your help drafting it. Could you lay out these three positions accurately, recommend one concrete evidentiary policy—or a defensible combination of them—that the council could actually vote on, and take the "keep one standard, let stakes affect only the sanction" objection seriously rather than dismissing it, since at least two members are sure to raise it and I'd like the memo to already have an answer ready for them? A page is about right.
