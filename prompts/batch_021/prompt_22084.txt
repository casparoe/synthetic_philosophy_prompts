While building a toxicity classifier for user-submitted text, I ran into a pattern that the standard tricks from sentiment analysis don't handle well. Sentiment models typically track negation scope, so that "not good" scores as negative even though "good" alone scores as positive, but this doesn't work for slurs: "She is not a [slur]" still reads as offensive, and the same charge survives when the slur is embedded in a question ("Did he call her a [slur]?"), a conditional ("If Sam is a [slur], I don't want to know him"), or someone else's reported speech ("Bob said Sam is a [slur]"). An ordinary descriptive or evaluative claim loses its force once you negate or embed it, but a slur's derogatory charge apparently does not, and I'd like to understand the linguistic and philosophical debate behind this fact. Please write an essay on the controversy over whether a slur's derogatory force is (a) part of the sentence's literal, asserted content, (b) a distinct but still linguistically encoded layer of meaning that, unlike ordinary content, is not cancelled by embedding, or (c) not really encoded meaning at all, but rather an effect of a social taboo on uttering the word itself. Weigh the strongest considerations for each position — giving real weight to the embedding pattern described above — and close with a clearly defended verdict rather than a non-committal summary.
