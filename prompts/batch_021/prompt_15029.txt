Ok, this has been bugging me since my social philosophy seminar last week, and I want a sanity check before I build a whole talk section around it. We're working through Lewis's Convention, and one of my students made a move I can't stop turning over: Lewis's whole definition turns on the idea that a convention is a regularity solving a recurring coordination problem where there's always at least one other regularity that would've worked just as well -- that's what makes it arbitrary rather than, say, a dictate of practical reason. Doesnt that mean conventions should actually be pretty easy to overturn once people clock that there's nothing sacred about them? If gender norms, or which side of the road we all drive on, are "mere conventions" in this strict Lewisian sense, it seems like the reformer's whole job should just be consciousness-raising -- point out that the alternative regularity works equally well, and the old norm ought to unravel fast, since deep down everyone already knows it's replaceable.

Im giving a talk on norm change to a mixed philosophy/policy crowd next month and wanted to build a section around exactly this idea, something like "arbitrariness as leverage for reformers." Rather than just hand-wave though, I want to ground it properly in Lewis's actual apparatus -- common knowledge, coordination equilibria, salience, precedent, all of it. So: using those tools correctly, what's the most effective strategy for getting a population to defect from an old convention onto a better one as fast as possible, and how exactly should reformers be deploying the fact that the old norm is "merely" arbitrary?
