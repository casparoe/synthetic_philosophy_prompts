Two people in my decision theory reading group got into a sustained disagreement over the "Death in Damascus" case, and I'd like a considered outside opinion on how the exchange actually went, point by point. The setup: Death tells you she intends to meet you tomorrow; you may stay in Damascus or flee to Aleppo. Death predicts what you'll actually do with great reliability, and by the time you choose, she has already gone to whichever city she expects you'll be in.

(1) Nadia said the rational choice is obviously to flee: Death's location tomorrow is already a fixed fact that your decision can't reach back and change, so you should treat it as independent of your choice and act on ordinary self-preservation rather than sit still.

(2) Colin objected that this doesn't actually favor fleeing over staying: if Death's location really is fixed independently of your choice, the two cities are symmetric, so at most Nadia's argument shows the choice is arbitrary, not that fleeing is smarter. Worse, he said, since Death predicts you specifically, whichever option you currently favor is exactly the one you have most reason to distrust, since that's the one she'll have anticipated.

(3) Nadia granted the symmetry point but said Colin's own logic proves too much: if favoring "flee" makes flee suspect, and the same reasoning makes "stay" suspect the moment you favor it instead, then no option can ever be recommended -- and a decision theory with nothing to say in a case like this has simply failed at its one job.

(4) Colin replied that this isn't a failure but the correct moral of the case: no choice made on purpose is stable here, and what the case actually shows is that you should decide by some method Death can't get ahead of, such as a coin flip, rather than resolve in advance to do one particular thing.
