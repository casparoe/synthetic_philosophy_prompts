I teach a seminar that touches on personal identity and philosophy of physics, and quantum suicide/immortality comes up pretty much every year -- usually in the flattened "if many-worlds is true I can never actually die" form students pick up from a physics youtube video or some philosophy-adjacent podcast. I want to push past that version this time. Lev Vaidman is pretty insistent that surviving a quantum suicide experiment (the Tegmark version, gun rigged to a quantum coin flip) can shift the survivor's own credence toward the Everett interpretation but could never be turned into evidence that would convince anyone watching from the outside, no matter how many trials get run. Can you explain the actual mechanics of why that asymmetry is supposed to hold -- whats different about the observer's epistemic position versus the subject's own?

And then push on it a bit further for me: im not even sure the subject is entitled to that update either. In an ordinary Bayesian update you need some track record of hits and misses to compare against, but here the only "track record" that ever gets registered is the surviving branch's -- there's no continuous observer accumulating both the "still here" and "I died" data points, since the latter, by construction, never sticks around to update anybody. That smells a lot like the observation-selection-effect worries that show up in Bostrom-style anthropic reasoning, the whole SSA vs SIA mess. Does that framework actually give us a principled way to decide whether the survivor's confidence bump is rational, or does the analogy break down somewhere? I'd love an explanation that gets into the actual mechanics here rather than one that just restates the cocktail-party version of the thought experiment.
