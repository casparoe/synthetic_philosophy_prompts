I've spent years reading rationalist blogs and forums, and I'm also part of a small in-person discussion group that grew out of that world -- the kind of group where most members have worked through the old Overcoming Bias and LessWrong archives and have strong opinions about decision theory. A few weeks ago we'd been discussing David Deutsch's "The Fabric of Reality," and the after-meeting conversation turned into a real argument between two members, Nadia and Grant, over whether the Many-Worlds Interpretation is the kind of thing that could count as good science by Popperian lights, or whether it's closer to unfalsifiable metaphysics dressed up in equations. I didn't take a side at the time, and I still can't work out who had the better of it at each step, so I'm hoping you can adjudicate.

Here's roughly how the exchange went:

1. Nadia opened by arguing that MWI simply isn't falsifiable in Popper's sense: no observation any experimenter could ever make would come out differently depending on whether MWI or a collapse theory is true, since the two are constructed to be empirically equivalent from inside any single branch. A theory that forbids no possible observation, she said, is exactly what the demarcation criterion is supposed to rule out.

2. Grant replied that Nadia was applying the criterion to the wrong unit. MWI isn't an extra posit bolted onto quantum mechanics -- it's just what falls out of taking the bare Schrödinger equation at face value and adding nothing to it. Collapse theories are the ones that add something further: a mechanism, invoked solely to make the math match our single-branch experience, that isn't derived from or even consistent with the rest of the theory. Inventing machinery for the sole purpose of protecting a theory from an awkward implication, he said, is exactly what Popper called an illegitimate "conventionalist twist" -- so if anything, MWI is the leaner, more falsifiable theory, not the less.

3. Nadia answered that this blurs an important distinction: some collapse theories, like GRW-style objective collapse models, aren't ad hoc rescues at all. They specify a definite new stochastic dynamics, with a fixed spontaneous localization rate, that in principle yields predictions diverging from standard unitary quantum mechanics -- for instance, in sufficiently sensitive large-mass interferometry. So at least one rival to MWI is more falsifiable than MWI by the plainest Popperian standard of forbidding specific outcomes, which shows that "fewer postulates" and "more falsifiable" can come apart. Grant's inference from one to the other, she said, doesn't go through.

4. Grant granted the point about GRW but denied it rescues Nadia's original argument. GRW-type theories, he said, are best understood as substantive rival physical theories in their own right, not as vindicating the textbook collapse postulate -- which posits no dynamics at all, generates no new predictions, and so can't be doing the falsifiability work Nadia originally needed it to do. MWI's being unfalsifiable in isolation doesn't make ordinary textbook collapse comparatively more scientific; if anything, he said, textbook collapse comes off worse than both of its rivals.

Neither of them felt the other had landed a knockout blow, and the conversation trailed off into logistics about who was driving whom home. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right about what at each step -- not just who wins overall?
