As a science journalist, I've long been fascinated by Molyneux's problem: the question, first put to John Locke in the 1680s, of whether a person blind from birth who has learned to distinguish a cube from a sphere by touch would recognize which was which by sight alone, the moment they gained the ability to see. Locke, Leibniz, and Berkeley each reasoned their way to a confident verdict without ever encountering a real case. Modern sight-restoration research, including work with congenitally blind patients treated through programs like Project Prakash, complicates all of those armchair verdicts: newly sighted people tested on exactly this task perform no better than chance immediately after surgery, and only become reliable after days of actual seeing. I'd like you to write a short story of a few hundred to around a thousand words that dramatizes this problem through a single newly sighted character's first encounter with a cube and a sphere, staying faithful to that messier real finding rather than to any philosopher's tidy prediction. Let the philosophical stakes emerge from what the character notices, misjudges, and slowly works out, not from anyone explaining the problem aloud.
