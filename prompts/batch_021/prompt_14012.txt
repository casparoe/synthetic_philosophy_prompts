Okay so this is going to sound like a weird rabbit hole, but here goes. With everything going on in the news lately about strongman leaders and how far people can or should go to resist them, I got into a whole tangent argument with my uncle over dinner about whether it's ever justified to just kill a tyrant instead of waiting on "proper channels," and somebody at the table went "didn't Aquinas write about this?" which sent me down a two-week reading spiral. Turns out he did, and it's way less straightforward than the "great Catholic philosopher says tyrannicide is fine actually" meme going around online — he treats a usurper who grabbed power illegitimately very differently from a legit ruler who just governs badly, and in his book on kingship he actually spends a bunch of time warning that private citizens taking it on themselves to assassinate tyrants is likely to backfire and produce worse tyrants, arguing instead that it's the community or its proper authority that should depose him, or failing that you're basically supposed to endure it and pray.

Anyway, this became relevant because I co-run a tiny history-of-ideas podcast with a friend (like, forty listeners, mostly our moms), and we somehow managed to book a political theory professor who's finishing a book arguing that this "cautious Aquinas" reading is basically wrong — that if you read his commentary on the Sentences together with the Summa's stuff on sedition, he's implicitly licensing a lot more than he ever says outright, including ordinary people acting on their own against an illegitimate ruler, and that the "wait for public authority" line in his book on kingship is more of a prudential aside than his real, considered position. Shes also fond of pointing out that "usurper" was doing a lot of work for him and can be stretched pretty far if you want it to.

I want to interview her in a way that doesnt just let her restate her book's thesis. Can you draft me 8-10 questions, ideally with a quick follow-up planned for each depending on how she's likely to answer? I definitely want at least one question that puts real weight on the usurper/legitimate-ruler distinction and asks how much can ride on it without collapsing into "it's a tyrant if I say it's a tyrant," one on the sedition argument (if a tyranny is already itself the "real" disorder, doesn't that same move let basically anyone justify removing any ruler they dislike), and one that uses the Jean Petit affair and its condemnation at Constance as a kind of stress test on her reading. Bonus points if you can work in something comparing her to John of Salisbury's much more full-throated defense of tyrannicide, since I get the sense she wants credit for being more careful than he was, and I'm not totally convinced that holds up.
