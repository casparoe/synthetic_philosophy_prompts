For the past few months I've been part of a small team building an internal tool that layers confidence scores on top of an LLM-based code-review assistant at work: instead of just flagging "these two SQL queries might not be equivalent," it now says something like "82% confident these two queries are equivalent" or "65% confident this recursive function terminates on all inputs." During a design review, two of my colleagues, Dan and Priyanka, fell into a surprisingly deep argument about what these numbers could even mean, and I haven't been able to settle it in my own head since.

Dan opened by objecting that putting anything other than 0% or 100% on a claim like "these two queries are equivalent" is a kind of category error: the claim is a determinate mathematical fact, true or false independently of what we happen to have checked, so a score of 82% isn't measuring some real uncertainty in the world -- it's just measuring our ignorance, and dressing that ignorance up in the language of probability is misleading, since genuine Bayesian probability requires giving logical truths probability 1 and giving identical probabilities to any two statements that are logically equivalent, which our tool plainly doesn't do. Priyanka answered that taking this objection seriously would make probabilistic language useless for almost everything worth reasoning about: working mathematicians say a conjecture is "probably true" long before it's proven, and any reasoner with finite time on its hands, human or machine, has to represent its partial progress through a proof or a search somehow, so refusing anything but 0 and 1 for mathematical claims just throws away a signal that is clearly tracking something real about how justified the tool's output is. Dan came back by pointing out that this doesn't actually rescue the numbers from incoherence: rephrase "these queries are equivalent" into a logically equivalent but syntactically different claim, and the tool assigns it a different score, which is exactly the kind of inconsistency you'd expect once you allow probabilities over statements without respecting logical equivalence between them -- it's not a minor wrinkle, it's the whole problem. Priyanka's closing move was that Dan had essentially rediscovered, on his own, something that already has a name in the AI-safety literature -- she vaguely recalled it being called the problem of "logical uncertainty" or "logical omniscience" -- and that the existence of a known open problem is not a reason to throw out confidence scores, since there are proposed frameworks (she mentioned something called "logical induction" from a paper she'd once skimmed) that assign evolving, imperfect credences to exactly these kinds of claims by deliberately giving up full logical coherence rather than pretending to have it, and that their tool's scores were best read as a rough, informal gesture in that direction rather than as classical Bayesian probabilities.

Neither of them conceded anything, and the meeting moved on before anyone could respond to Priyanka's last point. Could you go through the exchange move by move -- Dan's opening objection, Priyanka's reply, Dan's rebuttal about logical equivalence, and Priyanka's closing move -- and say who has the better of the argument at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also be genuinely curious whether there's a cleaner resolution to the underlying tension that neither of them quite landed on.
