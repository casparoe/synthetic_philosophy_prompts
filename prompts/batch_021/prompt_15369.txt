I'm putting together a lecture for a graduate seminar on classical Indian epistemology, and I keep struggling to present one piece of Nyāya theory of inference (anumāna) in a way that registers as more than a historical curiosity. I've been re-reading Matilal and Ganeri on Nyāya theories of knowledge over the summer, but neither quite lines up with the two questions I want to put to my students, so I'd like your help thinking them through first.

The background, briefly: Naiyāyikas hold that a valid inference requires grasping a genuine vyāpti (invariable concomitance) between the hetu (reason) and the sādhya (property to be proved), but they are well aware that many apparent pervasions are only conditioned by some further factor, an upādhi -- as in the stock case where "fire" looks pervaded by "smoke" only because of the mediating presence of wet fuel, so that a red-hot iron ball, which has fire without smoke, refutes the unrestricted claim. Ruling out upādhis and thereby securing a pervasion fit to ground inference is done partly through repeated observation of agreement and difference, and partly through tarka, a form of suppositional or counterfactual reasoning that derives an absurdity from denying the pervasion.

What I want from you is an explanation of how this apparatus actually works, with real technical precision, and then to put that explanation to work on two questions I haven't seen addressed carefully anywhere. First: is tarka doing independent epistemic work in licensing the move from observed to unobserved cases, or is it parasitic on an antecedent grasp of the very pervasion it is supposed to secure -- once the vocabulary is stripped away, is this offering anything beyond what Hume's problem of induction already shows is missing? Second: suppose someone mistakes drifting fog for smoke on a distant hillside and infers fire, and it turns out there really is a fire on that hillside, hidden behind a ridge and entirely unconnected to the fog -- something structurally close to a Gettier case. Do the resources already built into Nyāya epistemology, in particular the classification of hetvābhāsas (especially asiddha, the fallacy of an unestablished reason) and the requirement that one's grasp of vyāpti itself be avyabhicārin, non-deviating, give the Naiyāyika principled grounds for denying that this counts as pramā? Or does handling it require some further move that the standard apparatus does not obviously supply?
