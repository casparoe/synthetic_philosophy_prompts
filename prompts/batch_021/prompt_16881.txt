The question was: "Aquinas is often invoked in later debates about the right to resist or kill a tyrant, yet his own considered position is more cautious than this reputation suggests. (a) Explain how Aquinas defines tyranny and the distinction he draws between a ruler who is a tyrant 'by defect of title' and one who is a tyrant 'in the exercise' of otherwise legitimate authority. (b) What course of action does Aquinas recommend against a ruler who is tyrannical in the exercise of legitimate power, both where a higher human authority capable of deposing him exists and where none does? (c) Briefly situate Aquinas's position in relation to at least one other medieval or early-modern discussion of the legitimacy of tyrannicide, indicating whether it converges with or differs from Aquinas's view, and how."

Here is the student's answer, reproduced in full: "Aquinas treats tyranny as a corruption of kingship: whereas a true king rules for the common good of his subjects, a tyrant governs for his own private advantage, and it is this perversion of the end of rule, rather than any particular degree of cruelty, that makes tyranny the worst form of government for Aquinas. Within this general category he distinguishes two ways a ruler can be a tyrant. The first is tyranny by defect of title, where someone has seized power without any right to it, whether by force or fraud, so that he is not a legitimate ruler to begin with. The second is tyranny in the exercise of power, where someone who was validly established in authority nonetheless governs oppressively, for his own benefit rather than the community's. Aquinas treats these two cases differently: because a usurper never held legitimate title, Aquinas allows that the community, or even a private person, does no wrong in removing him, since no genuine authority is being violated. A ruler who is tyrannical only in the exercise of otherwise legitimate power is a harder case, and here Aquinas is considerably more cautious. In De Regimine Principum, the treatise he wrote for the king of Cyprus, his preferred remedy is that the community should appeal to whatever public authority originally had the power to install the ruler -- an assembly of electors, say, or a higher lord -- and have that authority depose him; he explicitly warns against leaving this judgment to private individuals, since once citizens are permitted to kill rulers they consider tyrannical, ambitious men will use the charge of tyranny as a pretext to murder good kings along with bad ones, so that the cure becomes worse than the disease. Where no higher human authority exists for the community to appeal to, Aquinas's fallback is essentially religious rather than political: the community should bear the tyranny patiently, pray for deliverance, and trust divine providence to remove the tyrant in God's own time, on the model of the early Church's endurance of persecuting emperors rather than armed rebellion against them. Taken together, then, Aquinas's mature position amounts to a general license for the community to remove any tyrant, usurper or otherwise, so long as it acts collectively through proper channels rather than leaving the matter to a single private individual -- which is really just a more careful restatement, a century later, of the position John of Salisbury had already reached in the Policraticus, where Salisbury similarly concludes that tyrannicide is licit only once it proceeds through settled communal channels rather than private initiative. So although Aquinas is sometimes remembered simply as licensing the killing of tyrants, his actual view is a fairly conservative synthesis of a consensus that was already largely in place before he wrote."
