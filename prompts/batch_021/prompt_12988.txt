I've been working through Quine's essay "On the Nature of Moral Values" (reprinted in Theories and Things), where he treats ethical predicates with noticeable suspicion. He is willing to grant mathematical posits a place in his ontology on the grounds that our best scientific theories cannot be formulated without them, yet he does not think moral predicates earn their keep in the same way, and he seems doubtful that moral discourse enjoys the kind of intersubjective checkpoints that let disputes in the sciences actually get settled. Suppose Quine had lived long enough to encounter Sharon Street's 2006 "Darwinian Dilemma" for moral realists, which argues that our evaluative dispositions were shaped by selective pressures bearing no obvious relation to any independent moral facts -- leaving realists stuck choosing between positing an implausible tracking coincidence or conceding that those dispositions are unreliable. Given his naturalized epistemology, his holism, and his selective, indispensability-based approach to ontological commitment, how do you think Quine would actually have responded to Street? Would he simply have signed on to her antirealist conclusion, or does his broader framework point toward some more distinctive move -- an error theory, a companions-in-guilt strategy, or treating moral discourse as not really fact-stating in the first place? I am less interested in your bottom-line verdict than in the reasoning you think he would actually have given to reach it.
