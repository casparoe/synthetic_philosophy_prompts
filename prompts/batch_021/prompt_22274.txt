A few of us in my school's philosophy club run a small podcast, and for the next episode we're trying a format we've only used once before: instead of a living guest, we script an "imagined interview" with a historical figure, staying as close as we can to what that person actually argued. I first came across I. J. Good while reading about the Bletchley Park codebreakers for a history assignment, and was surprised to find that the same man who worked alongside Turing later became a statistician and proved a small but well-known result in Bayesian decision theory -- what's usually called Good's theorem, from his 1967 paper "On the Principle of Total Evidence" -- namely, that a rational, expected-utility-maximizing agent should never expect free evidence gathered before a decision to leave them worse off than deciding without it. A couple of us in the club got into an argument over whether the result is really as strong as people treat it, so for this episode I don't want questions that just have him restate the theorem in his own words -- I want questions that go after the places in the argument that seem least stable once you look closely.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions for this segment? I'd like at least one question pressing on the fact that the proof quietly treats gathering and processing evidence as free, tied to a follow-up asking whether that sits uneasily with the "Type II rationality" idea Good himself introduced a few years later to account for the cost of thinking -- does conceding that computation has a price also concede that the 1967 theorem has much less to tell a real, resource-limited agent than people assume? I'd also like a question on the gap between a guarantee that holds only in expectation, averaged over evidence not yet seen, and the fact that any particular piece of news can still leave one specific decision-maker worse off than ignorance would have, with a follow-up on whether that gap is why the theorem gets invoked more confidently than its actual statement supports whenever someone argues that a person should always want to know more. And I'd like one question that puts to him the fact that Frank Ramsey apparently jotted down much the same result in a note that went unpublished during his lifetime, and that Savage proved a version of it independently more than a decade before Good's own paper appeared -- asking how he would respond to the suggestion that the theorem carrying his name isn't really his. For the remaining questions, please draw on whatever else you find genuinely interesting about the theorem, its assumptions, or its later role in debates about people who seem to deliberately avoid information, and give every question a short follow-up keyed to the answer Good seems most likely to give.
