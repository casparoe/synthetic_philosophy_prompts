Since retiring from a career in experimental condensed-matter physics — superconductivity, mostly — I have taken up a fairly new enthusiasm: a philosophy discussion group that meets monthly through the local university's extension program. Last month's session was nominally about personal identity, but it drifted into the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics and, in particular, the old "quantum suicide" thought experiment, in which a device triggered by a genuinely random quantum event has, say, even odds of killing you outright each time it is fired. Two of the regulars, a mathematician named Daniel and a philosophy postdoc named Sofia, ended up in a four-move exchange about it that the moderator never quite resolved, and it has stayed with me since.

Daniel opened by arguing that fearing the device is simply confused if many-worlds is true: on every trial the wavefunction branches into a surviving copy and a non-surviving one, and since no continuous stream of subjective experience runs through the non-surviving branch, you should expect, from the first-person point of view, to go on finding yourself alive no matter how many times the trigger is pulled — "quantum immortality," he said, is just this observation taken seriously. Sofia replied that Daniel was helping himself to an illegitimate step: it is true, but trivial, that any survivor will find themselves surviving, and that holds in an ordinary single-world probabilistic universe too; what should actually govern your willingness to play is the decision-theoretic weight assigned to branches beforehand, and the standard Everettian derivation of the Born rule — the sort of argument Deutsch and Wallace give — requires a rational agent to weight branches by amplitude-squared measure rather than by branch count, so a rational Everettian ought to be exactly as reluctant to pull the trigger as an ordinary single-world Bayesian who puts the odds of dying at fifty-fifty. Daniel accepted that point but pressed a further claim: whatever the sensible betting odds beforehand, isn't it still a fact that, after any finite number of trials, a branch of strictly positive measure exists in which "you" have survived — so isn't some thread of you, in the fullest sense many-worlds allows, genuinely undying, quite apart from what it is prudent to wager on? Sofia's response was that this last point, though true, is idle: exactly the same reasoning guarantees a surviving branch for literally any wildly improbable escape, suicide device or not, so it cannot be what people actually have in mind when they invoke quantum immortality for comfort, and Daniel's retreat to it quietly gives up the claim that was supposed to be interesting in the first place.

Could you go through their four moves in turn and say, for each one, who has the better of it and why, rather than simply handing down an overall winner? I would also welcome your view on whether Daniel and Sofia are, between them, overlooking some consideration that ought to bear on the dispute. Please close with a single sentence stating, on balance, which of the two ends up closer to right.
