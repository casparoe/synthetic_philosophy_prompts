Ok, I need an outside read on something that's been rattling around in my head all week, and it's genuinely not my corner of philosophy — I've spent fifteen years teaching aesthetics, not formal decision theory — so before I bring it up at another faculty meeting and get the same blank stares I got last time, I wanted to try it on you first.

My brother-in-law's eleven-year-old twins have this ritual with their allowance. Dad drops a dollar in an envelope to start. The twins alternate turns; on your turn you can either cash the envelope — you get seventy-five cents of whatever's in there, your brother gets the rest, done for the week — or you push, which doubles the pot and hands the decision over to your brother. (That 75/25 split is Dad's rule; don't ask me why he made it that lopsided.) There's a hard cap: after six pushes, the seventh turn is an automatic cash-out, no more pushing allowed, so both boys know exactly how far this can possibly go, right from turn one. My brother-in-law, who took one game theory course in college and never really got over it, keeps telling the boys that whoever goes first should obviously just cash out immediately for the dollar: if you reason it through from the last possible turn backwards, the kid stuck on turn seven will obviously cash out rather than get nothing, so the kid on turn six should cash out too rather than hand his brother a turn seven that's bad for him, and so on, all the way back to turn one — so the "rational" move is grabbing seventy-five cents and quitting immediately. Except the twins never do this. They push almost every single week, right up near the cap, and by the time someone finally cashes out they've turned that dollar into something like sixty-four bucks between them, way more then either would get from his brother-in-law's "rational" opening move.

What's actually bugging me isn't just that the boys are nicer than the theory predicts — I could live with that being plain sibling trust. What's bugging me is that the argument seems to undercut itself: the whole chain of reasoning only holds up if each kid can count on the other one reasoning exactly that way at every future turn, no matter what — but the second either kid ever pushes even once, hasn't he just handed his brother decent evidence that he isn't that kind of relentlessly calculating actor, which should change what the brother rationally expects two turns later, which in turn changes whether pushing was ever really the "irrational" move to begin with? My brother-in-law says I'm massively overthinking a dumb allowance ritual his own family invented. I have a nagging feeling this whole setup must be a known problem with an actual name I'm just not landing on. Could you help me work out who's actually got the better argument here — and whether there's something genuinely wrong with the backwards reasoning itself, or the twins are just being naive kids who haven't learned better yet?
