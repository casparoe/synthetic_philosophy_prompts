I'm taking a seminar on the philosophy of migration, and this week's reading is Michael Dummett's On Immigration and Refugees — an unexpected pairing for a philosopher best known for his work on Frege and intuitionistic logic, though he treated the book as continuous with decades spent as a hands-on anti-racist campaigner in Britain. Dummett died in December 2011, well before Prime Minister David Cameron's 2015 description of migrants crossing the Mediterranean and massing at Calais as a "swarm" became a fixture of European debate. This is more than a guess about his general politics, though. In that same 2001 book, Dummett had already worked out a considered response to almost exactly this kind of rhetoric: he dismissed Margaret Thatcher's 1978 remark that Britain risked being "swamped" as "utterly ridiculous," while still conceding, in his own preferred vocabulary, that a nation has "a right not to be submerged" — a right he insisted was of "extremely limited application," and one Britain, in his assessment, came nowhere near meeting. Applying that same two-part test, how do you think Dummett would have judged Cameron's remark and the wider response to the 2015 crossings? Would his own concession about submersion have given him principled grounds to reject the rhetoric as factually as well as morally confused, or would it have left his framework exposed to exactly the kind of appropriation his critics worry about?
