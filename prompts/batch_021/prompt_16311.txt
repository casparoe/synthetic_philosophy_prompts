I'm drafting the closing chapter of a novel in which an elderly country doctor, near the end of his life, finally tells his grown grandson about a patient's recovery in the 1950s that the family has called miraculous ever since; the grandson, who narrates the book, has to decide, essentially in real time, how much weight to put on an old man's single, decades-old memory. Since the whole chapter turns on more or less the question Hume asks in "Of Miracles" — how much a report like this should move a reasonable listener — I sent an early draft to two friends from my writing group who both studied philosophy at university, mainly as a check on whether the grandson's own reasoning would hold up under pressure. Dinner turned into an argument about Hume's essay itself rather than my pages, and I've tried to set down the exchange below because, days later, I still can't tell who came out ahead.

(1) Callum opened by saying Hume's argument is basically decisive as it stands: a law of nature rests on completely uniform past experience, so a report that it's been broken has to be weighed against the whole of that experience, and since testimony is at best "probable" while the case for the law is as close to a "proof" as empirical reasoning gets, no testimony could realistically tip the balance. (2) Nadia said that can't be the full picture, because one of Hume's own examples cuts against it: the Indian prince who won't believe travelers' reports that water can turn solid is, on Hume's telling, reasoning understandably but wrongly, since the frost report isn't "contrary" to the prince's experience, only outside it — which is different from a genuine violation of a law he does have full experience of. So the real test was never just how surprising a report is, but whether it describes a law being broken or merely extended into an unfamiliar case, and Callum's version erases that line. (3) Callum answered that this actually helps his side rather than hurting it: the only way to sort "extension of a law" from "violation of a law" is to already know which regularity is the real law, and we only know that from uniform experience — which is just the same weighing Hume describes to begin with, so the Indian prince case isn't a way around the argument, it's an application of it. (4) Nadia replied that it shows something stronger: Hume needs an independent, non-circular way of fixing in advance which regularities count as laws before he's entitled to say testimony against them must always lose, and without one, "law" just means "whatever has had no counterexample yet," which makes his conclusion true by definition rather than by any actual weighing of evidence. (5) Callum granted that this might be a fair hit against the essay's first half, but said it leaves the second half untouched: there Hume argues empirically that no historical miracle report is actually backed by enough witnesses of the right character and independence, that people have an appetite for wonder that makes them careless with such reports, and that every religion's miracles count as evidence against every other religion's, so they cancel out — none of which trades on how "law" gets defined. (6) Nadia's last word was that the cancelling-out point proves less than Callum thinks: a Hindu miracle undercuts a Christian miracle only as evidence that Christianity is the uniquely correct religion, not as evidence that some unusual event occurred at all, so Callum is quietly sliding between "evidence for my religion's exclusive truth" and "evidence that something anomalous happened," and Hume's argument needs the second, much stronger claim to reach the conclusion he wants.

Could you referee this move by move rather than just picking an overall winner?
