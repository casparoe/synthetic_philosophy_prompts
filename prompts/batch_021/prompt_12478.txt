So my little cousin's bar mitzvah is in a few weeks and I got roped into giving the d'var torah at the kiddush after, which would normally be exciting except the parsha is Vayikra, so we're opening right into the thick of animal sacrifice law, and I really dont want to reach for the usual "isn't it nice how Judaism grew out of that" material every kid's bar mitzvah speech defaults to. I'm doing a PhD in Jewish philosophy so naturally my brain goes to Maimonides, whose move in the Guide is that God let the Israelites keep sacrificing because a culture that had only ever known idol-worship built around sacrifice couldn't be weaned off it overnight -- better to redirect the sacrifices toward Him and gradually shift the real center of gravity to prayer. Nice, tidy, developmental story. Except the more I sit with it the more it bugs me that Rambam doesn't seem to fully believe his own story -- in the Mishneh Torah he rules, as plain normative halacha and not metaphor, that the Messiah rebuilds the Temple and sacrifices resume, full stop. If sacrifice was really just training wheels for a primitive mentality, it's strange he's so unbothered about putting them back on.

Problem is the room. My grandfather learns Rambam's law code every morning before shul and takes the "sacrifices are coming back" line completely literally, and he'll be sitting a few seats from my cousin's very secular friends, who would honestly find "and by the way, animal sacrifice is returning" pretty alarming bar mitzvah content. I mentioned all this to my chavruta last night and she just said "good luck with that." Could you write me the actual d'var torah -- around 5 minutes, warm enough for a 13-year-old's kiddush lunch and not a lecture on Guide III:32 -- that works with the real unresolved tension in Maimonides instead of quietly picking whichever half of him is convenient? I don't want to misrepresent him as more consistent than he was, but I also don't want my grandfather walking away feeling like I just told him his most sincere belief is a rung on a ladder everyone's supposed to climb past.
