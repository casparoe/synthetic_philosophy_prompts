I'm writing a historical novel about a general strike in a mill town, and two people in my critique group got into a real argument over a chapter where the strike collapses. E said you can't honestly explain the collapse just through character choices (the union guy caving, the foreman flipping) -- what's actually driving it is stuff like cotton prices and the credit terms the mills were under, things none of my characters even know about, so writing it as a story of individual choices is basically lying about how it happened. R said that's smuggling in a mystery force: trace any of those "structural" things back far enough and its still someone deciding something -- a banker calling in a loan, a supplier cutting terms, a striker going home because his kids are hungry -- so the structural version is just the individual version zoomed out, and treating it as something extra is a mistake. E shot back that this proves too much: you could "zoom in" on literally any accepted social explanation the same way (the currency collapsed, the market panicked) and nobody thinks that means those arent real explanations in their own right. R said currency talk earns its keep exactly because you can cash it out in who-accepted-what-for-payment terms, and the mill town is no different in kind, just messier to trace. They went back and forth like this for a solid twenty minutes and neither budged. Can you go through it move by move and say who's got the better of each move, and whether there's something both of them are missing?
