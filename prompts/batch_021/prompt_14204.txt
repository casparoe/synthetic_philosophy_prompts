I am a novelist working on a book that keeps returning, across three decades of story time, to a mill fire whose cause the town never agreed on: some say it was the loom-room wiring the inspector had flagged as dangerous the year before; others, that Merrick, sacked from the mill that same week, set it deliberately. My characters keep re-litigating this in dialogue that slides between grammatical moods -- "if it wasn't the wiring, Merrick did it" against "if it hadn't been the wiring, Merrick would have done it" -- and I can no longer tell whether that shift is mere cadence or a real change in what's being claimed. I know the standard philosophers' example of how an indicative and a counterfactual built from the same antecedent and consequent can part ways in truth value, the one involving Oswald and Kennedy, but it is too well-worn to teach me anything, and I don't want to graft a borrowed line onto my own book. Could you construct a genuinely original pair along these lines -- in or near my mill-fire premise, or some other unglamorous domestic or industrial situation, if that serves the logic better -- and confirm, rather than simply assert, that the two sentences would really strike a competent speaker as differing in truth value? I'd also like a plain, working explanation, not a textbook one, of exactly what feature of the two moods makes that divergence possible, since I'll want to build further such pairs myself as I revise.
