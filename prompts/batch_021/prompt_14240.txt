I'm a retired physicist (condensed matter, mostly neutron scattering) and about two years ago, once I finally had time on my hands, I started sitting in on a little philosophy discussion group that meets at our library once a month -- a handful of retirees working through whatever topic someone proposes, nothing academic about it. Last month we did the cosmological argument, specifically the Leibniz-style "contingency" version (the one that runs on the Principle of Sufficient Reason and treats the whole universe as one big contingent fact needing an explanation), not the kalam one we covered a while back. Two of the regulars, Howard (retired patent attorney) and Deb (taught high school theology before she retired), got into a real back-and-forth over what I gather is called the "modal collapse" objection, and I can't stop turning it over, so I was hoping you'd help me sort out who was actually landing punches, move by move.

Howard opened (move 1) by saying the argument undoes itself: if PSR is true and the ultimate explanation for the universe has to bottom out in a necessary being with a fixed nature, then whatever that being explains should hold in every possible world, since that same being with that same nature exists no matter which world you're in -- so the argument doesn't just get you a necessary being, it gets you a necessary universe, which is absurd (there could easily have been one fewer galaxy, or none at all). Deb's answer (move 2) was that this only follows if God's nature produces effects the way a physical law produces effects; but God is supposed to be a free agent, and a free choice made by a necessarily-existing being can still have gone the other way, so nothing downstream gets necessitated. Howard's reply (move 3) was a dilemma: either there's a further explanation for why God chose this universe rather than some other one, in which case we're right back to needing something that bottoms out in necessity and the collapse just gets pushed back a step, or there's no further explanation, in which case the choice itself is a brute contingent fact sitting at the foundation of an argument whose entire selling point is that no contingent fact gets left unexplained. Deb's comeback (move 4) was that there is an explanation, it's just not a necessitating one: God chose a good, varied universe because it was good and varied, and reasons can fully explain a free choice without forcing it -- the same way "I got the salad because I'm watching my weight" explains my order without meaning I couldn't have gotten the fries.

Nobody at the table agreed on how this shook out and I honestly went home still turning it over. Could you go through moves 1 through 4 and say, for each one, who actually had the better of it -- not just who won overall? I'd also love to know if there's some standard move from the literature that neither Howard nor Deb reached for, one that would've cut through this more cleanly, or whether it genuinely stays this tangled even among people who work on this stuff professionaly.
