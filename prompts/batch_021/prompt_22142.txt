For my school's AI ethics elective, each of us has to present an obscure logic puzzle with some real connection to AI safety, and I've spent the last couple of weeks stuck on something called the Löbian obstacle, which I stumbled onto after a Gödel's-incompleteness rabbit hole on a rationalist blog. As far as I can tell, Löb's theorem says that a sufficiently powerful, consistent formal system can only prove the statement "if I can prove P, then P is true" for exactly those P it could already prove on independent grounds — it can't get that assurance for free, for an arbitrary P, without already knowing the answer. The reason this shows up so much in AI safety writing (I keep seeing it attributed to a "tiling agents" paper by Yudkowsky and Herreshoff) is that it seems to block an agent from certifying a successor, or even its own future self, as trustworthy just because that successor "proved" its plan was good: a general rule like "accept whatever the successor proves is good" runs straight into Löb's theorem unless the successor is somehow strictly weaker than the original, or unless provability gets swapped out for something softer, like the probabilistic credences that some later proposals use instead.

I can't decide whether this is a real, permanent dent in the whole project of building, or being, a reasoner that can trust its own future judgment, or just an artifact of demanding an unreasonably strong, all-or-nothing notion of trust that clever formal engineering has basically defused by now. Could you write me a dialogue — a dozen or so exchanges — between two people who take opposite sides on this: one who thinks the Löbian obstacle reveals a genuine and unavoidable limit on self-trust for any sufficiently expressive, consistent reasoner, so that the standard fixes only relocate the problem rather than solve it, and one who thinks it's a real but ultimately tamed technical wrinkle, no more philosophically devastating in the end than Gödel's own theorems turned out to be for working mathematicians. I'd like both of them to be genuinely sharp, with no strawmanning, so that each response actually engages with the specific move the other side just made rather than just restating their own position in slightly different words.
