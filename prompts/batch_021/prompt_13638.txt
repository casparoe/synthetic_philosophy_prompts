So I'm a PhD student in political theory, and I'm stuck on a chapter of my dissertation about entrenched "eternity clauses" in constitutions -- the provisions that get walled off from amendment entirely. The standard move (basically Elster's, from Ulysses and the Sirens, though half of what I know here comes filtered through a comparative constitutionalism reading group I'm in) is to frame these clauses as a kind of Ulysses contract: the constitution-making generation ties itself to the mast, the way Ulysses did, precisely so it can't later be talked into steering onto the rocks by the Sirens of momentary passion or a populist majority. Holmes has that essay on precommitment and the paradox of democracy that pushes this further, arguing the mast isn't just a constraint but what lets Ulysses hear the song and survive at all -- i.e. the rules are enabling, not merely restrictive.

Here's my hangup though: Ulysses binds his own future self. Something like Article 79(3) of the German Basic Law, which puts human dignity and the basic democratic order permanently beyond the reach of amendment, is really the 1949 framers binding a long chain of future generations who never signed anything and never will. That's a pretty different structure of agency from one guy and a mast. My advisor waves this away as a technicality, but it feels to me like it goes to the heart of whether the clause is democratically legitimate at all, not just a footnote on the theory. Could you walk me through what you think are the most important implications of the Ulysses-contract/precommitment framing for how we should assess the legitimacy of a clause like 79(3), given that wrinkle? I'm less after a verdict then after a sense of what actually follows (and what doesn't) once you take the analogy seriously -- happy to set aside India's basic-structure doctrine and similar cases for now, just want to get the core case right first.
