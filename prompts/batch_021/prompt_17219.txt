I spent something like 35 years doing fieldwork on shorebird foraging behaviour, which meant a lot of optimal foraging models -- classic adaptationism, though I never thought of it that way at the time. Now that I'm retired I've fallen into reading philosophy of biology in my spare time, and I've been re-reading Gould and Lewontin's old "Spandrels of San Marco" paper plus a bunch of the responses to it. I keep getting stuck on the same issue: the adaptationists insist optimality models are genuinely testable, while the Gould/Lewontin camp says most adaptive stories are untestable just-so stories dressed up as science. Everything I've read just recites those two lines past each other instead of actually arguing it out.

Could you write me a dialogue between two evolutionary biologists who actually hash this out properly? One's a committed adaptationist in the optimality-modelling, Maynard Smith tradition, who thinks assuming selection first is the right default; the other's a pluralist in the Gould/Lewontin mold who thinks drift, developmental constraints and byproducts deserve equal billing from the start. Don't let it stay abstract -- have them argue over a specific case, like Gould's old chin example (his claim that the human chin is just leftover material from two growth fields intersecting, not selected for in it's own right) -- and get them into the weeds of what would actually count as evidence one way or the other, not just trading "just-so story" and "it's testable, actually" back and forth. There's also supposedly a finer-grained distinction (Godfrey-Smith, I think?) between adaptationism as an empirical claim, an explanatory priority, and a methodology -- feel free to work that in if it helps. Both characters should be genuinely sharp, i don't want either one to be a pushover for the other.
