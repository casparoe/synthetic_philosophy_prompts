Random phil of bio thing that's been bugging me while I put together my quals reading list. So the standard line is that Wright's etiological account (function = whatever a trait was selected for) and Cummins' causal-role account (function = whatever a part contributes to some capacity of the containing system you're trying to explain) are these two big rival camps, and people write entire papers about which one gets "function" right. But the longer I sit with it the more I think the "selection history" part is basically decorative. Take the textbook case: the heart's function is to pump blood, not to make a thumping noise, and under either account you get the same answer — Cummins because pumping is the causally relevant capacity for the circulatory system we care about, Wright because pumping is what got selected for. And in general, whatever got selected for ancestrally is (almost by definition) also whatever's currently keeping the lineage going, so isn't "history" vs. "current contribution" mostly a distinction without a difference, except in some contrived thought-experiment case where the environment suddenly flips?

My advisor's response whenever I bring this up is to just gesture at vestigial organs and "evolutionary mismatch" cases, but that always struck me as swatting away the objection with a couple of weird exceptions rather than actually showing the two accounts are doing different explanatory work most of the time. So I guess what I'm asking is: is there something bigger I'm missing about why the field treats etiological vs. causal-role as such a fundemental fault line, or is this specific distinction kind of overhyped relative to how much ink's been spilled defending each side?
