I'm a novelist, and, as my family's designated wordsmith, I've been asked to deliver the eulogy at my mother's memorial service this coming Saturday. She spent twenty-six years as a state appellate judge and, unofficially, an entire adulthood as a close and combative reader of Pascal in the original French; her Pléiade edition of the Pensées is so worn along the spine that whole gatherings of pages have worked loose. Her favorite fragment, which she liked to produce at family dinners whenever someone complained that a verdict or a statute was unfair, was the one that runs, "la justice sans la force est impuissante ; la force sans la justice est tyrannique" — and she meant it exactly, not as a jurist's weary cynicism about how the law actually works, but as something closer to a theological claim: that we are, in the condition we're in, simply not equipped to see true justice directly, so that what we call law is custom backed by force and dressed up after the fact as though it had been justice all along — which she took to be less a scandal than a mercy, the alternative being each of us enforcing our own private verdict on everyone else. She was equally fond of a neighboring fragment in which he observes that whether killing a man counts as murder or as heroism depends on nothing loftier than which side of a river he happened to live on, which she treated as further proof that the line is drawn by custom rather than discovered by reason.

None of this, in her case, ever loosened her grip on the individual case in front of her; by every account she was almost pathologically scrupulous about getting each one right, and on the bench she drew a hard, visible line between rulings that merely settled something and rulings that got dressed up as moral triumphs, reserving for the second kind a contempt she otherwise rationed carefully. She left a note, clipped inside the Pensées, that I've been asked to read aloud at some point in the service: "Do not let anyone stand up and say I believed in Justice. I did not. And do not let anyone conclude, therefore, that I believed in nothing."

Roughly half of Saturday's room will be family who have never read a word of Pascal and need all of this to land as a portrait of a specific, exacting, and warm woman, not as a lecture delivered over her casket; the other half will be former colleagues and clerks who understood exactly what she meant and will notice immediately if the eulogy flattens it into either "she thought law was naked power" or "she secretly believed in Justice all along, really." Could you draft the eulogy itself — about seven minutes read aloud, not an outline — in a way that works for both halves of that room, honors the note to the letter, and actually resolves, rather than quietly steps around, how a person could hold that view of law and still care that much about getting each case right?
