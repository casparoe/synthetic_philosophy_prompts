I'm drafting a novel that follows the closed-door deliberations of a three-member jury for a prestigious literary prize, and I want a pivotal chapter to hinge on a genuine paradox of collective reasoning rather than on the usual friction of clashing egos. Under the prize's charter, a novel wins only if it is judged both formally original and emotionally truthful; each juror privately reaches an internally consistent view on originality, on emotional truth, and on whether the book deserves the prize overall. The trouble is that tallying the panel's majority opinion criterion by criterion can favor the book, while tallying the majority of jurors' own overall verdicts can reject it, even though every individual juror has reasoned impeccably. Please write a short story of roughly 900 to 1,200 words that dramatizes this bind precisely enough that a reader versed in judgment-aggregation theory would recognize the discursive dilemma (List and Pettit's generalization of Kornhauser and Sager's "doctrinal paradox") at work, while the piece still reads as absorbing literary fiction to those who have never heard the term.
