So this happened at work and I haven't been able to let it go. Slow Friday, and my coworker Deepak dropped a puzzle in our team channel that he'd found via some math-puzzle newsletter: you flip a fair coin over and over until it lands heads for the first time, and if that first heads happens on flip number n, the payout is (2^n)/n dollars -- except it alternates between you winning that amount (n odd) and you paying that amount (n even). So heads on flip 1 wins you $2, heads on flip 2 costs you $2, heads on flip 3 wins you about $2.67, heads on flip 4 costs you $4, and so on forever, with the swings getting bigger every round. Someone pointed out that if you multiply each payout by its probability (1/2^n) and add those up in the obvious order, n=1 then n=2 then n=3..., you get 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ... which is the classic alternating harmonic series and converges to ln(2), about 69 cents. Apparently this is a real thing called the Pasadena paradox -- like a weirder cousin of the St. Petersburg paradox, except here the claim isn't just that the expected value is infinite, it's that it's not even well-defined.

Being engineers, Deepak and another coworker, Erin, couldnt just leave it at the math, obviously they had to simulate it, and that's where they ended up in a whole back-and-forth that I got dragged into as referee afterward. Roughly reconstructed from Slack:
(1) Deepak wrote a quick script, millions of trials, running average of the payout, expecting it to settle near $0.69 -- instead it kept drifting around even after ten million trials. His take: 'one of our sims has a bug, expected value is expected value, the order you add up a bunch of independent trials can't change the answer, that's just associativity.'
(2) Erin says it's not a bug: she'd read that the series only converges conditionally (the all-positive version, just adding 1/n with no alternating signs, flat out diverges), and there's a theorem that conditionally convergent series can be reordered to add up to literally any number, or to blow up entirely -- so there isn't really one 'true' expected value sitting out there to estimate in the first place.
(3) Deepak pushes back: a simulation isn't rearranging anything, it's sampling outcomes one at a time in the order they'd actually occur and averaging those, which isn't some arbitrary reordering -- so his running average should still be homing in on something, maybe just slowly.
(4) Erin points out that his own trace isn't 'slow,' it's still making huge jumps ten million trials in, and that this tracks with the average of the absolute value of the payouts also being infinite (same harmonic series, just without the alternating signs) -- once that's infinite, the law of large numbers doesn't actually guarantee convergence at all, so 'run it longer' isn't obviously a fix.
(5) Deepak says fine, mathematically, but who cares -- no real bet works like this, real payouts are bounded because casinos and counterparties have finite money, so 'expected value is undefined' is a fact about a toy example, not a real problem for how anyone actually uses expected value.
(6) Erin says that's dodging the question -- the game is just a clean, minimal case that exposes how 'the' expected value can quietly stop being a coherent concept at all, and unbounded, heavy-tailed payoffs aren't just a toy setup, that's basically the shape of a lot of insurance and tail-risk finance.
