Derek Parfit's fission cases are a familiar touchstone in rationalist discussions of personal identity: a person's brain is divided, each hemisphere transplanted into a different body, producing two people who both wake up with the original's memories and personality intact. Parfit uses such cases to argue that identity over time is not what matters in survival, and in Reasons and Persons he explicitly presents this conclusion as vindicating the Buddhist doctrine of no-self — concluding that fission is nothing like death, since it merely doubles rather than destroys psychological continuity.

Centuries earlier, the Buddhist philosopher Vasubandhu (fl. 4th–5th century CE) defended a strikingly similar reductionism in the ninth chapter of his Abhidharmakośabhāṣya, often translated as the "Refutation of the Theory of a Self." Arguing against the Pudgalavādins, he holds that a person is nothing but a conventional designation (prajñaptisat) for an unbroken causal series (santāna) of momentary aggregate-events, with no further self needed to explain memory or moral responsibility. Yet his account of that series, including across rebirths, is always implicitly one-to-one: a being's final moment conditions the arising of exactly one successor, never several.

Using the specific resources of Vasubandhu's own argument, rather than the generic no-self conclusion, predict whether he could accept Parfit's verdict that fission is nearly as good as ordinary survival, or whether his apparatus instead forces him to treat it as the termination of one santāna and the simultaneous arising of two new ones — a double rebirth rather than a survival. Defend your answer by identifying exactly which of his commitments decides the question.
