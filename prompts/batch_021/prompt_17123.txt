Eight months ago I moved from a fairly ordinary job in nonprofit communications to become development director at a small research institute whose staff spend their days on formal decision theory -- specifically, on how advanced AI systems might threaten or extort one another, and, by extension, us, including through what they call "acausal" threats, where the threatening party and the threatened party never have ordinary causal contact, only a kind of logical or predictive correlation (the example that always gets a room's attention is a future AI that "punishes," after the fact, anyone who knew about it today and didn't help bring it into existence). One of the institute's signature ideas, worked out by a couple of our researchers a few years back, is called "surrogate goals": the proposal that an agent facing this kind of threat can deliberately cultivate an extra, cheap, substitute thing to care about, so that a threatener who only wants leverage ends up aiming at the substitute instead of at whatever the agent actually values -- defusing the threat without anyone giving in, and without the agent having to stop caring about what it really cares about. Even the people who came up with it will tell you it isn't finished. All of this is supposed to serve the institute's actual mission, which is to reduce the risk of astronomical suffering from how transformative AI systems might interact, not to win an argument in decision theory for its own sake.

Every autumn the institute runs a workshop that mixes these researchers with the handful of donors and family members who keep the place funded, and this year, since our executive director is on leave, I've been asked to give the opening remarks myself for the first time. The trouble is that the one story that makes "acausal threats" vivid to a non-specialist is, almost beat for beat, the plot of Roko's basilisk -- the thought experiment that got this whole corner of the field mocked for years as people who'd invented a new hell to worry about. Last year's workshop ended with a donor asking, half as a joke, whether she personally needed to worry about being punished by a future AI, and nobody in the room managed a satisfying answer before everyone drifted off to lunch. Our senior decision theorist has asked me, also half-joking, not to summon the basilisk from the podium, since talking about it carelessly is part of how it became a problem in the first place -- but I can't just avoid it either, since it's the actual reason half the room does this for a living, and someone will raise it at the coffee break regardless. Six months ago, if a friend had described this exact dilemma to me, I would have laughed at them.

Could you write the opening remarks for me? Roughly eight to ten minutes, meant to be spoken aloud, with at least one line that will genuinely land as a joke for the theorists without stranding everyone else. It needs to explain clearly enough for my donors why acausal threats are treated as a serious problem here and surrogate goals as a serious, if unfinished, answer to it; address the Roko's-basilisk question head-on rather than letting it hang over the rest of the day; and still leave the researchers in the room feeling I got the actual idea right.
