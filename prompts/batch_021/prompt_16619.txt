I'm a retired molecular biologist who's gotten pretty into philosophy lately, since I hung up my lab coat. Something that happened years ago in my old lab keeps nagging at me now that I've been reading about the norms of assertion -- the debate over whether you're only allowed to assert what you know, versus what's true, versus just what you honestly believe.

At a lab reunion dinner, two former postdocs of mine, Sam and Nora, got into an argument about something Sam had written in a grant renewal years earlier: that a certain signaling pathway "drives metastasis in vivo," based on solid correlative data in mice, before anyone had actually run the knockout experiment that would establish causation rather than mere association. The knockout came through two years later, and he turned out to be right.

(1) Nora said the sentence was dishonest the moment he wrote it, whatever happened later, because at the time he didnt know it -- he dressed up a lucky guess as settled fact.
(2) Sam said it wasnt a guess, it was a strong hypothesis backed by real data and solid mechanistic priors, and honest belief like that is all assertion requires -- otherwise nobody could write a confident sentence in a grant application ever again.
(3) Nora replied that's exactly backwards: grant conventions have trained everyone to write speculation in the confident register of settled fact, and reviewers read "drives" as established, not suspected. Sure, a paper's intro does something similar with its working model, but everyone reads an intro as the story-before-the-proof -- that's a genre convention writer and reader are both in on. A renewal's results section asserting an actual causal driver is a different, stronger register, and that's the line Sam crossed.
(4) Sam shot back that there's no principled place to put that line -- renewals are themselves full of "preliminary" data, reviewers know that going in, and if you have to hedge every claim that outruns direct proof then most of science writing already commits the very sin Nora's pinning on his one sentence.

Neither of them budged, and I couldn't referee it myself. Could you go through moves (1) through (4) and say who's got the better of it at each step, not just who wins overall, and flag anything you think both of them are missing? End with a one-line verdict in exactly this format: \verdict{Sam} or \verdict{Nora}.
