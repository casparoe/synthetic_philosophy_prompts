A friend and I are both auditing the same philosophy of religion seminar this term, and after we worked through Wittgenstein's 1938 lectures on religious belief (collected in Lectures and Conversations on Aesthetics, Psychology and Religious Belief), we ended up arguing about them for most of a train ride home. She defends what several commentators call the "quietist" reading: when Wittgenstein discusses someone who says he believes in a Last Judgement, the point isn't that this is a badly evidenced empirical claim but that it functions as a picture regulating the believer's whole life, so that calling it "reasonable" or "unreasonable" by the standards we'd use for an ordinary hypothesis is already a category mistake — a believer and a non-believer aren't disagreeing about a fact so much as operating with different concepts of what "believing" even comes to here. She also cited Wittgenstein's irritation at a fellow lecturer, Father O'Hara, who had tried to defend the Resurrection using the same kind of evidence-and-probability talk you'd use for a scientific claim, which she takes as direct textual support for her reading.

I pushed back with what's usually called Kai Nielsen's charge of "Wittgensteinian fideism" (a label she'd reject for herself, preferring "quietist"): if only internal criteria count, then astrology, a conspiracy theory, or a cult could just as well claim to be playing their own self-contained language-game and wall themselves off from outside criticism in exactly the same way, which seems like too cheap a victory to have won by conceptual analysis alone. She answered that the view doesn't actually rule out internal criticism — believers can call other believers superstitious or self-deceived by the lights of the practice itself — and that my objection quietly smuggles back in the very idea Wittgenstein was attacking: that there is one neutral, practice-independent standard of "reasonable belief" against which every practice must answer. Neither of us landed a decisive blow before we had to get off the train.

Could you write a dialogue between a philosopher defending this Wittgensteinian quietist view of religious belief and a philosopher pressing the fideism charge against it, picking up roughly where we left off? I want the quietist to have to say something specific about where internal religious self-criticism ends and unfalsifiability-in-practice begins, engaging directly with the Last Judgement passage rather than resting on slogans, and I want the critic to be forced to answer the return charge that insisting on a single, context-independent standard of "reasonable belief" is itself a substantive and contestable philosophical commitment rather than a neutral default. Neither speaker should be allowed to just repeat a one-line dismissal — each turn should engage with the specific point the other has just made — and I'd like it to end with the two of them trying to work out whether they actually disagree about religion at all, or only about what "belief" means in that sentence.
