I'm a novelist currently drafting a book in which one strand follows two philosophy graduate students—one drawn to Peirce, the other to James—whose long-running argument about the nature of truth is meant to anchor their friendship. I want their disagreement to rest on the real texts rather than the potted contrast most readers already know, so I've been rereading Peirce's 1901 entry on "Truth and Falsity and Error," written for Baldwin's Dictionary of Philosophy and Psychology, and I've gotten stuck on his central definition there:

"Truth is that concordance of an abstract statement with the ideal limit towards which endless investigation would tend to bring scientific belief, which concordance the abstract statement may possess by virtue of the confession of its inaccuracy and one-sidedness, and this confession is an essential ingredient of truth."

I can follow the first half well enough, but the second half stops me: how can a statement's own confession of its inaccuracy and one-sidedness be an essential ingredient of its truth, rather than simply an admission of how far it falls short of truth? Could you walk me through the logic of that clause, and then explain how the conception of truth it implies differs from the more familiar Jamesian idea of truth as whatever "works" in experience—specifically, whether the difference amounts to more than a matter of emphasis?
