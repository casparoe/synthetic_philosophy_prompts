I spent about thirty-five years as a nuclear physicist, most of it measuring decay rates and calibrating gamma sources (we had an old Cobalt-60 button sitting in a lead pig in the corner of the lab that outlived three generations of grad students), and it never once occured to me to ask what I actually meant when I told those students a given nucleus has, say, a 50% chance of decaying in the next hour. Since retiring I've been slowly working through a philosophy of probability book my son-in-law (a philosopher, of all things) recommended, and it turns out that exact question, what a single-case probability claim is even about, is a genuinely live dispute. The frequentist in the book wants to say the 50% figure is really a fact about the long-run frequency in some suitably chosen reference class of similar decays, and more or less admits that applying it to one specific nucleus that will only ever decay once is a kind of derivative fiction. The propensity theorist (Popper's line, as I understand it) wants to say no, that one nucleus has a genuine, primitive disposition to decay, full stop, no reference class required, but then apparently runs into a nasty technical problem, something like what's called Humphreys' paradox if I'm remembering the name right, about how propensities are supposed to be one-directional causal tendencies, yet the probability calculus (Bayes' theorem specifically) commits you to a "propensity" running backwards too, which doesn't seem to make sense for something causal.

Could you write me a dialogue between these two, a committed frequentist and a committed single-case propensity theorist, standing in front of that one specific nucleus and a detector, arguing about what its 50% claim actually means? I'd definately like them to engage each other's actual weak spot rather than just restating their general views past one another, the reference class problem for the frequentist, the backwards-propensity worry for the other one, and I don't want either side to just win by default. My son-in-law is convinced the propensity theorist is toast; I'm not so sure, and I'd like to see the strongest version of the argument on both sides before I have to defend a position at Sunday dinner.
