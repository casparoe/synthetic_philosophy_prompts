I recently joined a three-year Rambam Yomi cycle at my synagogue, working through the Mishneh Torah a chapter a day, and we've just reached Sefer Avodah, the laws governing the Temple and its sacrificial service. Because I read the Guide of the Perplexed in a college course a few years ago, I remembered Maimonides' account in III:32 of animal sacrifice as essentially a divine concession: rather than commanding the Israelites, fresh out of Egyptian and Chaldean idol-worship, to abandon sacrifice altogether -- something he maintains is psychologically impossible for people to do all at once -- God redirected their sacrificial habits toward Himself instead, in what's often called his "weaning theory," much as a nursing child is weaned off milk gradually rather than switched to solid food overnight. Read that way, sacrifice sounds like a purely instrumental, time-bound accommodation with no value once its pedagogical work is done. But the Mishneh Torah I'm now studying codifies the sacrificial cult in loving, granular detail as permanently binding halakhah, and in the Laws of Kings Maimonides describes the messianic king as rebuilding the Temple and restoring its service as part of the ideal, final order of things, not as something humanity will finally have outgrown. Abarbanel and later critics pressed exactly this worry, noting that Cain and Abel, Noah, and the patriarchs were already offering sacrifices long before there was any Egyptian or Chaldean star-worship for anyone to be weaned off of. My chavruta insists this is all easily reconciled once you're careful about what each work is actually doing, but he hasn't convinced me, and nothing I've read on my own has fully settled it either.

Could you write an essay that actually takes a stand on this? Lay out the strongest version of the case that Maimonides' legal and philosophical writings are in genuine, unresolved tension -- perhaps even outright contradiction -- regarding the value of sacrifice, and the strongest version of the case that the tension is merely apparent, whether the dissolving move is an esoteric/exoteric distinction between the Guide's intended audience and the Code's, a distinction between why a practice was originally instituted and what its status is once instituted, a developmental story about Maimonides revising his views between the two works, or something else entirely. Please don't leave both sides evenly balanced and stop there: tell me which reading you find genuinely more defensible, and defend it against the strongest form of the opposing view, engaging with the actual texts rather than resting on generalities.
