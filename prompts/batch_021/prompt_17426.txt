Last weekend, at a friend's dinner party, I ended up in a long and surprisingly heated argument with my friend Jonah over something his uncle had done. The uncle, who keeps a small sheep farm, had his old sheepdog Baxter put down — not because Baxter was ill or in pain, but because his hips had given out and he could no longer work, and the uncle saw no point in feeding a dog that couldn't earn its keep. What struck me was how careful Jonah's defense of this was. He didn't deny that the decision was distasteful; he just insisted that whatever was distasteful about it wasn't a wrong done to Baxter, because Baxter isn't the kind of being capable of being wronged in the first place — on Jonah's view, that status is reserved for creatures with some kind of rational, self-aware standing. So, he said, the uncle failed himself, not Baxter: he acted on, and further entrenched, a callousness that matters mainly because of how it will eventually surface in his dealings with people. Baxter, on this account, is incidental to the story rather than its victim.

I told Jonah this looked like a dodge: if nothing is owed to Baxter, I couldn't see what was supposed to be doing the moral work in calling the uncle's character "corrupted" rather than just "different," and I said his reasoning would apply just as well to someone who smashes a vending machine in a fit of temper, which nobody thinks reveals anything worse than a short fuse. Jonah answered that the cases aren't parallel: a creature that can act as though it suffers gets under our skin in a way inert property never does, and it's precisely this mechanism, not anything owed to Baxter, that explains why the uncle's act corrodes something in him that kicking a machine wouldn't touch. I pushed back that this answer seemed to quietly reinstate the very thing he'd denied — if Baxter's apparent suffering is what's doing the explanatory work, it's hard to see how Baxter has been written out of the moral story after all. We went around on this for the better part of an hour, neither of us landing a clean blow, and I've been unable to let it go since. I'd like your honest assessment of who was closer to right: is there a stable, non-question-begging version of Jonah's position available to him, does my "you've smuggled Baxter back in" objection actually succeed or does it just trade on an equivocation of my own, and is there a better way of drawing the line he's after — between an act being bad and an act being a wrong done to someone — than either of us managed at the table?
