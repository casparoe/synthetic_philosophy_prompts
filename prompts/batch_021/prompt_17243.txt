So my in-person reading group (it started as a LessWrong meetup and slowly turned into "people who read too many blogs argue about whatever book we picked") just finished Nietzsche's Genealogy of Morality instead of our usual decision-theory papers, and two of the regulars, Marco and Ada, got into an argument afterward at the pub that I still haven't been able to sort out. (1) Marco's opening shot was that the whole first essay is a genetic fallacy in a nice suit: Nietzsche spends the essay showing that slave morality's good/evil grew out of ressentiment among the weak and priestly types, as opposed to the masters' original good/bad, and then writes as if that causal story discredits the values themselves. His example was something like "so what if compassion started out as a coping mechanism for people who couldn't win any other way, that doesn't make compassion bad now, any more than chemistry growing out of alchemy discredits chemistry." (2) Ada said that's not actually the target: Nietzsche isn't disputing the truth of "compassion is good" as a claim (she doesn't think he even thinks morality has that kind of truth-aptness to begin with), he's going after the value's psychological grip on you, which is a different thing to attack, so calling it a genetic fallacy assumes a target that was never really in play.

(3) Marco's comeback was that this makes the whole book toothless: by the third essay Nietzsche clearly wants us to conclude something evaluative -- that the ascetic ideal is a kind of sickness, a "will to nothingness" we'd be better off without -- and if mere origin-tracing can't license any evaluative conclusion, that conclusion has to be smuggled in via the causal story, otherwise the "revaluation of values" project never leaves the ground. (4) Ada answered that the payload isn't "false because born of resentment," it's closer to: a value that only has its grip on you because you believe a false story about where it came from (that it's God-given, or just "what good has always meant") loses its authority once you see the actual mechanism -- not through a fallacy, but because a premise it was quietly resting on turns out false, like finding out a "sacred" tradition was actually invented at a marketing meeting three years ago. (5) Marco granted that this works for values whose whole authority is staked on a myth of origin, but pointed out that plenty of people now defend compassion or equality on totally secular, self-aware grounds with no myth attached, so by Ada's own logic a geneaology shouldn't cost them anything. (6) Ada's reply was that secular defenders still quietly inherit an unexamined evaluative instinct -- "suffering matters, minimizing it is what decent people do" -- as a brute premise, and that instinct is exactly what's being diagnosed, so the critique still bites, just one level down, against an assumption instead of a doctrine. Marco's last word was that this just relocates the genetic fallacy instead of avoiding it, and thats where they left it, both a little annoyed at the other for not conceding.

I've read enough of the debunking-arguments stuff that floats around rationalist blogs (the evolutionary-debunking-of-moral-intuitions genre) to suspect this whole fight is secretly about the same underlying issue, but I can't work out who actually has the better of it at each step. Could you go through moves 1 through 6 and say who comes out ahead on each one specifically, rather than just declaring an overall winner? And then, separately, tell me what you think both Marco and Ada are missing -- like whether there's a cleaner way of stating the real disagreement that would show one of them has been arguing past the other the whole time.
