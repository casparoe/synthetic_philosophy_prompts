I've spent the past few months reporting on debates in AI alignment, and I keep running into a particular argumentative move that gets used to justify worrying about "power-seeking" behavior in advanced systems: the claim that so-called coherence theorems force any sufficiently capable, goal-directed agent to act as an expected-utility maximizer, on pain of being money-pumped into ruin. This line of argument is popular in AI-safety circles, associated especially with writers like Eliezer Yudkowsky, but when I went back to the primary decision-theory literature rather than the popularizations, I noticed that the classic money-pump argument — the one built around a cycle of trades that bleeds an agent dry — targets cyclical, intransitive preferences specifically. The requirement that actually does the heavy lifting in these AI arguments is completeness: the demand that the agent have some preference or indifference between every pair of options whatsoever. Extending the money pump to rule out incompleteness seems to require an extra premise beyond bare coherence — roughly, that an agent facing two options it doesn't rank against each other will be willing to trade freely between them, and to accept a slight sweetening or souring of the deal without changing its mind — and that premise looks far more contestable than anything needed for the transitivity pump.

For a piece I'm drafting on this, I'd like you to make the strongest case you can for the following thesis: that the money-pump argument for completeness is substantially weaker than the money-pump argument for transitivity, so that an agent with genuinely incomplete preferences (as opposed to merely indifferent or uncertain ones) can avoid exploitation and need not collapse into an expected-utility maximizer, provided it adopts a sensible policy for resolving choices among the options it does not rank. Please don't just assert this; work through a concrete toy example (a small set of options and proposed trades) to show where the pumping strategy actually breaks down, and then confront the two objections that seem most threatening to your case — first, that any determinate policy for choosing among unranked options effectively reconstructs completeness through the back door, and second, that such a policy makes the agent dynamically inconsistent in a way that opens up a different kind of exploitation. I'd rather you take those objections seriously and show why they do or don't succeed than wave them away.
