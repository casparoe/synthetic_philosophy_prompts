For the past year or so I've been reading about anthropic reasoning as a personal hobby, mostly through blog posts and online discussion threads, because I find the Doomsday Argument fascinating: the idea that, simply from knowing roughly where you fall in the sequence of all humans who will ever be born, you should shift your estimate toward humanity ending sooner rather than later. The more I read, the more it seemed to me that this puzzle has a clean fix: just adopt the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA) instead of the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA), since SIA gives extra prior weight to hypotheses on which more observers exist, and that extra weighting exactly cancels out the pessimistic shift the Doomsday Argument depends on. On the strength of this, I've started telling people that the whole debate is basically settled: just reason according to SIA, since it's the version of anthropic reasoning that avoids the obviously absurd doomsday conclusion, with no real cost attached.

A friend of mine who has read more formal philosophy than I have pushed back on this, saying that SIA comes with "its own doomsday argument, just running in the opposite direction," something to do with a "presumptuous philosopher," but he couldn't really explain it well and neither of us could piece it together afterward. Since switching to SIA still seems to me like a strictly better move with no downside, I don't see what cost he thinks I'm missing, or why serious researchers in this area haven't already converged on SIA (or SSA) as the obviously correct principle. Could you help me understand what the real trade-offs are between these two assumptions, what this "presumptuous philosopher" idea is supposed to show, and why this apparently remains an open question rather than something that's already been cleanly resolved?
