I'm a philosophy PhD student working in formal epistemology, and this term our seminar on the epistemology of disagreement has moved from the informal "peer disagreement" literature (Elga, Christensen, Kelly) into the more formal treatment, starting with Aumann's original 1976 paper and the later Geanakoplos–Polemarchakis result on iterated exchange of posteriors. Working through this material alongside an actual disagreement I've been having has left me a bit puzzled, and I wanted to check whether my reasoning holds up.

For several months now, a close friend in my cohort and I have had a running disagreement about our credence that a particular pre-registered replication of a well-known finding in social psychology will come out positive. We've each laid out our full reasoning to the other more than once over coffee — I know she's currently at about 30%, she knows I'm at roughly 65% — and neither number has moved across our last several conversations. As I understand Aumann's theorem, two Bayesians who share a common prior cannot go on disagreeing once their posteriors on the same event are common knowledge, since each person's announced posterior already incorporates everything relevant that could be inferred about their private evidence, leaving nothing left to prompt further updating. My friend and I were trained in the same statistics sequence, work from the same general methodological background, and have by now explicitly told each other our numbers on repeated occasions, so it seems like the theorem's conditions are close enough to being satisfied in our case. If that's right, then our stalled disagreement looks like fairly direct evidence that at least one of us — maybe both — is violating Bayesian rationality somewhere. I'd appreciate help thinking through whether that inference actually holds up, and, if it does, whether the theorem gives us any principled way to tell which of us is more likely to be the one going wrong.
