So my writing group made me read this philosophy book a while back, L.A. Paul's stuff on "transformative experience," and its been rattling around in my head ever since — I think it might actually be the seed for my next novel. The idea that grabbed me isn't just "big decisions are hard," it's that some decisions are structured so that you literally can't get the information you'd need to make them well, even in principle, because knowing what an experience is like requires having already had it, and having it might reshape what you even want afterward. Her go-to case is deciding whether to become a vampire — immortal, blood-drinking, and (this is the part that matters) experiencing and valuing the world in a totally different way once you cross over — you can't poll vampires on whether it was worth it, because their post-transformation preferences aren't yours, and you can't just imagine your way to an answer, because that's exactly the kind of knowledge imagination can't supply.

I remember she splits this into "epistemically" transformative experiences (ones that teach you something you couldn't have known any other way) and "personally" transformative ones (ones that change your core values or identity), and the vampire case is nastily both at once, which is supposedly what breaks ordinary expected-utility reasoning here — you can't assign values to outcomes you don't yet have epistemic access to. She's also got this idea of "revelation," that sometimes just doing the thing is the only way to get the knowledge that would've told you whether to do the thing, which feels almost circular when you say it plainly out loud.

What I'd love from you is an actual short story, not a summary of any of the above — 800 to 1500 words, real scenes and dialogue, not an outline — built around a character facing a decision with exactly that structure. Please don't reach for the vampire angle, or the having-a-kid example either (that's apparently the other go-to in this literature) — invent a new scenario, any genre or period, where "just find out" is the only route to the relevant knowledge, the choice is irreversible, and the self making the decision might not survive, preference-wise, to appreciate whatever answer it gets. I write mostly literary fiction with a speculative edge, so lean that direction if it helps, but honestly just surprise me. I don't need any of the philosophy spelled out on the page — I'd rather a reader who's never heard of any of this feel it in their gut by the end.
