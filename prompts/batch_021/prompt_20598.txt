I'm a PhD student in public policy, currently working as a research assistant on the evaluation team for a state-run job-training pilot that places long-term unemployed adults into subsidized apprenticeships. We're drafting the interim report for the legislative oversight committee, and at this week's lab meeting two people on the team — Derek, a postdoc who leads the analysis, and Simone, another RA — got into a genuine disagreement about which number should serve as the report's headline result. This is my first substantive assignment in the program, so before I say anything at next week's meeting I'd rather work out for myself who actually has the better of this exchange, which so far I haven't managed to do.

Here is roughly how it went. (1) Derek pointed to the topline comparison: of the 600 pilot participants, only 42% were employed twelve months later, against 63% of the 600 people in the matched comparison group who never enrolled — so, however uncomfortable it is, he argued the honest headline is that the program is associated with worse outcomes, and that is the number the committee's staff will fixate on no matter what caveats surround it. (2) Simone countered that the comparison isn't apples-to-apples: the program specifically targeted people who had already been out of work a long time, so 500 of the 600 participants had been unemployed more than six months at intake, versus only 100 of the 600 comparison-group members. Once you split the data by spell length, participants actually do better in both slices — 78% versus 70% employed among the short-spell cases, and 35% versus 28% among the long-spell cases — so it's the disaggregated numbers, not the pooled ones, that show the program's real effect. (3) Derek granted that the split looked better for the program but pushed back that the six-month cutoff is a crude proxy: caseworkers used their own judgment about who seemed "job-ready" when deciding whom to refer, so there could easily be systematic differences between participants and comparison members even within each spell-length group, meaning one stratifying variable doesn't obviously settle the matter. (4) Simone replied that a partial, imperfect adjustment for a measured, pre-existing difference between the groups is still clearly better than no adjustment at all, and that Derek was discounting a known, documented difference in favor of a merely speculative one. (5) Derek came back with a different objection: even granting the stratified numbers, presenting "78% vs. 70%" and "35% vs. 28%" side by side implicitly treats the two subgroups as equally important, when the program's actual caseload is overwhelmingly long-spell (500 of 600) — so any single number meant to inform whether the legislature should fund an expansion ought to reflect that real mix, rather than an implicit fifty-fifty split between the groups.

Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of the argument at each of the five numbered points, flagging anywhere you think both of them are missing something? I'd also value your own recommendation, in a sentence or two, for how the twelve-month employment comparison should actually be constructed for the committee so that it isn't misleading in either direction.
