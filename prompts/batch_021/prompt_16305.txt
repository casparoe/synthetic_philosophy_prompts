I'm about a third of the way into a novel that alternates between the letters of a fictional gentleman who moved in John Locke's circle during the 1690s and the diary of his present-day descendant, a hedge-fund manager in Connecticut who has withdrawn his two children from an elite private school in order to homeschool them, citing -- rather selectively -- Locke's "Some Thoughts Concerning Education" as his authority. To keep the modern chapters honest rather than a straw man, I want this descendant's reasoning, and the objections other characters raise to it, to track what Locke actually argued in that text, rather than the popularized "blank slate" Locke that everyone reaches for from his Essay Concerning Human Understanding, which I'd like to set aside here entirely.

Locke had a great deal to say about the corrupting effect of grammar-school Latin drilled in by rote and enforced with the rod, about the moral risks of putting a boy among his peers rather than keeping him under a private tutor, about the folly of turning learning into either a hated "task" or something bought with sweets and toys, and about cultivating in a child a regard for esteem and a fear of shame in preference to both punishment and bribery. None of that, of course, gave him any occasion to consider the SAT-and-AP-transcript arms race that now governs elite college admissions, or the reward-and-badge mechanics built into today's "personalized learning" apps, which dangle points in front of children the way earlier moralists worried about sweets. Reasoning as tightly as you can from his specific, actual arguments about motivation, virtue, and the proper use of a child's sense of reputation, what do you think Locke would have made of each of these two features of contemporary schooling -- and would he have regarded them as different in kind, or merely as the same old vices in new dress?

I'd eventually like to put a version of the answer into the hedge-fund manager's mouth in an argument with his sister, who suspects he's fooling himself about how Lockean his choices really are, so I'm especially interested in any point at which his practice, as I've sketched it, seems to depart from what Locke would actually have endorsed.
