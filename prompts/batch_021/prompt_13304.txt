Since retiring from a career running an experimental physics laboratory, I have been spending much of my time reading philosophy on my own, and a puzzle from my working life has resurfaced that I would like some outside perspective on.

For my last several projects before retirement, I had grown uneasy about how much latitude an experimenter has, once the data are finally in hand, to steer an analysis toward the result they were hoping for, often without much awareness of doing so. So I adopted a rule: before touching any data, I would write out the complete analysis plan, seal it, and send a copy to Elena, a former postdoc of mine whom I trusted enormously, with explicit instructions that she was not to let me deviate from that plan afterward, however persuasive my reasons for doing so might seem to me at the time. The idea was to let my pre-data self, whom I trusted, keep some rein on my post-data self, whom I trusted rather less.

This arrangement has come back to bite me on what was meant to be my very last project. After the data came in, we discovered that one of our instruments had drifted out of calibration for roughly the middle third of the run, in a way that plausibly biases exactly the comparison we cared about most. Correcting for this strikes me as an obviously legitimate reason to depart from the plan, but Elena will not budge: she reminds me that I specifically instructed her not to be moved by whatever compelling-sounding justification I produced after the fact, since anticipating that I would eventually produce one, and would believe it sincerely, was the whole point of the arrangement. I can mount a good case for either side, which is precisely my difficulty. I suspect this is a recognized kind of problem — it feels distantly related to arguments I have since come across about whether a constitution should be able to bind the hands of future generations — but I cannot place my own case within any such framework precisely, and I would be grateful for help thinking it through: is Elena right to hold me to my own earlier instructions even now, or does an independently verifiable equipment fault count as exactly the sort of unforeseen circumstance such a rule ought to yield to? And is there any test I could apply from the inside, in the moment, to distinguish a genuine exception from the very kind of self-serving rationalization the rule was designed to guard against, or is that distinction only ever available to someone standing outside the decision, looking in?
