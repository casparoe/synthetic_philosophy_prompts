I've spent a fair amount of my spare time over the past year reading about the history of mathematics as a hobby, and one story I keep coming back to is Thomas Hobbes's long-running feud with the Oxford mathematician John Wallis over squaring the circle. From what I've read, Hobbes became convinced, well into his sixties, that he had found a genuine compass-and-straightedge construction for a square with exactly the area of a given circle, and he treated the discovery as confirmation of something he already believed: that geometry was the one kind of reasoning whose conclusions no honest person could refuse once the demonstration had been walked through step by step, which is exactly why he thought the same method could put politics and ethics on equally solid footing. Wallis and his university colleagues spent something like two decades publicly, and often quite viciously, tearing the proof apart pamphlet by pamphlet, and as far as I can tell they never let the matter drop, even once Hobbes was deep into his eighties.

Here is what confuses me. Mathematicians did not actually prove that squaring the circle is impossible until the 1880s, roughly two centuries after Hobbes died, once it was finally shown that pi cannot be the solution to any polynomial equation with whole-number coefficients. So it seems to me that Wallis and the rest of the Oxford men could not have rigorously known that Hobbes's construction failed; they simply found him personally and politically intolerable, given how much outrage Leviathan had already caused, and used their standing at the universities and the newly founded Royal Society, which pointedly never admitted him, to discredit him under the cover of mathematics. One writer I read even mentioned, almost as an aside, that Hobbes held strange views about what a "point" or a "line" really was, tied to his conviction that only bodies truly exist, without ever explaining why that would matter to whether his construction actually worked. So was Hobbes's whole outlook on geometry basically vindicated by this episode, with the real lesson being about credentialed insiders closing ranks against an independent thinker rather than anything genuinely wrong with his mathematics? I have started using this story as my go-to example of institutional gatekeeping whenever I argue with friends about expertise, and I would rather find out now if I have the history backwards than keep repeating it.
