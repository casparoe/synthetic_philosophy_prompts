For my IB Philosophy extended essay, I'm looking at how closely David Lewis's game-theoretic account of convention, in his 1969 book Convention, actually tracks the "Humean" idea it claims to be making precise. While working through the relevant section of Hume's Treatise (Book III, Part II, Section II, "Of the origin of justice and property"), I got stuck on this passage (Treatise 3.2.2.10, SBN 490):

"This convention is not of the nature of a promise: For even promises themselves, as we shall see afterwards, arise from human conventions. It is only a general sense of common interest; which sense all the members of the society express to one another, and which induces them to regulate their conduct by certain rules. I observe, that it will be for my interest to leave another in the possession of his goods, provided he will act in the same manner with regard to me. He is sensible of a like interest in the regulation of his conduct. When this common sense of interest is mutually express'd, and is known to both, it produces a suitable resolution and behaviour. And this may properly enough be call'd a convention or agreement betwixt us, tho' without the interposition of a promise; since the actions of each of us have a reference to those of the other, and are perform'd upon the supposition, that something is to be perform'd on the other part. Two men, who pull the oars of a boat, do it by an agreement or convention, tho' they have never given promises to each other. Nor is the rule concerning the stability of possession the less deriv'd from human conventions, that it arises gradually, and acquires force by a slow progression, and by our repeated experience of the inconveniences of transgressing it."

What I can't reconcile is that Hume seems to describe this very same convention in two different ways: first, as resting on a sense of common interest that is "mutually express'd, and is known to both" - which sounds like there is some identifiable point at which each party comes to know that the other knows - and second, as something that "arises gradually" through "repeated experience," with no such moment ever occurring. Are these two descriptions actually consistent, and if so, how? And is Lewis's later official definition of convention, with its common-knowledge clause, meant to capture Hume's first description, his second, or some way of holding both together? I would like to understand this properly before I use the passage as evidence in my essay.
