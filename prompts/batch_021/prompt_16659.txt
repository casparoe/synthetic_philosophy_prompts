I'm a PhD student in philosophy finishing a dissertation chapter on the empirical challenge to virtue ethics, and I keep running into the same problem: most discussions I read (including my own early drafts) rehearse Milgram and Zimbardo, gesture vaguely at "character skepticism," and then stop, without getting into the more careful moves that have been made on both sides since Doris and Harman's original papers. Before I commit to a position, I'd like your help pressure-testing the debate at that finer-grained level.

Could you write a dialogue between a neo-Aristotelian virtue ethicist and a situationist philosopher in the tradition of Doris and Harman, set as an exchange after a colloquium talk so the tone can be informal but substantive? The situationist should press specific evidence beyond the usual headline cases — for instance, Isen and Levin's "dime in the phone booth" helping study, or Darley and Batson's "Good Samaritan" study of seminarians told they were running late — to argue that the cross-situationally consistent, "global" character traits virtue ethics needs are empirically unsupported. The virtue ethicist shouldn't just retreat into vague talk of context-sensitivity; they should directly engage moves like Sreenivasan's argument that situationists mischaracterize what trait attribution actually requires, and press the question of whether Aristotle's own account — on which fully realized virtue, as opposed to mere enkrateia (continence), is rare and depends on cultivated phronesis rather than raw behavioral consistency — was ever committed to the sort of trait the experiments are supposed to refute. I'd like genuine back-and-forth, with each side responding to the other's actual argument rather than talking past it, and I'd rather the exchange end on an unresolved note than a tidy synthesis, since I don't think the literature has earned one yet.
