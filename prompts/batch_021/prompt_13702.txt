I spent my career as a condensed-matter physicist, and now that I'm retired I've been filling the gap with the philosophy of science I always meant to read but never had time for durring my working years. A couple of old colleagues and I turned our standing Sunday breakfast into an informal reading group a while back, and last week it nearly turned into a real argument, so I want a neutral party to sort it out.

We'd been talking about the parade of atomic models -- Thomson's "plum pudding," Rutherford's tiny nucleus, Bohr's little solar system, and finally the quantum-mechanical orbital picture -- and how textbooks always narrate this as each model getting "closer to the truth" than the last. (1) Sasha said this is just obviously right, and that anyone who denies you can rank successive false theories by closeness to the truth is really just smuggling in the claim that theory choice is arbitrary. (2) Priyanka, who's on a Popper kick, said Popper actually coined a technical notion of verisimilitude to make "closer to the truth" precise -- roughly, theory A beats theory B if A's true consequences include all of B's plus more, while A's false consequences are no more than B's -- and that it's a real, provable result that this definition can never rank two theories that are both false, since a false theory always ends up with some true consequences the rival lacks and some false ones the rival lacks too, so on Popper's own test the two end up simply incomparable, neither ranked above the other. (3) Sasha said fine, Popper's specific test must have been badly built, because Bohr's model obviously resembles the actual atom (heavy positive nucleus, discrete energy levels) more than Thomson's does -- so "closer to the truth" should just mean resembling reality more closely, not sharing more logical content. (4) Priyanka answered that "resembling more" isn't obviously well-defined either, since how similar two models look can depend on which properties or vocabulary you choose to describe them in, and she doesn't think there's a way to fix the vocabulary that isn't arbitrary -- so now "closer to the truth" seems to be relative to a choice of description, which is a strange thing for a supposedly objective feature of a theory to be. (5) Sasha's final shot was that no working physicist actually hesitates over whether Bohr improved on Thomson, in any reasonable vocabulary you'd pick, so if the philosophical machinery makes that ordinary judgment come out murky or language-dependent, it's the machinery that's broken, not the judgment.

Neither of them budged, and honestly both sounded right to me at different points, which is exactly the problem. Could you go through the five moves and say, for each one, who's got the better of the argument and why, rather than just handing down an overall winner? And if there's a way out of this that neither of them hit on, I'd love to hear it too -- I suspect there's alot more to this literature than what a physicist-turned-amateur like me has stumbled onto.
