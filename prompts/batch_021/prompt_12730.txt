So I had this whole argument with my friend last night about ontological arguments. She's taking a logic class and brought up Gödel's version, which is really just Leibniz's old "concept of a most perfect being" argument rebuilt out of modal logic. He treats "positive property" as a primitive, defines something as God-like if it has every positive property there is, shows that being God-like that way is at least possible (not self-contradictory), and then adds an axiom that "necessary existence" itself counts as one of the positive properties -- which is the move that gets him from merely possible to necessarily existing. I said that's obviously the same trick Kant already killed off: existence isn't a real predicate, tacking "exists" onto a concept doesn't add anything to it, a hundred real thalers contain no more coin than a hundred possible ones, Gödel's just laundered it through modal logic so its harder to spot. She said not so fast -- Kant's objection is about adding "exists" to the concept of a particular object, but that's not what Gödel's axiom does, its a claim about which properties get to count as "positive" in the first place, a totally different logical level, so maybe Kant's actual argument doesn't even make contact with it. Neither of us knows Kant's texts well enough to settle this ourselves. So: if you walked Kant through Gödel's actual proof -- the positive-property setup, the possibility step, the necessary-existence axiom -- would his existing objection really reach it, or would he need a new objection tailored to that specific move -- and if so, what would it look like?
