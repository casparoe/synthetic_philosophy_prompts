I spent about thirty-five years as a marine biologist before retiring, and it's only in the last year or two that I've properly gotten hooked on philosophy -- philosophy of religion of all things, despite not being religious myself. It started when my grandson, who's doing a philosophy minor, sent me a paper on the "divine hiddenness" argument and I just couldn't put it down. A few of us retired faculty now run a little amateur podcast out of somebody's basement, nothing fancy, and next month's guest is a philosopher who's built his reputation defending the divine hiddenness argument against theism -- the one that runs, roughly: a perfectly loving God would make sure nobody ends up a "nonresistant" nonbeliever, such nonbelievers plainly exist, so no such God exists.

I listened to a couple of his older interviews to prep, and every time a host asks how we could ever confirm that some particular person's nonbelief is genuinely nonresistant rather then just resistant and self-deceived about it, he falls back on the same move: we don't need to verify every case, a handful of clear ones will do, and the rest follow "by parity." As a scientist that answer bugs me a bit, honestly -- sounds like drawing a sweeping conclusion from a small, hand-picked sample.

Could you draft seven or eight interview questions that don't let him just coast on that move or restate the syllogism? I'd like at least two, asked in sequence, that tighten the screws on the "parity" answer specifically, each with a short note on the reply he'll likely give so I've got a real follow-up ready. I'd also like one question that makes him explain why hiddenness is actually a distinct argument from the old problem of evil rather than the same worry about suffering wearing a new costume, and one that presses on whether the argument, even if sound, only rules out a personal, relationship-seeking God and leaves something like deism or that "epistemic distance" idea (God keeping his distance to protect real freedom) untouched. Save the last question for asking him point-blank what evidence, if any, would actually change his mind -- the philosophical equivalent of a null result -- rather than letting him retreat to the bare argument again.
