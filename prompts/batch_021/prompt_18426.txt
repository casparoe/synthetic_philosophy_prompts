I am drafting a historical novel centered on a fictionalized Nietzsche in the last lucid years before his collapse, and I want a dinner-table scene in which he defends his eating habits to a skeptical guest without the exchange reading like potted biography. The trouble is a tension in his thought that I can't quite resolve on his own terms. In the Genealogy of Morals, Nietzsche treats ascetic self-denial -- suspicion of appetite, the mortification of bodily pleasure -- as a symptom of life turning against itself, ressentiment dressed up as virtue. Yet in Ecce Homo, and in his mocking discussion of Cornaro's diet book in Twilight of the Idols, he prescribes for himself a regimen that is austere and exacting to the point of monasticism, fretting over climate, quantity, and the supposedly ruinous effect of beer on the German mind. Before I write the scene, I'd like an answer that is actually right, not merely convenient for the plot: does Nietzsche have principled grounds, in his own terms, for treating his self-imposed dietary discipline as something other than the ascetic self-denial he diagnoses elsewhere as pathological? I suspect the answer turns on his account of the philosopher's "economy of forces" in the Third Essay, but I'd like to know whether that argument actually closes the gap or merely relocates it.
