After forty years in comparative animal physiology, I have spent my retirement auditing philosophy courses at the university where I once ran a lab, largely for the pleasure of it. This term, the instructor of a philosophy of biology seminar, aware of my background, asked me to help mark the take-home exams. Before I finalize my comments on one answer, I would like a rigorous outside assessment, since my grasp of the secondary literature is still shakier than I would like.

The exam question read: "Explain the etiological and the causal-role (dispositional) accounts of biological function. Apply both accounts to the vertebrate heart, which pumps blood but also produces audible sounds through valve closure. Which account gives the more satisfying treatment of the possibility of malfunction, and why? Illustrate your discussion with at least one further complication from the literature, such as vestigial organs or evolutionarily novel traits."

The student answered:

"There are two classic and rival analyses of what it means to say that a biological trait has a given function.

On the etiological account, first developed by Cummins (1975), a trait T has the function of producing effect E just in case E is the effect that explains why T was selected for and is still present in the population: ancestral hearts that pumped blood effectively out-reproduced those that pumped poorly, and this selective history is exactly what explains why hearts persist today. On this view the heart's function is to pump blood, full stop; the sound produced by valve closure was never itself selected for, so it counts as a mere by-product rather than a function.

On the causal-role account, associated with Wright (1973), a trait's function is whichever of its effects we cite in explaining some capacity of a containing system that we are already interested in understanding. If our target is the circulatory system's capacity to deliver oxygen, we cite the heart's pumping, and that becomes its function in that context; if our target is instead a clinician's capacity to diagnose valve disease by auscultation, we cite the heart's sound production, which then counts as a function too. Which effects count as functions is fixed by our explanatory interests, not by the trait's causal history.

The two accounts pull apart most sharply over malfunction. A heart with a stenotic valve that pumps blood poorly still has the function of pumping blood on the etiological story, because that is what hearts of this type were selected to do; this particular token is simply failing to discharge its function. The causal-role account struggles here, since it defines function by what a trait currently contributes to a capacity of interest, so a token contributing nothing seems to have no function left to fail at, rather than a function it is failing to perform. This is usually cited as the etiological account's chief selling point.

A further complication is posed by vestigial organs, such as the human appendix. Because the appendix no longer aids digestion the way it did in more herbivorous ancestors, and there is no ongoing selection maintaining any digestive effect, the etiological account has no basis for assigning the appendix a digestive function today, and must simply count it as functionless. The causal-role account fares better here, since it need only point to some capacity the appendix currently contributes to -- for instance, housing beneficial gut microbiota -- and call that its function instead.

So while causal-role analysis is attractive for not depending on speculative reconstructions of evolutionary history, it is the etiological account that earns its place by making sense of malfunction; the trouble it has with organs, like the appendix, that have entirely outlived the selection pressures that built them, is the price of that advantage."
