I spent most of my career running a lab in sensory physiology, working mainly on retinal processing in amphibians, and one of the pleasanter surprises of retirement has been having the time to finally read philosophy of mind properly, rather than skimming it in the margins of neuroscience papers. Lately that has meant working through the literature on teleosemantics: the project of explaining what makes a mental or neural state about something in particular -- a fly, say, rather than a shadow -- by grounding that "aboutness" in the state's evolutionary function, rather than positing it as an unexplained primitive. The example that keeps recurring in this literature, from Millikan onward, is essentially the one I used to put up on slides myself: the frog retinal ganglion cells that Lettvin, Maturana, McCulloch and Pitts described back in 1959, informally called "bug detectors," which fire just as readily for a small dark pellet flicked past the animal as for an actual fly.

I belong to a small reading group, mostly current and former graduate students, that works through material like this every couple of weeks, and two of the others, Nadia and Felix, fell into a genuinely substantive disagreement over dinner afterward about whether teleosemantics can give the frog's detector a determinate content at all. (1) Nadia opened by observing that the "selected effects" story only tells us the trait was selected because it did something that improved ancestral fitness, but in the relevant ancestral environment "catching flies," "catching small dark moving objects," "acquiring nutritious prey," and "propagating the relevant genes" were all equally true descriptions of what the trait did that got it selected -- so selection history alone cannot tell us whether the state represents FLY rather than NUTRITIOUS-OBJECT or SMALL-DARK-MOVER, even though these differ in extension (drifting detritus is nutritious without being a fly) -- and hence teleosemantics hasn't solved the problem it was invented for, only relocated the same disjunction problem one level down. (2) Felix answered that this ignores the rest of Millikan's proposal: content is fixed not by whatever merely caused the detector to fire, but by the "Normal explanation" of why the downstream consumer systems -- the orienting and tongue-flick circuitry -- succeed when they act on its output, and that explanation has to cite whichever properties the consumer mechanism is actually built to exploit, such as size, contrast and trajectory, rather than properties like "being nutritious" or "propagating genes" that do no work in explaining the consumer's success -- so the theory does rule out some candidate contents non-arbitrarily. (3) Nadia's rejoinder was that this only relocates the indeterminacy rather than closing it, since the tongue-flick system is itself a selected trait with exactly the same multiplicity of equally true teleological descriptions, so "whatever the consumer needs in order to succeed" picks out a determinate content only if we already know, independently, what counts as success at the relevant grain -- which is exactly what is in dispute. (4) Felix's final move was to concede that some residual indeterminacy is probably ineliminable (the theory likely cannot adjudicate "fly" versus "small dipterous insect"), but to deny that this is a special defect, since our everyday content ascriptions are just as coarse and interest-relative -- nobody worries that the concept "water" is silently indeterminate between water and heavy water -- and since biological content is a theoretical posit serving the explanatory needs of ethology rather than a metaphysically perfect carving of the world, demanding of teleosemantics a fine-grainedness we would never demand of any other functional or scientific kind term is unreasonable.

Neither was willing to concede the point, and I came away genuinely unsure who had the better of the exchange, which, given how new all of this still is to me, may not be saying much. Could you go through the disagreement point by point -- (1) through (4) above -- and say as precisely as you can who is right at each step, rather than only giving an overall winner? I would be especially interested in whether Felix's appeal to the consumer's "Normal explanation" in (2) actually blocks the regress that Nadia presses in (3), or merely relabels it, and in whether (4) really answers (3) or quietly changes the subject from circularity to coarseness. Please also flag anything important that both of them seem to be missing, and close with your overall assessment on its own line, in exactly the form \verdict{Nadia}, \verdict{Felix}, or \verdict{Draw}.
