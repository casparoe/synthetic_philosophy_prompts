Over the past few months I've been part of a small reading group working slowly through classical Islamic philosophy, and we just spent three sessions on the causation material in Al-Ghazālī's Tahāfut al-Falāsifa -- the seventeenth discussion, in which he draws on the Ash'arite commitment to unconstrained divine power to attack the philosophers' claim that fire necessarily burns cotton whenever the two are brought into contact. I wrote my undergraduate thesis on Hume's Enquiry a few years ago, so the resemblance jumped out at me immediately: both writers seem to run the same core move, insisting that a "necessary connection" between cause and effect is never actually given to us, and that the real basis of the link we do observe is habitual or "customary" association rather than logical necessity. I've started drafting a short piece for the group arguing that Ghazālī essentially discovered Hume's problem of causation some six centuries early, using nearly identical reasoning, and that standard histories of philosophy have been oddly slow to credit him for it.

Before I bring the piece to the group, I'd like to stress-test the framing on you. Since Ghazālī and Hume apparently reach the very same conclusion -- that there is no necessary tie between cause and effect, only custom -- by more or less the same route, I can't work out why they end up doing such opposite work with it: Hume's version is what lets him chip away at design arguments and miracle reports, while Ghazālī's version is deployed to defend the philosophical possibility of miracles against the Aristotelians. Is this simply a case of two thinkers not quite following their own argument through to its natural conclusion, or is there some way of squaring "the same" skeptical argument about causal necessity doing such divergent theological work in their respective hands?
