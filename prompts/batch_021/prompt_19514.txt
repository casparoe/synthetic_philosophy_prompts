I've been falling down a total rabbit hole this week for a piece I'm reporting on science denialism, and I keep getting stuck on this thing epistemologists call the "dogmatism paradox" (I think it goes back to a Kripke seminar that Gilbert Harman wrote up sometime in the 70s, though I'm fuzzy on the details). As best I can reconstruct the argument: if I actually know that p is true, than I also know in advance that any evidence which might later turn up against p has to be misleading, since p is true and misleading evidence is exactly what apparent counter-evidence to a truth would look like. But if I already know ahead of time that such evidence is gonna be misleading, that seems to license me to just disregard it whenever it shows up, without even bothering to look at it first. That feels like it should be a terrible, closed-minded way to reason, and yet every step getting there looks individually reasonable, which is what makes it a paradox rather than just a bad argument.

What's been nagging me is what this means for actual science reporting. Editors love to say "always hear the other side" and "stay open to new data," but the scientists I interview will happily say things like "we know the MMR vaccine doesn't cause autism" or "we know evolution happened," and the logic above seems to say that once you really know something like that, you're off the hook for even engaging with the next contrarian paper that lands in your inbox. So I guess my question is: what do you think are the most important implications of the dogmatism paradox for how science journalists, and the experts we quote, should actually treat new evidence against claims we consider settled? Is there some principled line between "justified confidence that lets you stop re-litigating everything" and the bad, question-begging dogmatism the paradox describes — or does taking the paradox seriously mean we're stuck picking one of those two uncomfortable horns?
