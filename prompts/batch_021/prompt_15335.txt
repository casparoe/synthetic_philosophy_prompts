I'm a PhD student in epistemology, and this year I'm also serving as the graduate-student representative on my university's Research Integrity Committee — usually a quiet administrative post, but this term it has collided with a seminar I'm taking on assertion and testimony, and I honestly can't tell anymore whether that's clarifying the case in front of us or just making me overthink it. I'd like your help drafting the short memo I'm supposed to circulate before Friday's meeting.

Three years ago, a lab in our psychology department published a preregistered, properly powered (N = 180), single-site study reporting that a brief "consider-the-opposite" writing exercise eliminates overconfidence on interval-estimation tasks; the abstract states, without qualification, that the intervention "eliminates the overconfidence effect." Last year, a fourteen-site adversarial collaboration, designed jointly with the original authors so it couldn't later be dismissed as rigged to fail, ran the same paradigm on a pooled sample of roughly 3,400 participants and found nothing. A subsequent audit turned up no fabrication, no undisclosed exclusions, and no departure from the preregistered analysis plan: by the evidential standards the field actually applied at the time, this was a clean, competently run study that happened to report a false positive. A rival lab has now filed a formal complaint that stops short of alleging fabrication but argues that flatly asserting the effect was "eliminated" — rather than something hedged, like "these results are consistent with elimination of the effect, pending replication" — was itself an epistemically reckless assertion, since single, unreplicated studies of this size and kind are known to fail to replicate a substantial fraction of the time, and the authors were simply never in a position to know what they asserted outright. The original authors respond that this would make almost every strong empirical claim in the discipline improper, since knowledge of a causal effect is essentially never available at the time of publication; if the relevant standard for scientific assertion is justified or reasonable belief given the evidence in hand, rather than knowledge, their claim was entirely proper, and there is nothing here to adjudicate.

Our committee's written policy defines misconduct as fabrication, falsification, or plagiarism, explicitly excludes honest error, and says nothing about overclaiming. I keep suspecting this dispute is really the old question of whether assertion answers to truth, to knowledge, or merely to justified or reasonable belief, dressed up as a research-ethics case: the complainants sound like they want something close to Williamson's knowledge account of assertion, echoing the familiar lottery-proposition worry about flatly asserting well-supported statistical claims, while the authors sound like they want something closer to Lackey's "reasonable to believe" standard. I don't see how to turn that debate into an actual committee decision, though. Could you draft the memo? I need a specific recommendation — dismissal, a finding short of misconduct, referral elsewhere, or something else, together with a time frame for what happens next — plus the reasoning behind it. Whichever side the recommendation doesn't favor, I'd like a genuine, substantive engagement with the strongest version of their position, rather than a quick dismissal of it as a misunderstanding of how science works. If a general standard for handling similar cases in the future falls out of the reasoning, that would be useful too.
