The question was: "Explain Bradley's regress and the role it plays in his argument against the reality of relations. Then explain how Russell tried to block the regress in his account of the unity of the proposition in the Principles of Mathematics, and say whether you think his solution actually works."

And here's the student's answer, word for word:

"Bradley's regress arises once we try to explain how a relation connects two terms. Suppose a relation R relates a and b. If R is itself just another entity, then it seems like we need some further relation R1 to tie R to a and to b; but then R1 itself needs relating to R, a, and b by a further relation R2, and so on forever. Since this series never terminates, Bradley concludes that relations, and the whole idea of things standing in relations to one another, cannot be ultimately real. For Bradley this is part of a larger argument for monism: only the Absolute, a single undifferentiated whole, is fully real, and our ordinary relational, subject-predicate way of describing the world is just a useful appearance that falls apart under analysis.

Russell, writing partly in response to Bradley, wanted to preserve the reality of relations and explain how a sentence like "A differs from B" expresses one unified proposition rather than a mere list of three items (A, difference, B). His solution was to say that relations are unsaturated: a relation like "differs" has a gap in it that gets filled by its terms, so that when it actually relates A and B it doesn't need a further relation to glue it to them, and the regress never gets started. This distinguishes a relation actually relating its terms from a relation merely being mentioned as a term in its own right, as in "difference is a relation." Russell felt this fully disposed of Bradley's regress, and it became the standard analytic answer to the unity problem, which is why he didn't return to the issue again after Principles of Mathematics and instead moved on to work on the logic of mathematics."
