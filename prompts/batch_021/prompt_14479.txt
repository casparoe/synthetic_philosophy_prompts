I'm a retired field biologist (spent about thirty years studying corvids for a university field station) and since retiring I've been going to a little philosophy discussion group at the library -- it's turned into a proper hobby. Last month we did Descartes on animal minds, and two of the regulars, Walt and Corinne, got into a real back-and-forth while the rest of us just watched.

(1) Walt opened by saying the whole "animals are just unthinking machines" idea is dead on arrival once you know about Betty the crow, who spontaneously bent a straight wire into a hook to fish a bucket out of a tube, or Alex the parrot, who combined his labels in ways he was never specifically drilled on -- that's not a fixed mechanical response, thats reasoning. (2) Corinne said that's not the bar Descartes actually set in the Discourse: his point was that reason is a "universal instrument" good for any circumstance whatsoever, while any organ or device, organic or man-made, is only built for particular jobs -- and he was happy to grant that specific animals or machines could outdo us at specific tasks. One clever hook, or one clever label-swap, doesn't show a general reasoning faculty; it shows a very good dedicated one. (3) Walt shot back that this makes her test unfalsifiable: a crow bending wire and a parrot labelling colours in English are both doing things no crow or parrot ever evolved to do, so if that still doesn't count, what would? (4) Corinne then pivoted to the pain question, saying it's a myth that Descartes thought animals feel literally nothing -- in one of his letters he grants they have the bodily organs to produce sensation-like states, he just denies these come attached to "thought," meaning a conscious subject whose actually having them. (5) Walt's comeback was that this distinction did zero practical work historically, since it's supposedly the exact reasoning later Cartesians used to justify vivisecting animals without anaesthetic.

Nobody in the group could agree who won. Could you referee it move by move -- who's got the better of each point, and is there anything about Descartes' actual view that both of them are missing? A score from -2 (way off) to +2 (dead on) per move would be great if that's quicker than prose, then finish with one line on who won overall.
