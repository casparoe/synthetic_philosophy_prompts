I'm taking an upper-level seminar in epistemology this term, and last week's unit was on higher-order evidence and what's called "epistemic akrasia" — roughly, believing some claim while also believing that your evidence doesn't actually support that belief, which is supposed to be the epistemic analogue of the practical akrasia involved in doing what you believe you shouldn't do. (The reading for that unit includes Elga, Christensen, and Horowitz, among others.) To make the puzzle concrete, our professor described a case along these lines: you're working through a tricky formal derivation on a timed logic exam, checking each step against the handout of permitted inference rules, and by the end you're confident every line follows validly from the last. Right as you finish, the proctor announces that a printing error caused 40% of the class, chosen at random, to receive a handout in which one inference rule had been subtly and undetectably altered; using the altered rule feels, from the inside, exactly like using the real one, so nothing about how the derivation looks to you can tell you which handout you actually got.

After class, two people from my discussion group, Ines and Marcus, ended up arguing about this over coffee, and I sat there for the whole thing without taking a side. (1) Ines said the rational response is to keep full confidence that her derivation is valid — she'd checked every line and it still held up on reflection — while separately lowering her confidence in the claim "I am currently in a good position to trust my own derivation," since these are two different propositions and nothing requires her credence in one to move in lockstep with her credence in the other. (2) Marcus said that combination is exactly what "epistemic akrasia" means, and pointing out that the two claims are about different things doesn't save it: it's structurally the same as believing it's raining while believing you have no good reason to think it's raining, and no rational agent can stably occupy that combination just by insisting the two beliefs live at different "levels." (3) Ines replied that full deference has an absurd cost of its own: if merely knowing that some fixed, non-negligible fraction of people are stuck in an error state they can't detect from the inside is enough to force you to lower confidence in the specific claim itself, then nothing anyone ever reasons their way to — including elementary arithmetic — could survive contact with the plain fact that people are fallible in ways they can't detect; since nobody actually accepts that much skepticism, there has to be some room to hold the line at the object level while registering the risk somewhere else. (4) Marcus answered that this slippery-slope worry proves too little: knowing in the abstract that people are fallible in undetectable ways is one thing, but having a specific, known base rate attached to this particular derivation is another, and it's exactly the second kind of information that's supposed to move you. If Ines genuinely updates on a known 40% corruption rate, he said, that update has to land on her confidence in the derivation's validity itself, not hover next to it in some separate, quarantined belief; keeping her first-order credence completely untouched while merely "flagging" doubt one level up just lets her enjoy the doubt without ever paying for it.
