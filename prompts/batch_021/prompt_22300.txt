For most of my career as a statistical physicist I made routine use of E. T. Jaynes' maximum-entropy methods without ever troubling myself much over their philosophical foundations. Since retiring I have joined a small philosophy reading group, and working back through Jaynes' *Probability Theory: The Logic of Science* alongside the secondary literature it has led me to, I have gotten stuck on his treatment of Bertrand's chord paradox. As I understand it, Bertrand observed that three seemingly equally natural procedures for choosing a "random" chord of a circle — picking two endpoints independently and uniformly on the circumference, picking a random radius and then a uniformly distributed point along it, and picking a uniformly distributed point within the disc to serve as the chord's midpoint — yield three different probabilities (1/3, 1/2, and 1/4, respectively) that the chord will be longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle. This is standardly presented as showing that the principle of indifference breaks down once the space of possibilities is continuous, since what counts as "equally likely" outcomes shifts with the choice of parametrization.

Jaynes' response, in his 1973 paper "The Well-Posed Problem," was to insist that Bertrand's question is not a counterexample to the principle at all, only an incompletely specified physical problem. He imagined a concrete randomizing procedure — a shower of straws falling onto a small circle scratched on the ground — and required the resulting distribution to be invariant under exactly the transformations (translation, rotation, rescaling) that leave the stated problem's description unchanged; this, he claimed, singles out a unique distribution, matching the "random radius" method, which he reports having confirmed by actually tossing straws. This has apparently not convinced everyone. Van Fraassen, if I have understood the secondary literature correctly, pointed to von Mises' older water-and-wine problem — where indifference over a mixing ratio and indifference over its reciprocal pull toward incompatible conclusions — as a structurally similar case in which no comparable symmetry group is available to do Jaynes' rescuing work, suggesting that his solution is an artifact of the particular geometry of Bertrand's example rather than a general vindication of indifference reasoning.

What I would like from you is an essay that adjudicates this dispute rather than simply reporting it. Does Jaynes' transformation-group argument succeed in rehabilitating the principle of indifference, at least wherever a determinate symmetry group happens to be available, or does the unavailability (or outright ambiguity) of any such group in so many other applications of indifference show that Bertrand's paradox exposes a defect in the principle that Jaynes never actually repaired? Please set out the strongest form of each position before committing to a definite verdict — a qualified conclusion is perfectly acceptable, but I would be disappointed by an essay that simply concludes that reasonable people can disagree.
