Here's the actual question from the worksheet: "Using Russell's theory of descriptions, (a) give the logical analysis of 'The present King of France is bald' and explain why Russell says this makes the sentence false rather than meaningless. (b) The sentence 'The present King of France is not bald' is scope-ambiguous — spell out both readings, say which one (if either) is true, and explain what this shows about the Law of Excluded Middle. (c) In a few sentences, explain how Strawson's response to definite descriptions differs from Russell's."

And here's what Priya wrote: "Russell's theory of descriptions says that definite descriptions like 'the present King of France' aren't really referring expressions or names — they're 'incomplete symbols' that only get meaning from the whole sentence once you analyse them using quantifiers. So 'The present King of France is bald' becomes: there is an x such that x is presently King of France, and for anything y, if y is presently King of France then y=x, and x is bald — ∃x(Kx & ∀y(Ky→y=x) & Bx). Since nothing satisfies 'is presently King of France,' the existential is false, so the whole sentence is false. This is nice because it avoids Meinong's view, where 'the present King of France' would have to denote some kind of subsistent non-existent object just so the sentence has something to be about, which Russell thought led to violations of the law of contradiction (like the existent round square). For the negative sentence, there's a scope ambiguity depending on whether the description has primary or secondary occurrence. Primary occurrence gives: there's a unique King of France, and he's not bald — ∃x(Kx & ∀y(Ky→y=x) & ¬Bx). Secondary occurrence puts the negation on the outside instead: ¬∃x(Kx & ∀y(Ky→y=x) & Bx). But either way, since there's still no King of France, both versions end up false — there's no king to be bald or not bald — so neither reading actually comes out true, which shows the law of excluded middle doesn't strictly apply here unless you're careful about scope. Strawson's response is different: he thought Russell mixed up sentences with the act of using a sentence to make a statement, and that descriptions are used to refer rather than being disguised existence-claims, so 'the F is G' presupposes there's a unique F instead of asserting it. Since that presupposition fails here, Strawson concluded the sentence doesn't succeed in saying anything at all, so it's meaningless — unlike Russell, who at least gives it a determinate truth value."
