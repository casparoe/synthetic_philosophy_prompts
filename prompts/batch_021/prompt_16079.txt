So I've been going down a rabbit hole on wild animal suffering lately (got into it through some effective altruism stuff), and there's an argument that keeps bugging me and I can't decide if it's actually solid or just an internet gotcha.

The basic argument: most wild animals are r-selected, meaning they have huge numbers of offspring and almost all of them die pretty much right after birth, usually from starving, disease, or getting eaten, so if you take their capacity to suffer seriously, most wild animal lives are net-negative and nature overall contains way more suffering than happiness. Ok but if thats true, doesnt it follow that stuff like clear-cutting a forest or draining a wetland, which kills off or prevents huge numbers of these short brutal r-selected lives, should count as a GOOD thing under a straightforward suffering-focused utilitarian view? fewer of those lives = less total suffering. most wild animal suffering advocates i've read try to dodge this by saying we should only do "careful," "research-based" intervention instead of destruction, but i dont see why the actual logic of their own argument doesnt just commit them to cheering on habitat loss.

Can you build the strongest, most rigorous version of this bite-the-bullet case for treating habitat destruction as a net positive, actually engaging with the mechanics of the r/K-selection argument instead of just gesturing at "nature is brutal"? i want to know if this is a genuine weak spot in the wild animal suffering framework or if theres some move that blocks the inference that im just not seeing.
