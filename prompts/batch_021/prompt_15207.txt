I sit on the ethics and professional-conduct committee at the accounting firm where I work, and a topic I'd always considered pretty academic became unexpectedly pressing a few weeks ago. As part of our continuing-education requirements for relicensing, the committee had to complete a module on "behavioral approaches to professional integrity" that leaned heavily on the situationist critique of virtue ethics — the argument, associated with writers like John Doris and Gilbert Harman, that classic social-psychology findings undercut the idea that people have the kind of stable character traits (honesty, courage, and so on) that virtue-based hiring and training programs presuppose. The instructor's punchline was that the firm should stop trying to screen candidates for "integrity" in interviews and instead redesign workflows so that misconduct is hard to commit regardless of who is doing the work. Afterward, two colleagues on the committee, Priya and Daniel, kept arguing about it over coffee long after everyone else had moved on, and I've been unable to decide who came out ahead.

(1) Daniel defended the module's premise: in Milgram's original obedience study, roughly two-thirds of ordinary subjects delivered what they believed were dangerous shocks to another person simply because a man in a lab coat told them to continue, and no personality measure the researchers tried could reliably predict in advance who would comply; likewise, in Darley and Batson's well-known study of Princeton Theological Seminary students, whether someone stopped to help a man slumped in a doorway depended almost entirely on how late they'd been told they were, not on how religious they were or whether they'd just prepared a talk on the parable of the Good Samaritan. If situational pressure swamps whatever we call "character" even among people picked for their moral seriousness, Daniel argued, then interview questions like "tell me about a time you faced an ethical dilemma" are theater, and hiring for integrity is a waste of the firm's money. (2) Priya answered that this over-reads a familiar pattern: the low correlations situationists cite, going back to Hartshorne and May's old studies of children's honesty, are correlations between one specific act and another specific act, and Seymour Epstein's aggregation work showed that traits which look statistically negligible at the level of a single behavior become far more reliable predictors once a person's conduct is averaged across many occasions — much as one item on a personality inventory predicts almost nothing by itself, while the full scale predicts quite a lot. So the evidence undercuts any single interview question as a character test, she said, but not the reality of character itself; if anything it argues for weighting a candidate's track record over how they perform in an interview. (3) Daniel granted that an aggregate trait might be real but said that was irrelevant to hiring, which is a bet on how one specific candidate will behave under one specific kind of pressure — a partner leaning on them to backdate a document — and that is exactly the single-act prediction situationist data says trait measures are worst at; a tendency only visible across a hundred occasions can't inform a decision made before you have anything like that sample. (4) Priya replied that the same complaint boomerangs onto Daniel's own fix: in Milgram's data, roughly a third of participants, facing the identical experimenter, script, and incremental commitment as everyone else, still refused to go to the end, and since the situation was held fixed, "the situation" cannot be what explains that split; whatever does explain it looks exactly like the kind of stable individual difference Daniel wants to write off, which means the residual he needs in order to justify situational controls is the very terrain character-talk was always meant to occupy.

Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who has the better of each specific point, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also like to know whether their disagreement ultimately turns on some deeper issue in the situationism/virtue-ethics debate that neither of them names outright. Please close with a one-line verdict in the form \verdict{Daniel}, \verdict{Priya}, or \verdict{Draw}, for whoever you think has the stronger overall position.
