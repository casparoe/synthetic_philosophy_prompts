My father spent forty years playing blackjack and poker for a living, and he used to say that in all that time he never once gambled. What he meant was that every bet he placed was sized according to what he called the Kelly criterion: people who talk about a bet's "expected value," he'd tell me, are imagining a thousand versions of themselves splitting up the outcomes between them, when in truth there is only one of you, walking a single path, and one catastrophic night can end that walk for good — so the only sane aim is to make your stake grow as fast as possible without ever risking a loss you can't come back from. He died last week, leaving a note asking that whoever spoke at his funeral not describe him as merely "cautious" or "risk-averse," since he insisted to the end that this was never what he had been doing. I'm the one giving the eulogy, and I don't have his gift for the mathematics. Half the room will be the old crew from his card games, who will know exactly what he meant by all that; the other half is family, several of whom spent decades quietly deciding he was too tight-fisted to ever lend money on a "sure thing." Please write the eulogy so that his lifelong discipline sounds like the deep wisdom it actually was to everyone in the room, without turning into a math lecture, and without breaking his one request.
