I'm a PhD student in political theory finishing a seminar paper on medieval resistance theory for a course on the history of political thought, and before sending the next draft to my advisor I'd like a second opinion on the central interpretive move. The paper tracks Aquinas's several discussions of tyranny and its remedies: the treatment of obedience to unjust rulers in Book II, distinction 44 of his commentary on the Sentences, where a ruler who has simply usurped power without any title at all is treated as someone who may be resisted the way one resists a robber, rather than as someone whose overthrow would count as sedition; the much more cautious practical counsel of De Regimine Principum, which catalogues the historical dangers of moving against a tyrant — worse successors, cycles of vengeance — and steers instead toward institutional remedies, such as deposition by whatever authority instituted the ruler, appeal to a higher power where one exists, and prayer as a last resort; and the later, more abstract treatment in the Summa's question on sedition (IIaIIae, q. 42), where he argues that overturning a tyranny is not itself seditious, since such rule was never really ordered to the common good, without specifying who exactly is licensed to do the overturning.

My thesis is that these texts are not actually in tension on the point usually attributed to them — namely, whether a private individual may kill a tyrant on his own initiative — because none of the three, read carefully, actually licenses that; what looks like a retreat from a permissive early view to a cautious later one is better described as a shift from a juridical question (is an act of this kind, described in the abstract, sinful?) to a prudential one (what should an actual political community do, given the risks of acting?). I hold this with some hesitation, though, since the Sentences passage is very often cited — including, I suspect, by people who should know better — as evidence that the young Aquinas straightforwardly endorsed tyrannicide by any private citizen, and I worry that I am reading a "public authority" qualification into that text more heavily than it can bear, simply because I want all three texts to cohere. I'd appreciate comments on three things in particular: whether my reconstruction of the Sentences passage is textually defensible or whether I am smoothing over a genuine change of mind; whether there is a cleaner, more standard resolution of this tension already on offer in the secondary literature that I am about to reinvent badly; and whether the juridical/prudential distinction I am leaning on is doing real philosophical work, or is merely a label for the fact that Aquinas sounds more careful whenever he isn't speaking hypothetically.
