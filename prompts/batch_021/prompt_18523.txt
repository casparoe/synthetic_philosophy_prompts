I'm taking a seminar this term devoted to Bertrand Russell, and we've just spent two sessions on Marriage and Morals (1929). What I keep getting stuck on is how far Russell's own framework can be stretched to cover relationship forms he never lived to see. His central move in the book is to separate the purposes for which people unite — sexual pleasure alone, companionship, and the rearing of children — and to argue that only the last of these actually warrants social or legal regulation; a partnership without children, he says flatly, "should be regarded as a purely private affair." This claim sits alongside his running argument, developed in the earlier chapters, that jealousy is not a fixed instinct but a habit installed by patriarchal anxiety about paternity and correctable through a more rational upbringing — which is also the spirit behind his engagement with, and claim to go further than, Judge Ben Lindsey's contemporaneous proposal for "companionate marriage," via Russell's own related notion of trial marriage. All of this sits uneasily next to the book's much less comfortable chapter on eugenics, where the sexual freedom Russell is describing turns out to be the prerogative of a rational, self-controlled, and disproportionately well-off minority, rather than something he thought the whole population was equally equipped to exercise responsibly.

None of the specific vocabulary of today's polyamory movement — "compersion" for the cultivated positive counterpart to jealousy, "metamour" for a partner's other partner, non-monogamy increasingly framed as closer to an orientation than a deliberated choice — existed until intentional communities coined it well after Russell's death. For a short response paper, I want to defend a specific claim about whether Russell would recognize contemporary polyamory as basically the fulfillment of his own argument, or whether his own premises — about whose sexual freedom counts as earned, about how jealousy is actually supposed to be overcome, and about what, if anything, a childless partnership still owes to the people around it — would commit him to genuine reservations about it. Could you help me work out where the more defensible answer lands? I would rather not be told he was simply "ahead of his time"; I want at least one point of real friction that follows from his actual arguments in the book, and not merely from his general reputation as a provocateur.
