I spent about thirty-five years as a clinical epidemiologist before retiring and getting hooked on philosophy, so an argument at a dinner party last week has been stuck in my head. Two friends of mine, Nadia (a public defender) and Ben (who builds risk-assessment software that some courts use for bail decisions), got into it over whether Ben's kind of tool is "biased."

(1) Ben said the tool is fair because its well calibrated: among defendants given the same risk score, Black and white defendants go on to reoffend at essentially the same rate. Same score, same meaning, regardless of race.

(2) Nadia said forget calibration, look at who the errors fall on. Among defendants who did NOT reoffend, Black defendants were rated high-risk far more often than white defendants who also didn't reoffend. So the tool's mistakes land disproportionately on innocent people, and that's the bias that actually matters if you're the one sitting in jail.

(3) Ben answered that this gap isn't a design flaw, it's just an automatic consequence of the two groups having different underlying reoffense rates in the data -- you literally can't equalize the error rates without breaking calibration, so nobody actually chose this.

(4) Nadia shot back that "nobody chose it" is a dodge: somebody chose to build a tool that prioritizes calibration over equal error rates instead of the other way round, and that choice has a victim, math or no math.

They dropped it there. Since I'm used to rating things on a scale from my old line of work, could you score each of those four moves from -2 (clearly wrong) to +2 (clearly right) and explain your reasoning? And separately -- I have a nagging feeling there's an actual theorem lurking behind this kind of standoff, something that makes part of it unavoidable rather than a matter of opinion. If so, what exactly does it establish, and what does it leave as a genuine value judgement the two of them still have to make?
