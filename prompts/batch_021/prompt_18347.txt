The question on the exam was:

"Explain how Rawls distinguishes civil disobedience from what he calls 'conscientious refusal,' and explain what, on his view, grounds the ordinary citizen's duty to comply with the law of a nearly-just society even when a particular statute is itself unjust. Then explain one distinction Dworkin draws, in his discussion of civil disobedience in *Taking Rights Seriously*, that cuts across Rawls's own categories, illustrating it with a historical example of your choosing."

And here, in full, is the student's answer:

"Rawls defines civil disobedience as a public, nonviolent, conscientious breach of law, undertaken for political reasons and addressed to the community's shared sense of justice, typically with the aim of getting an unjust law or policy changed. Because it appeals to fellow citizens rather than rejecting the constitutional order altogether, the disobedient accepts the legal penalty rather than evading it — this is what Rawls means by acting within the limits of fidelity to law. Conscientious refusal, by contrast, is simply non-compliance with a specific legal demand, such as conscription, on personal moral or religious grounds. It need not be public, need not appeal to anyone's sense of justice, and need not even aim at changing the law: a pacifist who refuses to fight is not thereby trying to persuade the majority of anything.

Rawls's whole discussion presupposes a society that is already 'nearly just'; he is not offering a general theory of resistance to tyranny. Within such a society, the ordinary citizen's duty to obey even a law she believes to be unjust is, on his view, an obligation grounded in the principle of fairness: having accepted the benefits of a cooperative scheme that is by and large just, she owes it to her fellow citizens to bear her share of its burdens, including compliance with laws she happens to dislike.

Dworkin's discussion of civil disobedience in *Taking Rights Seriously* adds a distinction Rawls's framework does not foreground: between disobedience grounded in one's own moral complicity (refusing to fight in a war one considers murderous), disobedience grounded in the claim that a law or policy is unjust to some other group, and disobedience grounded merely in disagreement about a policy's wisdom, where no fundamental right is at stake. King's Letter from Birmingham Jail is really an argument of the second kind: King insists that an unjust law is 'no law at all,' a line he draws from Aquinas, before going on, in a thoroughly Rawlsian spirit, to accept arrest rather than evade it."
