In my school's philosophy club, one meeting started with a member bringing in the old Ship of Theseus riddle, but it turned into something more precise once our faculty advisor introduced the actual metaphysical disagreement that riddle is usually used to illustrate: endurantism versus perdurantism. As she explained it, endurantists hold that an object persists by being wholly present at every moment it exists, so that "I" am supposed to be fully here today and was fully here as a six-year-old. Perdurantists, by contrast, hold that persisting objects are really four-dimensional wholes composed of distinct temporal parts, so that "me at six" and "me now" are just different slices of one long space-time worm — no more strictly identical than my left hand and my right foot are identical, even though both belong to me. She brought up David Lewis's problem of temporary intrinsics to motivate the perdurantist side: if a single, wholly-present thing can be bent at one time and perfectly straight at another, how can one object really have two flatly incompatible shapes, unless those shapes belong to two different parts of it rather than to one persisting whole?

I can't stop turning this over, because it seems to have real consequences for how we think about memory, love, and responsibility for who we used to be, even though I can't quite picture what it would actually feel like to live as if perdurantism were true. Could you write me a short story that puts a character concretely in that position? I'd rather you avoid the ship of Theseus or a teleporter scenario, since those are the two examples we already covered in club — please invent your own premise from scratch (a technology, legal system, ritual, or relationship, for instance) that forces someone to confront themselves, or someone they love, explicitly as a series of temporal parts rather than as one continuous person. I'd like the ending to leave the underlying metaphysical question genuinely open rather than quietly settling it.
