Among the arguments G. E. Moore uses against hedonism in Principia Ethica is a thought experiment in which we are asked to compare two worlds, one exquisitely beautiful and the other as hideous as can be imagined, stipulating that neither will ever be seen, felt, or otherwise experienced by any being whatsoever. He concludes that the beautiful world is nonetheless the better of the two, so that beauty retains some value even in total isolation from any mind — a premise he later builds on to argue that personal affection and the appreciation of beauty together comprise the greatest goods we can conceive. As a novelist, I keep returning to the isolated-worlds case while thinking about manuscripts that never reach a reader at all: Hopkins's poems sitting unpublished for decades, Dickinson's hoard of verse discovered only after her death, Kafka's instruction that his unfinished novels be burned unread. Please argue against Moore's verdict on the isolated worlds: make the strongest case you can that beauty which neither has been nor ever could be perceived by anyone contributes nothing to the value of the world, so that whatever made the silenced work of such writers matter cannot have been a beauty existing independently of any reader.
