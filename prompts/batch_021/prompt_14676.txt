A friend of mine from graduate school and I spent most of a long evening last week arguing about David Lewis's account of convention — specifically, about how far his apparatus of coordination equilibria and common knowledge really extends into the informal rules that govern daily life, as opposed to those rules being held in place by something his framework was never meant to cover, such as sanction, habit, or authority. We traded examples back and forth for hours, from canonical ones like which side of the road to drive on to smaller, invented ones from our own department, and never quite settled where the line falls. I'm now a TA for the undergraduate seminar that covers exactly this material, and one of the homework questions asks students to perform precisely this kind of adjudication on a single case. Having just had that argument, I'd rather it not color my grading in either direction, so before I enter a mark I'd like an independent, careful read of the answer below.

The question, as assigned, read: "In Convention (1969) and 'Languages and Language' (1975), David Lewis restricts his analysis to situations of coordination: recurring situations in which each party's best action depends on what others do, and in which there is more than one coordination equilibrium — a combination of actions such that no one could have done better by unilaterally acting otherwise — on which the parties are all roughly equally happy to settle. On his account, a regularity R counts as a convention in a population just when, informally stated, the following all hold: (i) almost everyone conforms to R; (ii) almost everyone expects almost everyone else to conform to R; (iii) general conformity to R is a coordination equilibrium that almost everyone prefers, given that almost everyone else conforms; (iv) there is some alternative regularity R' that could have served the same purpose just as well; and (v) all of this is common knowledge. Consider the unwritten office rule that whoever empties the coffee pot puts on a new one. Using Lewis's own criteria — not just your intuitive sense of the word 'convention' — assess whether this rule is best understood as a Lewis convention, and explain your reasoning."

Here is the student's answer in full: "Lewis's account explains conventions as arising from coordination problems and persisting because of common knowledge, not because anyone enforces them. The coffee-pot rule fits this picture well. It is clearly arbitrary in Lewis's sense: an equally workable rule could have taken hold instead, such as 'whoever arrives first each morning starts a pot,' and nothing about coffee itself favors one rule over the other. Once the emptier-refills rule became established, each new instance reinforces everyone's expectation that it will be followed again, which is exactly the kind of self-perpetuating salience — what Lewis calls a focal point — that lets a convention survive without anyone needing to enforce it or even remember how it started. Since the rule is arbitrary, commonly known, and self-perpetuating through precedent, it satisfies Lewis's conditions and counts as a genuine convention, structurally on a par with his own example of which side of the road to drive on. It's true that any individual coffee-drinker sometimes feels tempted to slip away without refilling the pot, but this doesn't threaten the analysis: the rule remains common knowledge whether or not any particular person is tempted to break it on a given day, and Lewis's theory is about what is commonly known and expected, not about whether the temptation to deviate exists."
