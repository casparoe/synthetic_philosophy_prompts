I've been down a total rabbit hole this week with a book about the fight between Hobbes and John Wallis over squaring the circle, and it's got me stuck on something that goes beyond just "who did the math right." Quick background in case it helps: in 1655 Hobbes added a chapter to his big treatise De Corpore claiming he'd solved the classical problem of squaring the circle. Wallis, who actually held the professorship of geometry at Oxford, tore the proof apart within about two months in a pamphlet called the Elenchus. Instead of backing down, Hobbes fired back the next year in Six Lessons to the Professors of the Mathematiques, and buried in there is a line thats basically his whole theory of knowledge in miniature: he says geometry and civil philosophy are the only two sciences capable of real certainty, because in both cases we make the objects ourselves -- we draw the lines and figures ourselves, we build the commonwealth by covenant -- whereas physics can never be fully certain since we didn't make nature and can only guess at its hidden causes from the outside. Apparently Wallis argued, and Hobbes seems to have basically agreed, that if his geometry got exposed as worthless the whole system resting on it would go down too, which might be why Hobbes spent the rest of his life refusing to concede a single one of his busted circle-squarings -- the fight literally ran until he died in 1679.

Could you write me a dialogue between Hobbes and Wallis set right after Six Lessons comes out, where they really hash this out? I don't want a rehash of the technical dispute itself (whose arc equals whose line, etc) -- I want them arguing about the bigger question underneath it: whether Hobbes's own terrible track record as a working geometer quietly wrecks his claim that geometry (and by extension civil philosophy) is uniquely certain because we ourselves construct its objects, or whether that claim can survive his very real failures at construction. Feel free to let Hobbes bring in his contempt for Wallis's symbolic algebra as ungrounded and unphilosophical if it helps his case. Just make sure both of them get their strongest moves in -- I don't want Wallis dunking on a strawman, or Hobbes waving off the criticism for no real reason -- they should actually be responding to each other's points.
