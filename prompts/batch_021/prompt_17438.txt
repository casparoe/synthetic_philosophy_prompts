Over a long career in geophysics I relied constantly on Bayesian inversion methods to infer subsurface structure from seismic data, so when a former colleague talked me into joining her philosophy-of-science reading group after we'd both retired, I assumed the material on Bayesian reasoning would be a comfortable fit. Mostly it has been, except for one knot I haven't managed to untie: what Clark Glymour called the "problem of old evidence." The trouble is easy enough to state. If a piece of evidence E is already known with certainty by the time a theory T is proposed, then P(E) = 1 for the relevant agent, and it follows from the probability calculus alone that P(E|T) = 1 as well, so conditionalizing on E cannot raise one's credence in T by even a fraction. Yet this runs directly against how confirmation is supposed to have worked in the textbook case: Mercury's anomalous perihelion advance had been known for decades before Einstein's field equations were shown to entail it without any adjustment, and this was treated as some of the strongest evidence in favor of general relativity, not as evidentially idle.

The two remedies I've since read about both leave me uneasy. Garber and Jeffrey relocate the genuinely new information away from E itself and onto the previously unrecognized logical fact that T entails E, which requires giving up the assumption that agents are logically omniscient. The alternative - a "counterfactual" strategy that Garber himself raised only to set aside as underdeveloped - asks us instead to evaluate confirmation via the credence the agent would have assigned to T had she not already known E, in effect subtracting E back out of her stock of background knowledge. Howson subsequently worked this idea out and defended it in earnest, while Eells, among others, catalogued a battery of difficulties facing it, including cases where a theory would never have been formulated at all without the evidence, or where the agent's knowledge of that very evidence had once saved her life. What strikes me most, and what I have not seen mentioned in any of the tidier overviews of this debate, is that Howson himself eventually disowned his own proposal, arguing that it covertly imports an "objective" probability distinct from the agent's actual subjective credences, and so was never really available to a genuine subjectivist in the first place.

I would like you to argue for the position that Howson's self-criticism, if correct, does not merely sink the counterfactual strategy but generalizes to the Garber-Jeffrey logical-learning strategy as well - that is, that any way of rescuing Bayesian confirmation theory from the problem of old evidence has to smuggle in some notion of evidential support that outruns anything that can be cashed out in terms of an agent's actual, or even hypothetical, subjective degrees of belief. Work with the strongest form of the Garber-Jeffrey proposal rather than a simplified version, state plainly where exactly you think the smuggling would occur, and say whether this looks to you like a symptom of a limitation the subjectivist was always going to run into, rather than a technical gap that further formal ingenuity might still close.
