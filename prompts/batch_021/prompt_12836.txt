This term I am teaching a graduate seminar on classical Islamic philosophy, and last week we spent a long session on the dispute over causality between al-Ghazālī, in the seventeenth discussion of the Tahāfut al-Falāsifa, and Ibn Rushd (better known in the Latin West as Averroes), in his point-by-point rebuttal, the Tahāfut al-Tahāfut. My students grasped the basic shape of the disagreement well enough: al-Ghazālī denies that fire's contact with cotton necessitates its burning, insisting that the true agent is God, acting according to a fixed custom (al-'āda) rather than any power residing in the fire itself, so that what we call a "causal connection" is really just an unbroken habit God could in principle suspend; Ibn Rushd, defending the philosophers, insists that things have determinate natures which really do necessitate their effects, on pain of making the created world, and any scientific knowledge of it, unintelligible. What we didn't get to before time ran out was watching the two positions actually grapple with one another's strongest objections instead of just restating themselves.

Could you write that missing scene for me: an imagined face-to-face debate between al-Ghazālī and Ibn Rushd over the fire-and-cotton case (imagined, since the two were never contemporaries — al-Ghazālī died some fifteen years before Ibn Rushd was born)? I would like Ibn Rushd to press al-Ghazālī on why, absent any necessity binding fire to burning, cotton reliably burns rather than turning to ice or bread, and on whether an occasionalist world even has a stable enough order for a genuine miracle to count as a deviation from anything. In turn, I would like al-Ghazālī to press Ibn Rushd on what actually licenses the move from "fire and burning have always been observed together" to "fire's nature necessitates burning," given that necessity itself is never among the things we perceive. Let the two of them go back and forth for several exchanges, each one answering what the other has just said rather than repeating an opening statement, and please don't tip the scales so obviously toward either side that there is no real question left for my students to argue about afterward.
