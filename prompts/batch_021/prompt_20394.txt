Write a dialogue, set just after a graduate seminar on the ontological argument, between two philosophers of religion who are both fluent in modal logic and agree that Gödel's ontological argument (in Dana Scott's standard formulation, with its axioms governing positive properties, essence, and necessary existence) is formally valid, yet who draw sharply opposed conclusions from that. The first philosopher treats the result as a genuine, if narrow, victory for theism: each axiom is independently plausible, so a valid argument from plausible premises should move a rational believer toward the conclusion. The second presses the "modal collapse" objection associated with Jordan Howard Sobel: that the very same axioms, run through S5, entail that every actually true proposition is necessarily true, erasing the distinction between the contingent and the necessary and thereby proving far more than any theist should want. Have the first philosopher try either to trace the collapse to a specific axiom or definition that could be revised without sacrificing the conclusion, or to argue that necessitarianism is a bearable cost, and have the second respond to whichever strategy is chosen rather than simply repeating the objection. Both should be equally sharp and engage with the argument's actual logical machinery rather than retreat to generic complaints about existence not being a predicate.
