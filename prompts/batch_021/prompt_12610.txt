I'm working on my EPQ (an independent research essay that runs alongside my A-levels), and I've picked a slightly unusual angle: I want to argue that Henry Sidgwick's decades of involvement in psychical research were not just an eccentric hobby running alongside his career as a utilitarian philosopher, but a direct consequence of a problem he had already uncovered in his own ethical theory. Before I take this section to my supervisor, I'd like an outside opinion. Here is the core of the argument so far:

Sidgwick's Methods of Ethics famously does not end in triumph. In its final chapter, he admits he cannot fully vindicate morality's authority over us, because two principles strike him as equally self-evident to reason: that each person is rationally bound to pursue their own greatest happiness, and that each person is rationally bound to pursue the greatest happiness overall, counting their own good as no more important than anyone else's. Usually these principles recommend the same actions, but not always: there are cases of genuine, uncompensated self-sacrifice where they flatly disagree. Since Sidgwick didn't think reason could simply rank one above the other, he called this the "dualism of practical reason" and treated it as a real fracture in the foundations of ethics, not a loose end to be tidied up in a footnote.

What interests me is his proposed way out. Sidgwick suggested the dualism would dissolve if it turned out, as a matter of fact, that self-interest and duty never truly come apart — for instance, if some moral government of the universe guaranteed that the sacrifices utilitarianism demands are always repaid, in this life or another. Structurally, this is the same move Kant makes with the postulates of God and immortality: reason can't prove them, but morality's coherence seems to need them. The difference is that Sidgwick, who had already given up a Cambridge fellowship over religious doubts, wasn't willing to simply postulate an afterlife for the sake of a tidy theory. He wanted evidence. That, I think, is why he co-founded the Society for Psychical Research in 1882, served as its first president, and pursued mediums and telepathy experiments with a scrupulous, frequently disappointed skepticism rather than a believer's credulity: he needed the world to be a certain way for his ethics to fully make sense, but his own intellectual honesty wouldn't let him assume it.

He died without resolving the question. My closing claim is that this makes the dualism more than a piece of trivia about a philosopher who also happened to like ghosts: by Sidgwick's own lights, the rational authority of morality over naked self-interest was left resting on evidence that never came.

I'd appreciate your honest opinion on two things: first, whether I've represented the philosophy accurately, especially the comparison with Kant, since I've mostly read secondary sources on him; and second, whether that closing claim actually follows, or whether I'm overstating how much of Sidgwick's ethics is genuinely put at risk by a dualism that never got resolved.
