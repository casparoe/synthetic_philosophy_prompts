I'm taking a philosophy seminar on rational choice this term, and an in-class exercise from last week has been nagging at me ever since. Our professor randomly and anonymously paired up all sixteen students in the room — each of us knew only that our partner was someone else currently enrolled in the seminar, nothing more — and every pair simultaneously wrote either "X" or "Y" on an index card, with no communication permitted beforehand or during. The payoffs, drawn from a small demonstration fund she keeps for exercises like this, worked as follows: if both people in a pair wrote "Y," each received $8; if both wrote "X," each received only $2; and if the two choices differed, whoever had written "X" received $12 while whoever had written "Y" received nothing. I wrote "X," reasoning that whatever my anonymous partner ended up writing, I would come out ahead by writing "X" rather than "Y." As it happened, my partner wrote "Y," so I left with $12.

Afterward, while comparing notes, a friend from the same seminar — who had been paired with someone else, written "Y" herself, and ended up with nothing when her partner wrote "X" — argued that my reasoning had a hidden flaw. Her point was that since my partner was drawn from the very same small group of sixteen people who had all just spent ten weeks learning the very same material about how to reason through choices like this one, I had good reason to expect that whatever thought process led me to my decision would lead my partner to the same decision. In effect, she said, I was not choosing an outcome for myself against some fixed but unknown partner choice — I was simultaneously fixing the outcome for both of us — so I should have written "Y," since that is the option that comes out best when both parties in a pair choose it, rather than treating my partner's choice as settled independently of my own reasoning. I find this argument oddly compelling, but something about it also strikes me as too convenient, and I cannot quite pin down whether it holds up or where exactly it would go wrong. I would like to work out my own view before this sort of case comes up again in seminar discussion — is my friend's reasoning actually sound, or is there a mistake in it somewhere?
