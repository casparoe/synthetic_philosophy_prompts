My grandmother taught AP Psychology and ran the philosophy club at my high school for nearly thirty years, and this past fall, her last semester before retiring, I was in both. She was known for one line she made every class repeat: "character is what you do when nobody's grading you" — the center of her case for virtue ethics over everything else we covered. A few weeks before she retired, she taught us Milgram, Zimbardo, and the Darley and Batson seminary-student study, and admitted, unprompted, that she'd never found an answer to the situationist critique that she actually believed. She died last week, before she got to finish that thought with us. I'm giving her eulogy this Saturday, about five minutes long, to a room that's roughly a third former students who will know exactly what I mean, and two-thirds family and neighbors who have never heard the word "situationism." Write the eulogy so that it genuinely works through the tension she left hanging, in a way she'd have recognized as honest, rather than just declaring she was a good woman and moving on — without letting it turn into a psychology lecture at her own funeral.
