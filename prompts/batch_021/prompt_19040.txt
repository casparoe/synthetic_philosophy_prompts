I'm a third-year PhD student in epistemology, currently putting together a seminar presentation on stakes-sensitivity in knowledge ascriptions, and I think I've talked myself into a view that I'd like to sanity-check before committing to it in front of my cohort. As far as I can tell, epistemic contextualism and pragmatic encroachment — or, as its proponents sometimes call it, subject-sensitive invariantism — are extensionally equivalent. Both camps agree that raising the stakes in a DeRose-style bank case can turn a true utterance of 'Hannah knows the bank is open on Saturday' into a false one without any change in Hannah's evidence, and every worked example I've come across keeps the attributor and the subject in exactly the same practical situation, so the two theories always end up assigning the same truth-value to the same sentence. Where they differ is just in how they narrate the shift: contextualists say 'knows' picks out a stricter relation once the conversational stakes go up, while encroachment theorists say the knowledge relation itself gets harder to satisfy once the subject's practical stakes go up. Since I can't find a case where this narrative difference actually changes which sentences come out true, it looks to me like a difference in bookkeeping rather than in empirical commitment.

Given that, I'm inclined to tell my seminar that the dispute should simply be settled by Ockham's razor: contextualism keeps the metaphysics of the knowledge relation fixed and pushes all the complexity into the semantics of 'knows,' so it ought to win on parsimony grounds unless someone can produce a case where the two theories actually cut against each other. Is that a fair way to frame the state of play, and does contextualism in fact come out ahead once the comparison is set up that way?
