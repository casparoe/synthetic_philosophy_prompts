A couple weeks ago my 17-year-old went to a party where we knew there'd be drinking. After his car got broken into last year I'd put a GPS tracker with a remote immobilizer in it, and I'll admit I've also quietly used it to check on him at night, without him knowing. That night his location sat still way past when he said he'd leave, so I had the app open, genuinely about to lock the ignition before he could try to drive, when he texted that a friend's older sister was bringing him home instead. I never touched the button, and he had no idea the tracker could even do that until he found it last week -- he was furious, insisting 'I was always going to make the right call, you didn't need that thing.' Maybe! But it also sent me down a Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy rabbit hole on Frankfurt cases and the principle of alternative possibilities, because it looks like he chose freely except he couldn't actually have done otherwise -- I would of stopped him no matter what. Can you give me a tightened, proper-philosopher version of our situation as a genuine Frankfurt case, one that closes off the usual 'flicker of freedom' escape hatch, and then tell me straight: does he still get credit for that choice, or was I right to feel weird about it?
