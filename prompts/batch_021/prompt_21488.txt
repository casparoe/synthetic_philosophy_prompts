"The lottery paradox and the preface paradox are often introduced as structurally analogous cases in which a set of individually rational beliefs turns out to be jointly inconsistent. Explain both paradoxes, and assess whether they ultimately call for the same diagnosis or whether there is principled reason to treat them differently."

And here, reproduced exactly as submitted, is the student's answer:

"Both paradoxes arise because we ordinarily assume that knowledge is closed under conjunction: if you know P1, P2, ..., Pn, you thereby know their conjunction. In the lottery case, imagine a fair lottery with 1,000 tickets, exactly one of which will win. Since the chance that ticket #1 loses is 999/1000, and likewise for every other ticket, we seem entitled to know, of each ticket, that it will lose. But we also know that some ticket will win, so the conjunction of all our individual beliefs — that every ticket loses — is guaranteed to be false. Kyburg, who first posed the puzzle in 1961, responded to this by concluding that the individual beliefs had to go: since we cannot consistently maintain that every single ticket will lose, the rational response is to withhold belief about any particular ticket and accept only the weaker, disjunctive claim that some ticket will win.

The preface paradox, introduced a few years later by Makinson, has the same basic shape but arises out of ordinary, everyday belief rather than an artificial betting scenario. An author has done her homework on every claim in her book and is individually justified in believing each one, yet she also reasonably believes, on inductive grounds about the fallibility of authors generally, that the book contains at least one error. So she knows P1 through Pn individually and also knows that not all of them are true — another violation of conjunctive closure.

Because the two cases share the same logical structure, I think a single diagnosis covers both: what they really show is that justified belief cannot require certainty, and that rational belief can fall short of deductive certainty without ceasing to be justified. Once this is granted, the appearance of contradiction dissolves in both cases, since we were never entitled to full certainty in the first place. The only real difference between the two is one of vividness: the lottery dramatizes the shortfall from certainty with an explicit numerical probability, while the preface paradox conceals the very same shortfall behind ordinary talk of being 'confident' in one's research."
