I'm a student member of my university's Honor Council, the student-run panel that hears alleged violations of our Academic Integrity Code, and I'd like help thinking through a case coming up for a full hearing next week. The Code defines a violation as "knowingly making a false statement in connection with academic work," and it carries a single sanction -- a two-semester suspension -- for any finding of responsibility, so the Council is generally wary of convicting on a close reading of someone's exact words.

The assignment at issue required a mandatory cover sheet asking students to "describe any assistance you received on this problem set, from any source." The student under review wrote: "Discussed the general approach to Problem 3 with a classmate; otherwise worked from lecture notes." Investigation has since established that the classmate actually worked through a complete solution to Problem 3 with the student line by line, dictating most of the key steps, and that the two submissions are nearly identical on that problem. The student doesn't dispute any of this but maintains that every word on the cover sheet is true, and that nothing on it asserts this was the full extent of the help received -- so whatever a reader concluded about completeness was the reader's own inference, not the student's statement. Roughly half the Council wants to find responsibility on the theory that a disclosure conveying a false impression of the truth is exactly what "false statement" should be read to reach; the other half worries that convicting here effectively punishes the student for what we inferred rather than what was written, which feels like a troubling precedent for a body whose only available sanction is a two-semester suspension.

I'm taking a philosophy of language seminar this term in which we just spent two weeks on Grice -- his distinction between what a speaker says and what she implicates, his account of speaker meaning in terms of communicative intention, and his taxonomy of the ways a conversational maxim can go unfulfilled (violated quietly, flouted openly, opted out of, and so on) -- and a few of us ended up arguing about this case over coffee precisely because it seems to turn on that apparatus, even though our Code never mentions implicature. Could you draft the written opinion I'll circulate to the Council before the hearing? I need a concrete recommendation -- responsible, not responsible, or whatever intermediate finding our Code permits -- with reasoning a skeptical Council member could follow line by line, a genuinely fair treatment of what is right about the student's "I said nothing false" defense rather than a dismissal of it as mere technicality, and, if the cover sheet's own wording strikes you as part of the problem, a brief note on how it might be revised.
