And here's the student's answer, typos and all (not mine, theirs):

"The paradox comes from combining two principles that look totally innocent on their own. Nicod's Criterion says a generalization like 'all ravens are black' is confirmed by its positive instances (something that's both a raven and black), disconfirmed by a counter-instance (a raven that isn't black), and that anything that isn't a raven to begin with is simply irrelevant to the hypothesis either way. The Equivalence Condition says that if a piece of evidence confirms some hypothesis H, it also confirms anything logically equivalent to H, since they say the same thing. 'All ravens are black' is logically equivalent to its contrapositive, 'all non-black things are non-ravens,' so by Nicod's Criterion a non-black non-raven — a white shoe, a green apple — confirms the contrapositive, and then the Equivalence Condition forces us to say the shoe confirms 'all ravens are black' too. Thats the paradox: something sitting in your closet ends up being evidence about birds you've never seen.

Hempel doesn't think this is a flaw in the logic, its a flaw in our intuitions, but the way he gets there is by first tightening Nicod's Criterion into what he calls the satisfaction criterion: evidence confirms a hypothesis just in case it logically entails the hypothesis 'developed' for only the objects that the evidence is about. Once you run the shoe through this sharper version, it actually stops counting as a confirming instance — restricted to just that one object, the development isn't satisfied the way it would be for an actual black raven, so the weird conclusion gets blocked right at the source rather than accepted. What Hempel spends the rest of the paper on is explaining why we ever felt the pull of the paradox in the first place, and his diagnosis is that we're smuggling in background knowledge — we already know there are way more non-black things in the world than ravens, and that extra premise is doing the work of making the shoe feel irrelevant, even though relative to the evidence alone it isn't.

Good's counter-example is a different kind of challenge, and I'd actually locate it as a problem for the Equivalence Condition rather than Nicod's Criterion. He describes a case where background knowledge narrows things down to two rival hypotheses about a bird population, set up so that finding a black raven is actually more likely if a non-uniform rival hypothesis is true than if 'all ravens are black' is true — so the black raven, a paradigm positive instance, ends up counting against the all-black hypothesis instead of for it. That means the same background-relative evidence can end up supporting two logically equivalent formulations of a hypothesis to totally different degrees, which is exactly the kind of mismatch the Equivalence Condition is supposed to rule out. And since Hempel's satisfaction criterion is exactly as purely syntactic as Nicod's — it only ever looks at the logical relation between evidence and hypothesis, never at background probabilities — I don't see why it would be any safer from this than Nicod's original criterion was."
