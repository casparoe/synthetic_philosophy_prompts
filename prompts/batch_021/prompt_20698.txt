Classical Nyāya epistemology treats anumāna (inference) as a valid pramāṇa only when the reason (hetu) stands in vyāpti with the property to be proved (sādhya): an invariable, exceptionless concomitance not vitiated by any hidden upādhi -- a conditioning factor whose presence would reveal the apparent concomitance to be merely incidental rather than genuinely universal. Suppose Gaṅgeśa, the founder of Navya-Nyāya and author of the Tattvacintāmaṇi, were confronted with a form of reasoning his tradition never had occasion to consider: inference from a merely probabilistic regularity, such as a "99.9 percent" forensic DNA match, or a strong but non-universal epidemiological correlation used to infer a causal mechanism. Reasoning strictly from Nyāya's own internal criteria for what disqualifies a hetu, would Gaṅgeśa's framework permit such reasoning to count as genuine anumāna, or is it structurally the sort of case Nyāya would have to treat as vitiated by an upādhi it cannot factor out? Answer by working through Nyāya's own commitments, rather than importing a ready-made modern verdict on statistical inference.
