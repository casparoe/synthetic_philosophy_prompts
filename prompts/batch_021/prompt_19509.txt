I'm a retired immunologist, and since leaving the lab I have been filling my retirement with philosophy of science — Cartwright, Pearl, and that literature on causation — largely for pleasure. Lately, though, it has started to bleed into one of my remaining institutional duties. I still sit, in an emeritus capacity, on the committee at my old teaching hospital that sets the standard clinical protocol for kidney transplant induction therapy, and we are stuck on a decision I now suspect is less a statistics exercise than a genuinely philosophical one about what our data can license us to believe.

Over an eleven-year registry of 800 recipients, split evenly between two induction protocols, the older and cheaper Protocol A shows a one-year graft survival rate of 88.8%, compared with 80.3% for the newer, more resource-intensive Protocol B — on its face, a real argument for keeping the standard as the default. But a committee member then split the same 800 patients by whether induction began within 24 hours of surgery or was delayed beyond it, and within both groups Protocol B comes out at least as good as A: 96% versus 95% among the 400 early starts, 75% versus 70% among the 400 late starts. The two groups are unevenly composed — three-quarters of the early starts were on Protocol A, three-quarters of the late starts were on Protocol B — and half the committee treats this as a textbook Simpson's paradox: stratify, locate the confound, trust the subgroup numbers, and move on. What unsettles me is that timing is not a pre-existing patient trait we merely happened to record: the surgical staff tell us Protocol B needs extra real-time coordination that is easy to defer once a case has already gone badly, and it is that same intraoperative difficulty — independent of which protocol is finally given — that predicts graft failure. So the "confound" the subgroup analysis is supposedly neutralizing may itself be a downstream symptom of the very trouble we are trying to measure, and I am no longer confident that stratifying rescues the truth here rather than laundering a different bias.

Could you draft the short written opinion I will circulate to the committee before we vote on a default protocol next month? I would like one concrete recommendation — adopt A, adopt B, or an explicit interim default while a specified further kind of data is collected, with a time frame stated either way — argued in language a transplant surgeon with no statistical training could follow, and I would like it to take the case for trusting the pooled figures of 88.8% versus 80.3% seriously on its own terms, rather than dismissing it as the error any first course in statistics would catch, since my reading has left me genuinely unsure that it is one.
