(2) Marisol said that's an overreach: the theorem is a conditional, and what the experiments actually rule out is a whole package of assumptions holding together at once -- locality, hidden variables that fix outcomes in advance, and "measurement independence" (the idea that those hidden variables aren't secretly correlated with which setting you'll choose to measure) -- not locality on its own. You can keep locality, she said, if you're willing to give up measurement independence instead (superdeterminism) or give up the idea that a measurement even has one single, definite outcome (many-worlds). (3) Deniz said that's true on paper but a dodge in practice: superdeterminism requires the universe to have rigged, decades in advance, hidden variables so they correlate with random-number generators that didn't exist yet -- a conspiracy theory with better math -- and many-worlds doesn't so much solve the puzzle as change the subject. (4) Marisol said "nobody takes it seriously" isn't quite true or quite the point: Bell himself apparently treated superdeterminism as a logically available exit he simply found distasteful, not one he'd disproven, and there are physicists ('t Hooft, Palmer) still building superdeterministic models today for exactly that reason; and the "changes the subject" line against many-worlds just assumes there's one definite outcome for a nonlocal influence to be about, which is precisely what many-worlds denies. (5) Deniz replied that even granting that, many-worlds still has to explain why the branching structure lines up exactly with the statistics that violate the inequality in the first place, and getting that alignment right is doing the same explanatory work "spooky action" would do, just wearing a different costume.
