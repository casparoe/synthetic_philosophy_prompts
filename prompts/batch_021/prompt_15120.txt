Thomas Hobbes wanted to place moral and political philosophy on the same footing as geometry, which he regarded as the one field of human inquiry that had already achieved genuine certainty. Yet Hobbes also spent much of his later life publishing what he took to be a valid proof that he had squared the circle, together with solutions to related classical problems, and he never really conceded the point even after John Wallis and others repeatedly showed his constructions to be flawed. I am not looking for the familiar anecdote about a stubborn old man being humiliated by better mathematicians; I would like to understand the episode from the inside, in terms of Hobbes's own philosophical commitments. Specifically, I would like to know what Hobbes actually believed about why geometrical demonstration delivers certainty in the first place, and what he found illegitimate about the more symbolic, algebraic methods of proof that Wallis championed. With that background, could you explain why Hobbes's confidence in his own constructions, and his refusal to accept the refutations, would have seemed to him like a natural consequence of his own principles rather than a simple failure of mathematical competence?
