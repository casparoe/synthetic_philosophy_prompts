My aunt died three weeks ago at eighty-three, and I've been asked to give her eulogy. She wasn't a philosopher -- she trained as a nurse -- but as a young woman she read Peter Singer's argument that letting a stranger on the other side of the world die when you could prevent it cheaply is no different, morally, from walking past a child drowning in a shallow pond, and she took it completely literally: modest apartment, no car, most of her income sent abroad for sixty years. She helped raise my cousins while their mother was ill, and they've admitted to me privately that they always understood themselves to matter to her no more than any other two children she might have helped instead. Late in life she found out there's apparently a name among philosophers -- something like 'agent-centered prerogatives' -- for the idea that morality might let a person weigh her own life and the people close to her more heavily than strict impartiality allows, and she told me, almost wistfully, that she had decided never to let herself use it. Tomorrow half the room will want to hear that she was simply a saint; my cousins, sitting in the front row, want someone to finally say out loud whether a good life really has to be this demanding, or whether something went a little wrong in hers. I can't settle that argument at a funeral, and I don't want to paper over it either. Please write the eulogy: a few minutes long, meant to be spoken aloud, that gives both parts of the room something true.
