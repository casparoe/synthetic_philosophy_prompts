A friend of mine, who is finishing a doctorate on theories of personal identity, and I spent most of a dinner last week arguing about what A. J. Ayer's 1988 near-death experience actually did to his lifelong case against survival after death, and neither of us walked away convinced. She thinks the retraction he issued that autumn gives away more than he lets on; I think it gives away almost nothing, once you are careful about which sentence is doing the conceding. Rather than keep going in circles, I wrote up my own attempt at sorting out the dispute, which I have pasted below, and I would like your comments on it: first on whether I have Ayer's actual position right, and then on whether the argument in the closing two paragraphs, which is the part I trust least, actually holds up.

What Exactly Did Ayer Concede?

Ayer's resistance to the idea that we survive our own deaths was never just a mood; it followed from a considered position on personal identity that predates the near-death experience by decades. In The Origins of Pragmatism, his early study of Peirce and James, Ayer had tried to make good on the Humean and Jamesian idea that a person is simply a suitably related series of experiences, and concluded that no one, including himself, had ever managed to specify what that relation could be without eventually falling back on the identity, over time, of the body those experiences belong to. Bodily continuity, on his account, is what licenses us to say that two experiences separated in time belong to one and the same self; remove it and there is no principle left for grouping experiences at all. Since death is precisely what destroys bodily continuity, and no substitute criterion had ever been made to work, Ayer's standing objection to talk of an afterlife was less that there is no evidence for it than that it is unclear what, exactly, is being proposed.

In June 1988, while hospitalised with pneumonia, Ayer choked on a piece of smoked salmon and his heart stopped for four minutes. He later described a vivid experience from that interval: a painfully bright red light he took to be in charge of the universe, two incompetent ministers responsible for the upkeep of space, and his own frantic, unsuccessful attempt to fix a suddenly disordered cosmos using relativistic tricks with time. He wrote the story up for the Sunday Telegraph that August under the title by which it is now generally known, "What I Saw When I Was Dead" — not the Shakespearean one, "That Undiscovered Country," that he had wanted — and closed by remarking that the experience had "slightly weakened" his conviction that death would be the end of him, though he still hoped it would be. That line, not the light or the ministers, is what made the papers.

It is worth noting, too, what the original article did not concede. Ayer had anticipated the obvious inference and blocked it in advance: a stopped heart, he pointed out, does not entail a stopped brain, and given how strong the independent evidence for the brain's role in thought already was, by far the more probable explanation of his experience was that his brain had simply kept working after his heart had not. On the question of whether consciousness needs a functioning brain, in other words, the piece that supposedly shook his convictions was actually rather conservative.

Seven weeks later, in the Spectator, Ayer wrote that the "slightly weakened" sentence had been careless to the point of falsity, and that his conviction that death is final had, if anything, never wavered. What had genuinely changed, he said, was not the belief itself but his attitude toward it: his interest in survival had previously been purely polemical, aimed at exposing the confusions of believers, whereas the experience left him thinking the various possible shapes survival might take were worth mapping out for their own sake, without this raising his estimate of their likelihood even slightly. He then spent several pages doing precisely that, working back through the series-of-experiences theory, the resurrection of the body, and reincarnation, and finding fresh trouble in each.

My own inclination is to think this distinction is more defensible than my friend allows. Treating a hypothesis as worth exploring in detail is not the same as raising one's estimate of its truth: a logician can spend a career charting exactly how a formal system fails without coming to think it more likely to succeed, and Ayer's tour of the possible shapes of survival ends, after all, exactly where his polemics always had, in trouble.

What unsettles me is that Ayer's own explanation for the change in interest was not idle curiosity or dialectical thoroughness but the experience itself — which sounds less like a trigger for disinterested inquiry than like a piece of purported evidence quietly doing some work on him. If that is right, then insisting the belief never moved starts to look less like a clean distinction and more like a redescription, in terms a verificationist can live with, of a small but genuine shift underneath it.

I would be glad for a close, candid reading rather than a general impression: tell me where the reconstruction of Ayer's position, or of the 1988 episode itself, is shaky, and then tell me plainly whether the closing argument survives the complication I have raised against it, or whether my friend had the better of it all along.
