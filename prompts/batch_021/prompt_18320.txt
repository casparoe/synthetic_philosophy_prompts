After a graduate seminar on the semantics of indicative conditionals, two of my students, Elena and Marcus, kept arguing in the hallway about what Lewis's triviality result actually establishes.

The dispute started from Adams' Thesis, often just called "the Equation": for propositions A and C with P(A) > 0, P(if A, then C) = P(C | A). (1) Marcus opened by saying the result is routinely oversold: it only shows that the Equation cannot hold as a general law binding every rational credence function at once. Nothing, he said, stops a single agent, at a single moment, from setting her credence in one particular conditional "if A, then C" equal to her own P(C | A) for the A and C she actually has in mind. (2) Elena replied that this understates the result considerably: the standard proof needs nothing more than one function P together with the two functions you get by conditionalizing P on C and on not-C. So the proof already rules out a lone agent maintaining the Equation across just those two updates -- no other agents' credences, and no appeal to "every" function, ever have to enter the picture. (3) Marcus came back that this just relocates the crucial premise: the proof only bites if we also insist that the Equation must survive conditionalization -- that whatever link holds between "if A, then C" and P(C | A) now must still hold once the agent learns C or learns not-C. Reject that bridging requirement, he said, and a single, frozen assignment of credence to the conditional is untouched. (4) Elena's rejoinder was that giving up the bridge is no free move: the whole point of the Equation was to explain why confidence in a conditional tracks conditional confidence in the consequent as evidence comes in. An Equation guaranteed to hold only at one instant, with no constraint on what happens the moment anything is learned, no longer does the explanatory work Adams wanted from it.
