During my working life I was an experimental physicist studying relaxation phenomena in disordered solids, and a good deal of that work involved thinking carefully about stochastic processes without ever pausing to ask what probability itself actually is. Since retiring I have tried to make up for that omission by reading through the philosophical literature on objective chance, and I have spent the last few weeks with David Lewis's papers on the subject. I believe I have a reasonably secure grasp of the Principal Principle in its original 1980 formulation: that a rational agent's credence in some outcome, conditional on learning the objective chance of that outcome together with whatever else is "admissible" at the time, should simply equal that chance.

What has brought me to a halt is a difficulty that Lewis raised against this very view -- one he rather memorably dubbed the "big bad bug" and treated at length in his essay "Humean Supervenience Debugged" -- together with the "New Principle" he proposed there in response. As far as I can tell, the trouble arises because Lewis wants chance, like everything else non-fundamental, to supervene on the total Humean mosaic of events, past, present, and future, as summarized by the best system of laws. That seems to leave room for what he calls "undermining" futures: courses of history that have some positive chance of occurring given the present chance distribution, yet which, were they actually to occur, would make it the case that the chances had been different all along. I can sense that something goes wrong here, but every exposition I have found moves quickly into propositions labelled X and T and time-subscripted operators, and I lose the thread of why this amounts to a strict formal inconsistency with the Principal Principle, rather than merely an odd but livable consequence of Humeanism about law. Could you set out, ideally with a small worked example (a coin with some fixed chance of heads, tossed a large but finite number of times, would do nicely), exactly how an undermining future forces a contradiction once the original Principal Principle is applied without qualification? And could you then explain, in similarly plain terms, what the New Principle changes -- what is gained by conditionalizing on an entire theory of chance rather than on the bare chance proposition -- along with your own assessment of whether this amounts to a genuine dissolution of the difficulty, or merely a more sophisticated way of relocating it?
