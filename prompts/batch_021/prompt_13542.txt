For the last three years my dissertation has centered on Frege's case against psychologism: his insistence, above all in the preface to the Grundgesetze, that the laws of logic are objective and answerable to no particular mind, culture, or historical moment, and that grounding them instead in the contingent psychology of actual thinkers is simply a confusion of what is true with what merely seems true to someone. I've always treated this as one of the most admirable, hard-won parts of his thought, almost a model of intellectual integrity independent of the man himself. A few weeks ago a colleague sent me the diary Frege kept for several weeks in 1924, the year before he died. It sat unpublished among his papers for something like seventy years, and it turns out to be full of vehement antisemitism, contempt for the Weimar constitution, and hope for some strongman to sweep the whole system away -- including the explicit view that Jews should be stripped of full political rights. I remember reading somewhere that Michael Dummett, who did more than almost anyone to build Frege's reputation in English-language philosophy, said that learning of the diary genuinely unsettled his feelings about the man, if not about the logic; I find myself in the same position, except I can't yet tell whether feelings are really all that's at stake.

What I'd like help thinking through is two questions at once, because I suspect I can't answer either in isolation. First, in general terms: what are the main philosophical positions on how, if at all, a thinker's odious extra-philosophical commitments should bear on our reading of technical work that looks unrelated to them? I gather there's a substantial literature on this occasioned by Heidegger, and I'd welcome that comparison. Second, and more specifically to my own case: I keep noticing a shape I don't know what to do with, where the same man who could not tolerate the idea that logical truth might be relative to the history or perspective of a particular people was, in politics, deeply committed to the idea that some peoples simply don't belong inside another's polity. Is that a real intellectual continuity between his anti-psychologism and his nationalism worth taking seriously, or is it the kind of spurious unity we're tempted to impose on a life once we already know how it turned out?
