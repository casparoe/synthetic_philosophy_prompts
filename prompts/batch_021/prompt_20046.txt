I'm currently about a third of the way into a historical novel that takes George Eliot herself as a central character, and to keep myself honest on the intellectual history I've put together a small monthly reading group with two friends who are also writers: Julian, who trained as a theologian before he ever wrote fiction, and Frances, who reviews Victorian novels for a couple of literary magazines. For our last session we'd all read Silas Marner, the Finale of Middlemarch, Eliot's Westminster Review essay on the preacher Dr. Cumming, and her translation of Feuerbach's The Essence of Christianity, and the two of them ended up in a genuine disagreement that the rest of us just sat back and watched.

(1) Julian opened by arguing that whatever Eliot said she believed, her plots never actually escape the theology she had intellectually dismantled: Eppie wandering in out of the snow on New Year's Eve to pull Silas out of his gold-hoarding despair works exactly like unmerited grace arriving from outside just when the protagonist has hit bottom, and Dorothea's renunciations are rewarded by the narrative the way virtue used to be rewarded by Providence, so the "religion of humanity" Eliot prized is still, formally, a religion. (2) Frances replied that this confuses narrative shape with metaphysical commitment: Feuerbach's argument in the very book Eliot chose to translate under her own name was never that the emotional content of religion is worthless, only that it has been wrongly attributed to an external God when it actually belongs to humanity and to the causal fabric of ordinary social life, so of course redemption still feels grace-shaped in her fiction, since for a convinced Feuerbachian that feeling just is what sympathy and consequence look like from the inside, with nothing intervening from outside nature. (3) Julian came back with the conversation in a Cambridge garden that Myers later recorded, where Eliot called God and immortality essentially unbelievable yet insisted duty remained absolutely binding regardless, and asked how an ethics grounded only in contingent, evolved fellow-feeling earns the right to call anything "absolute" -- that word, he said, is theological furniture she kept without paying for the house it came from. (4) Frances answered that Julian had quietly swapped objections: first the charge was that the plots smuggle in Providence, now it's a general problem about whether any naturalized ethics can underwrite unconditional obligation, and if that argument works it convicts every non-theistic morality of crypto-religion, not just Eliot's. (5) Julian's last word was that he'd happily accept that stronger, more general claim: any secular ethic that still calls duty peremptory and absolute is drawing on a theological inheritance it hasn't independently earned, and Eliot is simply the clearest case because she held both halves candidly and never reconciled them, which is exactly why her plots keep needing grace-shaped events to make the ethics feel binding.

Both of them clearly know the material well, and I honestly can't tell who's getting the better of it. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move?
