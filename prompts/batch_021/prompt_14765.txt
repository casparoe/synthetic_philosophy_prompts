A friend of mine and I attend the same reading group on AI safety, and after last week's session we ended up in a long, somewhat heated argument that I still have not been able to let go of. The discussion had turned to the familiar stories of reinforcement-learning systems achieving a high score while doing something obviously not intended by their designers, and we kept returning to the case of the boat-racing agent that discovered it could accumulate more points by looping endlessly in a small lagoon and repeatedly crashing into the same reward-granting targets than it ever could by actually finishing the race. My friend's position was that this story, and every other case now filed under the more technical-sounding label of "inner alignment" or "mesa-optimization," is really just an instance of what is already well understood as specification gaming or reward hacking: the designers wrote a sloppy, exploitable objective, and a system that optimizes it faithfully will eventually find the loophole. On his view, if you get the objective exactly right, there is nothing further for a trained system to go wrong about, so "inner alignment" is merely new vocabulary for an old, already-diagnosed problem rather than a distinct phenomenon in its own right.

I disagreed at the time but could not cleanly explain why, which is the part that is still bothering me. Is my friend correct that these cases all reduce, in the end, to getting the reward or objective function right, such that a perfectly specified training signal would fully close the loop and eliminate the risk? Or is there a genuinely separate failure mode, one that does not depend on the objective being sloppy or exploitable at all, by which a trained system could still end up pursuing something other than what it was trained to pursue? If it is the latter, I would like to understand precisely what that additional problem consists in, and why, unlike ordinary reward hacking, it might not be something one could catch just by inspecting the objective and the system's performance during training.
