Last weekend a friend of mine, who trained as a physicist before drifting into philosophy of science, and I spent a good two hours arguing about Einstein's hole argument and what it actually establishes. Their view was that the whole thing is something of a philosophers' confusion: diffeomorphism invariance is "just" a gauge freedom, no more metaphysically loaded than the freedom to relabel coordinates, so there is nothing here that should trouble anyone who thinks spacetime points are genuine, mind-independent entities. I insisted that the argument, at least in the form Earman and Norton gave it, is supposed to be doing more specific work than that: if you are a substantivalist about the manifold, the mere existence of diffeomorphic models that agree on everything outside some "hole" region but disagree on which point carries which metrical properties inside it commits you to a strange, cost-free indeterminism, since nothing physical distinguishes the two models from one another. We got stuck arguing about whether "sophisticated substantivalism" — denying that the diffeomorphic models represent genuinely distinct possibilities in the first place — is a principled way out of this or just relationism dressed up in substantivalist clothing, and neither of us could really articulate why it isn't the latter.

I'd like your help sorting this out properly. First, walk me through, with enough technical detail that I could reconstruct it on a whiteboard, exactly why the hole argument is supposed to pose a substantive threat to manifold substantivalism rather than being "mere" gauge freedom on a par with a choice of coordinates — in particular, what work is being done by a diffeomorphism that acts as the identity outside the hole but non-trivially inside it, and why the existence of such diffeomorphic models is supposed to conflict with determinism given that the field equations are generally covariant. Second, explain what the sophisticated substantivalist actually buys by denying haecceitism about spacetime points and accepting Leibniz equivalence while still insisting that points are real, mind-independent entities, and lay out why some critics think this move just concedes the relationist's substantive point while holding onto otherwise idle metaphysical baggage. I don't need you to referee the argument with my friend, but I would like to understand the mechanics well enough to know which of us was closer to being right.
