I'm about two-thirds through a novel and I've hit a scene I keep chickening out of writing properly. My protagonist's mother, Sabine, a cellist who spent her whole adult life genuinely obsessed with Schopenhauer (not in a "philosophy is deep" dinner-party way, an actual specific way), has just died, and her son Tomas — who is not remotely a philosopher and is barely functional right now — has to get up and give her eulogy at a memorial packed with her old conservatory colleagues. She used to argue, more than once, over wine, that music isn't like painting or poetry for Schopenhauer at all — that the other arts have to go through the Platonic Ideas to get at the will, while music skips that step entirely and copies the will directly, which is also supposedly why music doesn't even need the ordinary world of things to exist in order to do whatever it is music does. Half the room is going to be people who've heard her make exactly this argument before and will know instantly if the eulogy is faking it. The other half is cousins and neighbours who wouldnt know the will from a hole in the ground and just want to cry appropriately for a few minutes.

Here's my actual problem, and why I don't trust myself to wing it: Schopenhauer's system is genuinely bleak about ordinary existence — everything's striving, brief satisfaction, boredom, more striving — so I don't want Tomas's eulogy to borrow the nice vocabulary while quietly ignoring how grim the rest of the picture is, that feels like cheating and Sabine would've hated it. But it's also not implausible that this is exactly where she found something like peace, given what Schopenhauer thinks aesthetic contemplation does in general and what he thinks music specifically is doing that the other arts structurally can't. I've been rereading the relevant bit of The World as Will and Representation to make sure I don't misrepresent it, but I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I'm worried about either flattening this into greeting-card mush or getting some crucial piece of it backwards. Could you draft the actual eulogy Tomas gives — three or four minutes, not a full graveside oration? I want the Schopenhauer material doing real structural work in it, shaping what Tomas can and can't honestly say about his mother and about death, rather than sitting there as a nice quote about music near the top. It should sound like it was written by a wrung-out thirty-four-year-old, not a philosophy TA, but it can't be something that makes the string quartet in row two wince.
