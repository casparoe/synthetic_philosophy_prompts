I'm working on a story about why even today's AI language models, despite being trained on basically the entire corpus of human logic and math, still flub simple inferences or seem to hold two contradictory beliefs at once. A philosopher I interviewed for it made the point that this isn't some brand new puzzle — logicians ran into a version of it decades ago, under the name "the problem of logical omniscience." As I understand it: if you model knowledge the classic Hintikka way, using possible worlds (an agent "knows" p just in case p is true in every world thats compatible with what they know), it falls straight out of the semantics that the agent also knows every tautology and every logical consequence of anything they know, since those hold in all the same worlds too. Which obviously isn't true of any real reasoner, human or machine.

What I find genuinely interesting digging into the old papers is that there's more than one patch for this, and two in particular keep getting mentioned side by side as rival fixes. One is to let "impossible worlds" into the model — worlds that don't respect the ordinary laws of logic, so entailment can quietly fail somewhere in the model and the closure result breaks. This shows up in Hintikka's own later work and in stuff by logicians like Rantala. The other is Fagin and Halpern's "awareness logic" from the late 80s, where you keep the usual possible-worlds picture around for a background notion of "implicit" knowledge, but define what the agent "explicitly" knows as the overlap between that and a separate, more or less primitive "awareness" set of propositions they've actually entertained.

Could you walk me through how these two actually compare, philosophically rather than just mechanically? My hunch is the impossible-worlds move basically gives up on logic constraining the model at all, while awareness logic just relocates the mystery — now you have to explain why the agent is aware of this proposition and not that one, instead of explaining the omniscience away. Is that fair? And if you had to defend one of the two as the more principled solution to logical omniscience rather than a workaround for it, which would you pick, and why?
