At a dinner gathering last month, two friends of mine — both among the first twenty or so people hired at the same small startup — fell into a good-natured but genuinely puzzling disagreement, and having turned it over ever since, I still cannot decide which of them has the better argument. The first, employee number fifteen, reasoned as follows: most startups that get off the ground stay fairly small, topping out at a few dozen people before stalling or folding, and only a rare few ever balloon into companies with thousands of employees. If her company is destined to stay small, then being employee fifteen out of an eventual roster of, say, thirty, is a thoroughly unremarkable position to occupy; if it is instead destined to become one of the rare giants, then being employee fifteen out of an eventual roster of several thousand would be a strikingly early, atypical position to occupy — practically the front of the line. Since it makes more sense to expect to find yourself in a typical position in your company's history than in an extraordinarily atypical one, she concluded that her own low number is itself mild evidence that the company will stay small.

Her colleague wasn't persuaded, for two reasons. First, he pointed out that the identical argument is available to every low-numbered employee at every startup, whatever its eventual fate, so plenty of people who happen to work at future giants will run it and conclude, wrongly, that their company is bound to stay small. Second, and more interestingly to him, he argued that companies which do become giants thereby create far more employee positions than companies that stay small, so someone reasoning about their own situation should also weigh the fact that simply being an employee anywhere at all is more likely to happen at a company that ends up huge, precisely because huge companies offer so many more positions to occupy in the first place. On his view, this second consideration favors "we'll grow huge," and he couldn't say whether it merely tempers his colleague's reasoning, cancels it outright, or is really a separate and unrelated consideration.

I mentioned the exchange over lunch to a couple of colleagues in my own department, half expecting someone to spot an obvious flaw, but opinions were split enough that I no longer trust my instincts here. Since I teach philosophy, my friends now assume I must have a tidy answer, which I don't. The disagreement feels like the kind of puzzle that should already have a clean, well-known resolution, but I can't reconstruct one I would fully stand behind. Is one of my friends simply making a mistake, and if so, exactly where does the reasoning break down? Is the other's second point decisive against the first argument, a mere corrective to it, or beside the point entirely? I would value your working through the case in some detail rather than handing me a bare verdict.
