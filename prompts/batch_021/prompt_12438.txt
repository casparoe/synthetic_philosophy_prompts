Last night, a friend and I argued for hours about whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible: cases like Agamemnon's choice at Aulis, forced to sacrifice his daughter or fail his fleet, where an agent truly ought to do A and truly ought to do B but cannot do both, through no fault of their own. I pressed the standard logical objection: the agglomeration principle (if you ought to do A and you ought to do B, then you ought to do both A and B) together with "ought implies can" seems to entail that such situations cannot occur, since they would require it to be both possible and impossible to do both A and B. My friend replied, in a move I later realized resembles Ruth Barcan Marcus's defense of dilemmas, that deontic consistency only requires our obligations to be jointly satisfiable in some possible circumstance, not in this particular unlucky one, so a local clash does not show that the underlying moral principles are themselves inconsistent.

I was not convinced, but could not pin down exactly why that night. Please construct the strongest case that this "relativized satisfiability" defense fails to rescue genuine dilemmas from incoherence: specifically, argue that agglomeration and ought-implies-can, applied to the concrete obligations an agent actually bears within the dilemma itself, rule out its ever being true that the agent faces jointly unsatisfiable oughts, regardless of what holds across other possible circumstances. Please also anticipate and answer the strongest objections a Marcus-style defender would raise against your argument.
