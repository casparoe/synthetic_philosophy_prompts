I'm a retired analytical chemist, and after forty-odd years in a lab I've been filling my retirement by finally reading all the philosophy I never had time for back in grad school. A few months ago I joined a little discussion group at the library (mostly retired folks, plus one ex-lawyer who cross-examines everybody like it's a deposition), and we've been slowly working through the American pragmatists. I got kind of excited when I found out Peirce actually started out as a working scientist, chemistry and geodesy for the US Coast Survey and all that, since it felt like the first philosopher I could actually relate to.

Here's my hang-up though: the group keeps treating "pragmatism" like one tidy package, but the more I read the less that seems true, especially on the question of truth. Peirce's line that truth is "the opinion which is fated to be ultimately agreed to by all who investigate" sounds to me like something out of the philosophy of measurement, truth as a limit you converge on but maybe never quite reach, like an asymptote. Then you get to James, and he's defining the true as whatever "proves itself to be good in the way of belief" -- which sounds a lot more like "whatever works for you personally, right now." This apparently annoyed Peirce so much that he renamed his own view "pragmaticism," a word he said was ugly enough that no kidnapper would want to steal it. Seems like a petty reason to rename your life's work, but it made me suspect the split runs deeper than just two guys with different temprements.

So what I'd like from you is an actual comparison, not just "they both care about practical consequences" -- I want the real mechanics of what makes a belief true on each view, and exactly where they part ways. Then don't leave it hanging with "both have good points": as someone who spent a career deciding which hypothesis actually held up against the data, I want you to pick a side and defend it. Is Peirce's convergence-of-inquiry picture the more defensible theory of truth, or is James onto something Peirce's version misses? Please weigh this against the standard objections to each -- that Peirce's "end of inquiry" is a place we never actually get to check our answer against, and that James's view seems to let comforting nonsense count as "true" so long as it works for someone.
