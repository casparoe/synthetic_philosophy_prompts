So I need someone to settle an argument that isn't even mine, because it's been eating at me for weeks and none of my friends will engage with it seriously. This isn't really my usual beat -- I cover science and ideas for a magazine, mostly physics and neuroscience -- but it happened at my own co-op's board meeting and I can't let it go. Our board delegates any renovation under $50k to a three-person subcommittee, and this month's case was a rooftop deck that, under the bylaws, needed to clear three seperate tests to get approved: it couldn't touch the landmarked facade, it had to pass an engineer's sign-off on the load-bearing beams, and it had to fit inside the reserve-fund cap. All three, or it's a no. Here's the part that's been bugging people: on the facade question, two of the three members said it was fine, with one holdout; on the structural question, a different two said it was fine, with a different holdout; and on the budget question, yet another two said it was fine, with yet another holdout. Every single condition passed 2-1. And yet the subcommittee's actual head count on the resolution itself came out 3-0 against, because each of the three members turned out to be the lone holdout on a different one of the three conditions -- nobody agreed with anybody about why the deck should fail, but all three agreed that it should, and that's how it went into the minutes. That should've been the end of it, except Devon, who was on the subcommittee (he's the one who thought the load-bearing math didn't check out), and Priya, the board president, who sat in on the meeting but doesn't get a vote on that particular subcommittee, ended up hashing it out in the hallway afterward, loud enough that half the building heard.

(1) Devon's opening move was that the real vote was 2-1 in favor on every legally relevant question, so calling the whole thing rejected means overriding the majority on all three issues at once -- which he called "throwing out people's actual reasons for a tally that never happened." (2) Priya's comeback was that nobody in that room voted on "the facade question" as its own resolution -- they voted once, on the deck, and three people said no, so slicing everyone's reasoning into three pieces after the fact and gluing the pieces back together a different way is inventing a vote that nobody actually took. (3) Devon replied that her method is arbitrary in its own way: swap in a different three-person subcommittee reading the same file, and you could easily get a different member failing on each different condition, flipping the up-or-down outcome purely because of who happened to be on the panel that week, whereas the per-condition majorities stay put no matter who ends up being the odd one out. (4) Priya said that instability argument cuts both ways, since his method is just as sensitive to how finely you slice the bylaws -- merge the facade and budget clauses into one combined question, or split the budget clause into two, and his clean 2-1 sweep can disappear -- and that at least her tally matches what's actually written in the minutes, instead of requiring someone to recompute votes nobody cast, which is exactly the kind of after-the-fact reshuffling she doesn't trust anyone to do fairly, including herself. (5) Devon's last word was that by her own logic, whoever decides it's "one combined vote" instead of "three seperate compliance questions" has already smuggled in an answer to the very dispute they're having, so her approach isn't neutral either, it just hides its framing choice better -- and then, a little sheepishly, he admitted that a straight per-condition count would actually override his own objection (he was the structural holdout) and approve the deck anyway, which he says is sort of the point, since it means he isn't just rooting for his preferred outcome.

Neither of them has really spoken to the other since, which feels extreme for a rooftop deck, but the more I sit with it the less obviously wrong either one seems. Could you go through the five numbered moves above and say who has the stronger argument at each step, not just who wins overall, and flag anything you think both of them are missing? And separately, I'm curious whether you think there's actually a principled way to say which tally -- 2-1/2-1/2-1 in favor, or 3-0 against -- is the real outcome of what that subcommittee decided, or whether this is just a case where the bylaws never specified an answer to a question that doesn't have one.
