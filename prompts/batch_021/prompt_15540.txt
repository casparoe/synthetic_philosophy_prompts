And here's the student's answer, copied exactly as written:

"The Principal Principle says that if you know the objective chance of some event A at time t is x, your credence in A should also be x — C(A | ch_t(A)=x) = x — as long as you don't also have 'inadmissible' evidence. Admissible evidence is basically evidence that doesn't jump ahead of what the chance already tells you: testimony from a genuine crystal ball that reveals the outcome in advance would be inadmissible, since conditioning on it would pull your credence away from x. Ordinary historical information, by contrast, counts as admissible, since it's exactly the sort of information chances are supposed to summarize in the first place.

A problem for this arises once you combine the Principal Principle with the Humean picture of laws and chance that Lewis also holds. On this view, chances aren't primitive facts but whatever falls out of the best systematization of the pattern of events across all of history, including the future. So if the world's future turns out a certain way, the best system fit to the whole of history might end up assigning a different chance at t than x. But this is really just the predicament we're always in with any scientific hypothesis: we assign credences to it based on current evidence, we can never be 100% certain, and further evidence could always come in and revise our estimate. This general risk that our current best guess about the chances is mistaken is what Lewis calls the 'big bad bug.'

Hall and Thau's fix for this is the New Principle, on which you condition your credence not on the bare chance value x but on a complete admissible theory T that specifies the entire chance function: C(A | H&T) = the chance that T assigns to A, given full history H. Because T is maximally specific, conditionalizing on it screens off any worry about which chances are the true ones, so the New Principle fully vindicates the idea that we should always defer to chance and closes the gap the bug opened up."
