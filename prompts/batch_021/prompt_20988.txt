In the 1840s, Frederick Douglass stood with William Lloyd Garrison in condemning the United States Constitution as irredeemably pro-slavery — "a covenant with death, and an agreement with hell," in the Garrisonians' own phrase — on the strength of clauses that seem intelligible only as guarantees to slaveholders: the three-fifths rule, the fugitive-slave clause, and the twenty-year protection extended to the slave trade. By the early 1850s, under the influence of Gerrit Smith and Lysander Spooner, Douglass had reversed himself, arguing that the document nowhere contains the words "slave" or "slavery," that ordinary canons of legal construction resolve such silences in favor of liberty rather than bondage, and that the preamble's stated purposes should govern the reading of any clause whose bearing on slavery is merely inferred rather than explicit.

Was Douglass right to change his mind? Write an essay that lays out the strongest version of each position — the appeal to particular clauses and the framers' evident design, against the appeal to textual silence, interpretive canons, and declared purpose — and defends a determinate verdict on which reading of the Constitution is the more defensible. A qualified verdict is acceptable (for instance, that the Garrisonians were right about what the delegates in Philadelphia meant to do, while Spooner and Douglass were right about what the ratified text actually says), provided you argue for it rather than merely asserting it; a conclusion that both sides simply have a point will not do.
