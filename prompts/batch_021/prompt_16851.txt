Since retiring from a career in theoretical physics, I have been spending my mornings on philosophy of science, and Einstein's hole argument has become something of a preoccupation. As I understand it: one takes a solution of general relativity's field equations on a manifold, applies a diffeomorphism that acts as the identity everywhere except inside some matter-free "hole," and uses it to push the metric field forward into a new field on that same manifold. Because the equations are generally covariant, this new field is itself a solution, and it agrees with the original on the entire matter distribution and on the metric outside the hole, yet disagrees with it inside the hole. Earman and Norton argued that this construction shows that anyone who holds that spacetime points possess a primitive identity independent of the fields defined on them is committed to a radical indeterminism. I would like an essay that weighs that argument against its principal rivals -- embracing Leibniz equivalence and relationism, retaining a "sophisticated," anti-haecceitist substantivalism about the points themselves, or opting for some structuralist alternative -- and that settles on a definite verdict about whether manifold substantivalism can survive the hole argument, rather than simply cataloguing the possible responses. A qualified verdict is fine (for instance, endorsing sophisticated substantivalism while conceding that it only works by quietly borrowing a relationist criterion of identity for points), so long as it is an actual verdict and not a survey.
