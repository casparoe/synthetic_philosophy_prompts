Here's the answer I need a read on:

"Both individuals and society are assumed to have preferences over lotteries on a fixed set of prizes that satisfy the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms (completeness, transitivity, continuity, and independence), so each can be represented by a utility function that is unique up to a positive affine transformation. Harsanyi motivates the link between the individual and social rankings by having us imagine an impartial spectator who doesn't know in advance which person's position she'll end up occupying, so she assigns an equal 1/n chance to being each member of society; requiring that this spectator's ranking of lotteries agree with everyone's ranking whenever they all agree (Pareto Indifference) is what forces her utility function into a weighted sum of the individual utility functions, U_0 = Σ b_i U_i + a. Because Pareto Indifference already stops the spectator from ever overriding a unanimous verdict, none of the weights b_i can come out zero or negative — that's what licenses calling the result "utilitarian" rather than just "affine." This is importantly different from Arrow's theorem, since Arrow works with ordinal, non-comparable preferences over certain outcomes and gets an impossibility result, whereas Harsanyi enriches the informational basis to cardinal utility over lotteries and gets a possibility result instead.

The standard objection, due to Sen, is that summing utilities presupposes you can compare utility gains and losses across different people, but the vNM axioms only pin down each individual's utility function up to that person's own positive affine transformation — they say nothing about how to line up one person's units with another's. So even granting the theorem, the weights b_i aren't really fixed: rescale someone's utility function (still a perfectly good vNM representative of the same preferences) and the "weighted sum" has to be rewritten with different weights to represent the same social ordering. Sen's point is that Harsanyi is smuggling in an interpersonal comparison that the axioms themselves don't license."
