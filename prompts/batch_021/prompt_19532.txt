I'm a software engineer who first ran into conditional probability through Bayesian inference rather than philosophy, so I was surprised to discover that Frank Ramsey had essentially proposed a Bayesian account of indicative conditionals back in the 1920s. In a footnote to "General Propositions and Causality," Ramsey suggested that to evaluate "if p, then q," one hypothetically adds p to one's stock of beliefs and gauges the resulting confidence in q — an idea later sharpened into "Adams' Thesis," that the probability of an indicative conditional equals the conditional probability P(q|p). In 1976, decades after Ramsey's death, David Lewis proved his triviality results, showing that no proposition can satisfy that equation relative to every reasonable probability function without the function collapsing onto a handful of degenerate values. The result is generally read as forcing a choice: either deny that conditionals express truth-apt propositions at all, or give up Ramsey's equation as anything more than a special-case coincidence.

Given what we know of Ramsey's broader commitments — his subjectivist, action-guiding account of probability, his deflationary leanings about truth, and his general willingness to dissolve rather than solve stubborn technical puzzles — what do you think he would have made of Lewis's result? Would he have granted that his test was refuted, or did his other views already supply the resources to sidestep triviality, for instance by denying that conditionals need truth conditions in the first place?
