I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of biology, and I would like to move my students past the stock illustrations of the Gould–Lewontin critique -- the panda's thumb, male nipples -- that they can already recite without ever having had to grapple with a case in which the adaptationist has serious, live proposals on the table. The case I have in mind is female orgasm. Gould argued, on analogy with male nipples, that it is best explained as a byproduct of the developmental pathway shared by the two sexes' genitalia, with no selective history of its own; against this, a series of adaptationist hypotheses -- tied variously to paternity confidence and sperm retention, to pair-bonding, and to unconscious assessment of a partner's genetic quality -- have tried to supply it with a specific function. What makes the case useful for teaching is Elisabeth Lloyd's survey of this literature, which refuses to let the byproduct view win by default: she holds each adaptive hypothesis to an explicit evidential standard (design features suited to the proposed function, not merely a statistical correlation with it) and argues that nearly all of them fail that standard, pointing among other things to the low and inconsistent rate at which orgasm accompanies intercourse absent other stimulation -- an odd fact for any story that ties orgasm's function specifically to intercourse.

I would like you to write a dialogue between two equally well-prepared characters, an Adaptationist and a Byproduct Theorist, who work through this disagreement rather than talking past one another. The Adaptationist should resist sliding from "no adaptive hypothesis has yet been vindicated" to "therefore it is a byproduct," and should press the Byproduct Theorist on whether the homology with male nipples is doing any real explanatory work, given that plenty of uncontroversial adaptations are also built from repurposed, shared developmental machinery. The Byproduct Theorist should press back by asking what, short of Lloyd's evidential standard, would stop any adaptive story here from being just another just-so story, and should make the positive case that the trait's actual profile -- its variability across women, and its loose coupling to the one act adaptationists most want it to be serving -- is better predicted by the byproduct account than by its rivals. Let the exchange end not in agreement, but with the two of them isolating the single empirical or methodological point on which their whole disagreement actually turns.
