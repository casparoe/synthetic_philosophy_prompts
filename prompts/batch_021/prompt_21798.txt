This term I am running a graduate seminar on the philosophy of action, and next week's session juxtaposes Donald Davidson's classic treatment of weakness of will in "How Is Weakness of the Will Possible?" with the hyperbolic discounting models that several of my students keep importing from behavioral economics. Davidson's puzzle turns on three individually plausible principles that jointly seem to rule out incontinent action: that judging one course of action better than another gives one a correspondingly stronger want for it, that the stronger want issues in action whenever one is free to act on either option, and yet that people plainly do sometimes choose the option they judge worse. His resolution rests on distinguishing an "all things considered" judgment, arrived at by weighing every reason available to the agent, from a merely conditional judgment reached by attending to only some of those reasons -- the akratic agent, on this account, acts on the latter while still holding the former. The discounting literature explains the same surface phenomena -- procrastination, succumbing to temptation, abandoning a diet -- quite differently: not via any mismatch between judgment and action, but via a hyperbolic rather than exponential discount function that produces a genuine reversal of preference as a reward draws nearer in time, so that the option that looked better from a distance simply stops being preferred once it is close at hand.

What I would like from you is a rigorous comparison of these two accounts, since I suspect several of my students are too quick to treat the discounting model as merely a mechanistic underpinning of Davidson's phenomenon rather than a rival characterization of it. Concretely: does hyperbolic discounting actually model akrasia in Davidson's sense, given that a clean preference reversal may involve no moment at which the agent judges one option best all things considered and yet chooses the other -- since the evaluation itself has changed, is there really anything "weak" about acting on it? Or can the discounting story be run so that it reproduces a genuine synchronic split between judgment and action, rather than a merely diachronic change of mind? I would also welcome your own verdict, informed by however you understand the efforts of philosophers such as Alfred Mele and Richard Holton to keep these two phenomena apart, on which framework -- or what combination of the two -- better explains why weakness of will has struck philosophers as a distinctive puzzle, rather than just another instance of people changing their minds over time.
