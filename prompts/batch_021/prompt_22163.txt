I'm teaching a philosophy of mind seminar this term and we just spent two weeks in the split-brain literature -- Sperry and Gazzaniga's original commissurotomy studies, Nagel's old piece on brain bisection, Puccetti's "two minds" thesis, and the more recent pushback from folks like Bayne and Schechter who think the simple "two minds" story is too quick. My students keep treating "is it one person or two?" like a trivia question instead of actually sitting with how strange the clinical picture is -- the cross-cuing, the left hand picking out an object the patient swears they never saw, the odd little conflicts some patients report between the two sides of their own body.

I want to throw something different at them this week instead of another paper, so could you write me a short story (a few pages, doesn't need to be long) that dramatizes this puzzle? One thing I'd really like you to avoid is the standard move where the two hemispheres basically turn into two chatty characters holding a dialogue inside the patient's skull -- every student who's seen a pop-sci video already pictures that, and it kind of begs the question by assuming there's cleanly two subjects in there to begin with. I'd rather the disunity show up sideways, through behavior and confabulation and the patient's own confusion about their own actions, in a way that stays close to how these cases actually present clinically. And please don't resolve the metaphysical question for the reader at the end -- Nagel's whole point was that none of our ordinary "one mind" / "two minds" concepts obviously fit this case, and the story should earn that same unresolved feeling rather then just picking a side.
