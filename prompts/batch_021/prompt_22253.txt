I'm a philosophy professor at a small college, which is apparently why I ended up as the resident philosopher on our Academic Integrity Committee, and something landed on our docket this week that I think is more interesting than my colleagues realize. A student in a colleague's course submitted an essay after running three sentences through Grammarly's "AI rewrite" feature to smooth out some clunky phrasing; she disclosed this herself when asked directly, which everyone agrees should count in her favor. The syllabus states that students "may not use AI to generate any part of the submitted work," full stop, with no further definition given anywhere. About half the committee thinks any AI touching the words counts as a violation, since Grammarly is, after all, an AI; the other half thinks "generate" obviously means composing from nothing, so a light rewrite is no different then spell-check always was. We've had a run of easy cases this term -- whole essays pasted straight from ChatGPT -- so this is the first one that's actually split the room down the middle.

Someone proposed we just redraft the rule to be more precise before spring term, and I said that probably wouldn't fix much, which did not go over well at 4pm on a Tuesday. What's bugging me is that this doesn't feel like ordinary vagueness, a fuzzy sorites-style boundary case -- it feels more like there's no fact of the matter, in either the rule's wording or whatever the drafting subcommittee had in mind last year, that settles this particular case one way rather than the other. It's the plus/quus problem wearing a school ID badge. I tried this line on my officemate and got a blank stare, so you're getting it instead: could you draft the memo I need to circulate before next Wednesday's meeting? I need it to (a) recommend a specific, defensible ruling on this actual student's case, (b) say what, if anything, the committee should do differently going forward given that no amount of redrafting can fully close a gap like this one, and (c) take seriously, rather than wave away, the fairness worry that it's unjust to sanction a student for crossing a line that nobody who wrote the policy can honestly say was really there in advance.
