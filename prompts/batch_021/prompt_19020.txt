So in my Theory of Knowledge class we've been going down a whole rabbit hole on skepticism, and my teacher brought up this philosopher Hilary Putnam who apparently has an argument that basically proves you can't be a brain in a vat. From what I understood, the idea is that if you really were a brain in a vat your whole life, the word "vat" in your head wouldn't actually refer to a real vat, since you'd never had the right causal connection to one (it'd just be hooked up to a computer feeding you fake signals), so the sentence "I am a brain in a vat" ends up refuting itself. Half the class was like, wait, doesnt that mean skepticism is basically solved? and my friend who's obsessed with simulation theory (the Nick Bostrom thing about how advanced civilizations might run ancestor simulations and we're probably in one) got weirdly quiet, because if the logic works for vats it seems like it should work the same way for simulations.

I kinda want to use this to finally win that argument with him, but before I do I want to make sure I'm not misusing it. Does Putnam's argument actually prove in general that we're definitly not brains in a vat / not being deceived, or does it only technically work for this one super specific "envatted since birth with zero outside contact" scenario? And even if it totally works for vats, is it fair to say the same reasoning knocks out the simulation hypothesis too, or is that a totally different kind of argument that just sounds similar? It feels almost too convenient that one clever move about how words get their meaning would just end the whole debate about whether we can trust our senses.
