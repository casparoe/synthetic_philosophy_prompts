I'm on the membership committee for the housing co-op I live in, a small place, about 40 units, and we've got a real argument brewing over our "sweat equity" rule. Right now anyone who applies has to put in 100 hours of unpaid labor before they're approved as a member — painting, yard work, committee work, whatever needs doing — on top of paying for their share. A couple of the newest applicants asked if they could just pay extra cash instead, since they've got money but not time (one's a single dad working two jobs, another has a disability that makes the physical stuff hard). A few of us are sympathetic to that. But a bunch of the long-time members are furious, saying that if you let people buy their way out of the hours you're basically letting anyone with cash skip paying their dues, and eventually we'll be full of members who never really bonded to the place and who vanish the second something hard comes up — like it was the same twelve people who showed up every night for two weeks when the boiler died last winter.

I keep coming back to this thing I read once about peacock tails, how the tail is such a huge, costly handicap to drag around that it actually works precisely because its wasteful — only a genuinely strong bird could grow one and still survive, so the peahens can trust it as a signal. Part of me thinks our sweat equity hours work the same way, like the whole point is that they're annoying and hard to fake, so someone willing to put in the hours is proving something that just writing a check can't. My partner says I'm turning a housing dispute into a philosophy seminar, which is probably fair, but he hasn't actually told me why the logic breaks down, and I dont want to build a real policy off a half remembered nature documentary.

We have to bring a recommendation to the general meeting next month, so I want to circulate a short written memo from the committee beforehand. Can you draft it for me? It needs to actually land on a specific policy — allow full cash buyout, ban it outright, or some middle-ground option like a sliding scale or hardship exception — and lay out the reasoning well enough that it could change someone's mind at the meeting, including a genuinely fair version of whatever side we don't end up recommending.
