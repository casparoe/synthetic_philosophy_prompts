so i sit on the small committee that manages a foundation's collection of historic violins -- old Italian instruments worth millions, loaned out to professional soloists, each covered by a "certificate" we maintain documenting its identity and provenance. odd case just landed on us. One violin, nicknamed the "Contessa," has almost none of its original wood left, having gone through the standard 19th-century conversion: new neck grafted onto the old scroll, new bass bar, new top after a crack in the 1920s -- totally normal, most old Italian violins went through this. But our restoration workshop apparently kept every original piece it ever removed, and a luthier we'd never dealt with has quietly reassembled those discarded original parts -- the real top, the real neck, carved by the maker himself -- into a second, playable violin. He wants the Contessa's name and certificate transferred to his instrument instead, since his actually contains more of the maker's own handiwork, even though it's a worse instrument to play. We don't have a written policy for this; our bylaws just say the certificate follows "the instrument," which nobody ever bothered to define. (Feels like one of those old ship-of-theseus threads, except now theres an actual bill of sale attached.) Write the committees decision memo: say which violin keeps the identity, or how you'd split it, give the criteria you're actually applying, a timeframe for revisiting if new evidence turns up, and take the luthier's argument seriously rather than just citing unbroken chain of custody and calling it a day.
