For my seminar on rational choice theory, we've just read Pettit and Sugden's paper on the backward induction paradox in the centipede game, and I'm stuck on where exactly the paradox is supposed to bite. I follow the standard argument that common knowledge of rationality forces both players to backward-induct all the way to immediate defection at the very first node. What I don't follow is their claim that this common-knowledge assumption cannot survive the game actually being played past that first move: once a player fails to defect when backward induction says she "should" have, it's unclear what basis the other player has left for predicting her subsequent moves, which seems to undermine the very inference that generated the original prediction in the first place. Could you explain why this is treated as a deep paradox about the coherence of common knowledge of rationality in dynamic games, rather than merely a case where one player's prior beliefs about the other turned out to be mistaken? I'd also be curious how far appeals to belief revision actually resolve the difficulty, or whether they just relocate it.
