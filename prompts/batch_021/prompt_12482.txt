At a dinner a few weeks ago, my cousin Daniel described finding a wallet with a little over $1,800 in cash in the stairwell of his apartment building and returning it untouched to its owner. Almost as an afterthought, he mentioned that part of what pushed him to return it was that the ID showed the owner lived two floors up, and that in a small building where everyone eventually hears about everything, keeping it and getting caught would have been humiliating. That offhand comment set off a long argument between two other people at the table, Renata and Oscar, about whether Daniel actually deserves credit for what he did, and I've been turning it over ever since without reaching a view of my own.

As best I can reconstruct it: (1) Renata said the reputational worry is beside the point, since he returned every dollar when he easily could have kept it, and that alone should be enough for full credit. (2) Oscar disagreed, arguing that there is a real difference between acting from honesty and acting from fear of being caught, even when the two look identical from the outside, and that Daniel's own comment suggests fear was doing genuine work. (3) Renata replied that this sets an impossible bar, since every human action is a tangle of motives, and if mixed motives disqualified an act from counting as honest then almost nothing anyone does would ever count. (4) Oscar said he wasn't demanding purity, only asking whether Daniel would have returned the wallet just the same if he'd found it in a city where no one could ever trace it back to him: if yes, the reputational fear was incidental noise, and if the honest answer is "probably not," it was pulling real weight. (5) Renata called that test unfalsifiable in practice, since the scenario can't be rerun and people don't have reliable access to their own motives anyway, so Oscar was grading Daniel against a fact nobody can actually check. (6) Oscar granted that it can't be verified with certainty, but said our inability to verify something perfectly doesn't make the distinction empty, since we make calls like this all the time, for instance when judging whether someone's apology feels sincere rather than performative, and added that caring what your neighbors think of you isn't obviously the same thing as caring about honesty itself, even though the two can feel identical from the inside.

Neither of them budged. I don't have any background in philosophy, so I honestly can't tell whether this is a well-worn question with a standard answer or a genuinely open one. Could you go through the exchange move by move, (1) through (6), and say which side has the better of it at each point, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also be curious whether you think Renata or Oscar is quietly leaning on an assumption that the other one never actually challenges.
