Last night, after a talk we'd both attended on the metaphysics of science, a friend of mine, who is finishing an MA in philosophy of physics, and I ended up in a long, unresolved argument about laws of nature. She defends something close to Armstrong's necessitarian picture. On this view, a generalization such as "all electrons have charge -e" counts as a genuine law, rather than a mere accident, because there is a further contingent relation of natural necessitation holding between the universals "being an electron" and "having charge -e" -- often written N(F,G). It is the obtaining of this second-order relation, over and above the first-order regularity itself, that is supposed to ground the law's lawhood and its capacity to support counterfactuals. I pushed back with what I understand to be a fairly well-known objection to views of this kind, sometimes called the "inference problem": positing N(F,G) as a further fact does not seem to explain why all Fs are Gs, since nothing about a merely contingent relation between two universals seems to rule out, even in principle, a world in which N(F,G) holds and yet something is F without being G. So the necessitation theorist appears to relocate the original puzzle of why the regularities hold at all one level up, rather than actually dissolving it. My friend thought this was uncharitable, and countered that calling N a relation of "necessitation" is precisely to stipulate that its holding guarantees the regularity, with no further bridge principle needed -- much as we don't demand some separate explanation of why an effect follows, once two events are already granted to stand in the relation of cause and effect.

We never resolved this, and I would like a clearer-headed treatment than either of us managed on the spot. Could you explain, in more careful detail than I have been able to muster above, exactly where the inferential or explanatory gap in Armstrong-style necessitarianism is supposed to open up, and then assess whether his reply (or Tooley's, or Dretske's) -- that the guarantee is simply built into the nature of the necessitation relation -- amounts to a substantive answer or more of a terminological sleight of hand? I am also vaguely recalling that someone, Maudlin perhaps, responds to this whole debate by declining to reduce lawhood to either regularities or relations among universals in the first place, and instead treats fundamental laws as ontologically primitive. If that rings a bell, I would be curious whether you think that move genuinely sidesteps the inference problem, or merely relabels it once more.
