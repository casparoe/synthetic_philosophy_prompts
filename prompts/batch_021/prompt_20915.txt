I've been turning over an idea usually called "moral trade" -- I believe the term traces back to a paper by Toby Ord, though I mostly encountered it secondhand through posts building on it on the EA forum. The core thought is a gains-from-trade argument applied to ethics: if two people disagree about which of two issues really matters morally, each can get more of what they personally think is important by trading concessions on the issue they don't care about, even though neither budges an inch on what's actually right. A concrete instance of this came up at a dinner after one of our local meetups. My friend Owen thinks factory farming is a serious wrong but is indifferent to recreational drug use; his partner Ines thinks recreational drug use is corrosive but doesn't think much of animal welfare. The two of them struck a deal: Owen gave up drugs for good and Ines went vegetarian for good, and each now thinks the world modestly improved by their own lights, at a cost paid only in preference, not principle -- what each gave up was something they merely enjoyed, never something either had thought was wrong to keep doing.

When I explained why I found this interesting, two other friends at the table, Dev and Sasha, ended up arguing about whether the arrangement was actually legitimate. (1) Dev opened by saying this isn't moral action at all: the reasons that make factory farming or drug use wrong weren't what moved either Owen or Ines -- each acted for a payoff -- so no moral credit accrues to anyone, and nothing has actually been fixed, only purchased. (2) Sasha replied that credit is beside the point: what matters is that less meat gets eaten and fewer drugs get taken, and outcomes don't care about motives -- if a tax hike had cut meat consumption overnight, nobody would deny that something of moral significance had happened just because no one's heart changed, so trade is simply a cheap way of getting more of what each side already believes matters. (3) Dev granted the outcome point but pushed further: if this kind of trade is fine, it should be fine to pay someone contemplating a wrong you care about -- a poacher, say, who's indifferent to whatever you'd offer in exchange -- to stop, and that starts to look less like moral trade than ransom, rewarding people for being willing to do something bad enough to be worth buying off, which teaches everyone watching that a bad habit is a marketable asset. If that case is troubling, he said, the trouble should carry over to Owen and Ines too, just fainter. (4) Sasha answered that the poacher case has a feature theirs lacks: it gives the poacher a reason to threaten or escalate the very behavior being paid for, whereas Owen's taste for drugs and Ines's for meat predate the negotiation and aren't being ramped up for leverage -- so the worry about rewarding bad actors is real, but it only infects trades where the "price" of the wrongdoing can be inflated on demand. (5) Dev wasn't satisfied: nothing in the structure of the deal itself rules out Ines quietly eating more meat than usual this month, or even taking up some habit she doesn't especially want, purely to have more "abstaining" to sell later -- so the only thing standing between this case and the poacher case is trust that neither of them is gaming the baseline, which is a fact about Owen and Ines as people, not a feature of moral trade as a framework. (6) Sasha granted that trade offers no structural guarantee against this, but pointed out that ordinary promises have the same vulnerability -- someone could run up a debt purely to be dramatically forgiven for it later -- and we don't treat promising as illegitimate on that basis, only bad-faith instances of it; whether Owen and Ines are acting in good faith is a question about them, not about moral trade in general. Dev thought this dodged the worry rather than answered it, and the conversation moved on before either conceded anything.
