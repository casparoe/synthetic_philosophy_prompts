Aumann's agreement theorem is usually glossed as showing that two Bayesians who share a common prior cannot knowingly disagree once their posteriors regarding some event become common knowledge, and the standard proof proceeds via the meet of the two agents' information partitions. What I think gets lost in most expository treatments is that the theorem only forces the two posteriors to a single common value—it gives no guarantee that this value coincides with the posterior a third party would reach by literally pooling both agents' private information. Could you explain, at the level of the proof's mechanics, why common knowledge of the posteriors is exactly enough to force equality, yet still leaves the resulting common belief potentially far from what genuine information-pooling would deliver? I'd also value your take on how much this gap should temper the theorem's popularity as a folk diagnosis of real-world persistent disagreement.
