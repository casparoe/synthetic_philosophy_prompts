So this is a weird one, but it's genuinely eating my week. I'm a mid-level economist at a federal agency and I just got pulled onto the internal working group rewriting our cost-benefit guidance for safety rules — first time I've been on one of these, lucky me. The specific rule we're stuck on comes down to choosing between two programs: Program A is guaranteed to save about 150 lives a year, no uncertainty attached. Program B is a newer, bigger intervention that, depending on how well it gets adopted, has a 75% chance of saving 220 lives and a 25% chance of saving basically nobody. B has the higher expected value (165 vs 150), but plenty of people preferring the sure thing anyway wouldn't normally raise any red flags — that's just ordinary risk aversion, no rule broken.

Here's the actual wrinkle. Since most of the public comments were more gut-reaction than worked-out reasoning, our behavioral economics team ran a companion survey with paired hypothetical choices, and as a standard check they also asked people the same pair of programs again but with every probability cut to a fifth of its size and the leftover probability dumped into "saves nobody" — so the guaranteed 150 becomes a 20% shot at 150, and the 75/25 gamble becomes a 15% shot at 220. If someone's preference for the sure thing the first time is just risk-aversion over lives, they should still prefer the scaled-down sure thing the second time around too, no matter how risk-averse they are — thats apparently just algebra, not some extra substantive assumption. Except that's not what happened: plenty of the same people who wanted the guaranteed 150 lives flipped to wanting the 15%-shot-at-220 once both options got turned into long shots, even though nothing about the two programs relative to each other changed. The behavioral folks called it a textbook "Allais reversal." When staff explained why no single, consistent way of weighing lives lost, however cautious, could produce exactly that flip, almost nobody walked back their answer — they just said something about the "probably saves nobody" framing felt different, not that they'd been wrong.

Now I'm the one who got voluntold to write the memo going to the full commitee before next Tuesday, and the group has split. One camp wants to hold the line on the independence axiom and just treat the reversal as a bias to be filtered out of how we read public input. The other camp, which includes my boss so I can't just wave it off, points out that Allais himself apparently never thought his paradox showed people were being irrational — he thought it showed independence was never obviously a requirement of rationality to begin with, just a mathematically convenient add-on, and that a preference for certainty that survives being carefully explained to you looks a lot more like a stable value than a mistake. I minored in philosophy over a decade ago and at this point all I remember is trolley problems, so I'm genuinely out of my depth on who's actually right here. Could you draft the actual memo — a real recommendation on whether, and how, our cost-benefit methodology should let this kind of certainty-preference count as a legitimate input rather than noise to be corrected away? I don't want it to just side with my boss because, well, she's my boss; I want your honest best call, but whichever way it lands, it needs to seriously engage with the strongest version of the losing side's argument, not the strawman version.
