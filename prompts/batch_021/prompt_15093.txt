This term I'm taking a seminar on the problem of evil, and after our session on skeptical theism, two of my classmates ended up in a real argument in the hallway afterward. Neither would budge, and I couldn't tell who was actually getting the better of it, so I wrote down the exchange as accurately as I could.

For context: we'd just discussed Rowe's argument from the fawn that burns to death slowly in a forest fire, and Wykstra's reply that our inability to see any reason that would justify such suffering doesn't license the conclusion that there is none -- on his CORNEA principle, we're only entitled to infer "no X" from "I don't see an X" when we'd expect to see X if it were there, and given the gulf between our minds and God's, we shouldn't expect to see His reasons.

(1) Naomi defended the skeptical-theist move: Rowe's inference from "no reason is apparent" to "probably no reason exists" is exactly the kind of noseeum inference CORNEA rules out, so the fawn case gives the atheologian nothing.

(2) Theo objected that this is too costly a move to be worth making: if we can't trust our sense that there's no justifying reason for the fawn's suffering, we equally can't trust it when we judge that nothing could justify, say, a particular act of torture -- yet that is exactly the judgment ordinary moral condemnation depends on. Applied consistently, skeptical theism should leave us unable to call anything gratuitously wrong.

(3) Naomi replied that Theo was collapsing two different judgments: whether an act is wrong given what we can reasonably know, versus whether it is, in the complete cosmic accounting, uncompensated by any greater good. Skeptical theism only denies we can make the second judgment; our everyday verdict that torture is wrong tracks reasonably foreseeable consequences, not God's full ledger, so it stands untouched.

(4) Theo answered that the distinction doesn't actually save her: when we say the Holocaust was gratuitously evil, we don't just mean it looked bad given limited information -- we mean, categorically, that nothing could have justified it, which is precisely a claim about the complete ledger. So skeptical theism either undermines Rowe's inference and this one together, or it undermines neither.

Could you go through moves (1) through (4) individually and say who has the better of each one, rather than just declaring an overall winner, and flag anything important that both of them are missing? I'd also be curious whether skeptical theism has a good answer to (4) that neither of them thought to raise.
