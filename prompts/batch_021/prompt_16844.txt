Me and my friend Jae got into it after philosophy reading group last week over Avicenna's flying man argument (sometimes called the floating man) -- you know, the thought experiment where he has you imagine someone created instantaneously as a fully grown adult, suspended in temperate air or a void, blindfolded, limbs spread so far apart that no body part touches any other, no sound, nothing -- basically zero sensory input ever, not even a memory of having had a body before. Avicenna's claim is that this person would still affirm "I exist," their own self, even while affirming NONE of their bodily parts exist, since they have no way to sense them. He uses this to argue that awareness of self doesn't depend on awareness of body, so the self must be something other then the body. Jae was adamant this is basically just Descartes' mind-body distinctness argument, 600 years early, and that it totally works. I said it's got the exact same problem people raise against Descartes' version -- something like what Arnauld pointed out -- that conceiving of yourself apart from your body doesn't mean its actually possible for you to exist that way, so the argument smuggles a modal conclusion out of a merely psychological premise. We also couldn't agree on whether Avicenna even means this as a rigorous demonstration or more like an intuition pump / "reminder" (which is literally in the title of one of the books it shows up in, Pointers and Reminders), meant to soften you up before the real proofs later on -- which would totally change whether the conceivability-possibility objection even lands on it.

Anyway neither of us backed down, so I said I'd get a tie-breaker. Can you write me an essay that actually resolves this? Lay out the strongest version of the flying man argument, then the best objections to it (the conceivability/possibility gap, whether the scenario really succeeds in bracketing ALL bodily awareness, and the "is this a demonstration or just a rhetorical reminder" issue), and then commit to an actual verdict on whether it succeeds in showing the self is distinct from the body, at least in whatever sense Avicenna needs for his larger theory of the soul to work. Don't just land on "there are good points on both sides" -- I want a real answer, even if it's a qualified one like "it establishes X but not Y."
