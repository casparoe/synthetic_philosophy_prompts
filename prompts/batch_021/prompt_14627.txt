As a software engineer, I've always found multiple realizability intuitive through the software/hardware distinction: the same high-level routine can run on very different chips, much as, on a non-reductive physicalist view, a single mental state can be realized by different neural configurations. What I've never been able to resolve is Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument against that picture: given the causal closure of the physical domain and the implausibility of systematic overdetermination, it looks like any mental property genuinely distinct from its physical realizer is causally preempted by that very realizer. Write a dialogue between a philosopher who accepts this argument and concludes that mental causation can only be secured by reducing mental properties to their physical realizers, and a non-reductive physicalist who resists that conclusion by defending the subset strategy -- the view that a mental property's causal powers are simply a subset of those of its physical realizer, so there is no distinct, competing cause left to exclude in the first place. Have them work through the standard case of a decision to raise one's arm in enough detail that they end up disagreeing about something precise: whether the subset strategy actually secures a distinct causal role for the irreducibly mental, or just concedes reduction under a friendlier description. Don't let either side land a clean, uncontested knockout.
