I'm a fifth-year PhD student in social/personality psychology, and this term I've been auditing a philosophy department seminar on moral psychology, mostly because half the syllabus turned out to overlap with debates I already knew from the other side, including my own dissertation work on the cross-situational consistency of personality traits. Two sessions ago the seminar covered the "situationist" critique of virtue ethics, and the room converged fairly quickly on a story I already had some sympathy for as a psychologist. Milgram's obedience experiments and Zimbardo's Stanford Prison Experiment, on this story, show that ordinary, decent people will behave cruelly or abjectly once the situational pressures are engineered correctly, which means that stable character traits like honesty or courage are not doing the causal work an Aristotelian account of virtue needs them to do; Gilbert Harman, we were told, frames this explicitly as a special case of the "fundamental attribution error" familiar from social psychology. Several people in the seminar concluded, and I was inclined to agree, that virtue ethics has essentially been running on borrowed time since Harman and John Doris turned this into an explicit argument, propped up mainly by philosophers who would rather not concede the point to the data.

What I'd like help with is this: assuming that story is basically right, how do serious contemporary virtue ethicists actually respond to it, and does their response amount to more than a refusal to update on the evidence? I ask partly because I intend to cite this debate in my own dissertation and don't want to present the philosophical side as though it has already conceded defeat if that isn't quite accurate, but mostly because I can't work out why, if Milgram's and Zimbardo's studies really show what the seminar took them to show, virtue ethics wasn't simply abandoned as a normative research program once psychologists had already made the relevant point decades earlier.
