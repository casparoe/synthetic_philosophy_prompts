As someone who writes about science and ideas for a living, I'm used to being the person who explains a tricky concept to a general audience — I didn't expect to need that skill for my own family. My aunt Rosalind, a retired surgeon, died three weeks ago at seventy-nine, and I've been asked to deliver her eulogy. Beyond thirty-odd years of perfectly ordinary, competent doctoring, she spent every Saturday for over two decades running a free clinic out of a converted garage for patients who had no insurance, and in her mid-sixties she gave a kidney to a former patient she had met exactly twice. She was also, thanks to a retirement-years philosophy discussion group she never quite stopped talking about, quietly obsessed with the concept of supererogation, and she left written instructions for her own funeral forbidding two specific things: nobody was to say the clinic or the kidney were "just what a doctor should do" (a comforting lie, she thought, that smuggles in an obligation nobody actually has), and nobody was to call her a saint or a hero either (a different comforting lie, in her view, that quarantines what she did into a category the rest of us get to feel excused from ever entering). She liked to say that Urmson's essay reviving the whole idea, "Saints and Heroes," and Susan Wolf's later "Moral Saints," which wonders whether anyone should even want to be one, had permanently ruined ordinary eulogies for her — and now I have to give one anyway, in front of the very reading group that ruined them.

I need the actual speech, not a seminar: five to seven minutes, for a room that's mostly her hospital colleagues and a large, entirely non-academic family who have never heard the word "supererogation" and won't be starting now, plus three or four of those reading-group friends who will notice immediately if the concept gets mangled. Could you write it so that it praises what she did as genuinely optional — and therefore more remarkable, not less — while also making the case, not just asserting it as a caveat, that it was available to any of us and doesn't leave the rest of the room quietly indicted for having lived smaller lives? I'd like her own philosophical fussiness to actually shape the structure of the eulogy, the way a real constraint should, rather than turning up as one explanatory paragraph before the speech moves on to the usual material.
