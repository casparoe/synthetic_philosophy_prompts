The question was: "Sen argues that neither equality of welfare nor equality of resources is the right space for egalitarian justice, and proposes the capability approach instead. Using the case of a person with a serious physical disability and a person with expensive tastes (say, someone who's cultivated a taste for rare wine), explain why Sen thinks welfare-based and resource-based views each go wrong, and how capabilities are supposed to handle the two cases differently."

Here's the student's answer:

"Dworkin's theory of equality of welfare says resources should be distributed so everyone ends up equally satisfied. The problem case is expensive tastes: if we equalize welfare, the wine lover needs more resources than everyone else just to reach the same satisfaction, which seems to reward them for cultivating costly preferences. Dworkin thinks this is unfair since we shouldn't compensate people for tastes they're responsible for forming, so he moves to equality of resources instead, using his auction-and-hypothetical-insurance scheme to work out fair shares while still being sensitive to genuine disability.

Sen thinks the disabled person is the hard case for resourcism: two people with identical resource bundles can differ hugely in what they're actually able to do or be, since turning resources into valuable activity depends on personal and social circumstances - giving a wheelchair user the same income as anyone else doesn't make them equally able to get around or participate in social life. So equal resources doesn't guarantee equal capability.

Sen's fix is to shift attention to capabilities: not the resources someone has, but the real freedom they have to achieve valuable "functionings" (states and doings like being mobile or being well-nourished). Since Sen builds his view around his own fixed list of central capabilities - including bodily health, affiliation, and control over one's environment - he can say justice requires bringing everyone up to a minimum threshold on each item on that list. That handles the disability case, since it means giving the wheelchair user extra resources to hit the threshold, without rewarding the wine lover, since expensive tastes are just preferences over already-adequate functioning rather than a capability shortfall."
