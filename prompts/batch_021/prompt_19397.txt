A direct-to-consumer genetic testing company recently sent me a polygenic risk score for a condition that runs in my family: a single percentage presented as my personal lifetime risk. On reading the methodology notes, I learned that this figure depends entirely on which reference cohort the underlying model was trained and validated on, and that an equally defensible choice of comparison population would have produced a noticeably different number for the very same genome. That discovery led me into the philosophical literature on the reference class problem. Most discussions I have found treat it as a specific embarrassment for frequentist interpretations of probability, on the assumption that a Bayesian who simply reports her actual credence, rather than an estimated population frequency, sidesteps the issue entirely. I would like you to argue against that assumption: make the strongest case you can that the reference class problem reappears, in essentially the same form, for subjective probability once we ask which population statistics a rational credence is answerable to, so that retreating to Bayesianism is not the clean escape it is often taken to be.
