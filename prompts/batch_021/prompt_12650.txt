I'm the co-president of my school's philosophy and puzzles club, and at our last meeting we spent almost the entire hour on Newcomb's problem. In the classic setup, a nearly infallible predictor has already either placed $1,000,000 in an opaque box B or left it empty, depending on whether it foresaw you choosing only box B or choosing both B and a second, transparent box A that always visibly holds $1,000; the standard dispute is over whether to one-box (take only B) or two-box (take both) once the boxes are sitting in front of you. One of our members, Marisol, then brought up a variant she'd read about where box B is glass too, so you can actually see whether the million is there before you choose, and the predictor's rule is adjusted to match: it fills B only if it predicts that you would take just B even after seeing it full. This set off a genuinely heated argument between two other members, Kavi and Renata, that the rest of the club ended up debating for the remainder of the meeting, and that our advisor eventually resolved only by shrugging and saying "you're both sort of right" — which nobody found very satisfying, so I was hoping you might do better.

Kavi went first, arguing that the transparent version makes two-boxing even more obviously correct than the original: once you can see the million sitting in box B with your own eyes, taking box A too is simply free money, since nothing you choose now can reach back and change what's already inside B. Renata replied that this reasoning defeats itself, because if the predictor foresees that you're the sort of person who grabs both boxes upon seeing B full, it will just never fill B for you in the first place — so a committed two-boxer only ever gets to look at an empty box B and walks away with $1,000, meaning the only people who ever actually get to stare at a full box B are those who would take just B even then. Kavi granted that going in with a one-boxing disposition might be the better policy to have, but insisted that's a separate question from what you should do at the actual moment of choice, once the boxes are already open and B's contents are causally locked in one way or the other; he wants to distinguish "which policy to adopt beforehand" from "which act to perform once you're standing there," and says only the second question is what rational choice, strictly speaking, is about. Renata's comeback was that this distinction is exactly the problem: an agent who plans to one-box but privately intends to grab both boxes the moment honoring that plan becomes inconvenient isn't the sort of agent a good predictor would ever be fooled by in the first place, and a theory of rational choice that tells you to abandon your own plan precisely when keeping it stops being convenient isn't really describing rational agency — just describing how to guarantee you end up poor.

Could you go through Kavi and Renata's exchange move by move and say who has the better of the argument at each stage, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I would also be curious whether you think there is some further consideration about the transparent-boxes case — something neither of them raised — that should actually be doing more work in this debate.
