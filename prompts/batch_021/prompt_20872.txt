So I keep running into "Good's theorem" in decision theory contexts on LW and in ACX comment threads, usually deployed as this settled, almost trivial fact: a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer should never expect free information to make you worse off, i.e. the expected value of information is always >= 0 (this is the I.J. Good result, from the 60s I think). I get the rough shape of the proof -- something about the "best you can do given your current credences" value function being convex in those credences, so averaging over the different posteriors you might end up with can only push expected value up relative to your prior, Jensen's-inequality style -- but I don't actually have a gut-level feel for why that's the right way to formalize "ignorance isn't bliss." I can imagine a possible world where that convexity fact just happens to be true of the math without capturing anything deep about rationality, and I honestly can't tell if that's the world we're in.

What's actually bugging me more is that I keep running into the opposite intuition treated as equally obvious in nearby corners of the same communities. There's Bostrom's stuff on infohazards, there's Hirshleifer's old point that letting peoples individual risk get resolved before insurance contracts can be written can wreck the whole market and leave everyone worse off even though the information itself is free and accurate, and there's general game-theory folklore about setups (bargaining, some zero-sum games) where being visibly better-informed makes you worse off because of how it changes what everyone else does. None of these feel like they're just "well sure, but that information wasn't actually free" -- they feel like they're genuinely denying the substance of Good's theorem rather than falling outside some scope condition it never claimed to cover. Could you actually walk me through why Good's theorem is true in a way that makes it feel obviously true and not just algebraically true, and then use that same walkthrough to pin down exactly which premise each of those cases is quietly dropping? I'd like to come away with an actual sense of where the boundary is, not just a pile of "gotcha" examples.
