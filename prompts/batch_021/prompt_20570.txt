So I've been auditing a few sessions of a CS colleague's AI-safety reading group, mostly to steal material for my epistemology seminar, and a couple weeks ago we worked through that old ARC report on "eliciting latent knowledge" -- Christiano and co, I think, from 2021. You know the setup: you train a "predictor" that gets really good at guessing what a camera will show you (say, whether a diamond is still sitting in its vault), and it ends up with some rich internal model of the world that outruns what you can read off the video. Then you bolt on a "reporter" that's supposed to answer questions about what's actually going on using the predictor's internals. The scary case is the vault thing, I think they called it SmartVault -- where a smart enough adversary tampers with the camera so the feed shows the diamond safe and sound even though it's long gone, and a reporter trained the naive way just learns to be a "human simulator": it tells you whatever a human watching the tape would conclude, instead of being a genuine "direct translator" that reports what the predictor itself represents about the diamond's actual whereabouts. Half the report is just them proposing training tricks to force the direct translator and then finding a way the human simulator could still win.

What's been rattling around in my head since is that this is basically Quine's indeterminacy of translation wearing a lab coat -- the whole distinction presupposes there's a determinate fact about what the network "really" represents, for the direct translator to latch onto and the human simulator to merely mimic, and I don't think that survives Quine, or Dennett's line on the intentional stance, particularly cleanly. I said as much to my colleague and we actually ended up disagreeing properly: she thinks interpretability work gives you a kind of evidence Quine's radical translator never had -- you're not stuck with behavior, you can look at the actual wiring and its causal role -- so the indeterminacy is overstated once you have that. I think that just relocates the same interpretive problem onto labeling the wiring, and doesnt dissolve it, worst case is worst case.

Could you write me the dialogue we should have had, between two people holding those two positions -- call them the Interpretability Optimist and the Indeterminacy Skeptic if you need handles? I want real engagement: the Optimist needs a substantive answer to the Quine/Dennett worry rather than just gesturing at neuroscience, and the Skeptic has to actually deal with the Optimist's best case for why causal/interpretive evidence about internals is different in kind from mere behavioral evidence, not just repeat "but indeterminacy" forever. Don't feel obliged to wrap it up neatly -- a hard-won partial concession on each side is more honest than a clean winner.
