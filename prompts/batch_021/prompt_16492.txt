This semester I'm taking a seminar cross-listed between the philosophy and comparative literature departments, "Time, Narrative, and Reality," and for our unit on literary treatments of time our professor paired Borges's essay "A New Refutation of Time" (written in the 1940s and later collected in Otras inquisiciones) with excerpts from the physicist Julian Barbour's 1999 book The End of Time. Both texts end up denying that time truly passes, but by very different routes. Borges gets there through a Berkeleyan denial of matter combined with a Humean denial of any persisting self: if each perceived moment is independent and self-sufficient, then "succession," and hence time, is only a habit of the mind rather than a fact about the world — yet the essay's famous closing lines have Borges admitting that the argument brings him no real relief, since he still cannot stop feeling that he himself is somehow made of time. Barbour reaches a similarly startling conclusion from the opposite direction, arguing from the timelessness of the Wheeler-DeWitt equation and a strict Machian relationalism that the fundamental reality is a static, mind-independent space of instantaneous configurations he calls "Platonia," in which the impression of an elapsed past is just a structural feature of certain unusually rich configurations ("time capsules") rather than anything that actually happened.

For my final paper, I want to stage an imagined encounter between the two, since Barbour's book appeared more than a decade after Borges died and Borges never had the chance to respond to it himself. Would he have welcomed Barbour as someone who had finally given his own literary intuition a rigorous physical grounding, or would he have resisted the comparison — for instance, by insisting that a "block" of eternally existing, mind-independent configurations is really just eternalism dressed up in new language, and so is quite different from his own idealist dissolution of time and the self into private, ownerless perceptions? I'm also curious whether the melancholy admission that closes Borges's essay, that no argument, however sound, relieves him of the felt reality of time, would have survived contact with Barbour's physics, or whether Borges might have had further, distinctly literary reasons for resisting Barbour's picture even while granting its logic. Since we have been using presentism and eternalism as our main reference points all semester, I would also appreciate your sense of where his resulting position would actually fall relative to that distinction, or whether it resists that distinction altogether.
