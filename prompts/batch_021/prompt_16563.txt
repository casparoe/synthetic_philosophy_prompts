So a while back I fell down this random rabbit hole reading about A.J. Ayer, the logical positivist guy, and found out something pretty wild: sometime in 1988 he choked on a piece of smoked salmon while he was in hospital and his heart actually stopped for a few minutes, and afterward he wrote this essay about it where he described seeing a bright red light that felt like it was "in charge" of the universe, plus some kind of officials (I think he called them something like ministers) who'd been put in charge of space and time and had messed up their jobs. The funny part is this is the exact same guy who built his whole career on the idea that a claim only really means something if you can say what observation would count as verifying it, and who was famous for basically arguing that talk of souls surviving death doesn't hold up under that test. Afterward he said he hadn't started believing in God or an afterlife in any religious sense, but that the experience had slightly shaken his confidence that death is just the end, full stop.

That got me wondering what he'd say about stuff that obviously didn't exist yet in his lifetime. There are companies now building chatbots trained on a dead persons old texts, emails and voice notes so family members can keep "talking" to them, and there's serious people in AI and longevity circles who think that eventually you'll be able to scan someone's brain and run it as software, and some of them straight up call that a route to life after death. Given how strict Ayer was about verification, and given that his whole account of personal identity seemed to hang on the continuity of someone's actual experience rather than some unchanging soul, what do you think he'd say about whether a really convincing chatbot version, or down the line a full brain upload, could count as that person surviving in any sense he'd accept? Or would he say the entire question is kind of empty/unverifiable in a new way, since how would anyone (including the "copy" itself) ever confirm there's actually someone home having experiences, as opposed to just very good behavior? Would love an answer that actually works through his specific arguments rather than just concluding "he was an atheist so he'd say no."
