I've spent the past few weeks reporting a longform piece on what epistemologists call the "value problem" — the puzzle of explaining why knowledge should count as better, or more worth having, than mere true belief, given that both seem to serve us equally well in practice. Several philosophers I interviewed for the piece kept steering me back to the same source: Plato's Meno, where Socrates first poses the puzzle using the example of a guide who can get you to the city of Larissa. Rereading the passage slowly, in the old Jowett translation that circulates freely online, I found that the argument Socrates actually offers there is considerably slipperier than the tidy one-paragraph summaries in the secondary literature suggest. Here is the bit I keep circling back to (Meno, roughly 97a–98a):

"Soc. And if a man knew the way to Larissa, or anywhere else, and went there, and led others with him, would he not be a right and good guide?
Men. Certainly.
Soc. And a man who had a right opinion as to which was the way, but had never been there and did not know, might he not also be a good guide, if he thought the truth?
Men. Certainly he might.
Soc. And as long as he has right opinion about that which the other has knowledge, he will be just as good a guide, believing the truth, though he does not know it, as he who has the knowledge?
Men. Exactly.
Soc. Then true opinion is in no way a worse guide to correct action than knowledge... For true opinions, as long as they remain, are a fine thing and do all sorts of good; but they will not stay long, and they run away out of the soul of man, and thus are of no great value until they are fastened by the giving of a reason [aitias logismos]. And this fastening of them, friend Meno, is recollection, as you and I have agreed to call it. But when they have been bound, in the first place they have the nature of knowledge; and in the second place they are abiding. And this is why knowledge is more honourable and excellent than opinion — because it is fastened by a chain."

What I can't quite work out is what kind of upgrade this "fastening" is actually supposed to be, and why it would make the belief more valuable rather than simply more durable. On one reading, tethering a true opinion just makes it more secure — less a matter of luck — so that it would go on delivering correct guidance across many more possible trips to Larissa, not just the one Meno and Socrates are discussing. On another reading, something more like grasping a reasoned account (a logos) is doing the work, which sounds like a different kind of achievement altogether, not simply a hardier version of the same lucky guess. Either way, I can't see why the upgrade would make the resulting state more valuable in itself, as opposed to just a better bet for next time: on the single trip actually under discussion, the lucky guide and the knowledgeable one arrive at exactly the same place, by exactly the same route. I'd also welcome help with a smaller, related puzzle: Socrates casually identifies this "fastening" with the very "recollection" he had invoked many pages earlier to solve Meno's paradox about the impossibility of inquiry. Is that meant as a substantive claim — that whatever process explains how we can learn anything at all is the very same process that upgrades opinion into knowledge — or is he just recycling a convenient word for two unrelated jobs? I'd be grateful for a close reading of what's actually being argued in this passage, and whether it succeeds in showing that knowledge is more valuable, and not merely more stable, than true belief.
