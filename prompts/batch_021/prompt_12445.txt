My aunt, who taught modal logic for almost forty years, died ten days ago, and I've been asked to give her eulogy at a service split between a handful of her old logic-department colleagues and a much larger extended family who have never heard of a modal operator. She was an atheist to the end, but her favorite thing to hold forth on at family dinners for decades was Gödel's ontological proof — not because she believed its conclusion, but because she thought it was one of the most elegant failures in the history of logic. She had little patience for the standard 'existence is not a predicate' complaint, and much more for the modal collapse problem: the fact that Gödel's own axioms seem to entail that every truth whatsoever is a necessary one. She also relished pointing out that the 2013 story about computer scientists running the proof through an automated theorem prover — which resurfaces every so often under headlines like 'computer scientists prove God exists' — only ever showed that the argument was valid, never that its premises were true, which was, to her, the whole point. Before she died she told my mother two things the eulogy must not do: it must not imply she found God at the end, and it must not turn her favorite argument into a punchline. Could you write it? About five minutes read aloud, genuinely comforting to a room that is mostly not philosophers, and precise enough that her old colleagues don't wince.
