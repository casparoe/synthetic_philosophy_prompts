Been reading around late Mill (the unfinished Chapters on Socialism, the later editions of Principles of Political Economy, bits of the Autobiography) and jotted down some notes on how I think his view hangs together. Would love comments on whether this is accurate / fair to him, or if I'm smoothing over real tensions he doesn't actually resolve.

My basic take: by the 1850s-60s Mill had drifted a long way from the "let competition rip" economics of his early Political Economy. He recants the wage-fund theory in 1869 (which opens the door to unions actually raising wages, not just redistributing the same misery around), and keeps beefing up the chapter on the "probable futurity of the labouring classes" with more sympathy for worker cooperatives eventually replacing the wage-relationship altogether. By the time of the unfinished Chapters on Socialism he's willing to grant that the socialists' critique of distribution - the accident of birth deciding who owns capital and who just sells labour - is basically right, even though he's still doubtful of Owenite/Fourierist schemes on the production side.

What I find interesting is that Mill never asks us to just trust theory (his own or anyone else's) on whether cooperative production actually works better than the standard capitalist firm. His answer is always: let small groups try it voluntarily and see what happens. That seems structurally identical to the "experiments in living" argument from On Liberty - we don't know in advance which modes of life are best, so the state's job is protecting a space where different arrangements can be tried and compared. So I want to argue late-Mill's socialism isn't really a change in his fundemental commitments, its the same liberal epistemic humility just applied to economic organization instead of personal life choices.

The bit I can't fully square: Mill never drops his Malthusian worries about population, and seems to think cooperative/socialist arrangements only really work if people have already internalised serious prudential restraint on family size - otherwise common ownership just removes whatever pressure currently keeps population in check. That feels in tension with how optimistic he is elsewhere about people's capacity to voluntarily cooperate. Is that a genuine unresolved inconsistency in his position, or is there a more standard way of reading how these two pieces of his thought fit together that I'm missing?

Keen for pushback on any of this, especially the "experiments in living" parallel - not sure if that's actually a well-known reading or if I'm just projecting my favorite Mill idea onto a different part of his work.
