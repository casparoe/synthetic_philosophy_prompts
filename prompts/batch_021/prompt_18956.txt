Question (20 pts total - 8/8/4): Kripke argues that ordinary proper names are rigid designators. Using the Hesperus/Phosphorus case, explain (a) why a true identity statement between two rigid designators has to be necessary, (b) why such a statement can still only be known a posteriori, and (c) how this bears on the necessity of theoretical identities like "water is H2O."

Sample answer:
Kripke, who borrows the term "rigid designator" from Donnellan's earlier work on the referential vs. attributive use of descriptions, defines it as an expression that picks out the same object in every possible world (or at least every world where that object exists). Ordinary proper names work this way, whereas most definite descriptions don't, since something like "the Morning Star" could in principle have been satisfied by a different object in a different world. "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" are both names for Venus, so they're both rigid designators for the same object. Given the necessity of identity (if a=b, it's necessarily true that a=b) plus the fact that both names are rigid, "Hesperus is Phosphorus" has to be necessary if it's true at all - there's no possible world where Venus is Hesperus but isn't Phosphorus.

That necessity doesn't make the sentence knowable a priori, though. "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" were introduced via different descriptions (the evening appearance vs. the morning appearance), so ancient astronomers who understood both names perfectly well still needed actual observation to discover they picked out the same planet. Since the two names differ in how their reference was fixed but agree in what they refer to, the identity statement ends up being analytic - true in virtue of the meanings of the terms - even though nobody could've known it was true without empirical work. This is Kripke's category of the necessary a posteriori, and it's the main reason he thinks the traditional assumption that necessity and apriority go together has to be given up.

The same pattern covers "water is H2O." If "water" and "H2O" both rigidly designate the same substance, the identity is necessary once true, even though it obviously took chemistry rather than reflection on meanings to discover it. So there's no possible world with water that isn't H2O, even though we can easily conceive of a substance that looks and acts like water but isn't - for Kripke that just shows conceivability and possibility have come apart, not that being H2O is inessential to water.
