Okay so I've been on a bit of a rationalist-fiction kick lately (rereading Three Worlds Collide, revisiting some of the old Sword of Good-era Yudkowsky stuff) and instead of just consuming more of it I want to try commissioning a piece of my own. The concept that's been rattling around in my head is "surrogate goals," from the acausal-trade / blackmail-resistance corner of the decision theory literature -- I think this is Oesterheld and co, though don't quote me on the attribution, my memory of who-wrote-what on this stuff is fuzzy. It's kind of Roko's basilisk's nerdier, more technical cousin: the idea, as I understand it, is that an agent (esp. a superintelligence that reasons about other agents it can never causally meet, only predict/simulate) which is vulnerable to threats because it truly cares about X can partially defang those threats by publicly and *credibly* precommitting to act as though some cheap, arbitrary Y matters exactly as much as X. A rational extortionist has no reason to threaten X anymore, since you'll just let them "have" Y instead -- but the whole scheme only holds up if the commitment to caring about Y is airtight, not just performed for an audience.

Could you write me a short story (whatever length feels right, 1000-2500 words maybe) that actually sits inside that mechanism instead of using it as background flavor for a generic AI story? Set it wherever you like -- a lone probe renegotiating its values somewhere past Neptune, a whole civilization of uploads doing this collectively, two acausal traders who've never and will never meet -- but I want the surrogate goal itself to basically become a character. What does it do to a mind (biological or otherwise) to successfully trick itself, at the deepest levels of precommitment, into caring about a prime-counting subroutine as much as it cares about its actual children? Does the ruse ever start eating the real values its supposed to be protecting? I'd rather the story sit with that discomfort than tidy it up by the end.
