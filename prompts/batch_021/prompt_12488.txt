In my philosophy of sex and love seminar, we recently read excerpts from Bertrand Russell's Marriage and Morals (1929), and I keep returning to his treatment of jealousy: rather than a fixed biological given, Russell treats its intensity as largely a product of custom and the "possessive" framing of marriage instilled by upbringing, such that a differently raised population could sustain considerably more sexual freedom without destabilizing family life. I'd like your help thinking through how he might defend or revise that view in light of a development he never saw: the emergence of polyamory as a self-conscious, organized relationship identity, complete with its own vocabulary (e.g., "compersion," "relationship anarchy") and practical literature aimed explicitly at unlearning jealousy through negotiated agreements and community support. Given that even committed, long-term practitioners in this world widely report jealousy as one of their most persistent and hard-won struggles, rather than something that simply dissolves with the right upbringing, would Russell most plausibly treat this as confirming evidence (progress is slow, but the direction is right) or as a genuine anomaly for his theory of jealousy's plasticity? Please ground your answer closely in the specific argument he makes about the sources of jealousy, rather than in his general reputation as a sexual progressive.
