I'm reporting a longer piece on the philosophical vocabulary that intelligent-design writers like to borrow from mainstream philosophy of science, and the same citation keeps surfacing: Karl Popper, in his own intellectual memoirs, conceding that Darwinian natural selection is barely more than a tautology -- "survival of the fittest" amounting, on this reading, to nothing more than "those who survive, survive" -- and that the theory is therefore closer to a metaphysical research programme than to a genuinely falsifiable scientific theory. Several of the writers I've been reading treat this as a confession from the very philosopher who set the falsifiability standard in the first place, and use it to argue that evolutionary biology as a whole has never actually cleared the bar Popper set for it, and that biologists have simply decided, by convention, to call natural selection scientific rather than doing the work of showing it meets his criterion.

I put this to an evolutionary biologist I interviewed for the piece, and she waved it away as "old news that's been dealt with," but couldn't tell me exactly how when I pressed her, and I'd rather understand the actual state of the argument than take that on faith or let it slide into print unexamined. So: granting that Popper himself reached this verdict about natural selection, on what grounds can working biologists still maintain that Darwinism clears the scientific bar he set, rather than conceding that it's the metaphysical framework his own analysis suggests it to be? I'd like to represent this fairly rather than flatten it into a soundbite for either side, so a careful account of where the dispute actually stands -- rather than just an assurance that it's fine -- would help a great deal.
