I'm a novelist working on a book in which the protagonist, Elena, periodically slips into adjacent branches of her own life -- timelines that split off from a few early decisions -- and starts to doubt whether the Elena she meets in each branch is her, or merely someone very like her. I read the chapter aloud at my writing group, and two regulars ended up in a real standoff over the metaphysics, which Priyanka, who is finishing a PhD on modal logic, later called "a textbook counterpart-theory-versus-transworld-identity fight."

Priyanka's opening claim was that the branch-Elenas can't literally be Elena: each branch is causally sealed off from the others, so at most a counterpart of her lives there -- similar enough for the story's purposes, but numerically distinct. Otherwise, she said, the book is committed to one woman simultaneously being a burned-out lawyer in one branch and a contented potter in another, and nothing can coherently have both careers at once.

Devon said that gets it backwards: if it isn't literally Elena who might have become a potter, the novel's whole premise -- that these are her roads not taken -- collapses into a story about strangers who happen to resemble her. When any of us says "I could have been a doctor," he said, we mean ourselves, not some similar stand-in.

Priyanka answered that the counterfactual only needs someone suitably similar to you being a doctor, which is no stranger than calling seven-year-old-you and forty-year-old-you "the same person" despite sharing none of the same cells -- identity-talk never demanded more than continuity and resemblance to begin with.

Devon replied that childhood and adulthood are stitched together within one continuous, causally connected life, whereas the branches split and never touch again -- so extending identity-talk to them means calling any sufficiently similar stranger "you," which nobody believes when it actually matters, such as standing trial for something a past self did.

Priyanka's rejoinder was that Devon now owes an account of what makes a branch "hers" rather than a stranger's, if not resemblance in the respects we care about -- and that if the story can simply stipulate which branches are Elena's, the same stipulation is available to fix her counterpart. Neither budged, and they both turned to me to settle it, but I only invented the branches -- I didn't referee their logic.

Could you go through the exchange move by move, say who has the better of it at each stage rather than just naming an overall winner, and flag anything philosophically important that neither of them raised?
