I spent thirty-six years as a field ecologist, mostly studying amphibian populations in montane wetlands, and only started reading philosophy and political theory in earnest after I retired four years ago, prompted, a little embarrassingly, by a former graduate student who kept sending me blog posts about prediction markets and mechanism design. That reading has now collided with my ordinary life: I sit on the nine-member board of a small regional land trust, which I joined mainly to have something useful to do with my Saturdays, and the board is at a genuine impasse.

Our acquisition budget this cycle can cover exactly one of two parcels, not both: a wetland corridor essential to two amphibian species we have monitored for over a decade, or an old-growth-adjacent stand that anchors a rare orchid population and the last local nesting pair of a threatened owl. We have argued about this for two months without resolution. A newer trustee, who left a quantitative trading career to do conservation work, has proposed that we settle it with a mechanism she calls "futarchy" and attributes to the economist Robin Hanson. The board would first vote to commit, publicly and in writing, to a single ecological-health index that an independent surveyor will measure on both properties in ten years' time, whichever we buy. We would then open two conditional betting markets to trustees and major donors, one on the index's value if we acquire the wetland and one on its value if we acquire the forest stand, and commit in advance to buying whichever parcel the higher-priced market favors. Roughly half the board welcomes this as a way of exposing that the real disagreement is factual (about which purchase actually helps biodiversity more) rather than a clash of values; the other half suspects it merely dresses up a values dispute as false precision, and points out that we would be fortunate to attract thirty willing traders, that none of us can promise to still sit on this board in ten years to honor the commitment, and that at least one major donor owns land bordering one of the two parcels.

I have been asked, as the trustee with the most quantitative background, to draft the recommendation memo the full board will vote on at next month's meeting, and I would like your help with it. It should reach a definite recommendation: adopt the mechanism as proposed, adopt some modified version of it, or reject it in favor of an alternative you specify. Whatever you recommend, please state the losing side's strongest objection in a form that side would actually recognize as a fair statement of its own view, rather than as a straw man.
