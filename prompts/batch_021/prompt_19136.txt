In "Truth and Politics" (1967) and "Lying in Politics: Reflections on the Pentagon Papers" (1971), Hannah Arendt distinguishes the ordinary political liar — someone who knows a given fact, deliberately hides or denies it, and therefore remains tethered to a shared sense of what is real — from the more modern figure of the image-maker, whose ambition is not to conceal some particular fact but to replace the factual record wholesale with a more coherent, more flattering substitute. In the Pentagon Papers essay she goes a step further, arguing that the defense intellectuals and policy planners who manufactured the official story of the Vietnam War, with their "problem-solving" approach to what were really questions of fact, ended up believing their own fabrications, because the apparatus of secrecy and bureaucratic groupthink deprived them of any outside vantage point from which the deception could have been checked. Self-deception, on her account, is what happens to accomplished liars once the community of independent witnesses that would normally correct them disappears. Crucially, the whole analysis presupposes a liar in the classical sense: someone exercising the same imaginative freedom that Arendt thought made political action possible in the first place, choosing to assert that things are otherwise than they in fact are.

I have been trying to work out how this mid-century framework should apply to large language models, a technology that emerged decades after Arendt's death in 1975 and that routinely produces confident, detailed, false statements — fabricated citations, invented quotations, nonexistent sources — with no one anywhere in the causal chain who knows the relevant fact and is choosing to suppress or alter it. This is no longer a purely hypothetical worry: fabricated citations generated by such systems have already been filed in real court briefs by attorneys who did not verify their research, so that falsehoods have entered the institutional record without any human intending to deceive.

Given all this, how do you think Arendt would have classified this phenomenon? Would she have treated it as a genuinely new category, distinct from both the traditional liar and the image-maker, or would she have assimilated it to her account of self-deception, on the grounds that the people building and relying on these systems are doing to themselves and each other what her Vietnam-era policy planners did? And if she held firm to the idea that lying, properly speaking, requires exactly the capacity for freedom and imaginative negation she considered distinctive of human beings, would she have concluded that "lying" is simply the wrong word for what these systems introduce into our common world — so that the danger they pose to a shared factual reality would need describing in the vocabulary she reserved for totalitarian propaganda's assault on the distinction between truth and falsehood, rather than in the vocabulary of the individual lie?
