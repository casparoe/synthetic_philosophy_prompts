I'm several drafts into a novel whose narrator is a widower recovering from cataract surgery who becomes fixated on the idea that his eyes are lying to him. It starts almost comically -- a walking stick that looks bent through a glass of water on his kitchen table, a coin that looks elliptical from where he happens to be sitting -- and slowly darkens into something closer to grief-hallucination, as he begins glimpsing his late wife in crowds and can't quite talk himself out of it in the moment. I want the philosophical scaffolding underneath this to be more than gestural, so I've spent the last couple of weeks reading around naive realism and disjunctivism, and I've run into a distinction I can't cleanly resolve on my own.

As far as I can tell, the classical argument from illusion (the bent stick, the elliptical coin) and the argument from hallucination (a subjectively perfect but wholly objectless experience) tend to get folded together into a single case against naive realism, but they seem to put quite different pressure on a disjunctivist trying to defend it. In the illusion case there's still an ordinary stick out there being perceived, just under misleading conditions, so it isn't obvious to me why the disjunctivist can't simply reject the inference from "looks bent" to "something bent is present to the mind" and otherwise leave direct, relational perception of the stick alone. The hallucination case seems to demand the more distinctively disjunctivist move: denying that the veridical case and the hallucinatory case share any common underlying mental kind at all, rather than just denying a dubious inference about looks. So my first question is whether these two arguments really call for two different disjunctivist strategies, and if so, whether a "moderate" disjunctivist who grants a common factor for illusions but withholds it for hallucinations occupies a stable position, or whether conceding the illusion case has already let a sense-datum theorist back in through the window.

Second, I'd like help placing Duncan Pritchard's "epistemological disjunctivism" on this map. Is he offering a way of preserving the epistemic payoff that disjunctivists want -- roughly, that only the veridical perceiver has factive rational support for her belief -- while remaining agnostic on the metaphysical dispute between McDowell- and Martin-style disjunctivists and their common-factor opponents? Or does his view covertly need the metaphysical thesis to be true after all, so that the "epistemological" label is doing less independent work than it appears to? I'm not after a survey of the whole debate here -- just enough of a working model of how these pieces fit together that I can trust my narrator's confusions to be the right confusions, rather than a philosophy student's straw man of them.
