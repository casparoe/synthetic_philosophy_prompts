So my friend Dev and I got into it for like two hours last night over something called "commitment races" that I ran into on a rationalist blog. The idea, as far as I understood it (and I might be mangling some of the details), is basically this: if two agents can bind themselves to a strategy in advance — like a general burning his own ships so retreat isn't an option anymore, or the old game-of-chicken trick where the "rational" move is to rip out your own steering wheel and throw it out the window before the other guy does — then whoever commits first and hardest usually wins the standoff, because the other side has no choice left but to adapt to your fixed position. The nasty part is that once both sides realize this, you get a race to commit as early and as irreversibly as possible, often before either side even understands the situation well enough to know if the commitment they're locking in is a good idea at all.

Dev's position was that this is just brinksmanship with a new coat of paint, and that total commitment is basically never fully credible anyway, so real agents always keep a little wiggle room. I think he's underrating how things change once you take the humans partly out of the loop — two trading algorithms, or two AI systems negotiating on behalf of rival labs, or countries with automated retaliation baked in, might genuinely not have that wiggle room, and that's exactly what makes the dynamic scary rather than just an old Cold War metaphor. Neither of us budged, and I went to bed annoyed.

What I'd actually like from you is a short story (doesn't need to be long, flash-fiction length is fine) built around the actual mechanics of a commitment race, not just a generic "two stubborn sides refuse to blink" standoff. I want it to earn the tragedy a bit — showing concretely why racing to commit early leaves everyone worse off than waiting and talking would have, ideally with a moment where a character sees the trap closing in real time and realizes that even backing down now would itself be just another costly, hard-to-reverse commitment. Setting is totally up to you — rival AIs, nations with itchy automated triggers, feuding companies, even two petty individuals in a much smaller standoff — whatever lets you make the logic of the trap feel real instead of abstract.
