So I've kind of fallen down a rabbit hole lately reading about how scientists (and detectives, and doctors doing diagnosis) supposedly reason their way to conclusions — this thing called "inference to the best explanation," basically: whichever hypothesis would, if it were true, explain the evidence better than the alternatives, is the one you should believe. Seemed pretty solid to me at first, like, isnt that just what good reasoning looks like? But then I stumbled on some old philosophy of science writing (forget exactly where, some blog quoting an older philosopher) making the point that this method has a built-in flaw: all it can ever tell you is which hypothesis is best among the ones you actually thought up, not whether that hypothesis is true, or even probably true — because the real explanation might not even be sitting in your little pile of "candidates" to begin with. I think this gets called the "bad lot" problem or something close to that.

Here's where I'm stuck. On a gut level I don't fully get why this is supposed to be such a devastating problem specifically for inference to the best explanation. Doesn't literally every kind of reasoning under uncertainty have this same issue — plain old induction, ordinary probability updating, whatever — since those also only ever range over whatever hypotheses happen to be on your radar? And apparently people also use this exact "bad lot" worry as a weapon against scientific realism, since the standard argument for realism (that our best theories are probably at least roughly true, because otherwise their success would be a "miracle") is itself basically an inference to the best explanation. So if the bad lot problem sinks IBE in general, doesnt it also sink the realist's favorite argument for taking science's picture of the world seriously? I guess what I want is someone to walk me through why this "best available vs. actually true" gap is supposed to bite so much harder here than in the reasoning we rely on constantly without a second thought — is there something special about explanatory reasoning in particular, or did everyone just kind of forget the problem is more general than that?
