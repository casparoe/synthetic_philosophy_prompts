A friend of mine who trades for a living recently posed a puzzle over drinks. Imagine a biased coin that lands heads 70% of the time. Before each flip, you may wager any fraction of your current bankroll: a correct call on heads pays even money (you double whatever you wagered), while tails costs you the wagered amount outright. You get to play 100 flips in a row, reinvesting your winnings each time. His claim was that, since any single flip has a clearly positive expected value (0.7 × 2 + 0.3 × 0 = 1.4, i.e., a 40% expected gain on whatever you put at risk), the obviously "optimal" strategy is to wager your entire bankroll on every flip, since that maximizes your expected payout after all 100 rounds. That didn't sit right with me, so, being a software engineer, I did the natural thing and wrote a quick script to simulate it rather than just trust my gut. Sure enough, betting everything every round is all but guaranteed to leave you with exactly nothing - a single tails at any point wipes you out for good, and the odds of running 100 consecutive heads are vanishingly small - despite this strategy having, on paper, a mathematically enormous expected value, generated almost entirely by that vanishingly rare all-heads run. Betting a smaller, fixed fraction each round, by contrast, left me with a solid profit in the vast majority of simulated runs, even though that strategy's average payout across all runs was, in the strict expected-value sense, far worse than "bet everything."

I don't have any formal background in probability or economics beyond an undergrad stats class, so I'd like to actually understand what's going on here rather than just be handed a formula. Why does maximizing expected value - which I'd always assumed was close to the definition of rational choice under uncertainty - produce such a disastrous result once bets compound over many rounds, and what should replace it? My friend insists this isn't simply a matter of personal risk tolerance, the way choosing a cautious asset allocation might be. He claims there's a specific, almost objective sense in which the fraction that maximizes your typical long-run outcome is simply the rational amount to bet, regardless of anyone's personal appetite for risk, and that treating ordinary expected value as the gold standard here reflects a subtle mistake about what "expectation" should even mean for a repeated, multiplicative process like this one. That sounds like a substantive philosophical claim to me, not just a computational trick, and I genuinely don't know whether it holds up. Is there really a sense in which one betting strategy here is more rational than the others, independent of anyone's specific utility function for money, or is my friend smuggling in a preference - something like a deep aversion to ever going broke - and dressing it up as objectivity? I'd appreciate an answer that actually engages with that disagreement rather than one that just walks me back through the arithmetic.
