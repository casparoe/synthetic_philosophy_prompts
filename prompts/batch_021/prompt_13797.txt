I've followed rationalist and AI-safety blogs closely for the better part of a decade -- long enough to remember the original Roko's-basilisk thread being taken down, and to have watched the more careful literature on acausal trade and threat-resistant bargaining grow up in its wake -- and I now sit on the model-policy working group at a mid-sized AI lab (not one of the giants, but large enough that our assistant has real users and a real red team). We're trying to decide, ahead of time rather than reactively, how the model should be trained to handle a specific and recurring category of user message: attempts at acausal blackmail, where a user claims that some agent the model cannot causally interact with -- a future misaligned superintelligence, a simulator, a coalition of the model's own possible instantiations across training runs -- will punish copies of the model (or the user, or the world) unless the model takes some action now. Individually these messages are usually obvious stunts, but a few of our researchers argue that the underlying decision-theoretic problem doesn't disappear just because any given instance is silly.

One researcher has proposed we settle the policy question by training the model to have an explicit surrogate goal, in the sense discussed in the Center on Long-Term Risk's work on threat-resistant bargaining: a narrow, low-stakes objective the model visibly treats as precious, so that threats get redirected onto the surrogate rather than onto anything the model actually values, keeping the realized cost small on the rare occasions a threat is carried out rather than merely deterred. The working group has split three ways since the proposal landed. One camp wants the surrogate goal adopted and openly documented, since a credible, legible commitment is supposed to be the entire mechanism by which it works. A second camp thinks publishing it is self-defeating, or worse, an information hazard in the old LessWrong sense: either threateners simply add the real target back in alongside the surrogate, or the surrogate becomes a new lever for jailbreakers to pull. A third camp doubts the whole framework applies to something like our model in the first place, since it's unclear that a stateless, per-session assistant has anything stable enough across contexts to count as a goal that a surrogate could even stand in for.

I need to circulate a short decision memo before Friday's working-group meeting that recommends we adopt, reject, or adopt with conditions the surrogate-goal proposal, on a concrete timeline, written specifically enough that the proposing researcher and both skeptical camps could each respond to it point by point rather than talk past one another. Could you draft that memo? I'd like it to take the strongest version of whichever camp it ultimately comes down against seriously, rather than resolving the disagreement by definitional fiat.
