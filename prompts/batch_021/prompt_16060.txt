My friend and I spent a long evening arguing about Gottlob Frege after I mentioned something I had recently learned about him. Frege, one of the founders of modern logic and the philosophy of language, was long regarded by admirers such as Michael Dummett as the model of a dispassionate, purely rational thinker. Yet a diary from the final months of his life, left out of his collected papers and brought to light only decades later, revealed him to be a virulent antisemite, contemptuous of parliamentary democracy, and sympathetic to a party Dummett himself considered a forerunner of the Nazis—notwithstanding his warm relations, in real life, with individual Jewish students such as Gershom Scholem. My friend concluded that we should now read Frege's philosophical work with real suspicion, since the mind behind it evidently was not as detached from prejudice as its impersonal style suggests; I wasn't persuaded, but struggled to explain exactly why the diary shouldn't matter to how we judge the philosophy itself. What do you take to be the most important implications of a case like this for how much weight a thinker's personal character and private convictions should carry when we assess work that presents itself as a matter of pure argument rather than personality?
