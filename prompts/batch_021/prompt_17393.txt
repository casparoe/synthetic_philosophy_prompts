As a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or jurisprudence, I recently read about Lon Fuller's eight "principles of legality": that laws should be general, publicly promulgated, prospective rather than retroactive, clear, internally consistent, possible to comply with, reasonably stable over time, and actually administered the way they are officially declared. It struck me how closely this list tracks ordinary best practices for writing and maintaining a good technical specification, which is part of why I'm skeptical of Fuller's claim that satisfying these eight principles amounts to an "inner morality of law." H.L.A. Hart pressed essentially this skepticism, arguing that Fuller's principles are really just conditions for efficacious rule-following as such, available to a regime bent on the most monstrous ends just as readily as to a just one, so that "morality" is the wrong word for them. Argue for Hart's view over Fuller's on this point, making sure to directly address what I take to be Fuller's strongest response: that a legal system which rigorously satisfied all eight principles could not, as a practical matter, remain thoroughly wicked.
