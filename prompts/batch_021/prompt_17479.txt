At a student council meeting last month, our class president—who is late to almost every meeting herself—publicly reprimanded another officer for walking in ten minutes late to a vote. When several of us pointed out that she does the exact same thing on a weekly basis, she replied that her own lateness doesn't make it any less true that the other officer's lateness was a problem. This set off a long argument among my friends: some thought she had a point, since showing that someone is a hypocrite doesn't actually show that their criticism is mistaken, while others felt she had basically forfeited any right to say anything. Out of curiosity, I brought this up with my ethics elective teacher, who explained that philosophers who write on the ethics of blame treat this as a genuine puzzle about what they call the "standing to blame," which they distinguish from whether the blame is apt (that is, whether the target really did something wrong). She pointed me to G.A. Cohen's essay "Casting the First Stone," which asks who has the standing to condemn terrorists, and which argues that a critic's own hypocrisy can undercut their position to blame even when the substance of their complaint is entirely correct.

I would like you to write an essay that weighs the main arguments on both sides of this controversy over hypocrisy and the standing to blame, and that reaches a definite verdict rather than ending on the fence. In your reasoning, please address: (1) whether "lacking standing" is really a separate failure from the blame itself being false or unjustified, or whether talk of "standing" is just a confused way of saying the criticism is somehow out of place; (2) whether the classic "tu quoque" reply ("you do it too!") is always a fallacious deflection from the target's own wrongdoing, or whether it can be a legitimate objection to the critic; and (3) whether it matters that our class president is a current, unrepentant offender rather than, say, a reformed one (imagine instead that she used to be chronically late but had fixed the habit before criticizing others). Please end by clearly stating whether, and under exactly what conditions, hypocrisy actually removes—rather than merely makes it awkward to voice—a person's standing to blame someone else for the same fault.
