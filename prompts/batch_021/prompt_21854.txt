The Church-Turing thesis is treated as remarkably trustworthy for something that has never been proved as a theorem, and for a while now I've been trying to understand why. The more I read about it, the clearer it becomes that Church and Turing didn't actually argue for the thesis in the same way, even though it now gets presented under both their names as a single settled package. Church's 1936 argument leans heavily on the fact that his lambda calculus and the independently developed Herbrand-Gödel notion of general recursiveness turned out to define exactly the same class of functions, treating that convergence of unrelated formal approaches as evidence that the class is a natural one rather than an arbitrary technical artefact. Turing's argument instead works by directly analysing what a human being is doing when carrying out a calculation by hand: breaking the process into elementary steps constrained by things like how many symbols can be told apart at a glance and how many distinct "states of mind" a person can reliably keep track of, then showing that anything meeting those constraints can be carried out by one of his machines. Could you compare these two lines of argument in some depth: what kind of evidence each one is really offering, and why Turing's is usually regarded as the more rigorous of the two? I'm also curious whether this difference bears on how the thesis itself should be classified: as an empirical claim, a piece of conceptual analysis, a stipulative definition, or something that resists all three labels.
