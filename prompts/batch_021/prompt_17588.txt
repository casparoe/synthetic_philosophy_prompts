And here's the student's answer:

"On the simple counterfactual theory, Mara's charge caused the collapse only if the collapse wouldn't have happened without it. But that's false here: if Mara's charge hadn't fired, Tom's would have gone off a second later and brought the building down anyway, so the collapse doesn't counterfactually depend on Mara's charge. By the same reasoning it doesn't depend on Tom's charge either, since Mara's would have been enough on its own. So the simple theory can't distinguish the actual cause from the preempted backup, which is exactly backwards from our judgment that Mara's charge, not Tom's, caused the collapse.

The interventionist avoids this by building a causal model with a variable for each charge and asking what happens to the collapse under an intervention that sets Mara's charge to 'doesn't fire,' while also setting Tom's charge to 'doesn't fire' so we're holding the whole circuit fixed at its actual state. Since neither charge goes off, the building stays up, so the collapse does depend on Mara's charge after all. This is really the chief advantage interventionism has over the counterfactual approach generally: only a causal-modeling framework can individuate the effect finely enough to actually solve preemption cases like this one."
