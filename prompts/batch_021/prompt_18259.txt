I spent most of my working life as an astrophysicist, modeling stellar populations in globular clusters, and it is only since retiring that I have properly turned to philosophy -- largely through a small discussion group of former colleagues that has, somewhat to our own amusement, taken to recording its monthly meetings as a podcast. Next month's guest is a philosopher of probability who defends the classical Carter-Leslie doomsday argument in its Bayesian form: the claim that simply noting one's rank among all the humans who will ever be born licenses a genuine update toward a shorter expected future for the species. I confess I find it rather pointed that Brandon Carter, who first floated the idea, was a physicist best known for his work on black hole uniqueness theorems and for coining the term "anthropic principle," and I suspect our guest, like most defenders of the argument, will lean on that pedigree to make the whole thing sound like applied statistics rather than a piece of metaphysics dressed up in Bayes' theorem.

I have been asked to draft the questions myself, since I am apparently the only one in the group who will not be intimidated by the probability theory involved. Could you write me eight to ten questions that go well beyond the popular-science version of the argument already available in any number of explainer videos? I would like at least one question on the reference-class problem -- why "all humans" rather than "all primates," "all observers whatsoever," or "all persons, including any future digital minds"; one that forces her to declare a side between the Self-Sampling Assumption and its rival, the Self-Indication Assumption, and to answer Bostrom's presumptuous-philosopher worry directly; one comparing her Bayesian formulation to Gott's delta-t version of essentially the same idea -- the one built on the Berlin Wall and on Broadway run lengths -- and asking whether the two are really the same argument in a different guise; and one pressing on whether the whole procedure requires treating the eventual total human population as already fixed and merely hidden from us, given that most of that population does not yet exist and may never exist, depending on choices nobody has made yet. For each of these, please attach a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, since I gather defenders of the argument tend to converge on a fairly small stock of standard replies. I would like the interview to close with one sharper question: short of a flaw being found in the mathematics, what would she accept as evidence that the argument is simply wrong rather than merely unsettling, and does she think accepting it should change anything at all about how we actually behave?
