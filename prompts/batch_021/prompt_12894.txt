The question set to the students read as follows: "Nagel treats the reduction of thermodynamics to statistical mechanics -- via the bridge law identifying temperature with mean molecular kinetic energy -- as the paradigm case of successful intertheoretic reduction. Fodor argued that multiple realizability blocks this Nagelian model from generalizing to the special sciences as a whole. (a) Explain why a Nagelian bridge law becomes unavailable once the relevant kind is multiply realized, and what Fodor took this to establish about the standing of psychology and economics relative to physics. (b) The renormalization-group explanation of universality in continuous phase transitions -- wildly different microscopic Hamiltonians all flowing, under coarse-graining, to the same fixed point -- is sometimes glossed in exactly the same terms. Is that gloss correct, or is something different going on in the physics case?"

And here, verbatim, is the answer I need help with: "Nagel held that one theory reduces to another when the laws of the reduced theory can be logically derived from the laws of the reducing theory together with 'bridge laws' connecting their vocabularies; his stock example is thermodynamics reducing to statistical mechanics via the identification of temperature with mean molecular kinetic energy, which lets the gas laws be derived from the postulates of kinetic theory. Fodor's multiple-realizability argument targets exactly this bridge-law requirement: because a special-science kind such as being in pain or being a monetary exchange can be implemented by an open-ended and physically heterogeneous set of micro-states, no single physical predicate is coextensive with the special-science predicate, so no genuine bridge law, and hence no Nagelian reduction, is available. Since psychological and other special-science properties therefore cannot be reduced, Fodor concludes that they are autonomous, higher-level properties that are not, after all, physical properties. The renormalization-group treatment of critical phenomena raises exactly the same difficulty in a purely physical setting: the fact that magnets and fluids share critical exponents near their respective transitions just is multiple realizability showing up inside physics itself, since wildly different microscopic Hamiltonians converge on the same fixed point. The RG story should therefore be read as a striking confirmation of Fodor's thesis, extended to a domain with no psychology anywhere in sight."
