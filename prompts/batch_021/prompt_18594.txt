For the past year or so I have been teaching myself set theory in my spare time, working through a couple of secondhand textbooks and some university lecture notes I found online, since I never had the opportunity to study mathematics formally. Two results that I originally learned months apart have started to seem flatly inconsistent to me, and neither the books nor a friend of mine who teaches high school math has been able to tell me where I have gone wrong. The first is Cantor's proof that the real numbers are uncountable, meaning there is no way to match them up one-to-one with the natural numbers so that every real number gets its own place in an endless list: a first one, a second one, a third one, and so on. The second is the Well-Ordering Theorem, which I gather is equivalent to the Axiom of Choice, and which states that every set whatsoever, including the real numbers, can be arranged into an order in which every non-empty subset of it has a smallest element.

What puzzles me is that having a smallest element in every subset seems, on reflection, to amount to exactly the property that would let you list a set out in order: you take the smallest element as your first item, the smallest element of everything remaining as your second, and so forth, and this process should eventually work its way through every element of the set. If that is right, the Well-Ordering Theorem seems to hand us precisely the kind of enumeration of the real numbers that Cantor's theorem rules out, and yet both results are presented in every source I have consulted as settled, non-contradictory mathematics, often within a few pages of each other. I must be misunderstanding one of the two theorems, but going back over the definitions has not shown me the gap. Could you help me locate the mistake in my reasoning, so that I can see how the real numbers being well-ordered manages not to contradict their being uncountable?
