For a seminar on classical Chinese philosophy of language, I have been working through Gongsun Long's White Horse Dialogue (白馬論) and its famous thesis that "a white horse is not a horse" (白馬非馬), and I keep going back and forth on how seriously the argument deserves to be taken. The reasoning itself is more careful than the slogan suggests: Gongsun Long distinguishes naming a shape from naming a shape together with a color, and observes that someone who asks for "a horse" can be satisfied by a yellow or a black horse, whereas someone who asks for "a white horse" cannot — so the two names appear to pick out different things. At the same time, this looks a great deal like the sort of equivocation between "is" of identity and "is" of predication that a Confucian critic working in the rectification-of-names (zhengming) tradition of Xunzi would want to expose as a merely verbal trick, one that confuses names in order to disorder the practical distinctions those names are meant to serve.

I would like you to write a dialogue between a School of Names logician defending Gongsun Long's thesis and a Confucian interlocutor arguing against it from within the rectification-of-names tradition, in which each side directly engages the other's strongest moves rather than restating its own position from scratch. The logician should be allowed to draw on the actual resources of the White Horse Dialogue — the shape/color distinction, the "seeking a horse versus seeking a white horse" argument, and, if it helps, a brief gesture at the companion "hard and white" discourse — rather than being reduced to a caricature of ancient sophistry, while the Confucian critic should press real objections: that the argument trades on an ambiguity in "is," that it cannot be reconciled with ordinary practices of riding, buying, or taxing white horses as horses, and that even if the logician is onto something real, this may show only that names behave differently from how Gongsun Long assumes, rather than that a white horse really is not a horse. Please have the dialogue close with the two characters trying to locate exactly where their disagreement lies: in the logic of the argument itself, in a deeper disagreement about what names and kinds really are, or in a disagreement about what naming is ultimately for.
