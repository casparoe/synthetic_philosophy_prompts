In my school's Theory of Knowledge class, we spent a week on inductive logic, and our teacher used Hempel's raven paradox as the running example: because "all ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "all non-black things are non-ravens," spotting a white shoe ought to confirm the raven hypothesis too, which feels absurd, since you shouldn't be able to learn something about ravens just by examining shoes. We resolved it the standard way, with the Bayesian move that a black raven really does confirm the hypothesis substantially, while a white shoe confirms it as well, just by an amount so tiny as to be imperceptible, since there are vastly more non-black things in the world than there are ravens. Everyone seemed satisfied, and we moved on to the next topic.

Curiosity got the better of me, though, and over the weekend I found that the statistician I. J. Good had published a short counterexample to exactly this solution back in the 1960s, as part of a direct exchange with Hempel. As best I can reconstruct it, Good described two possible states of the world, each perfectly consistent with everything we are normally told in the puzzle: in one, ravens are rare and all black; in the other, ravens are far more numerous and only mostly, not entirely, black, but arranged so that this second world actually contains more black ravens in absolute terms than the first. He then argued that, on these assumptions, learning that some particular bird is a black raven should shift your credence toward the second world and away from the first, meaning the sighting disconfirms "all ravens are black" rather than confirming it even slightly. That seems to undercut the very premise the classroom solution relied on: that a positive instance always confirms its generalization at least a little, however small the effect.

I would like an essay that actually settles this for me rather than just laying out both sides and shrugging. Please explain, using the real Bayesian mechanics of the situation (priors over rival hypotheses, likelihoods, and how a cleverly chosen rival can "absorb" the probability that a black raven would otherwise send toward "all ravens are black"), exactly how Good's example manages to flip the sign of the confirmation. Then weigh whether this shows that the standard probabilistic dissolution of the raven paradox is fundamentally incomplete, quietly depending on ruling out Good-style rivals without independent justification for doing so, or whether Hempel's own response, that confirmation is always relative to background knowledge and that Good's case is simply an unsurprising instance of that fact, wins out instead. Please close with a definite verdict on whether Nicod's criterion, the idea that a positive instance always at least somewhat confirms its generalization, should be treated as false once cases like Good's are admitted, or whether some specific, independently motivated restriction can save it, and if the latter, say what that restriction would need to look like.
