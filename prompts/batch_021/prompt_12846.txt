A friend of mine who works on formal verification and I spent most of a long dinner last week — primed by a reading group on AI alignment we'd both sat in on earlier that day — arguing about self-trust in reasoning systems, and neither of us budged. The starting point was the Löbian obstacle from the tiling agents literature: a proof-based agent that only self-modifies into a successor when it can prove the successor is trustworthy runs into trouble the moment it tries to prove a general soundness schema like "whatever my successor proves is true." By Löb's theorem, a consistent system can prove "Prov(P) → P" for a given P only if it already proves P outright, so a blanket, upfront soundness guarantee for a successor is either vacuous (if the system is already inconsistent) or simply unattainable (if the system is consistent and sufficiently strong, since securing that guarantee in general terms amounts to the system proving its own soundness, which Gödel's second incompleteness theorem forecloses). My friend's position was that this is basically a curiosity of insisting on formal, first-order proof as the standard of trust, and that it quietly dissolves once you move to probabilistic reflection, bounded or heuristic reasoning, or things like logical induction — real agents, including us, just don't reason this way, so the "obstacle" is an artifact of a toy model rather than a discovery about self-trust in general.

I wasn't convinced, and I kept coming back to the sense that this is a sharpened, formal cousin of much older worries: the ancient problem of the criterion (you cannot certify your own standards of justification without already relying on some standard of justification) and the Cartesian circle (using clear and distinct perception to vindicate clear and distinct perception). If that analogy holds, swapping proof for probability, or for some cleverer architecture, looks like rearranging the furniture rather than fixing the foundation — you would expect some version of the same bind to resurface wherever a system is expressive enough to represent and reason about its own standards.

I would like you to argue for my side of this, more carefully than I managed at the table: that the Löbian obstacle is best understood as a formally precise instance of the general problem of the criterion, one that afflicts any sufficiently self-referential epistemic agent rather than proof-bound ones specifically, so that technical escapes such as probabilistic reflection principles, ordinal or parametric hierarchies of proof systems, and logical induction are best read as ways of locally managing the bind rather than dissolving its underlying source. Please engage seriously with the strongest form of my friend's objection — in particular, the fact that some of these proposals arguably achieve things, such as consistent (if imperfect) self-referential probability assignments, that no move available within classical epistemology ever did — rather than setting it aside, and say explicitly which parts of the analogy you think hold up under pressure and which, if any, you think actually break down.
