Putting together a coursepack for my causation seminar and I'd rather give the students a dialogue than another dry section on causation by omission, so here's a job for you.

Write a dialogue between two philosophers of causation who disagree about whether omissions can be real causes. One thinks yes: if you'd watered the plant it wouldn't have died, and that counterfactual dependence is basically enough to make your not-watering a cause of its death — you just also need some norm or reasonable expectation to pick out which omission counts, otherwise every single person on earth who also didnt water it comes out as an equal cause, which is nuts. The other thinks that's a bait and switch: causation is a real physical relation, something getting produced or transferred, and you can't produce an effect out of nothing — so all this causal-sounding talk about omissions is really just moral or legal responsibility wearing a metaphysical costume, and the fact that the first view needs a norm to do the real work just confirms it.

Get them arguing over one concrete case (a lifeguard who didn't notice a struggling swimmer, say, or a nurse who forgot a dose) and make them actually respond to each other's best moves instead of just restating where they started. Neither one should get to win too easily.
