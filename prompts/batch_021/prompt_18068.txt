I've been going down a rabbit hole on voting theory latley and can't decide how seriously to take the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem. Everyone treats it as this devastating result -- basically "any voting rule worth using can always be gamed by a sufficiently informed voter" -- but then in the same breath people bring up things like random-ballot/random dictatorship schemes, restricting the domain to single-peaked preferences (median voter type rules), and results showing that actually computing a successful manipulation can be NP-hard for rules like instant-runoff. It's not clear to me whether those are real escapes from the theorem's conclusion, or just re-labelings of the same underlying problem that don't actually rescue anything.

Could you write me an essay that works through this properly? First lay out what the theorem does and doesn't say, and how it differs from (and relates to) Arrow's impossibility theorem, since I think those two get mashed together way more than they should. Then go through the three escape routes I mentioned above one at a time and be honest about how much weight each one can actually bear against the theorem's conclusion. I don't want a wishy-washy "well it depends" ending though -- pick a side and defend it: is strategy-proofness basically a lost cause for any voting rule you'd actually want to use, or is there a real (not just technical) sense in which one of those escapes, or some combination of them, gets us functioning non-manipulable democratic choice?
