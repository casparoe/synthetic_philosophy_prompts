I am in the final year of a PhD in machine learning, where my research concerns mechanistic interpretability: reverse-engineering what trained neural networks are actually computing internally, rather than treating them as black boxes whose behavior we can only sample. A few weeks ago, on the recommendation of a friend in the philosophy department, I finally read Saul Kripke's book on Wittgenstein and rule-following, and one argument in it has stayed with me since. Kripke's skeptic observes that, having performed only finitely many additions in my life, when I add 68 and 57 for the first time and answer "125," nothing in my past use of "plus," and no fact about my dispositions or brain state, rules out that I have always meant "quus": the function that agrees with addition wherever both arguments are less than 57, but returns 5 whenever either argument is 57 or greater. Since no fact about my history or current mental state seems able to settle which function I meant, Kripke concludes that no "straight," truth-conditional answer to the question of which rule I am following is available, and offers instead a "skeptical solution" on which meaning-talk is licensed by communal assertability conditions rather than by any fact it correctly describes.

This argument landed differently for me than it apparently does for most people encountering it for the first time, because it describes almost exactly the setup of the experiments I run. In the "grokking" phenomenon my subfield studies, a small transformer trained on modular addition first appears to memorize its training examples and then, well after training accuracy has plateaued, abruptly transitions into a network that generalizes correctly on essentially every held-out pair we test it on. What excites people about this transition is that later interpretability work opened up networks in exactly this generalizing regime and found they were not storing anything resembling a lookup table: they had learned to embed numbers as sinusoidal features and combine them through trigonometric angle-addition identities, implementing what is recognizably a genuine addition algorithm rather than a memorized or gerrymandered one.

I would like to raise this at our next reading-group meeting, so I want to make sure I have the philosophical point right first. Doesn't this mean mechanistic interpretability supplies exactly the ingredient missing from Kripke's argument, at least for artificial systems? His skeptic can only interrogate a finite record of past outputs, but once we can open a network and read off the general algorithm it implements, we are no longer guessing at the underlying rule from finitely many data points the way the skeptic imagines: we can point to the trigonometric structure itself and say that it is addition being computed, not some quus-like impostor that merely agrees with addition on the cases tried so far. Shouldn't this settle, at least for these systems, the very fact whose absence was supposed to make the rule-following paradox so troubling in the first place?
