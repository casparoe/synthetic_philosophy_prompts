For my seminar on practical reason, we've just finished discussing Bernard Williams's "Internal and External Reasons," and I'd like to see the dispute staged rather than simply summarized. Write a dialogue between a philosopher defending Williams's internalism about reasons and one defending an externalist view of the sort associated with critics such as John McDowell or Thomas Nagel. The internalist should use the Owen Wingrave case to argue that a reason-claim is only intelligible if there is some sound deliberative route from the agent's actual motivational set, S, to the relevant motivation; the externalist should press the case of a thoroughly cruel, self-satisfied person for whom no such route is available, and insist that this person still has reason to stop being cruel. Have the internalist address directly whether accommodating that case by expanding S to fit is a genuine answer or an ad hoc move that drains internalism of its content, and have the externalist respond to what the internalist actually says rather than merely repeating the objection. Both figures should be sharp and equally well-versed in the literature, and neither should have an easy time of it.
