In a few paragraphs: (a) characterize the heterodox Christology at the center of his private theology; (b) identify one specific piece of textual evidence he used to argue for it; (c) explain the institutional risk that made him cautious about going public; and (d) address whether it's fair to say Newton's scientific naturalism pushed him toward Deism."

Here's the student's answer: "Newton was a lifelong closet antitrinitarian: he rejected the Athanasian doctrine that the Father and Son are coequal and consubstantial, and held that the imposition of that doctrine, chiefly through Athanasius's influence around the time of Nicaea, had corrupted a purer, original Christianity. On this point he's often bracketed with the Socinians, and I'd say that's basically right — like the Socinians, Newton denied that Christ existed prior to his human birth, treating him as a supremely exalted creature raised up by God rather than a real, pre-existent divine person. His sharpest textual weapon was the Johannine Comma at 1 John 5:7, which he argued, in the piece he eventually sent to Locke ('An Historical Account of Two Notable Corruptions of Scripture'), was missing from the earliest Greek manuscripts and never once cited by the church fathers who were busy fighting the Arians — strong evidence, on his reading, that it was a later interpolation invented to give the doctrine of the Trinity a scriptural anchor it otherwise lacked. He prepared this piece for anonymous publication in Holland through Le Clerc and then lost his nerve and had Locke pull it back before it reached print, which tells you how seriously he rated the danger of exposure. That danger was concrete and institutional: fellows of Trinity College were required to take holy orders within several years of their MA or else forfeit the fellowship, and Newton escaped this only via a royal dispensation, secured in 1675, that permanently excused the Lucasian professor from ordination. As for whether the science pushed him toward Deism, I'd say yes, on balance. Newton's whole program was to show that the heavens run on a small set of exceptionless mathematical laws, and once that's done, God's job naturally shrinks to setting the initial conditions and stepping back — which is more or less the Deist picture. It fits, too, that Newton's own theological project, like the Deists', was fundamentally about stripping away centuries of priestly corruption to recover a simpler, more rational original religion."
