I'm on the small grants committee for my university's Effective Altruism club. Every year we pool member dues plus a little alumni money and vote on where it goes, and honestly it's usually a non-event because everyone just defaults to whatever the standard GiveWell-style picks are. This year one of our newer members got really into suffering-focused ethics after we covered negative utilitarianism in my practical ethics seminar a few weeks ago (kind of a funny coincidence, timing-wise), and she's pushing hard for the whole pot to go to a wild-animal-welfare group running a fertility-control pilot on an overpopulated feral goat herd on a reserve near campus. Apparently without any predators left the herd crashes every couple winters and a few hundred goats starve slowly over weeks, and her pitch is that preventing that amount of suffering is way more cost-effective per dollar then anything we'd fund on the human side.

A couple of the older members are not having it. One of them keeps bringing up this old argument -- I want to say it's from the 50s, might be misremembering -- that if you take minimizing suffering as seriously as strict negative utilitarianism does, you end up committed to saying that a being who could just painlessly switch off all sentient life would be doing something good, and she thinks funding this pilot is a small step onto that same road rather than a normal animal-welfare grant.

I have to write the memo the committee votes on this Wednesday, and I need an actual recommendation in it -- fund it, don't, fund it with conditions, whatever you think is actually defensible -- not a wishy-washy summary of both sides. What I really need help with is doing justice to whichever side loses: if you land on funding it, take the "where does this reasoning actually stop" objection seriously instead of waving it off, and if you land on rejecting it, don't let the reasoning collapse into "ecosystems are natural, we shouldn't meddle," because that isn't really what's driving the skeptics on our committee.
