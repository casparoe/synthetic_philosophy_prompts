I'm a backend engineer with no philosophical training beyond losing an argument with a coworker about whether 'meaning is use' has any bearing on naming variables in code review, but I've been asked to give the best man speech at my college roommate's wedding next month, and there's one part of it I want to get right rather than fake. His fiancée is finishing a philosophy PhD dissertation on Wittgenstein's rule-following considerations and what they imply about the possibility of a private language, and for the eight years the two of them have been together, our entire friend group has called what they have 'their own private language' -- a decade of nicknames, half-finished sentences, and references none of the rest of us has ever fully cracked. I want to build the toast around that, because it's the truest and funniest thing I know about them.

The trouble is that when I said as much to her over dinner, I got a long, good-natured explanation of why a 'private language' in that sense is exactly what she spends her professional life, following Wittgenstein, arguing is impossible -- something about a diary kept for a recurring sensation labeled 'S,' and a box that might as well be empty, since whatever's inside it cancels out of what the word 'beetle' actually means. When I looked the argument up myself afterward, every source I found just asserted that Wittgenstein 'proved you can't have a private language' without explaining why in any way I could actually use at a podium, which is no good to me: half the room will be her cohort, in from three continents, who will notice immediately if I flatten the argument or get it backwards, and the other half is my extended family, who has never heard of Wittgenstein and just wants a good toast before the first dance. Could you write the speech? About five minutes, built around one real joke, and it has to actually take the private language argument seriously enough to use it -- saying something true and warm about these two rather than either sentimentally claiming they have a private language in the sense she'd say is incoherent, or hedging so hard on the philosophy that the toast loses its nerve.
