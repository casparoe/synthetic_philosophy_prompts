I'm drafting a novel set amid the quarrel that broke out when d'Alembert's 1757 Encyclopédie article urged Geneva to open a public theatre, and Rousseau answered the following year with his blistering Lettre à d'Alembert. Before I put an argument in Rousseau's mouth, I want to be sure I have it right, rather than the flattened version most people know: that he thought theatre trades in illusion while civic festivals cultivate authentic community. His sharper claim is that a playwright, to succeed commercially, must cater to an audience's existing prejudices rather than correct them, so the stage can only ratify a society's morals and never reform them. He tries to prove this through a close reading of Molière's Misanthrope, arguing that the play's comic machinery quietly teaches audiences to laugh at Alceste's rigid honesty while treating Philinte's flattering accommodation to vice as good sense. Could you reconstruct that reading in some detail, explaining why Rousseau thought the play tilts the joke onto its more virtuous character, and how he uses the example to clinch his broader argument that drama can only follow public opinion rather than lead it? I'd also appreciate a sentence or two situating this within his larger distinction between being and appearing.
