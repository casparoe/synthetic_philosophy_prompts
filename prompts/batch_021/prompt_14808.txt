Kurt Gödel shows up constantly on rationalist blogs for the incompleteness theorems and the modal ontological proof, but a third piece of Gödel lore gets far less scrutiny. In letters to his mother, Marianne, he argued that because the world is rationally constructed, it would amount to a kind of cosmic waste for the vast, mostly unrealized capacities each person develops over a lifetime simply to be extinguished at death -- and that some form of afterlife must therefore exist. This usually gets flattened into a one-line slogan and dismissed as an obvious non-sequitur.

I want a sharper objection argued as forcefully as possible instead. Please first reconstruct the argument fairly, on its own terms rather than the slogan version, and then argue that the premise it depends on -- that a rational world contains no fundamental waste or permanently unrealized potential -- is exactly what Gödel's own incompleteness theorems should have taught him to distrust, at least for any formal system rich enough to encode arithmetic. Give me the strongest one-sided case for this tension, not a balanced survey of objections to the afterlife argument.
