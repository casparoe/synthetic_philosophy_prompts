Ok so this went down in a decision theory reading group I'm part of (it's a spinoff of an old SSC meetup, we've been slowly working through Sen and Harsanyi and that whole social choice cluster for months now). We'd just finished the session on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem when someone brought up an actual dilemma from a mutual aid fund that two of the members help run: they had exactly one emergency grant left, $2k, and two applicants who each needed that exact amount to avoid the same bad outcome (both about to lose their housing over that precise sum) -- equally deserving as far as anyone in the group could tell, nothing to distinguish them on any criterion the fund actually tracks, and no way to split the money and have it help either of them. Two people in the group (I'll just call them M and K) got into a real argument about how you should decide a case like that, and it turned into a fight about the theorem itself. Trying to reconstruct it as faithfully as I can:

(1) M said you obviously flip a coin -- giving each applicant a genuine fifty-fifty shot is the only fair way to treat two equally deserving people when there's only one grant to give.

(2) K said that's just theater: since the two of them are interchangeable by hypothesis, the expected benefit of flipping a coin is mathematically identical to the expected benefit of just handing it to whoever emailed first, or whoever the treasurer happens to like more. So treating the coin flip as required is privileging the feeling of fairness over any actual difference in outcomes -- nobody ends up better off in expectation because you flipped.

(3) M pushed back: if the theorem really implies that, we should be suspicious of the theorem, not of the coin flip. It only forces that conclusion because one of its premises is a Pareto condition applied directly to lotteries, not just to settled outcomes -- roughly, if everyone's indifferent between two gambles, society has to be indifferent too. But that's the very premise in dispute here: M isn't indifferent between "I get a real shot at the coin flip" and "it's already been decided against me," even if the two options come out equal on paper.

(4) K said that's not an objection, that's just refusing to accept the conclusion of a valid argument. If you grant that each person's preferences over gambles satisfy the usual rationality axioms, and you grant the Pareto condition, the weighted-sum conclusion is forced -- that's what makes it a theorem and not a suggestion. If M wants to keep the intuition, the burden is on M to say which specific axiom is false, not to just keep asserting that the coin flip feels important.

(5) M said fine, here's the specific target: the axiom worth doubting is the Pareto condition applied to lotteries themselves, rather than to certain, already-resolved outcomes. And apparently M isn't just making this up -- somebody (Diamond? I think that's the name, this is where I got a bit lost) wrote an actual response to Harsanyi distinguishing a stronger "ex ante" Pareto condition from a weaker "ex post" one, arguing the ex ante version is specifically what does the controversial work, since it's what rules out caring about fair procedure once the expected payoffs match up.

After that it was mostly the two of them repeating (3) through (5) louder. A score from -2 (clearly mistaken) to +2 (clearly right) for each move would be a nice way to structure it if you're up for that, but the reasoning behind each score is really what I'm after.
