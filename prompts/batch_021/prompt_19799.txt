I spent my career as an experimental physicist working on phase transitions in condensed matter. Perhaps that explains why, since retiring and beginning to read philosophy in earnest, I have become so preoccupied with the sorites paradox and the literature on vagueness that has grown up around it. Some of the transitions I studied in the laboratory really do occur at a precise, if idealized, point: a first-order transition happens at an exact temperature for a given pressure, even if its approach looks gradual from far away. I had always assumed that predicates like "heap" or "bald" were simply not like that: there is no analogous exact point, only a broad region of indeterminacy — which is more or less what supervaluationists say when they hold that a borderline heap is neither determinately a heap nor determinately not one, because the admissible ways of making "heap" precise disagree about it.

What has unsettled this assumption is Timothy Williamson's epistemicism, which I initially dismissed as a philosopher's trick: surely no one seriously believes there is some exact number of grains, say 10,417, marking the true boundary of heaphood, merely unknown to us? A friend with a doctorate in philosophy tells me epistemicism is taken far more seriously among specialists than its reputation among outsiders would suggest, and that dismissing it this quickly is a mistake. As I understand it, the crucial move is not just to assert that vague predicates have sharp boundaries, but to explain why those boundaries would have to be unknowable even in principle. This is done by way of something called the margin-for-error principle: roughly, that knowing exactly where the boundary lies would require our belief-forming practices to be sensitive to differences in usage far finer than they actually are, since the boundary itself is fixed by the whole pattern of a linguistic community's use of the word and could easily have fallen a grain to either side of where it actually falls.

I can restate this argument, but I cannot yet feel why it is supposed to be more satisfying than the supervaluationist's admission of genuine indeterminacy, rather than merely a strategy for preserving classical bivalence at any cost. Could you explain, at the level of intuition rather than formal semantics, exactly what work the margin-for-error principle is doing in Williamson's argument, and why it is meant to make our ignorance of the sorites boundary principled rather than an ad hoc face-saving device bolted onto an implausible metaphysical claim?
