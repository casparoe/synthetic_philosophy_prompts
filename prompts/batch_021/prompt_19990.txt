"Nozick distinguishes historical principles of justice, which look at how a distribution actually came about, from end-state or patterned principles, which only look at the resulting pattern (e.g. 'to each according to need,' or strict equality). He thinks only historical principles are defensible, and his entitlement theory is his historical alternative: a distribution is just if it results from (1) just acquisition of unowned goods, (2) just transfer between people, including the Lockean proviso that a transfer can't leave anyone else worse off than they would otherwise have been, and (3) rectification of past violations of 1 and 2.

The Wilt Chamberlain example is supposed to show patterned principles can't be maintained without constant interference. Say D1 is whatever distribution you like, egalitarian or not. Wilt Chamberlain signs a contract where he gets 25 cents from every ticket sold. A million fans each choose to go watch him play, each transferring a quarter out of their D1 holdings. Now Wilt has $250,000 more than anyone else, so we're at D2, which doesn't match the pattern anymore. But every single step from D1 to D2 was a free transfer nobody's complaining about, so if D1 was just, D2 has to be just too, even though a patterned theorist would have to call D2 unjust and forcibly redistribute to restore the pattern. Since this keeps happening every time people transact, no pattern can stay stable without the state continually interfering with ordinary transactions, which is what Nozick thinks is unacceptable.

The most influential response is G.A. Cohen's: he argues the whole thing turns on D1 already being illegitimate, since it reflects unequal natural talents that nobody actually deserves - Wilt's bargaining power only exists because of a talent he didn't earn. So the argument only feels compelling once you've already granted that undeserved talent-based inequality is fine, which is precisely what's at issue between Nozick and his egalitarian critics."
