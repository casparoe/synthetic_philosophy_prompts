Question: Explain how Fitch's Paradox generates its contradiction. Make sure you (1) state the two assumptions/premises the argument runs on, (2) walk through the crucial step where the contradiction pops out, explaining in plain terms why "knowing that p is true and that nobody knows it" can't actually happen, and (3) say what you think the paradox actually establishes, and who (if anyone) should really be worried by it.

Answer: Fitch's paradox runs on two assumptions that both sound totally reasonable on their own. The first is the knowability principle: every truth is at least possible to know, so if p is true then it's possible that somebody knows p (p → ◇Kp). The second is just plain non-omniscience: nobody knows everything, so there's got to be at least one true thing that nobody actually knows (there's some p where p is true and it's not known).

The clever move is plugging in a specific p: not some ordinary fact, but the whole conjunction "p is true and nobody knows p," call it p*. Since we're assuming there IS an unknown truth, p* itself comes out true. So the knowability principle says p* has to be at least possibly knowable, somebody could, in principle, know that (p and nobody knows p). But think about what that would actually require: to know p*, you'd have to know both halves, meaning you know p AND you know that nobody knows p. Except the moment you know p, you ARE somebody who knows p, so "nobody knows p" is now false, which means the very thing you supposedly know isn't even true anymore. So knowing p* is flat out impossible, not just unlikely. That flatly contradicts what the knowability principle just told us had to be possible. Since the contradiction traces back to assuming there's an unknown truth, that's the assumption that has to go, meaning, weirdly, you're stuck saying every truth actually IS known, full blown omniscience.

Random fact I picked up while reading about this: the proof wasn't even originally Fitch's — a referee (anonymous to him at the time) sent Fitch basically this exact argument in 1945 in the reports on an earlier paper of his that never ended up getting published, and it only came out decades later, when someone went digging through the old files, that the referee had actually been Alonzo Church. Fitch published his own, generalized version of it in 1963.

What it actually shows, I think, is bad news mainly for realists, the people who think truth is basically just whatever's knowable in principle. If you're committed to that, this argument seems to prove that either you give it up or you accept the absurd result that nothing is ever actually unknown. And the whole argument leans on knowers being logically omniscient, able to know everything that logically follows from what they know, which is a pretty strong assumption not everyone would grant.
