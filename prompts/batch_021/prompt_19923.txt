I am several drafts into a novel set in South Africa during the mid-1980s state of emergency, centered on a white security policeman whose casual cruelty is meant to indict the system that produced him, not to win the reader's sympathy. In a handful of scenes, he and his colleagues use a racial slur against Black characters; I have been careful that it is never spoken by anyone the narrative regards with warmth, and later chapters shift point of view to the people on the receiving end of men like him, so the cost of that language is never left merely implicit. This is my third novel but my first attempt at material this far from my own experience, and I read widely in oral histories and in testimony from the Truth and Reconciliation Commission to get the texture of the period's speech right. Part of my reasoning for writing the actual word, rather than gesturing around it, was that softening it felt like a small dishonesty about what those years actually sounded like.

An early reader who grew up under apartheid told me that however clearly the novel condemns this man, the word still does something to her, as a reader, that the surrounding disapproval does not undo, and that I ought to find another way to mark his bigotry. A second reader thinks this objection proves too much: since I am quoting a character rather than speaking in my own voice, it asks a single word to do work that plot, context, and consequence are already doing, and, taken to its logical limit, it would forbid ever depicting this kind of racism in dialogue at all. I do not want to hide behind the claim that I am only quoting him if that turns out to be a weak defense, but neither do I want to discard a considered choice merely because it made a reader flinch. Is there a principled way of working out how much protection that defense really offers, and the point, if any, at which it runs out?
