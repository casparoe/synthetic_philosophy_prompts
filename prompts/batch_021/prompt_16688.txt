My aunt Carol died three weeks ago at seventy-nine, and I've been asked to give her eulogy this Saturday, to a room that will be mostly retired nurses and her now-grown former foster kids rather than anyone with a philosophical background. For over forty years she took in medically fragile children nobody else would foster, paid for more than one of their surgeries out of her own retirement savings, and never took a vacation because, as she put it, "somebody had to be there." Everybody who loved her wants to hear at the service that she went above and beyond, that she did more than anyone could reasonably ask of a person -- and a local paper actually profiled her in exactly those terms a few years ago. She was furious about it, in her quiet way. She told me afterward that she didn't think what she'd done was optional or extra; she thought it was simply owed, and that praising it as heroic just let everyone else conclude they'd never be on the hook for anything like it. She was Lutheran in an old-fashioned, unfussy way, and I don't think that reaction was only stubbornness on her part -- I think she genuinely didn't believe there was a category of doing more than duty required.

Since she died I've learned that philosophers actually argue about exactly this -- whether there's a genuine category of acts that are good to do but not required (I gather the word for it is "supererogatory"), or whether her harder view, that anyone in a position to help as much as she could is simply obligated to, is closer to correct. I'm not asking for a summary of that debate; I'm asking you to write the actual eulogy, and it has to do something specific. It can't fall back on "beyond the call of duty" or "a saint among us," since that's exactly the framing she spent years rejecting, but it also can't tip into implying that the nurses and parents in that room who didn't do what she did have failed some duty of their own -- whatever she privately believed about that, turning her funeral into an accusation isn't something I'm willing to do. I'll have about seven minutes. Please write something that lets that room grieve her and feel proud of her without quietly cheating on either side of that tension.
