In philosophy of mind, higher-order theories of consciousness share the premise that a mental state's being phenomenally conscious cannot be explained purely by that state's intrinsic, first-order character: some further, appropriately related higher-order representation of the state is also required. Where these theories diverge is over what, exactly, that higher-order representation must be like. On David Rosenthal's higher-order thought (HOT) theory, the subject must actually token a roughly simultaneous, non-inferential thought to the effect that she is in the first-order state. On the higher-order perception, or "inner sense," model developed out of David Armstrong's work and elaborated by William Lycan, what's needed instead is something more like a quasi-perceptual scanning or monitoring state directed at the first-order state, rather than a discrete conceptual thought about it. And on Peter Carruthers' dispositional HOT theory, no higher-order state need actually occur at all: it suffices that the first-order state be suitably poised or available to a higher-order, mindreading-style mechanism that could, if it were deployed, generate such a thought.

I've been drafting a term paper for a seminar on self-representation and consciousness, and I'd like a test case that genuinely forces these three views apart, rather than one of the standard examples used to argue for or against higher-order theories as a family -- e.g., cases of confabulated or "targetless" higher-order thoughts, or the objection (sometimes labeled the "problem of the rock") that purely extrinsic, relational theories make phenomenal consciousness too cheap to confer. Those tend to cut against, or in favor of, all three views alike, rather than discriminating between them.

Please construct a single, detailed scenario, involving one subject and one first-order mental state tracked across a short sequence of moments or manipulations (for instance, involving attention, perceptual masking, or degraded conceptual access), such that HOT theory, higher-order-perception theory, and dispositional HOT theory come apart, issuing three different verdicts about whether, or exactly when, the subject becomes phenomenally conscious of that state. For each of the three verdicts, briefly identify the specific theoretical commitment -- concerning the required etiology, format, or mere availability versus actual occurrence of the higher-order representation -- that forces it, so that the example does real discriminating work rather than simply restating each theory's official definition.
