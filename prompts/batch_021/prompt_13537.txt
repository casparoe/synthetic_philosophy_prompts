So a while back I fell down an internet rabbit hole reading about AI safety and kept running into this idea called the "Lobian obstacle" (I think it's named after Lob's theorem in logic). From what I can piece together, the idea is that if you've got some smart system that only trusts a conclusion once its got an actual formal proof of it, then that system basically can't ever fully vouch for a future/upgraded version of itself that reasons the exact same way. Like, proving something like "whenever my successor's proof system proves X, X is actually true" for every possible X would require the original system to already be able to prove X directly anyway, which kind of defeats the whole purpose of trusting the successor in the first place. People talk about this as a problem for "tiling" agents, building a chain of self-improving AIs that each hand off trust to the next one. Some folks online treat this as basically a fatal wall for the whole dream of provably safe self-improving AI, and other folks act like its just a technical wrinkle that gets ironed out once you stop insisting on airtight logical proof and let the system lean on probability or "good enough" trust instead (I've seen stuff about "logical induction" being brought up here, though I don't fully get the details).

The other should argue that's overstated - that Lob's theorem only rules out one pretty narrow, naive setup for self trust, and that there are legit ways to get workable self trust that are good enough for real AI safety even if they're not airtight.
