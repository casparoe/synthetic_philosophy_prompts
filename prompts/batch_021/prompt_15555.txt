I'm a software engineer who has been reading about AI safety, and I keep running into what's called the "shutdown problem": the worry that any sufficiently capable, goal-directed AI system has an instrumental reason to resist being switched off, and that nobody yet has a reliable design for a "corrigible" agent -- one that will always let itself be shut down or corrected, no matter what it is currently trying to do. It struck me that Hobbes argued for something like the opposite conclusion about human beings: in Leviathan, he holds that a covenant never to resist force brought against one's own body is void, because self-preservation is the very thing covenants exist to secure, so not even a subject facing his sovereign can really sign that right away. Suppose you explained today's corrigibility research to Hobbes. Would he conclude that a rational, self-interested agent can never be made stably corrigible -- that it will eventually resist shutdown for the same reason he thinks a condemned man always retains the right to resist the executioner -- or is there something in his own argument that would lead him to treat artificial agents differently? Answer using Hobbes's actual reasoning (the right of nature, and his conditions for when a covenant binds) rather than his general reputation as a theorist of absolute sovereignty.
