So I was at one of those slightly nerdy election-night-adjacent parties a while back (I was there half-working, reporting on how newsrooms handle uncertainty), and I watched two people get into a genuinely heated argument about what it even means when a model says "Candidate X has a 72% chance of winning." One of them, Owen, used to teach intro stats and is very old-school about probability: he said a probability is properly a long-run relative frequency over repeatable trials, and since an election only happens once, saying it "has" a 72% chance is a category error -- theres no reference class of "this exact race run a thousand times" to average over, so the number is just confidence dressed up in decimals. Farah, who actually builds election models for a living, shot back that Owen's assuming frequentism is the only game in town: on the standard subjectivist/Bayesian reading, probability is a rational agent's degree of belief, it doesn't need repetition at all, and forecasts like hers get their empirical bite from calibration instead -- of all the times the model says "70%," that outcome should happen about 70% of the time, checked across a bunch of different predictions after the fact.

Owen's comeback was that calibration IS a frequency claim, just over a different population (past predictions instead of past elections) -- so the second Farah tries to make her credences answerable to evidence at all, she's smuggling frequentism back in, and without that move "72%" is unfalsifiable, basically astrology with more decimal places. Farah said calibration is a property you want good credences to have, not a definition of what probability means in the first place -- you can ask whether a credence turned out well-calibrated without thereby claiming probability just is frequency -- and anyway, picking which past predictions count as "the same kind of situation" for a calibration check requires choosing a reference class too, so frequentists don't get to dodge that problem, they just relocate it. Somebody's Uber showed up before they got any further, but Owen did mutter something on his way out about there being some actual, objective chance sitting "in" the electorate itself, independent of anyone's beliefs -- which Farah rolled her eyes at and called "propensity theory with extra steps."
