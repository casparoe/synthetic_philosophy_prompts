I'm several chapters into a speculative novel built around the conceit that every possible outcome of every choice is, in some sense, actually happening somewhere—nothing is left merely "possible." While trying to make this premise feel intellectually serious rather than a device borrowed from superhero movies, I came across David Lewis's modal realism, which holds that every possible world is exactly as concrete and real as the one we inhabit. It struck me, and a physicist friend I described the idea to agreed, that this is essentially the philosophical version of the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics, which has reality constantly branching so that every possible outcome of every quantum event is realized in some branch. Philosophy and physics, on this reading, seem to have arrived at the same conclusion from opposite directions: one by reasoning about what possibility itself must consist in, the other by taking the mathematics of the wavefunction at face value.

For the novel's afterword, I want to tell readers that the book's premise, however outlandish it sounds, tracks something real, since two independent disciplines with entirely different methods converged on the same multiverse. What I haven't settled is how to weight the two sources in making that pitch: should I present the physics as the stronger claim, since it rests on an empirically successful theory rather than pure conceptual argument, or does Lewis's philosophical argument actually carry the greater weight, precisely because it doesn't depend on any particular physical theory that future experiments could overturn? I'd like to know which framing is more defensible before I commit to it in print, since I'd rather get the relationship between the two right than lean on a comparison I've only half-earned.
