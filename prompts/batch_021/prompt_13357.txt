I'm taking a seminar on formal epistemology this term, and for the last two weeks we've been working through the problem of logical uncertainty and, specifically, the logical induction framework that Scott Garrabrant and his co-authors introduced in 2016. I follow the formal construction well enough: a logical inductor is defined as the limiting price sequence of a market in which computable trading algorithms buy and sell shares in the truth of logical and mathematical sentences, the prices are constrained so that no computable trader can exploit the market for unbounded profit, and the theory proves that these prices converge to 1 or 0 on any sentence that is eventually proved or refuted. What I keep getting stuck on, including in office hours, is a more basic question: what is any of this actually supposed to license a person to believe about one specific open sentence, right now, before a proof exists one way or the other?

For a short response paper I want to use the Collatz conjecture as my test case, since it is simple enough that I can explain it to my roommate, remains entirely unproved, has been checked by brute-force computation across an enormous range of starting values without a single counterexample turning up, and has a well-known heuristic "drift" argument that treats the iteration as a random walk which decreases on average — an argument most number theorists find suggestive without treating it as anything close to a proof. Terence Tao's 2019 result, showing that almost all orbits, in a suitable density sense, eventually become small, is real partial progress without settling the matter either way. A friend of mine in the computer science department insists that everything logical induction tells us here just cashes out to "defer to whatever the relevant experts currently believe," which feels like it misses the point of introducing all this formal machinery in the first place, though I haven't been able to articulate exactly why. Could you describe the most important implications of the logical induction framework for how I, someone who is obviously not running a computable trading market in my head, should actually go about setting and revising my own credence in a conjecture like this one — and be explicit about where that guidance runs out and shades back into ordinary mathematical intuition?
