Ok so I've been chewing on the "Ulysses contract" thing and want a sanity check on the analogy. The toy case everyone uses: sober-Ulysses knows exactly what temptation is coming (the sirens, specifically), gets his crew to tie him to the mast, and once they're past he unties himself and presumably agrees afterward it was the right call. Same guy the whole way through, restraint is temporary, targeted at a known threat.

Elster and a bunch of constitutional theorists after him reuse that exact image for stuff like supermajority amendment rules or entrenched bill-of-rights clauses -- "the people" binding their future selves against faction or passion, same logic as the mast. But the more I poke at it the less it holds up for me. It's not the same agent doing the binding and getting bound, its mostly people who weren't even born yet. Nobody's guaranteed to agree after the fact that the restraint was wise, the way untied-Ulysses presumably would -- future majorities routinely think the founders got it wrong. And "some future faction's passion" is a category, not a prediction, wheras the sirens' song is a specific, known threat that sober-Ulysses could actually plan around.

Can you actually run this comparison properly instead of just gesturing at the metaphor? Where does the individual-precommitment case and the constitutional-precommitment case really line up, where do those disanalogies bite hardest, and is there a version of "constitution as Ulysses contract" that survives them -- or does the self-binding language basically only earn its keep at the personal, akrasia-and-Beeminder scale and not the constitutional one?
