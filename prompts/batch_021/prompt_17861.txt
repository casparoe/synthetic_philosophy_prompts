Last night a friend and I had a long, surprisingly heated argument that started over something mundane. I had baked a tray of brownies for a small gathering and had personally checked every ingredient label, so when a guest with a mild nut sensitivity asked whether they were safe, I told her, "Yes, I know there are no nuts in these," and she ate one without hesitation. Later that evening, another guest arrived with her six-year-old son, who carries emergency epinephrine for a severe, life-threatening nut allergy, and someone offered him a brownie. When I said exactly the same thing—"don't worry, I know these are nut-free"—my friend suddenly balked: "Do you actually know that, or do you just think it?" My evidence had not changed at all between the two moments; I had checked the same labels in the same way both times. It struck me as absurd that whether I "know" something should depend on how catastrophic it would be to be wrong, but my friend insisted, with equal conviction, that this is simply how knowledge works. We argued about it for the better part of an hour without resolving anything. Was either of us right, or is there something substantive underlying this disagreement beyond just using the word "know" differently?
