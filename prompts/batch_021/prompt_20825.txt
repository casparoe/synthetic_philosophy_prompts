A recent thread in my rationalist-adjacent reading on meta-honesty and glomarization sent me back to Augustine's two treatises on lying, and I keep snagging on the same joint in his position. He insists that lying is intrinsically sinful regardless of motive — even an "officious" lie told purely to protect an innocent person is culpable — yet he also holds that staying silent, refusing to answer, or speaking evasively is permitted even when you know it will leave your questioner with a false belief. Since his own definition of a lie already builds in the will to deceive, I want a careful explanation of why silence or calculated evasion aimed at producing that very same false belief doesn't count as satisfying that same intentional condition: is the further requirement that one actually assert a believed falsehood doing real, independent moral work for him, or is it a formal loophole letting the same deceptive intention operate under a different name? Then press that answer against the case he has to confront in Contra Mendacium, written against Consentius — Rahab's lie to protect the spies, which scripture seems to commend rather than merely excuse — and explain whether crediting her faith rather than her falsehood is a principled application of the assertion/silence distinction or an ad hoc patch to save the doctrine. Don't just hand me his conclusion that lying is always wrong; I want the joints of the argument and where they creak.
