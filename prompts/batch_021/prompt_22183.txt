Student's answer: Wittgenstein's point is that religious belief does not operate like an empirical hypothesis that evidence could confirm or disconfirm. When he considers a believer and a non-believer disputing the reality of a Last Judgement, he denies that they are affirming and denying the same proposition in the way two witnesses might disagree about what happened at an accident. For the believer, the picture of a Last Judgement functions as something that regulates his entire life: it is bound up with how he weighs his actions, what he fears, and what he hopes for, in a way that has no equivalent for the non-believer, who may use exactly the same words without their exerting any such hold on him. The disagreement, then, is not over a shared fact so much as a difference in what the words are doing in each person's life. It follows that Wittgenstein's account amounts to the same verdict the logical positivists reached by a different route: because religious statements are not answerable to evidence, they fail to state facts, and are therefore meaningless in the technical sense Ayer gives that word, mere pseudo-propositions dressed up as assertions. Wittgenstein arrives at this from within ordinary language rather than from a verifiability criterion, but the destination is the same.
