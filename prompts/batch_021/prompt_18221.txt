My sister — a philosopher whose dissertation is on the ethics of promising — is getting married next month, and she has asked me to give the toast, her reasoning being that since making difficult ideas legible to a general audience is my job, I ought to get the philosophy right rather than lean on sentiment. Her one rule: I am not allowed to say that the vows are "magic words" that bind the two of them together, since her own work argues against exactly that folk picture, in favor of something closer to Scanlon's account of why breaking a promise wrongs someone — by exploiting expectations you deliberately encouraged, not by defying some independent power the words themselves carry. Roughly half the guests will be her philosophy department, who will notice if I fake it; the other half is family who have never heard of Scanlon and just want three warm, funny minutes. Write the toast, about three minutes long: it should use a real, defensible answer to why promises bind as its emotional core rather than as decoration, and it needs to land for both halves of the room.
