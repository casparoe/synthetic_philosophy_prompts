Ok, this is a weirdly specific thing but it's been eating at me all week. I've had panic disorder since I was 19 (I'm 29 now), and about three years ago I started on a combination of meds that basically wiped out the panic attacks completely -- I can fly, do public speaking, ride crowded trains, all the stuff that used to reliably set me off, and I feel totally fine, not even a flicker of anxiety, through any of it. I'm now applying for a long-term disability policy through a broker (apparently every lawyer and doctor buys one of these in their late 20s so I figured I'd get on it too), and the medical questionnaire asks point blank whether I "currently have or are being treated for" panic disorder or anxiety. I called my psychiatrist to ask how she thought I should answer it, and she said something that's kind of stuck with me since: technically yes, I still have it, the meds are masking it and not curing it, and she's watched other patients relapse within weeks of going off the same combo. But the disorder itself, by literally every definition I can find, including the one on the insurer's own form, is defined by actually having panic attacks, and I haven't had one in three years. So by that standard it really seems like I don't have it anymore, or at least not in any sense that should matter to whoever's underwriting this thing.

I brought this up with a couple friends and they split into two camps almost instantly and got weirdly heated about it, which is how I know it's more interesting than just an insurance technicality. I don't want to lie on a medical form, but I also don't want to volunteer myself into a higher premium or a permanent exclusion rider over a condition I can't currently point to any actual evidence of. My psychiatrist's "masked, not cured" line feels right on some level, but it also seems like it could just as easily be used to say anyone on any long-term medication -- insulin, allergy shots, blood pressure pills, whatever -- still secretly "has" their underlying condition forever, which cant be the right way to think about this. Setting aside the actual insurance-law technicalities (I'll ask a broker about those separately), is there a principled way to tell apart a condition that's genuinely gone from one that's just being actively suppressed, or is this basically a definitional wash with no real fact of the matter underneath it?
