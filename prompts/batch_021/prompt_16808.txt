At a dinner party, two guests fell into an argument about a news story everyone at the table had just read. Two workers at the same distribution center had each defeated the same safety interlock on a packing machine so they could move faster, a shortcut apparently common at the plant. One worker's tampering caused a machine to crush a coworker's hand; the other's tampering, on a different line, never hurt anyone before a routine audit caught it. The company fired the first worker and cooperated with prosecutors on criminal charges, but merely suspended the second.

One guest said the disparity was clearly right: a response to wrongdoing has to track the harm actually done, since answering for the injury, reassuring the rest of the floor that the plant is safe, and demonstrating that tampering has real consequences are all things the harsher response accomplishes and the milder one doesn't need to. The other replied that this punishes luck rather than choice: both workers made the identical decision, with identical disregard for an identical risk, and which machine happened to have a hand in it that day had nothing to do with either one's judgment. If what a person deserves tracks what they decided to do, the two of them deserve the same response.

The first guest said the principle proves too much: if it were right, the law's near-universal practice of punishing completed harm more severely than reckless near-misses with the same mental state would be a mistake everywhere it occurs, yet utilitarian penal theorists have long defended exactly that gap on the ground that it gives a wrongdoer a reason to stop short of completion — a reason that disappears the moment near-misses are punished as heavily as harms. The second guest said that rationale doesn't actually fit these facts: neither worker stopped, repented, or was deterred by anything; one was simply caught by an audit before misfortune struck, so there is no desistance here to reward, and the only thing left to justify the harsher penalty is the harm itself, which was exactly what was in dispute.

Neither would budge, and I couldn't settle it myself.
