Question: In the original position, the parties are asked to choose principles of justice from behind a veil of ignorance. Explain why Rawls holds that the parties would agree on the two principles of justice, ranked in lexical priority, rather than on a principle of maximizing average utility. Your answer should address: (a) the role of the veil of ignorance in blocking appeals to the "principle of insufficient reason"; (b) the content and status of the priority rules governing the two principles; and (c) whether Rawls's argument for the difference principle depends on an assumption that the parties are risk-averse, with reference to Harsanyi's objection.

Student response: In the original position, the parties are deprived by the veil of ignorance of all knowledge of their particular talents, social position, and conception of the good, and even of their own psychological propensities; they know only general facts about society, economics, and human psychology. Because they lack any basis for assigning probabilities to the various social positions they might occupy, Rawls denies that they can invoke a principle of insufficient reason and treat every position as equally likely, which is the premise Harsanyi needs in order to derive average utilitarianism from the original position. Absent secure probabilities, Rawls argues that the parties would instead reason according to the maximin rule, ranking the available sets of principles by their worst outcomes and selecting whichever ranking has the best worst outcome.

This reasoning yields the two principles of justice, ordered lexically: first, equal basic liberties for all; second, fair equality of opportunity together with the difference principle, which permits social and economic inequalities only insofar as they benefit the least advantaged representative group. The priority rules mean that gains to the worse-off cannot normally be purchased by sacrificing equal liberties, and that positions must be fairly open before the resulting distribution of income and wealth is assessed by the difference principle. Where the two goals genuinely conflict, however, Rawls allows the difference principle to take precedence, since a scheme of liberties that left the least advantaged badly off would not be one the parties could stably accept.

Harsanyi's objection is that this whole procedure smuggles in an unmotivated aversion to risk. If the parties really are ignorant of which position they will occupy, ordinary Bayesian decision theory recommends assigning equal probability to each and maximizing expected utility, which amounts to maximizing average utility across social positions. Adopting maximin instead, and caring only about the worst outcome, is formally equivalent to what an expected-utility maximizer would do only under a very high, arguably infinite, degree of risk aversion. Rawls's argument for the difference principle therefore tacitly relies on the assumption that the parties are strongly risk-averse; it is this assumption, rather than the mere absence of probabilities, that ultimately does the work of ruling out the average utility principle in favor of maximin.
