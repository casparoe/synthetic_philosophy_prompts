I've been reading Overcoming Bias and LessWrong for well over a decade, and a discussion at my local rationalist meetup's reading group last week left me more convinced than ever that the professional philosophical literature on peer disagreement is strangely behind the curve. Robin Hanson has argued for years that genuinely rational Bayesian agents who share a common prior cannot knowingly remain in disagreement once their posterior probabilities become common knowledge to one another -- Aumann's agreement theorem guarantees it -- and that persistent, acknowledged disagreement between smart, honest people is therefore itself strong evidence that somebody (maybe both parties) is being irrational, dishonest, or failing to share the priors they should rationally share. That looks to me like a decisive vindication of conciliationism: if two people who each regard the other as an epistemic peer keep disagreeing after laying out their full reasoning to one another, Aumann's theorem tells us that at least one of them has made an error somewhere, so the only rational response to a recognized peer disagreement is to move substantially toward the peer's credence rather than dig in.

Given that, I don't understand why steadfastness is still treated as a serious contender in the disagreement literature. Thomas Kelly's total evidence view and Peter van Inwagen's writings on reasonable philosophical disagreement both want to preserve room for rationally holding firm on a view even after a recognized peer disagrees, appealing to things like one's own first-person confidence in having reasoned carefully. But if the math already shows that two rational agents cannot stay in a state of standing, common-knowledge disagreement, doesn't steadfastness amount to relabeling ordinary stubbornness as an epistemic policy? I'd appreciate help understanding exactly what steadfasters think they're preserving here, because from where I sit, Aumann's theorem seems to have already closed off their position before the philosophical debate even got going.
