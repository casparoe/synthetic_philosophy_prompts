For the last three years, four other novelists and I have run what we privately call "the pool." At the start of each new book, everyone wires two thousand dollars into a joint account, and we set our own quarterly page targets against our own stated deadlines. Whoever misses a checkpoint forfeits that quarter's stake -- not to the rest of us, since that would leave everyone quietly rooting for company in failure, but to a political cause each person named in advance as the one they'd most hate to see funded. It's a slightly deranged arrangement, and I've watched it produce three finished manuscripts, including my own most recent one, out of writers who had spent years circling their subjects without landing anywhere.

I mentioned all this recently to an old friend from graduate school who now teaches economics, expecting her to find it charming. Instead she was faintly appalled on my behalf. Her argument was that the pool cannot be doing what I think it's doing: if finishing the book on schedule is truly what I want, all things considered, then I would simply do it and the pool adds nothing; if it isn't truly what I want, then the pool is just coercing a later self into an outcome an earlier self wasn't entitled to impose on her, and calling that "help" is a nice piece of self-deception. Either way, she said, a rational person has no business paying to be threatened by her own money. I didn't have a good rebuttal on the spot, though I remain unconvinced she's right, if only because so much of literary history is made of writers rigging exactly this kind of trap for themselves -- I've heard, though I've never run down the sources closely enough to vouch for them, that Victor Hugo had his valet lock away his clothes so he couldn't decently leave the house before a deadline, and that Jonathan Franzen once disabled his own laptop's networking hardware for the length of a novel.

So I'd like to know, in terms sturdier than my instinct that she's missing something: is there a genuine sense in which a rational, self-aware person could still want to be bound like this, or does the very fact that I need the pool concede her point before the argument even starts?
