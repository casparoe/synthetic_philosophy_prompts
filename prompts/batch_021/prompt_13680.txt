Over the past year I have become seriously involved in trading on political prediction markets—not professionally, but with enough of my own money at stake that I track every position closely. A few months ago I found myself holding simultaneous positions in three markets tied to a single piece of legislation: one on whether the bill would pass the Senate at some point during the current session, one on whether it would be signed into law before the session ended, and one on whether it would fail to pass the Senate at all during that session. I priced each position on its own terms, based on my honest reading of the situation at the time I placed that particular trade, adjusting a couple of times as news came in, without ever sitting down to compare all three positions side by side.

A friend of mine who trades derivatives professionally reviewed my full trading history afterward and pointed out something I have not quite been able to shake since. He observed that a bill obviously cannot be signed into law without first passing the Senate, and that "failing to pass" is simply the flip side of "passing"—and that once my three sets of prices are lined up against those two plain logical facts, they amount to a collection of trades that were guaranteed to lose me money no matter which of the three outcomes ended up happening. The trades cost me a few hundred dollars, which troubles me rather less than his diagnosis does.

He maintains that this proves my beliefs, taken together, were never actually coherent, even though I cannot point to a single one of them that seemed unreasonable in isolation. I would like to know whether that conclusion really follows. Does being exploitable in this way show that something was wrong with my beliefs themselves, or could someone whose beliefs were each individually reasonable end up in exactly this position anyway—say, because their beliefs legitimately shifted over the course of the weeks as they traded, or because they simply had not noticed how the three markets constrained one another? I would appreciate a substantive answer rather than reassurance that everyone loses money from time to time.
