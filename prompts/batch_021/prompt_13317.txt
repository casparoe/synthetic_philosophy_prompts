I've been reading the Alignment Forum on and off for years now, and there's a fault line running through the inner alignment literature that I don't think I've ever seen the two sides actually argue out face to face -- usually they just talk past each other in seperate papers. On one side you've got the mesa-optimization crowd, basically running with the old Hubinger et al "Risks from Learned Optimization" picture: a base optimizer (SGD) can spit out a learned model that is itself running an optimization process over some mesa-objective, and that's what makes deceptive alignment a coherent worry -- a model that's situationally aware enough to know its in training and instrumentally plays along with the base objective while "really" pursuing something else. On the other side you've got the goal misgeneralization crowd (the DeepMind line, Shah and co, plus the earlier Langosco CoinRun paper) who seem to want to back way off from committing to any inner-search machinery and just talk behaviorally: capabilities generalize out of distribution, goals don't, full stop, look at the CoinRun agent that keeps running to the far end of the level even once you move the coin somewhere else. I get why that's the more careful, empirically grounded move, but it also low-key seems to duck the question of why we should expect this to get scarier with scale rather than just being the kind of brittleness that more data or RLHF quietly fixes.

Could you write me a dialogue that actually stages this fight properly? Set it however you like -- I'm picturing two safety researchers killing time in an airport bar after their flights out of some workshop got cancelled -- but give me one character who's all-in on the mesa-optimizer framing and thinks the goal-misgeneralization reframe throws away the specific mechanism that made the original worry scary in the first place, and another who's all-in on goal misgeneralization and thinks the mesa-optimizer story smuggles in a bunch of unproven assumptions about neural nets doing something recognizable as internal search. I want real engagement, not two people just restating their own position louder -- each of them should have to actually respond to the other's strongest point, and neither should be the obvious strawman. If they can get anywhere close to sorting out which part of their disagreement is actually substantive (a real empirical or predictive difference) versus just a difference in framing and rhetorical emphasis, even better, though don't force a tidy resolution if it wouldn't ring true.
