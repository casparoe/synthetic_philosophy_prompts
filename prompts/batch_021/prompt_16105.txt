I'm a philosophy PhD student who co-runs a small podcast on ethics with two other students in my program, and next month we're recording an episode with a guest who has developed an account of the standing to blame on which hypocrisy is not merely bad form but a genuine bar to blaming. On her view, blame is a mode of moral address that presupposes equal standing between blamer and blamed, and the hypocrite forfeits that standing by implicitly claiming, for herself, an exemption from a norm she is unwilling to extend to others. In her published work she leans heavily on the "unclean hands" doctrine from equity law to insist that the standing to blame and the truth of the verdict being delivered are separate questions, and after several talks she has gotten quite practiced at parrying the charge that this whole apparatus is just an ad hominem tu quoque dressed up in jurisprudential language. I've been working through this literature for a dissertation chapter and want the episode to actually test the view rather than let her replay her best lines.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that push past a bare restatement of the position? I'd like at least one question on whether the unclean-hands analogy really secures the distinction between standing and warrant or just relocates the puzzle one level up; one on how the account should treat a reformed wrongdoer who has repudiated their past conduct but never made any amends for it; one on whether losing standing with respect to one norm (say, dishonesty) should bleed into losing standing to blame violations of an unrelated norm (say, cruelty); and one that presses the worry that, since virtually everyone has broken some moral rule at some point, a sufficiently demanding hypocrisy condition threatens to leave almost nobody with standing to blame anybody. For each of these, attach a short follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give, so the exchange doesn't simply stall out at the first parry, and end the set with a question asking what sort of case would actually count as evidence against her view rather than just another example it can absorb.
