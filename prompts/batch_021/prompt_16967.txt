Every election cycle I end up having the same argument with my brother-in-law, who insists that voting is pointless because the odds of any single ballot actually deciding a presidential or even a statewide race are, in his words, worse than the odds of getting struck by lightning on the way to the polling place. Curious whether anyone takes this seriously, I went digging and found that economists and political scientists have been fighting over exactly this "paradox of voting" for decades. The two responses I kept running into were, first, tacking some sort of "sense of civic duty" or satisfaction term onto the cost-benefit math so that voting comes out worthwhile even when the odds of being decisive are close to zero, and second, the "expressive voting" idea (I think this one is credited to the economists Geoffrey Brennan and Loren Lomasky) that casting a ballot is more like cheering at a stadium than like trying to swing the outcome, so the tiny odds of being pivotal are simply beside the point. Neither has ever fully satisfied me: the first feels invented after the fact just to make the numbers come out right, and the second doesn't explain why campaigns and voters alike seem to care so much more about turning out when a race is genuinely close, which is exactly what you'd expect if people did care, on some level, about actually swinging the result.

What I haven't seen laid out anywhere in plain language is a third possibility, one I've only ever seen gestured at online under a label like "team reasoning" without finding a real explanation attached to it: the idea that "will my vote decide it?" is simply the wrong question, because the unit that should be doing the cost-benefit reasoning here was never the lone individual to begin with. I'd like you to actually build that case rather than just describe it. Please argue, as forcefully as you can, that it can be rational for a citizen to vote in a competitive election even if that citizen is entirely self-interested and gets no expressive thrill or sense of duty from the act itself, on the grounds that the correct standard of rational choice for a situation like this is something other than one person's individual, causally-focused expected-utility calculation. In making the case, say something concrete about what unit of agency should be doing the reasoning instead and why that still counts as rationality rather than wishful thinking dressed up in fancier language, explain why the account doesn't just quietly collapse back into the civic-duty or expressive-voting fixes I mentioned above, and address head-on whether the fact that turnout rises as races get closer actually supports your view or sits more comfortably with the individual-odds picture you're arguing against.
