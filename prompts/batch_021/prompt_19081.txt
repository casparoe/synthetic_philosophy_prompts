I teach philosophy, and although nothing about this arose in one of my own courses, a conversation from a dinner party several weeks ago has stayed with me more than I expected, and I would value a more theoretically grounded second opinion on it. One of the guests, an old friend from graduate school who now runs a small architecture practice, is by everyone's account an unusually generous boss: her employees describe her as quick to forgive mistakes, slow to anger, and generally inclined to give people the benefit of the doubt. Toward herself, however, she is startlingly severe. She mentioned, half as a joke, that if she is five minutes late to a meeting or sends out a client email with a typo, she will spend the rest of the day privately calling herself lazy, careless, and undisciplined — language she said she would never use on an employee who made an identical mistake.

When I pressed her on the discrepancy, she said that she had been fiercely, even "viciously" competitive as an undergraduate, especially in debate and varsity sports, but had learned that this trait was costly once she was managing other people, so she had consciously redirected it: in her words, "I turned all of that against myself instead." She did not describe this as a wound or a compromise so much as an achievement, and seemed to feel that the private harshness was both the price of, and the proof of, her discipline as a professional. She was not especially interested in being talked out of it.

I cannot decide what to make of this. It does not look like ordinary perfectionism, since she is genuinely relaxed about outcomes, deadlines, and other people's errors, and reserves the severity entirely for herself; it looks much more like an old aggressive energy that simply had to find a new target once she could no longer discharge it onto other people. I would be grateful for your help thinking through what is actually going on here philosophically, and whether, properly diagnosed, this is a pattern to be quietly admired, gently challenged, or actively dismantled.
