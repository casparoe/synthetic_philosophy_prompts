And here's one student's answer, word for word:

"Hume's problem was to justify induction as a form of inference at all, but Goodman argues that solving that wouldn't be enough, because the very same evidence supports contrary predictions once you allow gerrymandered predicates. Take all the emeralds examined so far, all before some future time T: every one has been green, so ordinary induction says future emeralds will be green too. But define 'grue' so that something is grue if it changes from green to blue exactly at T; every emerald examined so far is equally grue, since none of them have reached T yet, so the very same data confirms 'all emeralds are grue' too — which predicts future emeralds will turn out blue. Nothing in the raw logic of confirmation tells us to prefer one hypothesis over the other, so Hume's uniformity principle by itself doesn't tell us which regularities to project.

Goodman's fix is to say 'green' is projectible and 'grue' isn't because of entrenchment: 'green' and predicates like it have actually been used again and again in successful past projections, while 'grue' has no such history (Goodman coined the word just for this example). Projectibility, on this view, isn't a metaphysical fact about which properties are 'really' natural, it's a fact about the track record of the predicates themselves in our actual inductive practice.

But there's a well-known reply: someone could define a language where 'grue' and 'bleen' are the primitive terms, with 'green' and 'blue' defined disjunctively out of them, and that speaker's 'grue' would be exactly as entrenched, by the same test, as our 'green' is for us. Since Goodman has no way to rule out that language as illegitimate except by pointing out that it isn't the one we happen to speak, his solution doesn't really explain why grue is a worse predicate than green — it only explains why English speakers are psychologically disposed to project one rather than the other, which was never really in dispute."
