A friend of mine — who is finishing a doctorate in logic and spends her weekends reading AI-safety papers for fun — pulled me into a two-hour argument last night that I still haven't let go of. It started with her trying to explain something she calls "the Löbian obstacle": the reason, on her account, why a self-modifying AI can never fully verify that a smarter successor version of itself will be safe. As I understood it, the trouble is this: if an agent only acts on formally proven guarantees, and it wants to hand control over to a successor built the same way, it needs to be able to prove something like "whenever my successor's proof system certifies an action as safe, that action really is safe." But Löb's theorem says that no system strong enough to reason about its own proofs can establish that kind of blanket reflection principle about a system as powerful as itself without thereby being able to prove literally anything at all — which means the very guarantee such an agent wants is one that a sane, consistent reasoner structurally cannot have. She thinks this is a narrow technical curiosity that engineers can route around with clever patches (strictly weaker successors, a different proof system one level down, probabilistic rather than deductive reflection); I kept arguing it names a genuine and completely general limit on any mind that tries to certify a more capable heir from the inside, with no outside check available. We went back and forth until past one in the morning and never agreed.
