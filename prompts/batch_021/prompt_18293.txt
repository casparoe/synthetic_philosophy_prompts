Write a dialogue between a philosopher defending a fitting-attitude (buck-passing) analysis of value — according to which something is good just in case it has features that provide reasons to favor it — and a critic who presses the "wrong kind of reasons" problem against this view. The critic should open by describing a case in which someone stands to win a great deal of money if they can bring themselves to admire something perfectly unremarkable, such as a patch of mud: the prospect of winning gives them a genuine reason to want to admire the mud, yet it would be absurd to conclude that the mud is thereby rendered admirable. The defender should try to save the analysis by distinguishing reasons grounded in an object's own value-relevant features from reasons grounded merely in the incidental benefits of having the attitude, while the critic should press on whether this distinction can be drawn without covertly presupposing an independent grip on which features are value-relevant, and should also ask whether the same maneuver is available for the structurally similar problem facing reasons for belief, illustrated by Pascal's Wager-style cases in which one has a prudential rather than an evidential reason to believe. The dialogue should end not with a clear winner but with both characters agreeing on exactly what the defender's distinction would need to deliver in order to succeed.
