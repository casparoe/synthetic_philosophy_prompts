Two of my grad students got into a real arguement after our seminar on higher-order evidence last week, and it's been bugging me since. Here's the case that kicked it off: Sam, a proofreader, rereads a page five times and is dead sure there's no typo on it. Then he's told about a study showing that proofreaders who feel exactly this confident after this many rereads are still wrong about 40% of the time, because familiarity breeds a false sense of thoroughness. Sam's gut feeling doesn't move an inch, but he comes to believe, given the stat, that he shouldn't be as confident as he feels.

Here's how the argument between my students went:

1. Nadia said Sam is just being irrational if his confidence doesn't budge — believing "no typo" while also believing "I shouldn't be this sure" is a textbook case of epistemic akrasia, and akrasia is irrational, same as it is in ethics.
2. Marcus said that's too quick: the 40% figure is about proofreaders in general, it doesn't show Sam misread anything this particular time. If he checks again and it still looks clean, downgrading that because of a base rate is like discounting your own eyes because eyewitnesses are unreliable on average.
3. Nadia said that's exactly why the stat counts as higher-order evidence rather than just more first-order evidence: it says Sam's checking faculty itself might be off in a way he can't catch from the inside, so a recheck can't rescue him, since the recheck runs through the very faculty being questioned.
4. Marcus said okay, but then Sam's total evidence supports both "no typo" and "don't trust that," and nothing settles which one should win — so why does the outward-facing stat automatically beat the inward-facing check rather than the other way round?
5. Nadia said because otherwise higher-order evidence could never do any work at all: any belief could dodge it by saying "I looked again and it still seems right to me."
6. Marcus said maybe that just shows rationality is handing Sam two demands he can't jointly satisfy, and him ending up akratic is a symptom of that bind, not a mistake he's making.

Neither one budged. Could you referee this turn by turn rather than just picking an overall winner?
