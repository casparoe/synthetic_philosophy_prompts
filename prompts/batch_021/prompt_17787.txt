I retired from experimental physics three years ago and have spent a good part of the time since finally reading philosophy in the unhurried way that thirty-five years of grant deadlines never allowed. One figure I have become rather attached to is Émilie du Châtelet, not least because she was a working physicist before she was anything else — she ran her own experiments on the nature of fire and heat, took the vis viva side against the Cartesians in the great dispute over what we would now call kinetic energy, and produced the French translation and commentary of Newton's Principia that, remarkably, remains the standard one to this day — and yet still found time to write a short essay, unpublished in her lifetime, called the Discourse on Happiness. I now think it is one of the more clear-eyed things ever written on the subject, and it has become relevant to me in an unexpectedly practical way.

Helen, my closest collaborator and friend of nearly thirty years, is retiring next month, and I have been asked to give the toast at her dinner. She has been, since our postdoc days, the one person in every department we have shared who actually had opinions about du Châtelet, and kept a postcard of her portrait taped above her desk for decades because, as she once told me, it was a relief in an overwhelmingly male field to have one real example of a woman permitted to be rigorous and passionate without anyone treating those as pulling in different directions. I would like the toast to be built around the Discourse itself rather than merely mention her, and two pieces of it seem made for the occasion. The first is du Châtelet's argument that the passions which carry us through youth — love, the desire for glory and recognition — become harder to sustain and less wise to keep at the center of a life as we age, and that the one passion capable of replacing them is the love of study, because its rewards come from ourselves alone rather than requiring the world's cooperation; she is insistent, though, that this taste has to be cultivated well before it is needed, since it cannot simply be picked up once the other passions have already gone slack. The second is a wonderfully unapologetic passage in which she defends her own love of gambling, admitting outright that it cannot be justified by the arithmetic of the thing and defending it anyway on the grounds that it keeps boredom at bay, provided one can afford to lose and loses with grace — a passage I expect will get a knowing laugh out of the handful of people in the room who have actually read the essay.

Here is my complication, and where I would actually like your judgment rather than a recitation of the above: on retiring, Helen is not taking up anything resembling study. She has instead, to her children's mild alarm, joined a weekly poker game with a seriousness normally reserved for tenure review, and the easy joke writes itself — she has finally found a vice with du Châtelet's own blessing. But I do not think that is quite right, because I do not think du Châtelet treats gambling and study as two solutions to the same problem: one is a defensible way of passing time without ruin, the other is what she actually recommends building a happy old age on. So the toast needs either an honest case for why the poker table is doing the work du Châtelet assigned to study, or it needs to make peace with the fact that it is not, and find the affection and the joke in that gap rather than paper over it. Could you write me the toast? It should run about five minutes read aloud, include at least one joke that only really lands if you know what the gambling passage actually says and not merely that she enjoyed cards, work equally well for the physicists in the room who will catch every reference and for family and old friends who have never heard of du Châtelet and should not have to pretend otherwise, and read, in the end, like a tribute to one particular person rather than a philosophy lecture that happens to use her name.
