I spent thirty-five years as an experimental particle physicist before retiring four years ago, and only in the last year or so have I taken up a rather late-arriving education in philosophy of science, most recently a run of reading on the foundations of statistical inference that I probably should have done fifty years before I ever quoted a five-sigma result. What has unsettled me is the likelihood principle, and in particular its consequence that the stopping rule an experimenter follows -- the reason she decided to stop collecting data when she did -- has no bearing on what the data, once collected, actually tell you, provided you reason about them in Bayesian terms. This cuts directly against the frequentist testing I built a career on, in which the validity of a p-value depends essentially on the sampling and stopping plan behind it, not merely on the data in hand. I have been discussing all this with two old colleagues from my department, both bemused that I now spend more time reading philosophers than I ever spent calibrating detectors, but neither could tell me who is right, which is part of why I am raising it here. It has also stopped being merely an abstract puzzle for me, because I now sit on my university's Research Practices Committee -- a body distinct from the formal misconduct office, charged with adjudicating disputes about methodological rigor -- and a case has landed on our desk that turns on precisely this point.

A postdoctoral researcher in the psychology department ran a two-condition behavioral study with an internal, unregistered plan to recruit 200 participants. She checked her results periodically as they came in and stopped at 137, once a two-tailed test had crossed the conventional threshold (p = .043). A graduate student in her lab, who later failed to replicate the effect independently and does not appear on the resulting paper, has filed a complaint alleging that this "optional stopping" is exactly the sort of undisclosed flexibility blamed for her field's replication crisis, and has asked the committee to find a violation of the department's data-integrity policy and require a correction to the published paper. The postdoc's written response, prepared with a colleague from the statistics department, concedes the sequential checking but denies any impropriety: she reports that a Bayes factor computed on the final data, using a prior she specified before she ever looked at the results, favors her hypothesis by roughly 8 to 1, and argues that since the evidential meaning of the data she collected does not depend on why she stopped collecting it, the complaint is really an objection to the frequentist framework's own sensitivity to stopping rules, and not to anything she actually did.

The committee is now split along precisely this line, and not, to my surprise, along the usual fault line between the hard scientists and the social scientists among us. Half of us want to hold the postdoc to the standard she herself reported her result under: a p-value whose validity she compromised by the very manner in which she gathered the data, whatever a later Bayesian reanalysis might say. The other half think it would be perverse to sanction her for violating a norm whose own philosophical rationale is the very thing she is disputing, particularly on the strength of a framework several of us privately doubt we could defend if pressed. I need to circulate a short written opinion before we meet next week, and I would like your help drafting it. It should reach a specific finding on this case, with a corrective action and a deadline if one is warranted, and it should also propose a policy for the department to adopt going forward, since this will not be the last complaint of its kind. Whatever it recommends, it should engage honestly with the strongest version of the losing argument rather than brushing it aside.
