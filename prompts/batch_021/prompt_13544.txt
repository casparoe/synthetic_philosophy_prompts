Alongside my own reporting on the life sciences, I've served for the past two years on the small grant committee that awards our foundation's annual long-form science-journalism fellowship, which gives one writer six months of support to develop a nonfiction book project from proposal to sample chapters. This cycle's strongest finalist has built her chapters around the female orgasm as a clean case of adaptation, arguing, with very little hedging, that it evolved specifically to let ancestral women assess a prospective partner's genetic quality, with orgasm frequency tracking his desirability as a mate. The writing itself is excellent. But one of our outside readers, an evolutionary biologist, has flagged the central claim as exactly the sort of thing Gould and Lewontin warned about in their 1979 spandrels paper: an adaptationist story built to explain a trait that may simply be a developmental byproduct, present because the clitoris and penis share an embryonic origin, in something like the way Gould separately argued that male nipples are carried along by selection acting on female breast tissue rather than being targets of selection in their own right. I'd never read past the famous "spandrels" metaphor before this came up, but chasing down the reader's citations sent me to Elisabeth Lloyd's book-length survey of the rival adaptive hypotheses for orgasm, which she argues all fail on the evidence, and reading it has shifted my own thinking more than I expected going in.

Not everyone on the committee is persuaded, and I don't think they're being obtuse. One member points out that the byproduct hypothesis is no more rigorously confirmed than the story it's meant to replace, and that Gould and Lewontin's own paper has been accused, notably by Dennett, of rigging the game: setting adaptationism up as a "Panglossian" straw man so that any specific selective hypothesis can be waved off as unfalsifiable storytelling, while "it's probably just a byproduct" gets treated as the modest default rather than a substantive empirical claim that needs support of its own. There's a program-level stake as well: if this degree of hedging becomes our bar for funding, we risk favoring cautious literature reviews over the confident, narrative-driven writing the fellowship was created to support. The committee votes Friday, and as secretary I've been asked to circulate a recommendation beforehand.

Could you draft that memo? It should come down clearly on one of three options -- fund the proposal as submitted, fund it contingent on specific and actionable revisions (say what they are and set a deadline for a revised outline), or decline it this cycle -- with reasoning precise enough that the writer and both camps on the committee could respond to it point by point. I'd especially like you to take the falsifiability objection to the spandrels critique seriously on its own terms, rather than letting "it might just be a byproduct" function as an automatic trump card, since that's the part of the disagreement I'm least confident I could adjudicate myself.
