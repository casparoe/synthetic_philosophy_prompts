After a long career in experimental particle physics, I have spent much of my retirement working through the philosophical literature on free will, and for the past year I have been leading a small non-credit seminar on the subject at our local lifelong-learning institute. As I have no formal training in philosophy myself, I would welcome a rigorous outside opinion on how to grade the participants' written work.

The set question for last session was: "Reconstruct Galen Strawson's Basic Argument for the impossibility of ultimate moral responsibility as a numbered sequence of premises leading to a conclusion. Explain why Strawson holds that the argument succeeds whether determinism or indeterminism is true. Finally, relate the argument to one other position on free will discussed this term, and state explicitly whether that position gives the regress a way out."

Below, reproduced in full, is one participant's submitted answer:

"Strawson's Basic Argument tries to show that no one can ever be truly, or 'ultimately,' morally responsible for their actions — responsible in the strong sense that would make it ultimately just to reward or punish someone for what they have done, independent of any further, forward-looking benefit that reward or punishment might have. The argument runs as follows. (1) When you act, you act in the way you do because of the way you are — because of your beliefs, desires, values, and other relevant mental characteristics. (2) So, to be truly responsible for what you do, you would have to be truly responsible for being the way you are, at least in the mental respects that explain the action. (3) But you cannot be truly responsible for being the way you are, since to be responsible for that you would have had to choose your own character in light of some prior set of values — and to be responsible for choosing in light of those values, you would already have had to have formed yourself in some still earlier act of self-creation, and so on without end; the regress can never be completed. (4) Therefore, you cannot be truly or ultimately responsible for anything you do.

Strawson insists that none of this turns on determinism being true. Even if some events in the causal chain running through your brain are genuinely indeterministic, the resulting randomness does not help, since an action that traces back to a random 'swerve' is not thereby more fully attributable to you as its author — if anything, it is less so. As Strawson puts it, borrowing the notion of a causa sui, or self-caused cause, from Sartre, true self-determination would require you to be the ultimate cause of yourself, which is an incoherent demand that no arrangement of the physical facts, deterministic or not, could ever satisfy.

This bears usefully on Robert Kane's libertarian account, which we discussed a few weeks ago. Kane, too, speaks of the 'ultimate responsibility' an agent must have over her actions, and he locates the required indeterminism in 'self-forming actions': choices made at moments of real inner conflict between incompatible desires, where the outcome is not wholly fixed by the agent's already-formed character. On this point, Strawson's Basic Argument and Kane's event-causal libertarianism actually converge rather than conflict: Strawson accepts that a self-forming action of just this kind is what allows the regress to terminate, since an SFA does not need to be traced back to some still-prior, fully formed self. Both philosophers therefore agree that a suitably placed injection of indeterminism into the process of character formation is what lets the buck finally stop."
