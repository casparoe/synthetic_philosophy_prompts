For much of my career I worked as a comparative ethologist, first studying tool use and problem-solving in corvids and later social cognition in captive chimpanzees, so questions about the moral status of nonhuman animals have never been far from my mind. Since retiring I've tried to shore up the philosophical background my scientific training never gave me, and for the past year a small reading group of retired colleagues and I have been working through the canonical texts of twentieth-century political philosophy. We recently finished Robert Nozick's *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*. The entitlement theory of justice and the Wilt Chamberlain argument gave us little trouble, but I've been stuck on the brief, almost apologetic passage where Nozick turns to the treatment of animals.

As I reconstruct his overall position, the whole point of treating individual rights as "side constraints" rather than as one more value to be maximized is that persons are the kind of beings who can shape their existence around a freely chosen life plan, and it is this capacity that makes it impermissible to sacrifice any one of them for the benefit of others. Nozick is explicit that animals lack this capacity, and so do not qualify for side-constraint protection. It seems to follow, then, that within his system animal suffering has no independent moral standing at all: unlike even a fairly unambitious utilitarianism, which would at minimum register animal pain as a cost to be weighed, Nozick's libertarianism appears to have no resources for counting animal interests as mattering in the least, so that nothing done to an animal could ever wrong anyone. That strikes me as a strange result for a theory that makes such a fuss about inviolability and the wrongness of treating any being as a mere means. Have I reconstructed Nozick's actual position correctly here, or is there something in his discussion that keeps animal welfare from vanishing from the theory altogether?
