I've been reading Overcoming Bias, LessWrong, and the wider rationalist blogosphere for years, including Yudkowsky's "Living in Many Worlds" and Tegmark's original quantum suicide proposal, and what I've never come across is fiction that takes the live technical disagreement seriously rather than coasting on the pop-science slogan that many-worlds means nobody really dies.

Write a short story whose protagonist has concluded that quantum immortality is simply correct and starts using a Tegmark-style quantum suicide device to force increasingly reckless gambles, reasoning that she will only ever find herself in the branches where she survives. Partway through, complicate her confidence with the measure problem: each trial shrinks the Born-rule weight of the surviving branch, even though no subjective experience of dying will ever show up to register the loss. If it fits naturally, work in something like Robin Hanson's "mangled worlds" proposal — on which branches of sufficiently low relative amplitude may fail to decohere cleanly enough to support a continuing observer at all — as a further wrinkle in her reasoning.

Let the philosophical content emerge through her choices and internal reasoning rather than lecture-like dialogue, and leave it genuinely open at the end whether she is actually going to keep surviving in any sense that should reassure her.
