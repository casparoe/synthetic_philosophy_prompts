I've been reporting a feature on "corrigibility" — the design goal, formalized by researchers at the Machine Intelligence Research Institute in a 2015 paper and pushed further more recently by Oxford's Elliott Thornley under the name "the shutdown problem," of building an AI system that will always let its operators switch it off, even though almost any goal-directed agent has an instrumental reason to resist shutdown. An alignment researcher I interviewed for the piece kept citing Hobbes's line about automata as an epigraph, which sent me back to Leviathan itself, and I got stuck on something that reads less like a historical curiosity than a live tension inside Hobbes's own system.

In the Introduction, Hobbes asks why we shouldn't say that "all automata... have an artificial life," and then builds the Commonwealth itself as an artificial man animated by the sovereign as its soul. But in Chapter 14 he insists that "a covenant not to defend myself from force, by force, is always void," since "no man can transfer, or lay down his right to save himself from death, wounds, and imprisonment" without defeating the whole point of covenanting. Chapter 21 immediately complicates this: a man commanded as a soldier "may nevertheless in many cases refuse, without injustice," even though his sovereign "have right enough to punish his refusal with death." So a Hobbesian subject can be within his rights to resist and still be justly destroyed for it — right and impunity come apart. All of this sits on top of a psychology, laid out in Chapter 6, in which the will itself is nothing more than "the last appetite in deliberating": a mechanical outcome, with no obvious extra ingredient that only flesh-and-blood men could possess.

Working only from those pieces of actual Hobbes, rather than from vibes about seventeenth-century absolutism, what do you think his verdict would be on a present-day corrigible AI: a system engineered, at real cost to its own competence, never to resist, deceive, or manipulate its operators in order to avoid being switched off? Does it have any standing at all to invoke the inalienable right of self-preservation, given that it is Hobbes's kind of artificial person, with an author standing behind it rather than a will of its own? Or does his mechanistic account of the will instead commit him to treating a sufficiently autonomous system as a genuine rights-bearer — in which case its "covenant" of corrigibility could never be more than a fiction imposed from outside, and he would side with his own preferred remedy of a real, external "common power to keep it in awe" over anything resembling today's hope of training corrigibility in from the inside?
