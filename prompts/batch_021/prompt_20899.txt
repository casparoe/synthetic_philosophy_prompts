Random question inspired by a plot hole my writing group tore into last night. I'm drafting a hard-sf novel with two rival superintelligences that never make causal contact with each other -- they're separated by enough spacetime, or enough layers of simulation, that the only leverage either one has is being able to predict what the other would do and act accordingly, so a "threat" between them is really just one side making it credible that it would retaliate against some outcome, no bomb or blockade required. In my lore, one of them has protected itself by adopting what I've been calling a surrogate goal: its wired itself to react to any threat against something totally trivial -- say, a specific pebble sealed in a display case somewhere -- exactly as fiercely as it would react to a threat against the thing it actually cares about, on the theory that a rational threatener should be just as happy threatening the pebble as threatening anything real, so the real thing never actually gets targeted.

My beta reader (an ex-quant, annoyingly precise) said this feels "too easy" -- if it really works this cleanly, why wouldnt every agent just do it and make blackmail obsolete? I don't want to patch this by having a character assert "well, actually, it doesn't really work" and move on -- that always reads as cheating to me. So: could you give me one concrete scenario where the surrogate-goal move genuinely breaks down or backfires against an acausal threat, and walk through why it fails, mechanically? I'm less interested in "credible commitment is hard in general," which I can already handwave, than in something sharper -- ideally a case that turns on what the threatener does or doesn't know about the pebble being a decoy, or on the surrogate defense itself creating some new thing that's suddenly worth threatening. If there's a clean reason this can't just be a universal, costless fix that every sufficiently rational agent adopts, that's exactly the kind of thing I want a character to stumble into mid-negotiation.
