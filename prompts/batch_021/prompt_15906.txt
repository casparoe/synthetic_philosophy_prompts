I'm several drafts into a novel set partly in Salamanca and partly in Rome during the last years of the sixteenth century, built around a fictional young Dominican friar who gets swept into the controversy that eventually produces the Congregatio de Auxiliis. I have the historical scaffolding under control — Molina's Concordia, the whispered charge of semi-Pelagianism, the decade of hearings that end with Rome declining to decide anything — but the scene the book actually needs, a real theological argument between my friar and the Jesuit he's been assigned to debate, keeps coming out flat. Every version I've drafted has one man restating his thesis while the other produces a tidy rebuttal, and I don't entirely trust my own instincts here — I read Aquinas long before I ever read Molina — to know whether that's because the historical debate really was that lopsided, or because I'm quietly strawmanning the Jesuit without meaning to.

Could you write that scene for me as a pure dialogue — no stage direction beyond speaker tags, something on the order of twelve to eighteen exchanges — in which each man has to answer the other's strongest point rather than his weakest? I'd like the Jesuit to explain what scientia media is actually doing for him: how it lets him hold that God knows, logically prior to any decree of the divine will, what any possible free creature would do in any possible circumstance, and why he takes this to be the only way of keeping grace efficacious without making God the author of every sin it fails to prevent. I'd like the friar's central move to be the objection that this "middle" knowledge has nothing to make it true — no decree, no nature, nothing — and I want to see the Jesuit actually attempt an answer rather than wave the question away. And I'd like the friar pressed in turn on whether his physical premotion amounts to anything more than the determinism he's just accused the Jesuit of secretly needing. If it fits naturally, let them fight the abstraction out over one concrete case I could actually use in the book: two men who hear the identical sermon under identical grace, and only one of whom converts — with neither man satisfied, by the end, with the other's account of why.
