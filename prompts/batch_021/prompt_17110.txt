I sit on a three-person refugee appeals panel that reviews appeals from initial asylum denials, and whatever two of the three of us agree on is what happens. I've got a file due this Thursday where my co-panelists are split right down the middle, so for the first time I'm actually the deciding vote, and I'd rather draft the panel's written opinion myself than just show up and say yes or no.

The claimant is a 23-year-old who was conscripted into his country's army, served eight months, and deserted a few weeks after his unit crossed into a neighboring country his government had just invaded. He's clear he isn't a blanket pacifist -- he says flat out he'd have fought without complaint if his own country had been the one attacked -- his objection is specifically to being made to fight in what he calls a war of naked aggression against people who never threatened his country, and he's not claiming anyone personally ordered him to do anything like shoot civilians; his unit was doing ordinary infantry work. If he's sent home he's facing something like 5-8 years in military prison for desertion. Our guidelines say objection to service only supports a claim if it reflects opposition to all war as such, or if the specific service would've required the applicant to personally carry out acts that are themselves serious violations of the laws of war -- read narrowly, he fails both prongs.

One co-panelist wants to deny on exactly that basis: her view is that the whole point of those two narrow categories is that we can't have every deserter from every unpopular war relitigating, in our hearing room, whether his own government's cause was just, and that going down that road turns the panel into amatuer just-war theorists with no good way to tell a principled objector from an opportunist. The other thinks that's a dodge that lets us off the hook for the fact that this guy did everything right -- served, then refused once the character of the war became unmistakable -- and that jailing him for years for declining to help carry out an invasion is exactly the persecution-for-conscience this whole system is supposed to catch. Can you draft the opinion I'll bring to Thursday's panel -- grant, deny, or grant on some narrower theory -- laying out the reasoning clearly enough that either of them could actually push back on specific steps of it, and giving whichever position you don't adopt its strongest form rather than a caricature? And if you land on a narrower theory, spell it out precisely enough that it'd work as an actual rule for the next borderline file, not just a one-off exception for this guy.
