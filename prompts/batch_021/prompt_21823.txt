I've spent the last few weeks reporting a piece on intellectual stubbornness in the history of science, and I keep coming back to Thomas Hobbes's extraordinary, decades-long insistence that he had squared the circle. As I understand the story (I've been working through Douglas Jesseph's history of the affair), Hobbes included a purported quadrature in De Corpore in 1655, the Oxford mathematician John Wallis picked the construction apart almost immediately, and Hobbes spent essentially the rest of his life — through pamphlet after pamphlet, into his nineties — refusing to accept that he'd made an error, even as his mathematical reputation collapsed around him.

What keeps this from being just a story about an old man's vanity, in my reading, is that Hobbes had staked a great deal of his political philosophy on the claim that civil science could be every bit as certain as geometry, for a very specific reason: both, on his view, are sciences of objects that we ourselves construct or define, so that — unlike natural philosophy, where we're stuck guessing at causes we didn't make — their properties can be known with complete certainty by anyone reasoning correctly from first principles. He even had a distinctive, almost physical picture of geometrical objects as generated by motion (a line traced by a moving point, a surface by a moving line), which is part of why he was so contemptuous of Wallis's more algebraic, symbol-heavy methods: to Hobbes, they weren't genuine geometrical demonstrations at all.

So here's the argument I'd like your help stress-testing: please argue that Hobbes's own persistent inability to recognize a straightforward geometrical error is not just an amusing footnote to his career but a real problem for this larger epistemological program — that it should at least complicate the picture when the philosopher most committed to the idea that self-constructed objects yield transparent, self-evident certainty turns out to be constitutionally unable to see that his own construction had failed. I'd like the argument to actually engage with his specific philosophy of mathematics rather than just retelling the anecdote, and if you think there's a strong rebuttal available to Hobbes or his defenders, I'd like to hear the best version of that too.
