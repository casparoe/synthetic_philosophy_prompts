I am plotting a novel in which two estranged business partners must decide, across several meetings, whether to keep growing a shared account before splitting it or to seize what is on the table immediately — structurally, this is the centipede game economists love to cite, but I don't want to use that game itself, since informed readers will spot it instantly and the scene will read like a lecture. Invent a different concrete situation, with genuine stakes rather than an abstract growing pot, in which two parties alternately choose between extending a mutually profitable arrangement and grabbing an immediate advantage, such that backward induction technically obliges the first party to seize that advantage on the very first turn. Then pinpoint exactly where that conclusion depends on each party being certain that the other is rational all the way down to the very last turn — what game theorists call common knowledge of rationality — and tell me, precisely, what ought to happen to that certainty, and to the argument built on it, the instant one party actually watches the other decline to seize the advantage when the logic said they would. I want my characters arguing about that fragility itself, not about trust or human unpredictability in some vague sense.
