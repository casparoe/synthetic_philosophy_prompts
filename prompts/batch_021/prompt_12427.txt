I'm drafting a dissertation chapter on free-will skepticism and want to test Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against its nearest nineteenth-century antecedent: Nietzsche's remarks on the causa sui in Beyond Good and Evil, §21, where he calls the notion a self-contradiction and traces the desire behind it to a kind of philosophical vanity — the wish to be wholly the author of oneself, and so to owe nothing to God, chance, society, or one's own past. Strawson's regress — from acting on reasons, to responsibility for the character behind those reasons, to the demand that this character itself have been chosen from some prior, already-unchosen character, and so on — turns on essentially the same impossibility. Compare the two treatments directly: is Nietzsche's aphorism doing argumentative work comparable to Strawson's regress, or is it closer to a psychological unmasking that exploits the same intuition without needing to defend it step by step? Then reach a verdict on whether Strawson's explicit, premise-by-premise formalization genuinely improves on Nietzsche's version, or whether it mainly just exposes weaknesses — for instance, about what "ultimate" responsibility for one's own character would even mean — that Nietzsche's rhetoric lets us glide past. Defend your answer, and support it by identifying at least one specific vulnerability that distinguishes the two arguments from each other.
