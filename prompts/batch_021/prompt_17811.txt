I spent most of my career as an experimental physicist, and it is only since retiring that I have had the leisure to read philosophy in any depth. My route into consciousness studies was Giulio Tononi's integrated information theory, and from there I was drawn into panpsychism, where I continually encounter what is called the combination problem: if the fundamental constituents of matter each carry some primitive form of experience, how do countless micro-experiences ever add up to the single, unified experience of a subject such as myself?

As far as I can tell, there are two broad strategies for handling this difficulty. So-called "bottom-up" panpsychists accept that macro-subjects are somehow built out of micro-subjects, and try to render this credible by positing a "phenomenal bonding" relation that would do for experiences what physical bonds do for particles. "Top-down" cosmopsychists instead take a single, all-encompassing cosmic subject to be fundamental, and treat finite subjects such as ourselves as in some sense derivative of it — which seems merely to trade the problem of combination for what I understand is called the decombination, or decomposition, problem: how does one undivided cosmic experience give rise to, or contain, bounded subjects like us in the first place?

I have not been able to decide for myself whether this second strategy amounts to genuine progress or is simply the first problem wearing a disguise. Would you write me a dialogue between a constitutive micropsychist, who defends phenomenal bonding against the standard subject-summing objection, and a priority cosmopsychist, who maintains that decomposition is a strictly easier problem than composition? I would like each to press the other on their weakest point rather than talk past one another, with the cosmopsychist in particular expected to give a principled reason — and not merely an appeal to intuition — for why decomposition should really be less mysterious than combination.
