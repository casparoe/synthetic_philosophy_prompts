I spent my working life as an evolutionary biologist, mostly studying life-history trade-offs in fish populations, and since retiring I have taken up auditing philosophy courses at the university near where I live -- partly to occupy the time productively, and partly out of a nagging feeling that I spent four decades reasoning about fitness and function without ever properly examining the conceptual scaffolding underneath. This term's philosophy of science seminar includes a short unit on the concept of disease, and the instructor, aware that the material sits closer to my own technical background than to most of the class's, asked whether I would help mark a batch of short written responses on the naturalism/normativism debate. I agreed, but I am wary of trusting my own judgment here: I can check the biology in these answers without much effort, but I have far less feel for how carefully a philosophy student needs to handle the surrounding concepts, and I would rather not let either my disciplinary loyalties or my inexperience with this kind of grading distort the result.

The question set to the students reads: "The naturalist about health and disease holds that whether an organism is diseased is a matter of biological fact, to be settled without appeal to values; the normativist denies this. (a) Explain how Christopher Boorse's Biostatistical Theory cashes out 'health' in purely naturalistic terms, making sure to explain the role of reference classes and the notion of a statistically typical contribution to survival and reproduction. (b) Present one normativist account of disease and explain the main respect in which it denies naturalism. (c) Some have argued that Jerome Wakefield's 'harmful dysfunction' analysis offers a way of splitting the difference between naturalism and normativism. Explain whether you agree, and why."

Below is one student's response in full. I would like your independent, rigorous assessment of it before I settle on my own comments. Student's response:

Naturalists like Boorse hold that whether something counts as a disease is a matter of biological fact that can in principle be settled without appeal to anyone's values, while normativists hold that disease-attributions are inescapably evaluative.

(a) Boorse's Biostatistical Theory (BST) holds that a part or process is functioning normally when it makes its statistically typical contribution to an organism's survival and reproduction, where "statistically typical" is fixed relative to a reference class -- standardly, an age group of a given gender within a species. Disease is then a reduction of one or more functional abilities below this species-typical level for the relevant class. On Boorse's view, both the choice of reference class and the notion of a typical contribution to survival and reproduction are settled by ordinary biological science rather than by anyone's values, so BST implies that calling something a disease is exactly as objective as saying that an engine or a thermostat is malfunctioning relative to its design.

(b) A clear normativist alternative is Lennart Nordenfelt's welfare theory, on which a person counts as healthy to the extent that they have the second-order ability, in standard circumstances, to realize their vital goals -- goals that are individually necessary and jointly sufficient for their minimal happiness. Because which goals count as "vital" and which circumstances count as "standard" are themselves evaluative questions about human flourishing rather than statistical facts, Nordenfelt denies that health can be reduced to statistically typical functioning: two people with identical biostatistical profiles could differ in health simply because they have different goals. This is exactly the respect in which the theory is normativist -- it builds an irreducibly evaluative notion, welfare, into the very definition of health, rather than treating health as a purely descriptive precondition for whatever a person happens to value.

(c) Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis holds that a condition is a disorder just in case it is a failure of some internal mechanism to perform a function that mechanism was naturally selected to perform. This tightens Boorse's own picture by cashing out "function" specifically in terms of natural selection history rather than mere statistical contribution to survival and reproduction, which lets Wakefield rule out cases -- like Boorse's own worry about a population in which a harmless trait happens to be statistically atypical -- that BST would otherwise misclassify as disease. Because the whole analysis still bottoms out in an evolutionary, mechanism-level story about what a trait is "for," I do not think Wakefield's account is really a hybrid of naturalism and normativism at all; it looks to me like a more careful naturalism that simply does a better job than Boorse's of pinning down what a proper function is.
