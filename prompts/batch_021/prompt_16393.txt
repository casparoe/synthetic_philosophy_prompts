I'm reporting a long-form piece on why cooperation survives in strategic settings where the tightest possible logic says it shouldn't, and the case study I keep returning to is the finite centipede game: the two-player game in which the pot on the table grows with every round, yet backward induction insists that the first player should take the smaller amount on offer immediately, since the same reasoning that makes the last player defect is supposed to propagate all the way back up the game tree to the very first move. A game-theorist friend I described the piece to waved this off as ancient history, closed by Robert Aumann's 1995 proof that common knowledge of rationality among the players of any finite game of perfect information is, by itself, enough to force the backward-induction outcome. But digging into the epistemic game theory literature convinced me the matter is not nearly so closed: a number of people who accept Aumann's proof completely as mathematics -- among them the philosopher Robert Stalnaker, the economist Elchanan Ben-Porath, and the philosopher Cristina Bicchieri, coming at it from different directions -- have each pushed back on the idea that it settles anything, because the proof has to say something about what a player believes at a node that, according to the very premise of common knowledge of rationality, she should never have reached. Aumann's construction handles this by treating rationality as a property of a complete strategy assessed at every node, including the counterfactual ones, so that the question of genuine surprise never really arises; his critics think that is exactly where a substantive, and rejectable, assumption about how knowledge behaves gets smuggled in.

Could you write me a dialogue that actually earns its keep on this point, between an epistemic game theorist who defends Aumann's result on its own terms and a philosopher trained in belief revision and conditional logic who denies that common knowledge of rationality forces backward induction once the off-path question is taken seriously? I'd like them to work through a concrete run of the centipede game together rather than trading slogans: have the game theorist make the strongest case for treating a strategy's rationality as all-or-nothing across every node, not just the ones actually reached, and have the philosopher press on whether "common knowledge," formalized the standard way, can coherently describe what either player knows once one of them does something that common knowledge of rationality said was impossible -- and on what, if anything, should replace it. Neither side should be a pushover, and each should respond to the other's actual last point rather than restate an opening position; I would rather the exchange end without an artificially tidy resolution than have one side simply win for the reader's convenience, as long as it's clear by the end exactly where the disagreement bites.
