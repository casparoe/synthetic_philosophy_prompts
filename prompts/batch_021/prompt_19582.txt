I spent most of my career doing research in evolutionary biology, mostly population genetics, and only got round to reading philosophy properly after I retired a couple of years back. Lately I've been working through some of Popper's writings on the philosophy of science, and I ran into this whole episode where he apparently said Darwinian natural selection was next door to a tautology, and downgraded the whole theory to a "metaphysical research programme" rather then a proper falsifiable scientific theory. This annoyed me a bit, honestly, since fitness was never just a circular label in any of the models I actually worked with -- it was something you measured and used to predict allele frequencies going forward. Then I read that he later did some kind of "recantation" on this, apparently in a lecture from around 1977, though the couple of summaries I've come across don't even agree on how much he actually walked back.

Could you help me untangle this a bit? First I'd like the precise logical shape of the tautology charge itself -- what exactly is supposed to make "survival of the fittest" collapse into an empty truism, and why did that seem so compelling to Popper even though, as far as I can tell, working biologists define fitness independently of who actually happens to survive in any given case. Second, I'd like to know what the "recantation" really amounted to -- did he genuinely reverse himself on the testability of the general theory, or was he mostly conceding some narrower, specific point while still keeping the "metaphysical research programme" label for Darwinism overall? A friend from my old department reckons this whole business is a big nothing-burger mostly kept alive by creationists quote-mining an old man's lecture, and I'd like to know whether thats fair, or whether Popper was onto something real that just gets lost in the potted summaries.
