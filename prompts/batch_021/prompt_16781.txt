Hey, so I'm taking a seminar this semester on classical Islamic philosophy and this week we're on Al-Ghazali's Tahafut al-Falasifa (the "Incoherence of the Philosophers"), specifically the seventeenth discussion where he goes after the philosophers' account of causation — the fire-and-cotton stuff. I already knew the headline version everyone picks up somewhere, "fire doesn't really burn the cotton, God does," but when I actually sat down with the primary text for our reading response I found the argument way more slippery than the one-liner suggests, and I can't tell if I'm just missing something or if there's an actual gap in the reasoning. Here's the bit I keep re-reading (roughly how it goes in the translation we were assigned):

"We do not deny that the burning occurs when fire and cotton touch, but we deny that this occuring is of necessity, in the sense that the fire has no power to abstain from burning so long as the cotton is fit to receive the burning, or that the connexion between them is like the connexion between cause and effect in the demonstrative sciences. Rather, this conjunction of fire and burning is of the sort that comes about through God's habitual practice, He creating the one alongside the other, without the fire's being productive of it or having any activity of its own; for fire is a dead thing, and burning is an act, and no act is intelligible except from one possessed of will. And that this connexion is not necessary is shown by this, that we are able to suppose the one without the other without thereby falling into any contradiction: thus it is related of Abraham that he was cast into the fire and yet was not burned, God having in that instance withheld the accident of burning, or changed some quality of Abraham's body, or by some other means — it is not incumbent on us to specify which, only that it is not impossible."

My problem is with the move from "we can suppose fire without burning, and burning without fire, without contradiction" to the actual positive claim that God is directly and separately creating both events every single time. Like, even granting that the connection isn't demonstratively necessary, why does that get Ghazali all the way to full occasionalism, rather than just to the weaker claim that fire has some real but non-necessary causal power — a disposition that could in principle fail? It feels like he's sliding from an epistemic point (we can't prove the necessity) to a much stronger metaphysical one (there is no causing going on in the fire at all, full stop), and I don't think the Abraham example actually bridges that gap, since it's one alleged counterinstance rather than a reason to think fire in general lacks causal power. Also, a friend of mine doing a physics degree insists this whole discussion is basically "Hume before Hume" and there's nothing more to it, but that seems off to me too, since Hume never concludes that something else (God) is doing the causing instead — he's way more agnostic about that. Could you help me pull apart what's actually doing the logical work here: is there a real gap between the "no necessary connection" premise and the "God is the sole agent" conclusion, and if so, how (if at all) does Ghazali try to close it?
