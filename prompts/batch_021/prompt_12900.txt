After the final meeting of a reading group I run on Hilary Putnam's *Jewish Philosophy as a Guide to Life* and his other late writings on religion, two of my graduate students, Elena and Marcus, kept arguing about it afterward. Putnam, following Maimonides, denies that God is an entity among entities, and holds that the question "does God exist?" already smuggles in a picture he wants to reject. (1) Elena said this just amounts to atheism with better branding: if "God" doesn't pick out something that either does or doesn't exist, calling yourself religious on this basis is secular ethics wearing religious clothes. (2) Marcus answered that this conflates "not an entity among entities" with "not real" -- exactly Maimonides's point about negative theology -- and that demanding God's reality meet the criteria appropriate to chairs and electrons is a category mistake, not a refutation. (3) Elena granted the point but pressed further: even so, if nothing about the world differs depending on whether God, in this thinned-out sense, is real, the term is doing no work -- "that's a category mistake" just relabels Flew's old falsification challenge rather than answering it. (4) Marcus replied that demanding a predictive difference already assumes the very picture -- meaning tied to third-person verification -- that Putnam spent his career attacking; on a broadly Wittgensteinian view, a concept's meaning shows up in how it functions within a practice and a form of life, not in what it predicts.

Neither budged, and I'm honestly not sure there's a clean winner. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move -- who has the better of it at each of the four numbered points, not just overall -- and then say what you think both of them are missing? In particular, I'd like to know whether Marcus's last move can be generalized to shield any claim whatsoever from refutation, and if not, what would count, on a Putnam-inspired view, as getting the concept of God wrong.
