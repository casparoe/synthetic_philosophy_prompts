I've spent a fair amount of time on my own thinking about Avicenna's "flying man" thought experiment. He imagines a person created whole in an instant, suspended in empty space with his limbs held apart so that they never touch each other or anything else, and unable to see or hear anything at all. Despite having no sensation whatsoever of his own body or of the outside world, Avicenna insists this person would still be immediately certain that he himself exists. He takes this as showing that our awareness of our own self doesn't depend on the body, and that the self and the body must be genuinely distinct things.

What strikes me is that modern sensory-deprivation research, using isolation tanks and similar setups designed to cut off sight, sound, and touch as completely as possible, comes about as close as anything could to actually staging this scenario. Yet from what I've read, people who spend extended time in this kind of near-total sensory deprivation often report hallucinations, disorientation, or a self that feels unstable or dissolving, rather than the calm, immediate self-certainty the thought experiment predicts.

Suppose Avicenna were somehow confronted with this research. Would he have to treat it as a genuine problem for his argument, or are there principled grounds on which he could say it doesn't really test what his thought experiment describes? Please reconstruct his strongest likely reply, then say plainly whether you think that reply succeeds.
