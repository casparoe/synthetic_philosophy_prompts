I'm a novelist working on a subplot built directly on Kavka's toxin puzzle. In my version, a dying matriarch's will leaves her fortune to her estranged grandson on one condition: by the reading of the will, he must have genuinely resolved to spend the following year living anonymously among the tenant families whose wages he once cut. No one will ever check whether he goes, and once the money clears he has no remaining reason to follow through -- yet the family solicitor, renowned for detecting insincerity, must be convinced beforehand that his resolve is real. Before I draft the scene, I want its psychology to withstand scrutiny from readers who know the philosophical literature. What are the most important implications of the puzzle for whether the grandson can sincerely and rationally form that intention at all, given that he can already foresee he won't want to honor his resolve once the money is his? Please work through how Kavka's own diagnosis -- that the case pulls the rationality of forming an intention apart from the rationality of the act intended -- compares with "resolute choice" views, which treat rationality as a property of whole plans rather than isolated decisions, and with planning-theory accounts in the spirit of Bratman's work on the stability of intentions. Then tell me whether any of them can explain what the solicitor could actually be detecting, as opposed to mere good acting.
