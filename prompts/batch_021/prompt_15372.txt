The question was: "Explain what it would mean for two agents to engage in 'acausal trade' despite having no causal contact whatsoever, and what work the notion of superrationality (or something like it) has to do to make this coherent. Then give one serious objection to the idea that such trade could ever be rational to engage in."

Here's my partner's answer, word for word:

"Acausal trade refers to a situation where two agents who can never causally interact with each other — say, because they exist in regions of the universe (or the multiverse) so distant, or so structured, that no signal could ever pass between them — nevertheless coordinate their behavior for mutual benefit. The trick is that each agent reasons about what the other agent is like, and each realizes the other is doing the same kind of reasoning about them. If I know that a distant civilization values, say, preserving certain kinds of art, and I know they're modeling my civilization's values in a similar way, I can act now, at some cost to myself, to help preserve what I believe they value, expecting that they are simultaneously reasoning their way into helping preserve what I value. Neither side ever learns how things went for the other, and neither causally affects the other at all, but the two acts of reasoning are linked because both parties are running structurally similar decision procedures on structurally similar problems.

This is where superrationality does the heavy lifting. Ordinary rational choice theory treats what other agents will do as just a fixed fact about the world I can't influence, so I should pick whatever maximizes my own expected payoff given my best guess about their behavior. Superrationality, a term coined by Douglas Hofstadter specifically to describe this kind of acausal trade between agents with no causal contact, says that if I know another agent is relevantly similar to me and facing a structurally identical decision problem, I should treat their choice as if it depends on mine, because in a sense it does: we're both instances of 'the kind of agent who reasons this way,' so my choosing to cooperate is good evidence — not that it causes, but that it correlates with — them also cooperating. For that correlation to be doing any real work rather than just being a coincidence, the two agents essentially need to be running the exact same decision algorithm on the exact same problem; anything short of that and there's no genuine link between the two choices. That's the same basic move that supposedly licenses trade between agents with no causal contact at all, just scaled up from a single prisoner's dilemma to a whole civilization's worth of decisions.

The strongest objection is that this treats a merely evidential connection as if it were a practical lever, which is a mistake. Standard causal decision theory says I should pick the act that causally brings about the best outcome, not the act that merely correlates with good outcomes elsewhere, so paying a cost now to benefit a distant agent I can never affect and can never verify complied with 'their end of the deal' looks like paying for nothing. A causal decision theorist would say: whatever the other agent is going to do is already fixed, independent of what I do right now, since there's no causal channel between us, so the only thing rational for me to do is maximize my own expected utility taking their behavior as a given fact I have no power over. On this objection, acausal trade confuses being good news for something with actually bringing it about, and it only looks appealing because we're bad at intuitively separating correlation from causation once the correlation gets this exotic."
