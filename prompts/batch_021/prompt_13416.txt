The question, worth 20 points, is: "In 'Freedom and Resentment' (1962), P. F. Strawson argues that the standing dispute between what he calls 'optimists' and 'pessimists' about the compatibility of moral responsibility with determinism rests on a mistake. (a) [8 points] Explain the distinction between the participant reactive attitudes and the objective attitude, and the role this distinction plays in Strawson's argument. (b) [6 points] Explain why, on Strawson's view, the truth or falsity of determinism is largely irrelevant to whether our practices of holding one another responsible are justified. (c) [6 points] State and briefly explain one influential criticism of Strawson's strategy from the later secondary literature."

Here is the student's answer: "Strawson's aim in 'Freedom and Resentment' is to change the subject rather than answer the traditional question. Instead of asking whether the truth of determinism is compatible with moral responsibility, he asks us to look at the actual structure of our interpersonal life and the attitudes that sustain it. (a) The central distinction is between the participant reactive attitudes and the objective attitude. The reactive attitudes — resentment, gratitude, love, hurt feelings, forgiveness — are responses to the quality of will, the goodwill, ill will, or disregard, that other people display toward us through what they do. Resentment is Strawson's paradigm case: it is the attitude we take up not only toward those who wrong us personally but toward any wrongdoing we witness, so that reading about a stranger's cruelty to some third party is, for Strawson, simply resentment felt at one remove. Standing opposed to this participant stance is the objective attitude, in which we see a person as an object of social policy, someone to be managed, handled, cured, or trained, rather than reasoned with or held to account as a fellow member of the moral community. We move into the objective attitude in two kinds of cases: when we adopt it strategically to protect our own composure in a difficult relationship, and when we judge that the other party is simply not an appropriate target for reactive attitudes at all, as with young children, the severely deranged, or people acting under compulsion. (b) On Strawson's view, our commitment to the participant attitudes is not a policy we have adopted for reasons, and so it is not something we could simply abandon for better reasons; it is, he suggests, too deeply woven into ordinary human life to be dislodged by a piece of metaphysics like the thesis of determinism. So even if determinism turned out to be true, this would not by itself give us reason to withdraw into the objective attitude across the board, because the pessimist's fear that determinism would rob responsibility of its point rests on a picture of what grounds responsibility — some further metaphysical fact about the agent — that was never really what sustained the practice in the first place. This is also part of why Strawson wrote the essay as a rejoinder to Frankfurt's counterexamples to the Principle of Alternative Possibilities: both were trying to show that an agent's responsibility does not depend on facts about what else the agent could have done. (c) One influential criticism, pressed by Gary Watson and R. Jay Wallace, is that Strawson runs together two questions that need to be kept apart: whether the practice of holding people responsible is justified as a whole, and whether it is fair to hold this particular person responsible on this particular occasion. Granting that the reactive attitudes as a system cannot be rationally abandoned does not yet tell us what distinguishes fair from unfair applications of them, and Wallace's own book can be read as an attempt to supply the missing criterion in more traditional terms."
