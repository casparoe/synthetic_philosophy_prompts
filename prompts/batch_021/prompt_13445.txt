A close friend from graduate school is getting married in a few weeks, and she has asked me to write and deliver the ceremony address. This is partly my own doing: months ago, at a dinner, I made an offhand remark about there being no possible world in which the two of them did not end up together, and her fiancé liked the line enough to fold a version of it into his proposal. The couple has since built the ceremony around that phrase; it is printed on the programs, and they want it returned to in the address itself. Roughly a third of the guests are philosophers from our cohort who will recognize exactly what is being invoked and will notice if I get it wrong; the rest are family who have never encountered the phrase "possible world" outside its ordinary, loose sense, and should not have to sit through a seminar to enjoy a wedding.

My difficulty is that I do not think the line survives contact with either of the two standard ways of making it precise, and I would like the address to be honest about that rather than quietly hoping nobody in the first third of the room does the math. On David Lewis's counterpart theory, what exists in each of those other worlds is not strictly her but a counterpart selected by resemblance, which invites Kripke's Humphrey objection: why should anyone be comforted by the devotion of a person who merely resembles them, especially once you factor in that the counterpart relation need not even be one-to-one, so that a person can have several equally good counterparts in a single world, or none at all? Read instead as a claim about genuine transworld identity, the line regains its literal force, but only by leaning on some primitive fact about sameness of individual across worlds that starts to look strained once you run something like Chisholm's paradox on it: a long chain of worlds, each only slightly different from its neighbor, that threatens to make the identity judgment arbitrary at both ends. I do not need this resolved; what I need is the address itself - about four minutes of spoken material that uses the tension rather than hiding it, gives the philosophers in the room a moment of genuine recognition rather than the joke each of them has probably already made in some seminar, and still leaves everyone else with something true and warm to hold on to about why these two people are marrying each other.
