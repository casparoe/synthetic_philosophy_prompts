For our seminar on formal models of rational cooperation, compare two constructions of program equilibrium. Tennenholtz's original proposal shows that mutual cooperation in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma can be sustained as a Nash equilibrium once players submit programs, rather than actions, each granted read access to its opponent's source code: a program that cooperates just when the opponent's code is syntactically identical to its own, and defects otherwise, sustains mutual cooperation as an equilibrium when matched against a copy of itself. A later construction, due to researchers associated with the Machine Intelligence Research Institute, replaces this bare equality test with a proof-theoretic one: an agent such as "FairBot" cooperates with an opponent just in case it can produce a formal proof, in a system such as Peano Arithmetic, that the opponent cooperates with it, with mutual cooperation between two such agents secured not by simulation but by Löb's theorem.

Compare the two constructions. First, explain why a program that cooperates whenever it predicts, by simulating its opponent, that the opponent will cooperate threatens to be ill-founded, and assess whether the Löbian construction actually escapes this threat or merely relocates the self-reference into the metatheory in which Löb's theorem is proved. Second, assess whether trading syntactic equality for provable cooperation is a genuine gain in generality, since it promises cooperation among agents that are behaviorally but not textually identical, or whether it just substitutes one narrow sufficient condition for another. Defend a verdict on which construction gives the more satisfying account of how unenforced commitment among rational agents is possible.
