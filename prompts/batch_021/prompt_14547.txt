I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for long enough that Hofstadter's old columns on superrationality were actually among the things that got me into decision theory in the first place, well before I found my way to the Less Wrong threads on timeless and functional decision theory, and eventually to the "multiverse-wide" extension of the idea associated with Caspar Oesterheld, according to which suitably similar rational agents should cooperate acausally even across regions of a very large or infinite universe, or multiverse, that will never come into causal contact with one another. I co-host a small decision-theory podcast on the side, and next month's guest is a philosopher who has built a good part of her reputation defending superrationality as the correct resolution of the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, and who has since defended the multiverse-wide extension in print as well.

I don't want this to become an episode where she simply re-explains the twin-Prisoner's-Dilemma argument that most of my listeners could already recite from memory. Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that press on the places I suspect the view is weakest, each paired with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give? I want at least one question on how much genuine similarity between two agents the argument actually needs, and on whether stretching it from literal duplicates to merely "sufficiently rational" strangers quietly assumes the very correlation it's supposed to establish -- she has a stock reply here, something like "two sufficiently rational agents facing the same problem are, for decision-theoretic purposes, the same agent," and I'd like a follow-up that doesn't just let her repeat that line. I also want at least one question on whether superrationality names a genuinely distinct decision rule or is really just ordinary expected-utility reasoning combined with an unusual empirical belief about correlated dispositions; at least one on the frequently drawn parallel with Kant's Categorical Imperative, since "do only what you would want every rational agent in your position to do" sounds a great deal like the Categorical Imperative in game-theoretic dress; and at least one that goes after the multiverse-wide extension head-on -- what actually licenses cooperating with agents one can never observe, communicate with, or gather evidence about, and why the same reasoning doesn't also commit her to cooperating with literally anything whose dispositions might, for all she knows, happen to be correlated with her own.
