I've gone down a rabbit hole this week for a piece on odd corners of AI-safety thinking, and I keep circling back to something called the "commitment races problem" that I think was first written up by Wei Dai on LessWrong a few years back -- I found it via a footnote in a Center on Long-Term Risk report and then lost an afternoon to it. Nobody in my actual newsroom has heard of it so I dont have anyone to sanity-check this with except you.

Here's the explainer paragraph I've drafted so far, before it goes anywhere near my editor: "Thomas Schelling pointed out, in The Strategy of Conflict, that in a game of chicken the driver who visibly and irreversibly throws his steering wheel out the window wins, because the only rational move left for the other driver is to swerve. Being able to commit first, and prove it, is a source of power. The commitment races problem takes that logic and asks what happens once the 'drivers' are AI systems, or the labs and states behind them, that can show each other their source code or otherwise make commitments no human ever could. If committing earlier is always better, the incentive isn't just to commit fast in ordinary clock time -- its to commit as early as possible in what Dai calls 'logical time,' before you've finished reasoning, before you've noticed your model of the other side might be wrong, maybe before anyone involved is wise enough to know if the commitment is any good at all. So you get a race to lock in strategies, including threats, under exactly the conditions where everyone is least equipped to choose a good one -- and if both sides do it, you basically get two Dr. Strangelove doomsday machines going off because neither side built in a way to back down."

Could you go over that for me? Specifically: does the mechanism actually hold up, especially the "logical time" part -- I want to know if that's doing real work or if its just a fancier redescription of ordinary brinkmanship. Is the Strangelove comparison earning its keep or hiding some way an AI commitment race would actually diverge from a nuclear one? And is there a serious proposed fix in the decision-theory/alignment literature that I'm leaving out entirely -- and if so, does it dissolve the problem or just push it back a level? Would also take a blunt "this is too inside-baseball" if that's your honest read.
