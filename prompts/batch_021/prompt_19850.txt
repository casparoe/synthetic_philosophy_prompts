In the ethics elective at my school, we spent a unit on just war theory that included excerpts from the philosopher G.E.M. Anscombe's 1956 pamphlet "Mr. Truman's Degree," written to protest Oxford's decision to award Truman an honorary degree. Her central claim is that ordering the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki made Truman guilty of murder, because on her view deliberately killing the innocent as a means to an end can't be redeemed by how good the resulting consequences are. Two of my classmates, Owen and Nadia, ended up arguing about the essay on the walk out of class, and I've been turning their exchange over ever since, because both seemed to me to be making real points, and I honestly can't tell who was getting the better of it.

Owen opened by saying the bombings were justified on the numbers: every serious estimate of what a land invasion of Japan would have cost, in Japanese and American lives combined, was far higher than the roughly two hundred thousand people killed by the two bombs, so refusing to drop them would have been the worse decision by any reasonable accounting of consequences. Nadia answered that this misses Anscombe's point entirely, since her argument was never about which choice minimized total deaths; the bombs worked by deliberately destroying civilian populations to break Japan's will to keep fighting, which on her view makes the civilians' deaths the intended means rather than a merely foreseen side effect, and that distinction is exactly what the doctrine of double effect is meant to track, so no death toll on the other side of the ledger can turn an intentional killing of the innocent into something other than murder. Owen pushed back that Hiroshima had a military garrison and Nagasaki had war industry, so perhaps the cities counted as legitimate targets and the civilian deaths were foreseen but unintended, the same way Anscombe would presumably allow bombing a factory while knowing nearby residents would die. Nadia replied that this gets the strategy backward: the point of the bombing, like the firebombing raids before it, was to demonstrate that Japan's cities and populations could be annihilated until the government gave in, so destroying the population wasn't incidental to achieving surrender but was the mechanism of achieving it, which is why, on her reading, Anscombe treats the campaign as the deliberate destruction of a city rather than an attack on a discrete target with regrettable side effects. Owen's last move was to ask whether Nadia could really hold that intention settles everything regardless of scale: if the bombings' toll of roughly two hundred thousand prevented several times that many deaths in an invasion, isn't there something to answer for in insisting on a distinction that costs so many extra lives? Nadia said that's the same mistake restated, since Anscombe's whole point is that once intentionally killing the innocent is on the table, no larger number on the other side of the scale is supposed to settle the question, and refusing to let arithmetic decide it is the substance of her argument, not an embarrassing cost of holding it.

Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who had the better argument at each stage, rather than just handing down an overall winner? I'm especially curious whether Owen's point about the military garrison and war industry actually creates a problem for Nadia's double-effect reasoning, or whether she has the resources to deflect it, and whether there's a good response available to Anscombe's position on Owen's final question about scale that neither of them thought of.
