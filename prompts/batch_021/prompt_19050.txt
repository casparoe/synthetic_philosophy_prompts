A colleague and I came up with a small savings challenge to fund a joint vacation two months from now: each week, whoever has the turn can either cash out the current pot for themselves (taking the larger share, leaving the smaller share to the other person), or add a bit of their own money to double the pot and pass the decision along. We have agreed there are at most eight turns left, since the money must be spent before we leave.

My colleague, who likes reasoning things out from first principles, points out that whoever holds the eighth turn will obviously cash out, since there is no future turn left to protect; knowing this, whoever holds the seventh turn should cash out as well, rather than pass the pot into a turn where it will simply be taken; and so on, back to this week — meaning the "rational" move is to cash out immediately rather than ever let the pot grow. I cannot spot a flaw in any individual step, yet the conclusion still seems wrong: if we simply kept passing, we would both end up far richer than either of us could get by cashing out now. Is he right that continuing to pass is naive, or is the argument flawed somewhere?
