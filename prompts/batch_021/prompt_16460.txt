I am several drafts into a novel narrated throughout by a woman who systematically underreports her own part in the story's central catastrophe while overreporting everyone else's — the classic architecture of the unreliable narrator, in the lineage of Stevens in Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day or Dowell in Ford's The Good Soldier. My reading group and I got into a disagreement about this last week that I have not been able to resolve on my own, and I would like your help settling it properly rather than impressionistically. One member argued that everything an attentive reader infers about my narrator beyond her literal sentences is still, at bottom, ordinary Gricean conversational implicature: the narrator flouts a maxim — Quantity, by conspicuously omitting material any cooperative narrator would supply, or Quality, by asserting things she plainly has no adequate warrant for — the reader notices the flout, and calculates the implicature exactly as Grice's machinery in "Logic and Conversation" describes for ordinary face-to-face talk-exchange. I do not think that can be the whole story, and I would like you to make the case for my side as rigorously as you can.

Specifically, please argue for the following thesis: an unreliable first-person narrator cannot, at the level of narrator addressing narratee, be the source of genuine Gricean speaker meaning for any of the implicatures a careful reader draws from her unreliability. The reason is that she does not intend her particular "tells" to be recognized as tells, and recognition of intention is exactly what Grice's 1957 account of speaker meaning requires for non-natural meaning to occur, as opposed to the merely natural meaning of symptoms and evidence — the sense in which, on Grice's own example, spots mean measles. What a careful reader picks up on, at the narratorial level, is therefore closer to natural than to non-natural meaning. On this view, the implicatures we intuitively — and, I think, correctly — attribute to the text become genuinely Gricean, properly calculable and cancellable and tied to an intending, cooperative speaker, only once we relocate "the speaker" one level up: from the narrator addressing the narratee to the author addressing the reader through and past her. Please engage directly with Grice's actual apparatus — the four maxims, the distinction between violating and flouting a maxim, and the calculability and cancellability tests for implicature — rather than resting on unanalyzed appeals to "authorial intention" or "dramatic irony."
