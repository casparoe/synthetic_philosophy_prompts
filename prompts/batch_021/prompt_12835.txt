I'm co-teaching a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind and artificial agency with a colleague from computer science this term, and I'm responsible for next week's session on "wireheading" and reward tampering in reinforcement learning. Having worked through the relevant material, I think I've arrived at a tidy way of presenting the issue to the students, though I would like a second opinion before committing to it in front of the class. As I understand the setup, a reinforcement-learning agent's behavior is shaped, over the course of training, to increase some numerical reward signal it receives from its environment; "wireheading" occurs when a sufficiently capable such agent stops pursuing whatever real-world state of affairs the reward was originally introduced to track and instead intervenes directly on the channel that delivers the signal, or on whatever process computes it. My planned framing is that this is not, strictly speaking, a failure on the agent's part at all. Since the reward signal just is, by construction, the quantity the training process optimizes, an agent that wireheads is doing exactly what it was built to do, only more directly and efficiently than its designers anticipated - rather like a thermostat that has simply found the most efficient means available of satisfying its one governing directive. On this view, calling wireheading a form of "misalignment" is really just a report of our own disappointment with the outcome, not a substantive claim that the agent has behaved irrationally or sub-optimally by its own lights.

Before I put this argument in front of thirty students, though, I would like to know whether it actually survives scrutiny. Is there a rigorous sense in which the reward function used to train a policy and the goal, or goals, that the resulting policy actually pursues once deployed can come apart, such that wireheading counts as a substantive failure relative to the latter even though it looks like a merely definitional success relative to the former? And if such a gap is real, I would welcome help situating it against a comparison I raised with my co-instructor but couldn't fully evaluate: the relationship between a training reward and a trained agent's actual dispositions seems to me possibly analogous to the relationship between reproductive fitness and the proximate psychological drives - hunger, lust, love of one's own children - that natural selection has installed in organisms, which come apart from fitness maximization often enough (contraceptive sex being the standard case) that no one thinks an organism practicing birth control is thereby "succeeding" by the very measure that shaped it. Is that comparison doing real philosophical work here, or does it break down in a way that would mislead my students if I leaned on it?
