So my brother has bipolar I, and after his last manic episode landed him in the ER (maxed out two credit cards and almost drove to Vegas at 3am) his psychiatrist brought up something called a "Ulysses contract" — basically a document he'd sign now, while he's stable and thinking clearly, saying that if he's ever manic again and refuses hospitalization, the treatment team and our family get to override him and commit him anyway, based on what he wants right now, not what he'll want in the moment. She said the name comes from Ulysses ordering his crew to tie him to the mast and ignore whatever he begs them to do once he hears the sirens. I went down a total rabbit hole reading about this over the weekend and found out political theorists use the exact same image to talk about constitutions — like how you need a supermajority to touch the Bill of Rights, so a future Congress cant just get rid of free speech on a whim because its popular that week.

Now I can't stop turning over how similar these two things actually are, vs how different. In both cases someone (a person, a founding generation) is binding a future version of themselves (a manic self, a future legislature) because they don't trust that future version to make the call, and locking the earlier decision in on purpose so it can't get relitigated in the heat of the moment. But it also feels off in places — like, is my brother mid-mania even the "same person" making a competing claim, in the way a future Congress is still recognizably "the government"? And a constitution can eventually get amended if enough people change their minds over time, but the whole point of his contract is that HE specifically doesn't get a vote on overturning it while he's symptomatic.

Could you actually dig into this comparison for me — psychiatric Ulysses contracts vs constitutional precommitment? I'm especially interested in where the analogy holds up and where it quietly breaks down, e.g. on revocability, on who counts as the legitimate "author" of the binding decision, and on what (if anything) justifies overriding someone's stated present-tense wishes on the authority of an earlier self. I keep seeing Jon Elster's name come up in stuff about precommitment and don't totally get what his angle adds beyond the basic sirens metaphor, so feel free to get into that too if it's useful.
