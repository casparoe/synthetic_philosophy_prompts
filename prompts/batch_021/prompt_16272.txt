This term I am teaching an upper-level seminar on just war theory, and next week's session is built around a case that tends to unsettle students far more than the usual trolley-style examples: Elizabeth Anscombe's 1956 pamphlet "Mr. Truman's Degree," written to oppose Oxford's decision to award Harry Truman an honorary doctorate. Anscombe's charge was blunt: in ordering the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, Truman chose the deliberate killing of enormous numbers of innocent civilians as the very means of ending the war, and on her view this is simply murder — an act that cannot be redeemed by whatever good consequences (a shortened war, an avoided invasion of the Japanese mainland) might be claimed on its behalf. The argument leans on a distinction that becomes central to her later work on intention and to her revival of the doctrine of double effect: the distinction between what an agent intends, as end or means, and what she merely foresees as a side effect of an otherwise permissible act. On Anscombe's reading, the bombings fail this test, since the killing of civilians was not an unfortunate side effect of some independently targeted military objective but was itself the mechanism by which Japan's surrender was to be extracted.

Before I set my students loose on this material, I would like a model of the kind of essay I want from them, so I am putting the question to you directly. Please write an essay that puts real pressure on Anscombe's argument rather than simply summarizing it. Consider the strongest objections available: that the intention/foresight distinction is suspiciously easy to manipulate by redescription (one can always characterize the intended target as "the enemy's will to resist" rather than "the civilian population"); that absolute prohibitions of the sort Anscombe favors are hard to sustain once the stakes are allowed to climb arbitrarily high; and that even non-absolutist just war theorists sympathetic to something like a "supreme emergency" exception — Michael Walzer's term — still end up judging the Hiroshima and Nagasaki bombings harshly, on the grounds that Japan's defeat was scarcely in doubt by August 1945. Weigh these considerations against Anscombe's own reasons for refusing any consequentialist calculus that could license the intentional killing of the innocent, and close with a definite verdict on whether her charge of murder against Truman is sustainable. I am not interested in an essay that lays out both sides and declines to choose.
