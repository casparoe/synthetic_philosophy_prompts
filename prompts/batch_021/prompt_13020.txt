So over the holiday break I picked up Fortune's Formula, Poundstone's book about Claude Shannon, Ed Thorp, and the Kelly criterion, mostly for fun, and now I can't stop bringing it up in my rational choice seminar, which I think is starting to annoy my students a little. The bit that's really gotten under my skin is the theorem that a Kelly bettor -- someone who always bets the fraction of their bankroll that maximizes expected log wealth -- will, with probability 1, end up richer than someone following any other fixed betting strategy, given enough repeated bets. One of my students, a math major who moonlights on rationalist blogs, has more or less convinced half the seminar that this amounts to a quiet refutation of expected utility theory: if maximizing expected log wealth is guaranteed to beat every other strategy eventually, she argues, then "maximize expected utility" was never the right slogan for rational choice under uncertainty in the first place, and what we actually want is this "geometric rationality" business -- growth-rate maximization -- as the real foundation, with von Neumann-Morgenstern utility demoted to a historical also-ran.

I only teach this material at the level of Allais and Ellsberg, not stochastic processes, so I cant quite tell if she's onto something real or if there's a sleight of hand in getting from "wins almost surely in the limit" to "is what rationality actually requires." Could you help me sort out where the argument holds up and where it doesn't? I'd especially like to understand whether Kelly betting and geometric rationality genuinely compete with expected utility theory as accounts of rational choice, or whether something's being smuggled in along the way.
