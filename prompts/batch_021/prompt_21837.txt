I'm a novelist, a few months out from delivering my second book, and I've been going back and forth on a decision that I suspect has a cleaner answer than my instincts are giving it credit for. My publisher's standing offer is a guaranteed advance of $40,000, paid regardless of how the book does. My agent has also floated a second structure, one a few of her other clients have taken: give up most of that advance in exchange for a much steeper royalty and a cut of subsidiary rights, which she's modeled as having an expected value closer to $68,000. What unsettles me isn't the average, though, it's the shape of the thing. If the book does what most books quietly do and sells modestly, I'd end up with something like $8,000 for two years of work; if it breaks out the way she thinks it plausibly could, I could clear a quarter of a million dollars or more. She keeps telling me that turning this down is irrational, since I'd be walking away from a higher expected payout for no good reason. When I pointed out that I'm broke enough right now that an extra dollar matters more to me than it will once I'm established, she agreed. She reran her numbers using a curve that discounts each additional dollar to reflect exactly that, and the richer option still came out ahead. Even so, some part of me keeps wanting the guaranteed number, and it isn't only about how tight things are for me at the moment.

I brought this up over dinner with a friend who teaches decision theory, half-expecting her to side with my agent. Instead, she got a funny look and told me this was "actually a live dispute" among people who study rational choice, though she refused to just give me the answer, insisting I work it out for myself first. So I'm asking you instead. Is my agent right that, once my finances are properly accounted for, holding out for the guaranteed advance is simply irrational? Or is there a real argument that a preference like mine can survive that adjustment and still be rational — and if so, what would actually distinguish it from plain old cowardice about risk?
