For my AP Macroeconomics class, we each had to pick a historical primary source connected to trade policy and present on it, and instead of just using the short excerpt printed in our textbook, I decided to track down and read the whole of David Hume's 1752 essay "Of the Balance of Trade." I had only ever encountered Hume before in the context of his skepticism about causation and induction, which we touched on briefly during a summer philosophy program, so I was surprised to find out that some economists actually credit this essay as one of the earliest formal economic models in the discipline's history. Most of the piece is written in fairly plain eighteenth-century prose, but there is one paragraph, right where Hume tries to dismantle the mercantilist fear that a country will "lose" its gold and silver through unfavorable trade, that I have reread several times without feeling like I have fully grasped the mechanics of it. Here it is exactly as it appears in the essay:

"Suppose four-fifths of all the money in Great Britain to be annihilated in one night, and the nation reduced to the same condition, with regard to specie, as in the reigns of the Harrys and Edwards, what would be the consequence? Must not the price of all labour and commodities sink in proportion, and every thing be sold as cheap as they were in those ages? What nation could then dispute with us in any foreign market, or pretend to navigate or to sell manufactures at the same price, which to us would afford sufficient profit? In how little time, therefore, must this bring back the money which we had lost, and raise us to the level of all the neighbouring nations? Where, after we have arrived, we immediately lose the advantage of the cheapness of labour and commodities; and the farther flowing in of money is stopped by our fulness and repletion."

Could you help me unpack this passage in some detail? Specifically, I would like a step-by-step reconstruction of the causal chain Hume seems to have in mind after the sudden loss of most of the nation's money, since he appears to be compressing several distinct economic mechanisms into just a few sentences. I am also unsure who exactly "the Harrys and Edwards" are meant to be and why Hume treats that particular historical period as such a natural point of comparison. Finally, I would appreciate help understanding why Hume thinks this single thought experiment is enough to refute the mercantilist anxiety about the "balance of trade" altogether, and whether the argument quietly depends on assumptions about how quickly prices and trade flows adjust that a modern economist might find questionable.
