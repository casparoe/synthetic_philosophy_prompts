I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy, currently drafting a qualifying paper on the metaphysics of laws of nature, and I've run into a specific difficulty that our seminar this term never quite addressed head-on. The trigger was a dinner conversation with a friend doing a cosmology postdoc, who was walking me through fine-tuning arguments: physicists routinely ask what the universe would be like if the fine-structure constant, the cosmological constant, or the electron-to-proton mass ratio had taken a slightly different value, while holding the functional form of the relevant law — Coulomb's law, the Friedmann equations, whatever it may be — fixed. This kind of talk seems to presuppose that a constant's numerical value is not itself part of what makes a law the very law that it is; otherwise the counterfactual wouldn't be about our laws running on different settings, it would just be about some other law altogether, and fine-tuning talk would rest on a category error. That got me wondering how much metaphysical weight this ordinary bit of physicists' practice can actually bear, once you look at what the going accounts of lawhood are prepared to say about it.
