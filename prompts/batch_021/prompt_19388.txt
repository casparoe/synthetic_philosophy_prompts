I'm a software engineer who has been reading about the debate over the "cognitive penetration" of perception — the question of whether beliefs and expectations can directly shape perceptual experience itself, rather than just the judgments we layer on top of it. The part I can't fully resolve is that theorists who defend the impenetrability of "early vision" (Zenon Pylyshyn's version of the thesis is the classic reference point) typically exclude shifts of visual attention from counting as cognitive penetration, treating attention as something that merely selects or gates the input a fixed, encapsulated system processes, rather than as altering how that system processes it. But attention itself seems to be guided by expectations, goals, and learned associations — exactly the kind of cognitive influence the impenetrability thesis is supposed to rule out. Is there a principled reason attention should be treated as categorically different from genuine cognitive penetration, or is this exclusion essentially an ad hoc patch that saves the thesis from its most obvious counterexamples? I'd like a careful reconstruction of the strongest version of the argument on each side, rather than the standard textbook gloss.
