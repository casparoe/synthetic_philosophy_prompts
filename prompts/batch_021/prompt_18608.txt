In a graduate research-methods seminar, students were asked the following exam question:

"A researcher testing a coin for fairness originally planned to flip it 100 times, but stopped early, after 60 flips, because the running two-sided p-value for the null hypothesis that the coin is fair had just dropped below 0.05. (a) Explain why this p-value cannot be taken at face value as a 5%-level test from a frequentist standpoint. (b) Explain why a Bayesian analysis of the same 60 flips is unaffected by the fact that the stopping point was chosen adaptively rather than fixed in advance. (c) Relate this asymmetry between the two frameworks to at least one methodological reform associated with the replication crisis."

One student answered as follows:

"(a) For a frequentist, the meaning of a p-value depends on the set of outcomes that could have arisen under repeated use of the very procedure that generated the data, including whichever rule was used to decide when to stop sampling. Here the researcher used a rule under which sampling continues until the p-value dips below 0.05, rather than one that fixes the sample size at 100 in advance. Because a test statistic monitored this way gets many more chances to cross the threshold than a test computed once at a predetermined n, if the researcher were willing to keep sampling indefinitely and stop the moment the threshold is crossed, a crossing would be guaranteed sooner or later even when the coin is perfectly fair; so the real false-positive rate of this procedure approaches 1, not 0.05. This is why optional stopping of this kind is treated as a questionable research practice.

(b) A Bayesian's posterior over the coin's bias combines the prior with the likelihood of the data actually observed, and the likelihood of 60 flips is proportional to p^k(1-p)^(60-k), where k is the number of heads, regardless of whether 60 was fixed in advance or reached through adaptive stopping. Because the stopping rule contributes only a multiplicative factor that does not involve p, it drops out of the posterior entirely. This is an instance of the likelihood principle, which was proved by Neyman and Pearson to follow from the sufficiency principle and the conditionality principle, and it is why Bayesians such as Savage held that optional stopping poses no special threat to Bayesian inference.

(c) One reform that responds to exactly this asymmetry is pre-registration, whereby researchers commit publicly, before collecting any data, to a sample size and analysis plan, so that the nominal and actual long-run error rates of their significance tests coincide."
