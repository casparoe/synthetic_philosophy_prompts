I am the faculty advisor to my university's student-run effective-giving society and sit ex officio on its five-member steering committee. Three years ago the society amended its charter so that full voting membership requires a public, open-ended pledge to donate at least ten percent of one's future gross income, for life, to a short list of charities the society judges most cost-effective; students who would rather not pledge may still attend as non-voting associates. Voting membership has fallen by nearly half since the amendment passed, and the exit conversations I've had with departing members suggest two quite different complaints are doing the work. One faction thinks the ten-percent, lifelong pledge is not nearly demanding enough: if the argument for effective giving is sound, they say, the right amount to give isn't a fixed percentage at all but whatever it takes until further giving would cost you as much as it benefits the recipient, so treating ten percent as sufficient just lets pledgers stop asking the question that got them into this in the first place. The other faction thinks the pledge is already too demanding to impose as a membership requirement: it asks each pledger to shoulder, unconditionally and for decades, a fixed personal sacrifice that only makes sense on the assumption that everyone who could pledge would, and since most people who could pledge don't, the policy is quietly asking compliant students to cover a collective shortfall that was never theirs alone to fix. The steering committee is now deadlocked between keeping the pledge exactly as it stands, weakening it to a lower or time-limited commitment, and replacing it with a tiered structure -- a small mandatory floor plus a larger aspirational target -- and they have asked me, as the resident philosopher, to put a recommendation in writing before the vote.

Please draft that memo, in a page or two. It should recommend one specific policy -- including what, if anything, to do about members who already hold the current lifelong pledge if the policy changes -- and it should defend the recommendation by engaging directly with the demandingness objection to consequentialism, rather than gesturing vaguely at the need for "balance." In particular, I would like it to work through the tension between arguments in the spirit of Singer's shallow-pond case, which push toward treating any fixed stopping point as morally arbitrary; considerations in the spirit of Susan Wolf's worry that organizing one's whole life around such an open-ended demand may not be something we have independent reason to want, whatever morality turns out to require; and proposals from the "nonideal theory" literature on beneficence -- Liam Murphy's work is the standard reference point -- that try to cap what any one person owes at their fair share of the good that would be achieved under full compliance. I would especially like the memo to say something honest about that last move, since capping individual obligation at the fair-share level looks uncomfortably convenient precisely in the case that actually obtains, where full compliance never arrives and the shortfall is real. The committee includes philosophy graduate students as well as undergraduates from unrelated departments, so the memo should be precise about what is philosophically at stake without presupposing coursework in ethics, and it should close with a clear, defended recommendation rather than a survey of the options.
