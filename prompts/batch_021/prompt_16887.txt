Here's the question we got:

"The two-envelope paradox: you're given two envelopes and told one has twice as much money in it as the other. You pick one at random. Before opening it, you reason: 'Let A be the amount in my envelope. The other envelope has either A/2 or 2A, each with probability 1/2, so the expected value of switching is 0.5(A/2) + 0.5(2A) = 1.25A, which is more than A, so I should switch.' But this reasoning would apply no matter which envelope you're holding, which seems to mean you'd want to switch forever. Explain what's going on: is the expected-value calculation correct, and if not, exactly where does it go wrong? Does your answer mean there's never a good reason to switch envelopes in real life?"

And here's my friend's answer:

"The paradox happens because the argument secretly uses 'A' to mean two different things. If we call the smaller amount X, then my envelope contains either X or 2X, each with probability 1/2, and correspondingly the other envelope contains 2X or X. That part of the setup is fine on its own. The actual mistake is assuming that no matter what specific number A you happen to see, there's still a flat 50/50 chance you're holding the small envelope versus the large one. That could only be true if the amounts were drawn from a probability distribution that doesn't actually exist, like a uniform distribution spread evenly over every possible dollar amount, which can't be normalized into real probabilities. Since no distribution like that actually exists, once you open the envelope and see an actual number, the odds are never really 50/50, so switching is never rational no matter what amount is written inside. This is basically the same fix Daniel Bernoulli used for the St. Petersburg paradox: he showed that money has diminishing marginal utility, which is why people are being perfectly rational when they refuse to pay a huge amount to play a game with infinite expected value, and that's really the same idea driving what's going on here too. So the 1.25A calculation is flawed because it smuggles in an impossible assumption about probability, and in real life the two envelopes are just equivalent, so switching is pointless."
