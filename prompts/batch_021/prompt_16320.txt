This semester I'm taking a seminar on decision theory and rational choice, and a few weeks ago the class spent a session on Hofstadter's notion of superrationality before our professor introduced something she called "acausal trade." The idea, drawn mostly from decision-theory work that has grown out of AI safety research, is that two agents who can never causally influence one another at all — because they are separated by a cosmological horizon that no signal will ever cross, occupy branches of the quantum multiverse that, once split, can never rejoin, or exist only as hypothetical possibilities in each other's reasoning — might still end up cooperating, each acting to benefit the other, purely because each can reason its way to a confident prediction that the other, being similarly rational, will do the same.

I haven't been able to stop turning the idea over since: it seems to demand that we call something "trade" even though neither side can ever verify that the other kept its end of the bargain, or even that the other exists at all. For the literary issue our department publishes at the end of term, I'd like to submit a short story built around a genuine act of acausal trade, written in the spirit of what Greg Egan or Ted Chiang might do with the premise, where the strange science functions as the engine of the plot rather than as decoration, the way it does in something like Permutation City or The Three-Body Problem.

Could you write me such a story, somewhere in the range of 1000 to 1500 words? You're welcome to set it wherever you like — two civilizations that will never share a light cone, minds running in unconnected simulations, an intelligence reasoning about a version of itself that it will never meet, or something else entirely — but I'd like the reasoning that lets each side trust the other, absent any causal contact, to be genuinely load-bearing in the plot rather than asserted and left in the background. Ideally, a reader who has never heard the term "acausal trade" before should come away from the story with a real feel for why it might be rational to honor a bargain that no one can enforce, and why that might be a little unsettling, too.
