I'm a retired physicist (forty-some years in condensed matter) who's been on a philosophy kick since I retired, mostly to keep the brain from turning to mush. Funny story: the first time I heard "Frank Ramsey" mentioned on a philosophy podcast, I assumed they meant Norman Ramsey, the atomic-clock guy from Harvard who I actually met once at a conference decades ago — turns out its a totally different, much younger Ramsey, a Cambridge prodigy who did serious original work in math, economics AND philosophy before dying at 26 in 1930. That sent me down a rabbit hole to his essay "Epilogue," apparently first read aloud (under the title "On There Being No Discussable Subject") to his old university discussion club, the Apostles, after dinner in 1925, then published posthumously as the closing chapter of The Foundations of Mathematics in 1931. Everyone who quotes this essay quotes the very end, where Ramsey says the stars look "small as threepenny bits" from where he sits and that he refuses to feel humbled by the universe's size, since stars can't think or love. Fine — lovely bit of writing, and I get why people love it.

What's actually got me stuck is a passage from earlier in the essay. Ramsey has just walked through Russell's reduction of ethics to psychology — the idea that questions of value collapse into questions about what people want and how to satisfy it — and then adds something I honestly can't tell is still reporting Russell's view or has quietly become Ramsey's own: "Of course his main statement about value might be disputed, but most of us would agree that the objectivity of good was a thing we had settled and dismissed with the existence of God. Theology and Absolute Ethics are two famous subjects which we have realized to have no real objects."

I'd literally just finished an anthology excerpt of Moore's Principia Ethica, where "good" is a simple, indefinable, non-natural property you just apprehend directly — about as far from "dismissed and settled" as you can get — and Moore was right there at Cambridge in those same years. So a few things have me stuck. First, is there an actual argument packed into that sentence for why giving up God should also mean giving up the objectivity of goodness, or is Ramsey just asserting it? It definately isn't obvious to me — plenty of people today combine a fully naturalist, godless worldview with some form of moral realism — so either Ramsey's leaning on a premise he never states, or he's just being breezy among friends after dinner. Second, what do you think "Absolute Ethics" is meant to pick out — a dig at Moore specifically, or some wider idealist target (Bradley, McTaggart, that whole crowd)? And third, if you had to place Ramsey's actual view in this passage into a modern metaethical box — error theory, emotivism, something else entirely — which would you pick, and how much confidence should I have in that label given this is one dense paragraph from an after-dinner talk and not a worked-out treatise?
