I spent some three decades as a cosmologist, much of it preoccupied with the question of whether space itself is infinite in extent. Since retiring, I have drifted into decision theory and ethics, where I recently encountered an argument that puts this old cosmological question to a curious new use. The argument, sometimes termed "evidential cooperation in large worlds," runs roughly as follows: if space (or the wider multiverse) is very large or infinite, it should contain vast numbers of agents who reason much as we do, hold values that sometimes conflict with ours, and are entirely causally cut off from us. Its proponents claim that our own choice is nonetheless evidence of what these unreachable-but-similar agents choose, on the grounds that shared reasoning under shared circumstances should correlate even without any causal channel linking them to us; on this basis, each of us is urged to act as though party to an implicit bargain, tempering our own values somewhat toward these strangers' values, in the expectation that enough of them are simultaneously tempering theirs toward ours, so that everyone ends up better off by their own lights than unilateral action would have left them.

Write an essay assessing whether this argument gives us any genuine, action-guiding reason to alter our behavior here and now, or whether it is at bottom a cosmological cousin of Pascal's Wager: ingenious in form but empty in practice, since none of its premises about who or what is out there can ever be checked. Weigh the strongest considerations on both sides, engaging with the underlying decision theory where it matters, and close with a definite verdict rather than a mere survey of open questions.
