I'm assembling a small cycle of linked short stories, each organized around a specific piece of technical philosophical machinery smuggled into an otherwise ordinary domestic situation — something in the spirit of what Calvino did with combinatorics or Borges did with infinite regress. The story I'm currently stuck on is meant to be built around Nāgārjuna's catuṣkoṭi, the "four-cornered" logic he runs through the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā to dismantle any claim that a thing possesses svabhāva — independent, inherent existence. I've drafted this one twice already and keep ending up with versions that state the philosophy rather than enact it, so I'd like your help.

Here is the situation I want the story built on. The protagonist has spent a decade reconstructing and translating into English a sequence of poems left behind, unfinished, by her grandmother, Vera, a poet who wrote in a language now spoken by almost no one and who died leaving only fragments — some finished stanzas, some single lines, a few pages so damaged that the protagonist has had to compose entire passages herself just to make the sequence legible. A prize committee has now shortlisted the finished sequence as "Vera's last poems," and a dispute has broken out (a rival translator, a family member, a committee member — I'll leave the specifics to you) over whether that attribution is honest. I'd like a short story, somewhere in the 1,200–1,800 word range, in which the narrative actually passes in turn through all four positions on whether the poem is hers — that it is, that it is not, that it is both, that it is neither — without any character ever naming Nāgārjuna, Buddhism, emptiness, or the tetralemma, or explaining the logic in dialogue or narration. The four corners should live entirely in the plot and in the protagonist's reasoning, never in exposition.

Please don't let her settle into a comfortable fifth position, some "it's complicated, really it's a bit of both" synthesis that quietly smuggles sameness back in as a further fact waiting to be discovered. I want the story to end the way Nāgārjuna's actual arguments end: with all four corners shown to be unworkable once you go looking for some intrinsic fact about sameness that the poem could hold on its own, and yet with the attribution going on to function perfectly well regardless — read aloud at the ceremony, printed on the cover, eventually cut into her grandmother's gravestone — because conventional designation was never waiting on that kind of independent, ultimate fact in the first place. And please, no incense, no meditating monks, no wise gesturing at "Eastern wisdom": I want something that could run in any literary magazine, with the logic doing real, load-bearing work rather than sitting on top as decoration.
