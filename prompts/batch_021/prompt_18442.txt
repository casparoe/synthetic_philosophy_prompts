I cover science and ideas for a living. At a workshop on trust in science communication last week, two of the speakers — a philosopher who works on epistemology and a longtime science editor — fell into a disagreement over coffee that I have not been able to stop turning over since. It began when the editor described her publication's policy: never report a claim on a single scientist's word alone, however eminent, without an independent second source or replicated data behind it.

(1) The philosopher objected that this proves too much. If testimony always requires independent, non-testimonial backing before it can justify belief, then almost nothing we believe is justified, since our grounds for trusting instruments, methods, and even the general reliability of memory were themselves acquired through someone's say-so at some point. Demanding a testimony-free foundation is demanding the impossible.

(2) The editor replied that she was not demanding a testimony-free foundation, only a second, independent source for any given claim — precisely the standard already applied to perception, where a single startling glimpse of something is not taken at face value until it is seen again or confirmed by someone else.

(3) The philosopher answered that perception does not actually work this way: a single clear perceptual experience is treated as good enough on its own, revised only if some specific reason for doubt arises, such as poor light or fatigue. The honest analogue for testimony is the same policy — trust the speaker by default, and revise only given a specific red flag, such as known bias, an incentive to mislead, or a track record of error — rather than impose a blanket demand for a second source regardless of whether any red flag is actually present.

(4) The editor answered that this default-and-red-flag policy is exactly what her rule implements, not a departure from it: scientists reporting on their own results face structural pressures — funding, priority, publication incentives — that amount to a standing, generic red flag present in every single-source scientific claim, so the "no red flag" condition that would license default trust is essentially never satisfied in her domain, even though it is routinely satisfied for ordinary testimony elsewhere in life.

Both of them, half in jest, asked me to find a philosopher who could settle the matter. Since I cannot corner you at a conference, would you go through the exchange move by move, (1) through (4), and say who has the better of it at each step, rather than simply declaring an overall winner? I would also be curious what, if anything, you think both of them are overlooking about how far testimony can justify belief on its own.
