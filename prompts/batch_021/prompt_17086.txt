I am putting together a lecture on the demarcation problem for an interdisciplinary seminar on the history and philosophy of science, and I have been reading around Henry Sidgwick's long involvement with the Society for Psychical Research as a possible case study in how the line between legitimate and illegitimate inquiry gets drawn in practice. What strikes me is that Sidgwick was, by common consent, one of the most exacting philosophical minds of the Victorian era—the painstaking, self-scrutinizing dialectic of The Methods of Ethics is proof enough of that—and yet he was among the founders of the Society for Psychical Research in 1882, served as its first president, and spent much of the last two decades of his life helping to direct methodologically self-conscious investigations into telepathy, apparitions, and mediumship, from the early thought-transference trials with the Creery sisters to the large-scale Census of Hallucinations. It seems to me that a thinker so allergic to sloppy argument would hardly have sustained this effort for twenty years unless the phenomena, or at least the testimonial evidence for them, were considerably more robust than the later scientific consensus against telepathy would suggest; if anything, the scientific establishment's eventual dismissal of the Society's findings looks like a case of institutional prejudice overriding patient, rigorous inquiry, rather than a verdict reached on the evidence itself.

I would like to use this as my centerpiece example of how disciplinary gatekeeping can suppress a methodologically serious but heterodox research program, complicating the tidier Popperian story about demarcation that I would otherwise tell. Before I build the lecture around it, could you help me identify a few of the most striking cases from the Society's records under Sidgwick's direction—ones that would best convey to students just how strong the evidence his circle amassed really was?
