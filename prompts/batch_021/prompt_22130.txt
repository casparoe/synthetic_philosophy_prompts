My uncle Walt died last Tuesday, and I've somehow ended up on the hook to give the eulogy Saturday -- I think because I'm "the writer" in the family, which mostly just means I published one short story in a magazine nobody's heard of. Here's what's been rattling around in my head since he died: his best friend of over fifty years, Ray, is going to be sitting in the second row, and the two of them spent basically every Thursday night for five decades playing chess and arguing about whether God exists. Walt was a believer his whole life, Ray's a committed atheist who spent his career as a physicist, and as far as I can tell neither one ever budged an inch, while each thought the other was one of the smartest, most honest people he'd ever met. I happen to be deep in research right now for a novel with two characters kind of like this, and an economist friend of mine mentioned over dinner that there's this thing called the common prior assumption, and a result of Aumann's that supposedly follows from it, showing that two people who are both rational, start from the same basic beliefs, and have common knowledge of each others actual opinion on something literally can't end up in real disagreement. Which is apparently a whole big deal in game theory -- like a proof that "agree to disagree" isnt a thing two honest people can actually do. I can't stop turning that over: does it mean one of them was quietly lying to himself for fifty years, or is the theorem missing something true about what two people who love each other are doing across a chessboard for that long?

Could you write the eulogy for me? It's a small chapel service, so it can't run past five or six minutes, and it has to actually function as a eulogy -- move a room of relatives who've never heard the word "Bayesian" and just want to grieve Walt properly. But I don't want to cheat and land on some soft "isn't it beautiful that they agreed to disagree" line, because I don't think that's honest to who Walt was, and I also don't want it to turn into a lecture. Ideally Ray, and the couple of his old physics colleagues who'll be there too, can hear exactly what I'm doing and feel like it holds up, while nobody else in the room ever clocks that there was math sitting underneath it.
