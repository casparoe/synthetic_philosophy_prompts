After a seminar on social choice theory, two of my graduate students carried an argument out into the hallway; each gave me their own account afterward, and I have pieced the exchange together as best I can.

(1) Sofia opened by claiming that Harsanyi's aggregation theorem settles the question of collective welfare in favor of utilitarianism: if every individual and the social planner satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms over the same set of lotteries, and the planner respects the Pareto principle, then the planner's utility function must be an affine combination of the individuals' utility functions. Anyone who accepts expected-utility rationality and unanimity, she said, has thereby already accepted utilitarianism, whatever they might say if asked directly.

(2) Rahul objected with Diamond's old example: give an indivisible benefit to one of two equally situated people. A planner who maximizes the sum of expected utility is indifferent between tossing a fair coin and simply handing the benefit to one of them outright, since both procedures yield the same expected sum. But the coin toss is intuitively fairer, and a theory that cannot tell it apart from an arbitrary assignment is missing exactly what a theory of just aggregation ought to capture.

(3) Sofia replied that this is a complaint about coarse bookkeeping, not about the theorem itself: if having gone through a fair procedure is something people can care about, that fact belongs in the description of the outcome, and once utilities are assigned over sufficiently fine-grained outcomes — including how a decision was reached, not merely who ends up with what — the theorem goes through exactly as before.

(4) Rahul answered that this concession costs more than Sofia lets on: once outcomes are individuated finely enough to include the value of fair process, it is no longer obvious that everyone prefers, ex ante, whichever lottery maximizes the utilitarian sum — so the substantive question of whether only individual betterness matters, or fair procedure matters in its own right, has simply been relocated into the choice of outcome space rather than settled by the axioms.

(5) Sofia's last word was that, even granting all this, the formal result — social utility as a weighted sum of individual utilities — stands untouched; what remains in dispute is only which outcome space and which weights to plug in, and that is a separate question from whether the aggregation rule itself is utilitarian in form.
