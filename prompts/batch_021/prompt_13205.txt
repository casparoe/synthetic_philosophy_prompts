I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent retirement finally reading the philosophy I never had time for during my career. My oldest friend and former lab partner — a chemist, ferociously empirical, with no patience for consoling pieties — is retiring from her laboratory at ninety, and I am giving the toast at a dinner split between former colleagues who will scrutinize every claim and grandchildren who just want something short and warm. I would like the toast built around Cicero's De Senectute, starting from the charge against old age most relevant to her evening — that it withdraws us from active affairs — and taking up one or two of the other three charges Cato answers: that old age weakens the body, strips away pleasure, and brings us near death. Engage his actual rebuttals rather than merely alluding to the work, and be honest about where they strain against what neuroscience and gerontology now tell us — his claim that memory need not fail if kept in practice, say, or that physical decline is often a debt from youthful excess rather than age itself — since she would notice any smoothing-over instantly. Please write the toast: about five minutes spoken aloud, built on the argument itself rather than decorated with a Cicero reference, and still warm enough for the grandchildren.
