A friend of mine and I spent most of dinner last night arguing about a public figure who built his career campaigning against corruption and was then revealed to have quietly accepted the same kinds of favors he had spent that career condemning. My friend's reaction was the standard tu quoque move: that the man had thereby forfeited any right whatsoever to criticize corruption in others. I pushed back that this confuses the truth of what he says with his standing to say it, and that hypocrisy seems like it should be a fact about the blamer's character rather than something that changes the moral status of the blame itself. Neither of us could actually explain why hypocrisy would undermine standing to blame, as opposed to just being a separate, unflattering thing to notice about the person doing the blaming, and it bothered me enough that I wanted a second opinion from someone who has thought about this more carefully than either of us.

Two accounts from the philosophical literature on blame seem to disagree with each other on exactly this point. R. Jay Wallace argues, in his work on hypocrisy and moral address, that blame is best understood as a second-personal act that presupposes the blamer and the blamed stand as equals under the same norm; on his view, hypocritical blame fails specifically because it treats that equality as running only one way, from blamer to target. G. A. Cohen, writing on who does and doesn't have standing to condemn political violence, instead frames the issue as a distinction between the message and the messenger: the hypocrite's message can remain entirely true, and the wrongdoer genuinely blameworthy, even though something about this particular messenger's act of delivering that message is compromised.

Could you compare these two ways of locating the problem with hypocritical blame? I'm especially interested in where they actually agree and where they quietly part ways, for instance on whether "losing standing" makes the blame itself false, merely inappropriate to voice, or something else again. After laying that out, tell me which account you find more defensible, using my dinner-table example (or a better one of your own choosing) to justify the verdict, and point out any case where the two accounts would issue different answers about whether my friend's tu quoque response was actually warranted.
