Last month I took my late grandmother's jade bangle to be appraised, and the gemologist mentioned, almost in passing, that "jade" is not really the name of a single mineral. Pieces sold as jade are actually either jadeite (a sodium-aluminium pyroxene) or nephrite (a calcium-magnesium-iron amphibole) — two chemically and structurally unrelated silicates that happen to share color, hardness, and workability, and that Western mineralogists did not reliably distinguish until the nineteenth century.

I studied a bit of Kripke and Putnam in college, and this discovery has stayed with me since. Their account of natural-kind terms like "water" or "gold" holds that such terms rigidly designate whatever hidden microstructure actually unites the stuff ordinary usage has been tracking, which is why "water is H2O" counts as necessary despite being knowable only a posteriori. Jade looks like it ought to be a textbook illustration of the same phenomenon, except there is no single microstructure for the word to have been tracking all along.

Write an essay addressing whether cases like jade constitute a genuine counterexample to the Kripke–Putnam picture of natural-kind terms as rigid designators fixed by a shared underlying essence, or whether the picture can be repaired without sacrificing what made it attractive in the first place. Engage seriously with at least one specific proposal for reconciling the theory with terms like "jade," and defend a definite verdict on whether a statement such as "this bangle is jade" can still express a necessary a posteriori truth — rather than concluding merely that the matter is unsettled.
