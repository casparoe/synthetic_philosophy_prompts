After forty years working in condensed matter and statistical physics, I retired a few years ago and have finally had the leisure to read philosophy of science properly, rather than in the fragmentary way one absorbs it over the course of a career in physics. Over the past year, a small reading group I joined worked through the old dispute between Philip Anderson's "More Is Different" and the more traditional reductionism defended in, for instance, Steven Weinberg's Dreams of a Final Theory, before moving on to philosophical work that tries to pin down what is actually at stake between positions of that kind, including Jaegwon Kim's writing on causal exclusion and Robert Batterman's work on asymptotic reasoning and renormalization.

The case I keep returning to is universality in critical phenomena: a uniaxial ferromagnet and a fluid near its liquid-vapor critical point have essentially nothing in common microscopically, yet they share exactly the same critical exponents, a fact explained not by tracing through either system's underlying physics but by renormalization-group arguments showing that both flow, under coarse-graining, to the same fixed point. I cannot decide whether this is a genuine, physically respectable instance of emergence that ought to worry a committed reductionist, or whether it is entirely compatible with reductionism and simply reflects our practical inability to derive macroscopic behavior "by hand" from microphysics — an epistemic limitation dressed up as a metaphysical one. Every time I try to state the reductionist thesis precisely enough to be substantive, it seems to survive the emergentist's best examples unscathed; every time I try to state the emergentist thesis precisely enough to be interesting, it runs into something like Kim's worry about what causal or explanatory work is left for a "merely emergent" property to do.

I would like you to write me an essay that actually settles this for me, rather than surveying the landscape and leaving me where I started. Lay out the strongest reductionist case — you may draw on Nagel-style reduction via bridge laws and on Weinberg's and Kim's arguments where they help — against the strongest case for treating universality and renormalization-group explanations as evidence of real emergence, drawing on Anderson and on Batterman's discussion of asymptotic reasoning. Then defend a specific verdict: full-blooded reductionism, some "weak" emergence a reductionist could happily accept, or something that genuinely deserves the label "strong" or ontological emergence. I would much rather you stake out and argue for one precisely stated position than conclude, diplomatically, that both sides have a point.
