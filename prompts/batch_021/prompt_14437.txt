This term I am teaching a graduate seminar on the intellectual history of existential risk, and last week we spent a session on John von Neumann's 1955 Fortune essay "Can We Survive Technology?" Three features of that piece struck the class as considerably more distinctive than the usual one-line summary of it as "Manhattan Project mathematician turns doomsayer." First, von Neumann's argument that advances in transportation and communication had been steadily shrinking the effective size of the globe, thereby eroding the margins of distance, delay, and error that had previously made political and military miscalculation survivable. Second, his refusal to hold out hope for any static, once-and-for-all solution to this predicament, whether through world government or comprehensive treaty, in favor of an ethic of continuous improvisation, expediency, and case-by-case management: learning to live indefinitely with acute danger rather than expecting to abolish it. Third, and this is the detail almost none of my students had encountered before, his explicit prediction that mastery of climate and weather would become a technology whose coercive and destructive potential could rival that of nuclear arms — made not long before his death in 1957, decades before anyone spoke of "geoengineering."

My students disagreed sharply about how von Neumann, transplanted into the present, would size up today's actual debates over solar radiation management: Harvard's SCoPEx experiment, quietly shelved after sustained opposition that included Indigenous groups in Sweden; the startup Make Sunsets, which has been launching sulfur-dioxide balloons and selling "cooling credits" with essentially no regulatory oversight, prompting Mexico to announce a ban on such experiments within its territory; and the looming prospect that a single wealthy state, or even a well-funded private actor, could unilaterally impose "termination shock" risk on the rest of the planet by starting, and later halting, large-scale aerosol injection. Some argued that von Neumann's picture of unstoppable technological momentum implies he would have regarded continued geoengineering research as simply the next rational frontier to master before rivals did; others thought his shrinking-globe logic implies he would have seen unregulated unilateral deployment as exactly the kind of collapsing safety margin he most feared, even as he doubted any treaty regime could genuinely contain it. Rather than a generic gesture at his Cold War credentials, I would like your best reconstruction of his likely verdict, one disciplined specifically by the commitments of the Fortune essay, together with an honest indication of where you think that reconstruction gets most speculative or is simply underdetermined by what he actually wrote.
