I'm a novelist a little more than halfway through a literary novel set largely inside a federal appellate court, and I've reached a scene I don't want to fake. My protagonist, Judge Miriam Kessler, sits on the Thirteenth Circuit — a federal appellate court I invented for the book — and has spent the first half of the novel earning a reputation as an unsentimental textualist: the sort of judge who got her seat precisely because she was expected to read statutes narrowly and leave the closing of loopholes to Congress. In the chapter I'm drafting now, she has to circulate the actual bench memo laying out her position to the two other judges on her panel before their post-argument conference, and I'd rather the legal reasoning be real than merely plausible-sounding, so I'm hoping you'll draft it for me in full, as she would actually send it.

Here is the case, all of it invented for the book. The Wetland Nesting Protection Act makes it unlawful "for any person to drain, fill, or otherwise alter a wetland that serves as nesting habitat for a species listed under section 2, during the nesting season for that species, without a permit issued under section 5." Two weeks before nesting season, a landowner named Alma Halloran — anticipating that an imminent listing decision would make future permits nearly impossible to obtain — drained and bulldozed a pond that had hosted a colony of the copper-throated marsh finch every spring for a decade, so that by the time the season opened there was no wetland left to protect. The U.S. Migratory Habitat Service sued for civil penalties and won in district court. On appeal, Halloran argues that the statute's own words confine liability to alterations occurring "during the nesting season"; she acted two weeks early, so on the plain text she falls outside the prohibition, and if Congress had meant to reach preemptive strikes it knew how to write "at any time" or "in anticipation of the nesting season" and chose not to. The government answers that Halloran's reading lets any landowner defeat the Act indefinitely just by acting a little earlier each season, and it defends a more purposivist reading by leaning on the Act's own findings section — which recites that such habitat is "frequently destroyed in anticipation of the nesting season, before any protection attaches" — together with a floor statement from the bill's sponsor describing this exact tactic under the state law the Act was modeled on.

Could you draft the memo Judge Kessler would actually circulate to Judge Okafor and the third panel member ahead of conference: a clear recommendation on whether to affirm or reverse, the reasoning a serious textualist would give for it, and a full-strength version of whichever side's argument she ends up rejecting — Judge Okafor is written as the sharpest mind on the court and won't be moved by a strawman. If there's a genuine point of strain in her own method here — say, over whether a findings section counts as "text" she's entitled to lean on even while she'd exclude the floor statement, or over whether the government's "this reading guts the Act" point is a legitimate absurdity argument or just a policy complaint dressed up as one — I'd like the memo to sit with that tension rather than resolve it too smoothly; my editor clerked on a court of appeals and will notice if the reasoning is cleaner than real judging ever is.
