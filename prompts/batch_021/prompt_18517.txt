I'm finishing a magazine piece on the science of "flow" — the state of total, effortless absorption that Mihály Csikszentmihalyi began studying in the 1970s and popularized in his 1990 book of the same name — and a philosopher I interviewed for background sent me down a rabbit hole into Moritz Schlick's 1927 essay "On the Meaning of Life." I hadn't come across it before, and it's a strange, striking piece that seems to anticipate a good deal of what flow research would later claim on empirical grounds.

Schlick's argument runs roughly as follows: an activity pursued purely as a means to some further end cannot itself be a source of meaning, on pain of an infinite regress of purposes; meaning can attach only to activity that is self-sufficient, valued for its own sake — what he calls, tellingly, Spiel, or play. Children are his paradigm case, since their activity is playful almost by definition; adults mostly lose this capacity under the weight of earning a living, and Schlick is willing to bite the bullet that most adult labor is, by his own criterion, close to meaningless. The exceptions are art, love, and above all "genius," which he characterizes as something like the capacity to carry a child's playfulness into serious, mature, creative work. He closes on a hopeful, faintly utopian note, suggesting that a better-organized and more technologically advanced society could extend the space for play across an entire life rather than confining it to childhood.

Schlick was murdered in 1936, decades before flow research existed, but flow is characterized in strikingly similar terms — deep absorption, intrinsic reward, a loss of ordinary self-consciousness — and operationalized through a clear-goals/immediate-feedback/challenge-skill-balance model, with researchers reporting it in activities as different as chess, rock climbing, surgery, and even some forms of routine paid labor. Do you think Schlick would have taken this as vindicating and empirically filling out his own theory, perhaps forcing him to soften his verdict on adult work once it turned out that some jobs are quite flow-friendly? Or is there something in the actual structure of his argument that should make him resist the equation — for instance, the fact that flow states are also reported by slot-machine gamblers and compulsive video-game players, cases that sit awkwardly with Schlick's picture of what meaningful play looks like? I'd like a reconstruction that actually engages with Schlick's specific reasoning, rather than a generic "he would probably have found this vindicating."
