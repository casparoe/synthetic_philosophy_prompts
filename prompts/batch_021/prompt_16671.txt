and here's the answer (real submission, name stripped):

"After commissurotomy the two hemispheres can no longer share information, so the patient effectively has two independent information-processing systems in one skull, and this shows up constantly, not just in Sperry and Gazzaniga's lab tests -- these patients go through ordinary life with each hemisphere quietly running its own show. The classic demo is the lateralized picture task: a snow scene is flashed to the right hemisphere and a chicken claw to the left, and the patient's left hand (right hemisphere) points to a shovel while the right hand (left hemisphere) points to a chicken; asked why he picked the shovel, the talking left hemisphere, which never saw the snow scene, just invents a reason on the spot, something about needing to clean out a chicken shed. This kind of confabulation is good evidence the two hemispheres are informationally walled off from each other. Thomas Nagel discusses exactly this case and concludes that the natural reading is that there are simply two separate minds, or subjects of experience, sharing one body, each with its own stream of consciousness, and that the surgery just reveals a duality that was there all along. I think this is basically the right way to look at it: if a system can hold contradictory beliefs and act on both at once without experiencing any conflict, the normal identity conditions for a single subject aren't being met anymore."
