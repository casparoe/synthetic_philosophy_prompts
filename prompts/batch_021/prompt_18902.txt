I am currently teaching a graduate seminar on early modern rationalist ethics, and we have spent several sessions reconstructing Leibniz's attempt to ground morality in his metaphysics of perfection. Two pieces of that picture matter for what follows. First, his repeated claim—in the Discourse on Metaphysics and the Monadology alike—that the world God selected is the one combining the greatest possible variety with the greatest possible order, and that perfection consists in this combination rather than in either factor alone. Second, his mature account of justice as "the charity of the wise" (caritas sapientis): genuine benevolence, for Leibniz, is never an undiscriminating desire to maximize pleasure or existence as such, but a wisdom-guided love that aims at other beings' real perfection and lasting felicity, where pleasure itself is defined as the perception of an increase in perfection.

One of my students asked, half in jest, what Leibniz would have made of Derek Parfit's Repugnant Conclusion, and the question has stuck with me because it seems to expose a genuine fault line in his own axiology rather than admitting an easy answer. Leibniz's principle of plenitude—his doctrine that possible essences "strive" for existence in proportion to their perfection, and that the best world actualizes as much compossible reality as it can—looks structurally close to the Total View in population ethics, the very view responsible for the Repugnant Conclusion: that a sufficiently vast population of lives barely worth living can outweigh, in aggregate value, a much smaller population of flourishing people. But his insistence that perfection requires order and harmony, not mere quantity of variety, and his insistence that true felicity is the perception of real perfection rather than a bare sum of minimally worthwhile states, both cut against that same aggregative logic.

What I would like from you is a careful reconstruction of how Leibniz himself, working from his own principles rather than from a generic "rationalists would say" script, would most plausibly have adjudicated this tension. Could a Leibnizian consistently reject the Repugnant Conclusion without being pushed, by the same reasoning, into an equally uncomfortable position that favors a small, maximally harmonious population over any larger one—in apparent tension with his independent commitment to plenitude? Please work through the strongest candidate position available to Leibniz on his own terms, and tell me honestly where you think his framework simply underdetermines the answer rather than forcing it.
