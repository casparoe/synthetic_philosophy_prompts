I've covered science and ideas for a regional daily for the better part of a decade, and I sit on our newsroom's standards committee, which meets next Tuesday to resolve a dispute that has been simmering since a correction we ran in the spring. A reporter's lede had flatly stated that a brief classroom writing exercise "closes nearly a third of the achievement gap between low- and high-income students," based on a single small randomized trial; a larger, multi-site follow-up has since found no detectable effect, and readers who had shared the original piece on social media were unhappy that we had reported an early, contested result as settled fact rather than as a hedged, attributed claim. The committee has since split into three camps over what should license a flat, unhedged assertion in our copy generally, as opposed to phrasing like "a new study suggests" or "according to researchers." One camp wants reporters held to whether the claim is actually true, period, regardless of what we could have known at the time. A second wants the bar to be that the reporter and the paper genuinely knew the claim when we published it — a bar the writing-exercise story clearly didn't clear. A third thinks both bars would silence most science and policy reporting and wants to license flat assertion whenever it was reasonable to believe the claim on the evidence available at deadline, even if that later turns out to be wrong.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate before the meeting? Recommend one standard, or an explicit hybrid, as our house rule, and show your work rather than just declaring a preference: apply it back to the writing-exercise story, then stress-test it against two cases the committee keeps getting stuck on. First, a single-site finding with a p-value of 0.03 that hasn't yet been retested anywhere else — is "statistically significant" close enough to "known," given how often single, barely-significant studies fail to replicate? Second, an investigative piece built on three separate sources inside an agency who, it later emerged, were all relaying the same unconfirmed internal rumor rather than verifying it independently — were we blameless in asserting it as fact even though it turned out false, and does the rule you're proposing actually agree? Please give the two camps you don't side with their strongest possible case before you set them aside, and close with the actual sentence you would add to the style guide.
