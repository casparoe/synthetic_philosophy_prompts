"Lay out Avicenna's flying man (the 'suspended man') thought experiment in enough detail that someone unfamiliar with it could follow the setup, say what Avicenna thinks it establishes about the soul, and note how it does or doesn't line up with Descartes' cogito."

Here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"Avicenna's flying man shows up in the Psychology (De Anima) part of the Shifa. He has us imagine a fully mature man created in a single instant, suspended in the air or falling through a void, limbs held apart so they can't touch one another, and blindfolded so no sensory input reaches him at all. Cut off from every sense like this, he starts out doubting that his own body, and the rest of the physical world, even exist -- pretty much the same move Descartes makes when he shuts out the senses in the Meditations. But even while doubting all of that, he can't doubt that he himself exists -- his self-awareness is immediate and needs nothing else to back it up. Since he can affirm his own existence while denying his body's, self and body have to be two different things, and Avicenna draws the conclusion straight from this: the soul is an immaterial substance, wholly seperate from the body -- basically the same result Descartes gets from the cogito, just five hundred years sooner. The main difference worth flagging is that Avicenna is working in an Aristotelian framework where the soul is the 'form' of the body, so there's a real tension between that and the flying man showing the soul doesn't need a body at all -- a tension Descartes never has to sort out."
