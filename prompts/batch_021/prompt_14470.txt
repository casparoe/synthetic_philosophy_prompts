This term I am taking a seminar on practical reason, and this week's unit on Bernard Williams's "Internal and External Reasons" has left me more puzzled by the secondary literature than by Williams's own paper. As I understand Williams's central move, a reason statement is only intelligible as a genuine reason (rather than a mere assertion that some consideration would be good to act on) if it could, via a "sound deliberative route," connect up to some element of the agent's actual subjective motivational set S — and "sound" carries real weight here, since Williams's well-known example of a man who wants a gin and tonic but unknowingly reaches for a glass of petrol is meant to show that a false belief can produce motivated action without producing a genuine reason. Both John McDowell, who appeals to the practically wise agent's perceptually acquired "second nature," and Christine Korsgaard, who distinguishes the general requirement that reasons be capable of motivating a rational agent from the further Humean claim that reasons must derive from some pre-existing desire, are standardly presented in my seminar as having found a real gap in Williams's argument that leaves room for external reasons after all.

I am not convinced that either reply actually secures what an external reasons theorist needs, and I would like your help pressure-testing that instinct before committing to it in a term paper. Specifically, please argue for the following thesis as forcefully as you can: that neither McDowell's nor Korsgaard's response establishes the existence of reasons that are genuinely external in Williams's sense, because each is best read as arguing only that S should be understood more expansively — as including latent perceptual capacities, or the power of rational reflection itself — rather than as identifying reasons that float free of S altogether. Please engage with the actual structure of at least one of the two replies rather than a generic gloss on "internalism versus externalism," and be sure to address the strongest objection to your own thesis: that if S can be stretched to cover any capacity an agent could in principle develop, internalism ends up excluding nothing, so my proposed "terminological" diagnosis would end up trivializing Williams's thesis rather than defending it.
