I'm a cognitive science major currently taking a philosophy of mind seminar, and we just spent two weeks on Dennett's intentional stance -- his claim that attributing beliefs and desires to a system is warranted exactly when doing so lets us predict its behavior better than the physical or design stance would, regardless of what's actually implemented underneath. Separately, I'm the undergraduate representative on my university's newly formed AI Advisory Committee, which has to sign off on any AI tool the administration wants to deploy campus-wide before it goes live, and the timing has turned out to be more relevant than I expected.

This week we're reviewing a proposed 24/7 "wellness companion" chatbot that the Dean of Students office wants to offer to students dealing with stress or homesickness. The vendor's user-facing materials describe it in frankly mentalistic terms: it "listens and understands what you're going through," "remembers what matters to you," and "genuinely wants to see you thrive." The committee is split. A psychology professor on the committee argues this language is perfectly legitimate: if the intentional stance is the right account of what having a belief or desire even consists in, then a system whose behavior is only tractable by attributing understanding and wanting to it really does understand and want things in the only sense that has ever been available to us, so objecting to the copy is just squeamishness about words. The counseling center's director disagrees, worried that lonely or vulnerable students will over-trust and over-disclose to something with no capacity for care or continuity of concern once the chat window closes, and that this is exactly a case where predictive usefulness and honesty come apart.

I need to help draft the committee's actual position before we vote next week. Could you write that memo? I need one clear recommendation -- approve the language as written, reject it, or approve with specific required changes -- and it has to do real philosophical work rather than assert a conclusion: engage directly with whether Dennett's framework actually settles the marketing-language question or is silent on it, and give the strongest version of the losing side's argument before explaining why it doesn't win. Please keep it short enough that we could read it aloud and act on it in a one-hour meeting.
