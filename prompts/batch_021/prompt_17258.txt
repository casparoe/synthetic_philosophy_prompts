Over the last year I've been working through the social-construction literature on my own, and I keep noticing that two of the writers I return to most, John Searle and Ian Hacking, seem to be describing quite different mechanisms even though both get filed under the same label. Searle's paradigm case is money: an object or ledger entry becomes currency because a community collectively imposes what he calls a status function on it, via a constitutive rule of the form "X counts as Y in context C," and that status only persists because the collective acceptance behind it keeps being renewed through everyday use and backing institutions like legal tender law. Hacking's paradigm cases are more like categories of mental illness or deviance: he argues these are interactive kinds, because classifying a person changes how that person behaves and understands themselves, producing a looping effect that has no real counterpart for kinds that are simply indifferent to how we sort them, such as rocks or chemical elements. What I can't work out on my own is which picture actually fits race, and whether the popular one-line comparison that "race is a social construction, just like money is" survives once you press on the mechanics: is racial classification driven by an explicit, renewable status-function-style agreement the way currency is, or is it closer to Hacking's looping story, where the act of classifying reshapes the people being sorted, or is it some mix that neither framework quite anticipated? Please write an essay that lays out both accounts, tests each against how racial categories have actually operated historically, and reaches a definite verdict about which model fits better -- or precisely how the two need to be combined -- rather than concluding that the question is too complicated to resolve.
