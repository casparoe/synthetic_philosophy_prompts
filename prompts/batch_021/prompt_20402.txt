So I'm about two-thirds of the way through a novel where the antagonist is a completely amoral asset manager, and my editor keeps pushing me toward a big confrontation scene where his estranged brother tries to guilt him into some kind of decency. I mentioned my problem with this scene to a friend who's doing her philosophy PhD, and she got me hooked on Bernard Williams' internalism about reasons -- the idea that you only really have a reason to do something if, through some chain of sound deliberation, it connects back to a desire or project or loyalty thats already somewhere in your existing psychological makeup, and if literally nothing in there could ever get you to "so I should do X," then strictly speaking you don't have a reason to do X, no matter how awful that sounds. Which is a problem for my scene, because I want the brother's speech to actually hand my guy a reason, not just a nice sentiment he's free to shrug off.

I already know the two examples everyone reaches for when they explain this stuff -- the gin and tonic that turns out to be a glass of petrol, and the man who's simply nasty to his wife -- and I don't want either of those, they're too well-worn at this point and I can feel them coming a mile off. Could you build me a new example that tests the same internal/external reasons divide, ideally with the texture of an actual scene rather than a thought experiment (specific people, a specific stake), where it's genuinely unclear whether the person has some internal reason buried in their existing cares that the right conversation could excavate, versus a case where an externalist (Nagel, McDowell, whoever) would insist they have a reason regardless of what's inside them -- and be explicit about which considerations are doing the work on each side? I want the case to be a real toss-up, not one that's rigged to obviously tip one way.
