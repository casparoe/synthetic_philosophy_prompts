I'm plotting a novel whose present-day thread follows a disaffected young scholar in Jerusalem who drifts into the orbit of a religious-Zionist circle preparing in earnest for the rebuilding of the Temple: reconstructing priestly vestments and vessels, training kohanim (the priestly caste) in the minutiae of ritual slaughter, even experimenting with breeding a red heifer whose ashes would be needed to purify anyone who serves there. She was trained as a rationalist in the Maimonidean mold before she lost her faith in that tradition, and I want Maimonides' own writing to needle her throughout the book, because on this exact question he is maddeningly hard to pin to one position. In the Guide for the Perplexed (III:32) he explains animal sacrifice as a concession to the psychology of a people raised amid Egyptian and Canaanite idol-worship: God permitted, even required, the forms of worship they already knew so as to redirect their devotion gradually toward Him, since a mind steeped in habit cannot be weaned from it all at once, and he suggests that prayer without slaughter is the higher form of service this concession was meant, eventually, to make possible. Yet in the Mishneh Torah (Hilchot Melachim) he states as plain law, in exhaustive and unapologetic detail, that the messianic king will rebuild the Temple and that sacrificial offerings will resume there.

For a scene I'm drafting, one of the circle's leaders corners my protagonist and forces her to reconcile these two Maimonides, and I'd like your help thinking through what a historically serious answer would actually look like. If Maimonides himself were somehow confronted with a movement that treats rebuilding the altar and reviving the sacrificial cult as a positive achievement to be brought about now, through human effort, rather than a superseded stage of religious development to be left to a Messiah who has not yet come, what do you think he would say? Would his own legal code commit him to blessing the project, or would the philosophical psychology underlying the Guide's account make him uneasy about it, and does the fact that he never squares the two texts (a tension later commentators such as Nachmanides already probed, if from a different angle) give us any real basis for guessing which impulse would win out? I would rather you work through the actual textual tension than simply tell me that Maimonides was "a rationalist who might have been uncomfortable with ritual."
