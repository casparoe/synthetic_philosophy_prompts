I recently joined a graduate seminar on the philosophy of general relativity, and, rather to my surprise, the very first session was devoted not to Einstein but to Newton. The instructor's point was that the "hole argument" over whether spacetime points have their own primitive identity apart from the metric field — which is actually the reason I applied to this program, since I'm hoping to eventually work on background independence in quantum gravity — is best understood as the modern descendant of a much older dispute, and that we ought to read that dispute in Newton's own words rather than in the tidy secondhand paraphrases most of us picked up as undergraduates. So I went back to the Scholium on space, time, and motion at the start of the Principia, in Andrew Motte's eighteenth-century translation, and while I can follow the setup of the famous bucket experiment easily enough, I'm having trouble pinning down exactly what Newton takes himself to have established by the end of it, especially once I try to state it precisely enough to compare with the moves people make in the hole-argument literature.

Here is the passage I keep getting stuck on: "If a vessel, hung by a long cord, is so often turned about that the cord is strongly twisted, then filled with water, and held at rest together with the water; thereupon, by the sudden action of another force, it is whirled about the contrary way, and while the cord is untwisting itself, the vessel continues for some time in this motion; the surface of the water will at first be plain, as before the vessel began to move; but the vessel, by gradually communicating its motion to the water, will make it begin sensibly to revolve, and recede by little and little from the middle, and ascend to the sides of the vessel, forming itself into a concave figure (as I have experienced) ... This ascent of the water shows its endeavour to recede from the axis of its motion; and the true and absolute circular motion of the water, which is here directly contrary to the relative, discovers itself, and may be measured by this endeavour ... And therefore this endeavour does not depend upon any translation of the water in respect of the ambient bodies, nor can true circular motion be defined by such translation."

What I can't work out is this: strictly speaking, all Newton has ruled out here is that the water's tendency to climb the sides depends on its motion relative to the vessel. He hasn't ruled out that the relevant motion might instead be relative to some more inclusive body — the fixed stars, say, or the material contents of the universe as a whole — which is more or less the line Mach took two centuries later. Is that gap already present in the bucket passage on its own, or is it only closed, if it's closed at all, by the separate two-globes thought experiment that comes a bit later in the same Scholium? And if the real work is being done by the two-globes case rather than the bucket, does that mean the bucket experiment by itself shows less than it's usually given credit for? I'd like to get clear on this before next week's session, since it seems like how one resolves it determines which side of the hole argument one finds intuitive to begin with.
