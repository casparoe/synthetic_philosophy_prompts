In a seminar I'm taking on the history of utilitarian ethics, we recently spent a session on Jeremy Bentham's unpublished writings on sexual morality — material such as the essay usually referred to as "Offences Against One's Self," which he kept out of print for most of his life, evidently for fear of the scandal it would provoke. Our professor walked us through how Bentham drew on his critique of what he calls, in the Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation, the "principle of asceticism" and the "principle of sympathy and antipathy" — the two rivals he identifies to the principle of utility — to argue that criminalizing "unnatural" sexual acts between consenting adults rested on nothing more than inherited disgust rather than any calculable harm to a third party. Given that such acts were still capital offenses in England during his lifetime, this struck the whole class as a remarkably radical position for the period, and a few of us ended up arguing after class about just how far that reasoning would actually travel.

That's the question I'd like your help thinking through. Suppose Bentham were somehow confronted today with the growing push, in various liberal democracies, to extend formal legal recognition — not mere toleration, but something like marriage-equivalent status — to consensual relationships involving more than two partners at once. Decriminalizing private conduct between adults is one move; redesigning the legal institution of marriage itself to accommodate multiple simultaneous partners is another, since it raises issues Bentham cared about a great deal elsewhere in his legal writings: the contractual and evidentiary complications of obligations distributed among three or more people, the effects on any children raised in such households, and the social utility of marriage as an institution for securing property and stabilizing expectations.

Would Bentham's hostility to basing law on mere antipathy commit him, on pain of inconsistency, to supporting such recognition? Or would the same cautious, consequence-weighing habits of mind that made him so insistent on evidence before touching any existing legal institution lead him to hesitate, or even to oppose the idea, whatever his sympathies? I'd like you to reconstruct his likely reasoning using his own conceptual apparatus as far as possible — the utility principle, his taxonomy of offences, and the felicific calculus — rather than just inferring from "Bentham was unusually progressive about sex" that he would automatically welcome this particular extension.
