I don't have any formal background in philosophy, but for the last couple of months I've been slowly working my way through a secondhand anthology on ancient logic during long commutes, and I've gotten stuck trying to compare two old arguments that keep turning up together in footnotes without anyone ever quite pulling them apart: Aristotle's sea-battle passage in On Interpretation (chapter 9, if I'm remembering the reference right), and something called the Master Argument, credited to a much more obscure figure named Diodorus Cronus. As I understand it, Aristotle worries that if it is already true today that there will be a sea battle tomorrow, the battle looks fixed in advance and nothing about tomorrow is genuinely open; and his way out, at least on the reading I keep coming across, is to say that a statement about an undetermined future event isn't yet simply true or simply false, even though "there will be a battle, or there won't" still has to be true, taken as a whole.

Diodorus seems to reach a similar-sounding conclusion - that whatever happens, happens necessarily - but by a completely different route that I can't quite fit together with Aristotle's. From the summaries I've managed to find (hardly any of his own writing survives), he seems to show that you cannot hold on to all three of the following at once: that every truth about the past is now necessary, that an impossible thing can never follow from a possible one, and that there is at least one genuine possibility that will never actually be realized. He apparently gives up the third claim, concluding that nothing counts as "possible" unless it either is true or will at some point be true - which seems to get you to a fatalist conclusion about the future without ever needing to deny that statements about the future are true or false right now, unlike Aristotle's way out.

What I would like help with is placing these two arguments side by side properly. Are they really just two versions of the same worry, so that Aristotle's solution quietly disarms Diodorus's argument as well, or do the two rest on different enough premises that Aristotle's escape route leaves Diodorus's argument completely untouched? And between the two, which do you think is actually harder to wriggle out of for someone who wants to insist that the future is genuinely open? I would be grateful for your reasoning, not just a verdict.
