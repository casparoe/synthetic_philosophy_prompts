**Question.** In "Alternate Possibilities and Moral Responsibility" (1969), Harry Frankfurt attacks the Principle of Alternate Possibilities (PAP) — roughly, that a person is morally responsible for an action only if she could have done otherwise — by means of a case involving an agent, Jones, and a counterfactual intervener, Black.

(a) Reconstruct Frankfurt's argument, specifying exactly what causal and counterfactual work Black's presence is doing.
(b) State the "flicker of freedom" strategy for resisting Frankfurt's conclusion, and explain whether, in your view, John Martin Fischer's treatment of this strategy vindicates or undermines PAP.
(c) Explain the "dilemma defense" advanced by Robert Kane and David Widerker against Frankfurt-style cases, and explain how Mele and Robb's "blockage" cases are meant to respond to it.

**Student's answer.**

(a) Frankfurt's case asks us to consider Jones, who is about to decide whether to perform some action A, and Black, who wants Jones to do A but prefers not to intervene unless he has to. Black has some way of ascertaining in advance whether Jones is going to decide to do A on his own; if it looks as though Jones will decide against A, Black intervenes — for instance, by direct manipulation of Jones's brain — so as to guarantee that Jones does A anyway. In the scenario Frankfurt actually describes, Jones decides to do A entirely on his own, without any push from Black, so Black's mechanism is never triggered. Frankfurt's claim is that Jones is morally responsible for doing A, notwithstanding the fact that, given Black's disposition to intervene, Jones could not have done otherwise than A. Since responsibility and the inability to do otherwise co-occur here, PAP must be false. What makes the case work, on Frankfurt's view, is that Black is entirely idle in the sequence of events that actually explains Jones's doing A: Black closes off the alternative without contributing to, or figuring in the explanation of, what Jones actually does.

(b) PAP's defenders have replied that Frankfurt's case does not really remove every alternative possibility. Black's mechanism has to be triggered by something — some "prior sign" occurring in Jones before his decision, such as an incipient neural pattern or a moment of hesitation — and Jones could presumably have exhibited a different sign, or no sign at all. This residual, admittedly minimal, alternative is what Fischer calls a "flicker of freedom." Fischer's own discussion of the strategy is best read as a qualified vindication of it: he grants that these flickers are "robust" in the sense responsibility requires, since the sign is nomically sufficient for Black to know what Jones will do, and that sufficiency is enough to tie the alternative to Jones's own agency. On this reading, Fischer's conclusion is that PAP survives Frankfurt's attack, but only in weakened form: the alternative that responsibility requires can be vanishingly thin, so long as some causally connected alternative remains open to the agent.

(c) A further challenge, developed independently by Robert Kane and by David Widerker in the mid-1990s and often called the "dilemma defense," targets the prior sign itself. The intervener needs the sign to be a reliable enough indicator that he can safely refrain from intervening whenever it appears; but reliability of this kind seems to require that the sign be deterministically, or at least nomically, connected to Jones's eventual decision. If we grant the case that much determinism, however, the example has simply assumed the truth of determinism in a context that is supposed to be neutral between compatibilists and libertarians — it begs the question against the libertarian defender of PAP. If, instead, the connection between sign and decision is allowed to fall short of determinism, Black cannot be certain of intervening in time, and Jones retains some power to decide otherwise even after the sign occurs. Either way, Frankfurt's argument seems not to touch the libertarian.

Mele and Robb's "blockage" cases try to defuse this dilemma by removing the need for a prior sign altogether. A deterministic backup process is set running from the very beginning, alongside Jones's own indeterministic deliberation; the backup would produce the decision to do A regardless, but is always preempted because Jones's own process reaches its outcome first. Nothing has to be read off a sign, because the backup process is already guaranteed to occur independently of anything Jones does. Since the case eliminates Jones's alternative possibilities without relying on a predictive sign, and without assuming that Jones's own psychology is deterministic, Mele and Robb take themselves to have shown, against the dilemma defense, that determinism is after all compatible with moral responsibility even by the libertarian's own lights.
