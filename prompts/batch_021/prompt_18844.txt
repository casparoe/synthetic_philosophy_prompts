I'm an economics PhD student writing a dissertation chapter on time-inconsistent preferences, and the philosophical literature on money pumps keeps raising a fork I'd like to see argued out properly rather than just asserted. Write a dialogue between two decision theorists watching a seminar demonstration in which a volunteer with cyclic preferences over three goods (X over Y, Y over Z, Z over X) is offered, one at a time, small-fee trades that walk her around the cycle back to her starting point, poorer by three fees.

The first theorist takes the demonstration as decisive: an agent disposed to refuse the final trade, once she sees the cycle closing, has thereby stopped holding a cyclic preference in any sense that matters -- so the pump shows that rationality requires acyclic preferences, and that appeals to resolute choice or to Larry Temkin's "essentially comparative" account of value are just ways of restoring transitivity at the level of policy while insisting that the underlying preferences remain cyclic. The second theorist denies that exploitability and preference structure come apart so cleanly: a resolute chooser, in Edward McClennen's sense, can decline the losing trade without her cyclic preference ever having changed, so the pump shows only that naive, sequential choosers get exploited -- a fact about choice dispositions, not a verdict on the rationality of the preferences themselves.

Have them press each other's weakest points: the first should ask whether "resolute" refusal is anything more than transitivity wearing a disguise, and the second needs an actual answer, not a restatement of the distinction. Keep the exchange to a tight, natural page of back-and-forth, give both sides equal argumentative skill, and don't let either concede outright or lapse into strawmanning the other.
