I'm a PhD student in bioethics doing a clinical ethics practicum, which gives me a non-voting seat on my hospital's ethics consultation service. The service is drafting a documentation policy and has split over a genuine disagreement: currently, a consult note states only the recommendation (e.g., 'withdrawing artificial nutrition would not wrong this patient') without the supporting reasoning, since the treating team is expected to trust the consultant's judgment much as they would a radiologist's reading of a scan. A minority of consultants, including our newest hire, argue this analogy fails for moral verdicts specifically -- this looks to me like a version of what philosophers call the problem of moral testimony -- since deferring to someone else's bare say-so, without grasping why the act is permissible, seems to leave the physician unable to act from her own understanding, which many think matters both for the moral worth of her choice and for whether she'll get the next, slightly different case right on her own. Others on the service reply that demanding a full ethical argument in every note is unworkable given caseloads of six or more consults a day, and that plenty of ordinary moral deference -- to parents, mentors, clergy -- seems perfectly fine. Please draft the short memo I'll bring to next week's meeting, recommending a specific documentation standard -- bare verdict, full reasoning, or some conditional policy in between -- with an implementation timeframe, and take the epistemic objection to moral deference as seriously as the workload objection rather than letting the latter win by default.
