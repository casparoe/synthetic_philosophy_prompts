As a retired experimental physicist who has spent the past year working through Henry Sidgwick's Methods of Ethics and, out of curiosity about the man behind the book, the early history of the Society for Psychical Research, I have been struck by how closely the two projects are connected. The unresolved "dualism of practical reason" with which the Methods of Ethics concludes seems to have been, for Sidgwick, not merely an abstract embarrassment for intuitionist ethics but a live problem he hoped psychical research might help settle empirically, by furnishing evidence of personal survival that could underwrite a moral order reconciling self-interest with duty. Yet Sidgwick could also be a hard-nosed experimentalist when the data demanded it, as when he rigorously excluded the Creery sisters' apparent telepathic successes from the evidential record once a signalling code came to light, over the protests of colleagues such as the physicist William Barrett. Please argue that this combination — a philosophically motivated hope for a particular result, together with a genuine willingness to discard convenient evidence — made Sidgwick's decades-long psychical research programme a rational and methodologically respectable enterprise, rather than a case of motivated reasoning that his admirers must politely overlook.
