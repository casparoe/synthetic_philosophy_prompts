Here's the question as I posed it (Question 4 on the exam, worth 20 points, sub-parts as marked):

The Knowability Principle (KP) says every truth is possibly known: ∀p(p → ◇Kp). Fitch (1963) showed that, given only very weak assumptions about the knowledge operator K, KP entails that every truth is actually known.
(a) [3 pts] Formalize KP and the claim that not everything is known (call this NO, for "non-omniscience").
(b) [10 pts] Reconstruct Fitch's argument that KP and NO cannot jointly be true. Be explicit about exactly which properties of K you're relying on at each step.
(c) [3 pts] State the resulting paradoxical conclusion, and explain in a sentence or two why it's supposed to be a special problem for the semantic anti-realist specifically, rather than just a curiosity about the K operator.
(d) [4 pts] Present one strategy from the literature for resisting this conclusion, and briefly assess whether you think it works.

And here's the answer, copied exactly as the student wrote it:

(a) KP: for every proposition p, if p is true then it's possible for someone to know that p: ∀p(p → ◇Kp). NO: there's at least one truth nobody knows: ∃p(p ∧ ¬Kp).

(b) The key move is to show that "p and nobody knows p" (p ∧ ¬Kp) is a proposition that can never be known, for any p, even though some instance of it is true if NO is true.

Suppose for contradiction that someone knows (p ∧ ¬Kp). Knowledge distributes over conjunction — if you know a conjunction you know each conjunct — so you know p and you know ¬Kp. But knowledge is factive (Kq → q, for any q), so from knowing ¬Kp we get that ¬Kp is actually true. So we've derived both Kp (from the first conjunct) and ¬Kp (from factivity applied to the second). Contradiction. So knowing (p ∧ ¬Kp) is impossible for any p — ¬◇K(p ∧ ¬Kp) — and since nothing here depended on any contingent fact about p, this holds of necessity, for every p.

Now suppose NO is true: some particular truth a is such that a ∧ ¬Ka. By KP, since (a ∧ ¬Ka) is true, it must be possible to know it: ◇K(a ∧ ¬Ka). But we just showed that this is impossible. Contradiction. So KP and NO can't both hold — given KP, we're forced to reject NO and accept ∀p(p → Kp): every truth is, in fact, actually known.

Worth flagging: every step above is intuitionistically kosher. We never once helped ourselves to excluded middle or double-negation elimination, so a logical revisionist can't use intuitionistic logic to wriggle out of this one either.

(c) The conclusion is absurd on its face — nobody actually thinks every truth is known by someone. It's a special problem for the anti-realist because KP is essentially a formal statement of their core commitment, that truth cannot outrun possible verification. So anti-realism, by its own lights, appears to collapse into the claim that we're all omniscient, which looks like a reductio of the position from the inside.

(d) One response, due to Dummett, restricts KP so that it only governs decidable propositions, rather than every proposition whatsoever. On this view, KP was never meant to be fully general: the whole motivation for tying truth to verification, for the anti-realist, is to make sense of statements we can effectively decide one way or the other — the same idea driving the switch to intuitionistic logic in mathematics, where a statement only gets asserted once you're holding a proof. Since "p ∧ ¬Kp" isn't the kind of statement that could ever be decided, KP was never supposed to range over it in the first place, and Fitch's derivation is blocked right at the instantiation step. This can look like a made-to-measure patch, but it does stop the argument, and it's at least continuous with commitments the anti-realist already had about decidability, so it isn't a bare ad hoc fix either.
