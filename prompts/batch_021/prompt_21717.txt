Question: Under the Uniform Determination of Death Act, death can be declared via either irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory function, or irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem. (a) Explain why some bioethicists have argued that "irreversible" in the first (circulatory) prong should really be read as "permanent," and why that distinction matters for donation-after-circulatory-death (DCD) organ procurement. (b) Explain how Alan Shewmon's research on prolonged survival after a whole-brain-death diagnosis is supposed to challenge the traditional justification for the second (whole-brain) prong, and how defenders of the whole-brain standard have tried to answer him.

Student answer: There are two ways to be declared dead under the UDDA: total loss of function of the whole brain (brainstem included), or the stopping of the heart and lungs. In practice most deaths in the US are still declared under the old cardiopulmonary standard - the brain-based standard mostly comes up for ventilated ICU patients and organ donors.

The reason people distinguish "permanent" from "irreversible" is that in DCD protocols, doctors only wait two to five minutes of asystole before declaring death, even though the heart could in principle still be restarted with CPR or ECMO in that window. So the cessation isn't irreversible in the strict sense, its just that everyone involved has agreed not to intervene. Bernat's answer is to swap "irreversible" (can never be reversed, even in principle) for "permanent" (will not be reversed, because no one is going to try), which lets DCD protocols count as real deaths. Critics say this quietly trades a metaphysical criterion for a predictive one, which is a problem if the whole point of requiring death first is that it's supposed to track a real fact about the patient rather than our intentions toward them.

Shewmon's work pushes in the same direction, but on the brain side. He documented patients correctly diagnosed as whole-brain dead who went on to survive for years on ventilators, with continued growth, wound healing, and in at least one case pubertal development - all signs of integrated organismic functioning that the whole-brain standard says shouldn't be possible once the brain, the body's "integrator," is gone. Defenders of the standard responded to this the same way Bernat responded to the DCD problem: since you can't actually show irreversibility of integrated functioning even in classic whole-brain-death cases, the President's Council on Bioethics just extended the permanence move to the brain side too in its 2008 white paper, stipulating that brain death counts as permanent loss of organismic unity even though Shewmon shows it isn't always irreversible.
