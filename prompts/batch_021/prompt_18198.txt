I recently sat in on a philosophy of biology course while reporting a piece on the adaptationism debates in evolutionary biology, and the instructor let me try grading one student's exam response as an exercise before she returns the marks. I'd like an independent, rigorous read to compare against my own attempt.

The question, worth 20 points, read as follows:

Feathers in the lineage leading to modern birds are generally thought to have evolved first for insulation, coming to be suited for flight only much later, after substantial further modification. Using the distinction between etiological ("selected-effects") theories of biological function, associated with Wright and Neander, and causal-role theories, associated with Cummins:

(a) (6 points) State, in your own words, what each theory takes a trait's function to be.
(b) (8 points) Consider an early feathered dinosaur whose feathers kept it warm and, as a side effect nobody had yet selected for, made it slightly better at gliding off high branches. Would each theory count "aiding gliding" as a function of those feathers at that stage? Explain why or why not for each theory.
(c) (6 points) Flightless birds such as the ostrich retain wings that cannot lift them off the ground. Which of the two theories has a harder time explaining why we are nonetheless inclined to say these wings have, or once had, a function, and why?

Here is the student's answer in full:

"Both theories are trying to say what makes something a trait's function, as opposed to just an effect it happens to have. On the etiological view, developed by Wright and refined by Neander, a trait's function is whatever effect explains why that trait was selected for and spread through the population — the heart's function is pumping blood because pumping blood is what caused ancestral hearts to be favored by selection. On the causal-role view, associated with Cummins, a trait's function is just whatever it currently contributes to some larger capacity we're interested in explaining, regardless of its history — an alternator's function is recharging the battery relative to our interest in how the car runs, and it doesn't matter how the alternator came to be there in the first place.

Applied to the feathered dinosaur: the causal-role theory would count aiding gliding as a function right away, because gliding is a real causal contribution the feathers make to the animal's capacity to move through the air, and this theory doesn't care about history at all. The etiological theory would say gliding counts as a function too, since gliding obviously helps the animal survive falls and evade predators, and natural selection is fundamentally about what helps organisms survive, so anything that clearly benefits survival should count as selected for on this view.

For the ostrich, I think the etiological theory actually has the harder time, because it ties a trait's function so tightly to its original selective history that once the conditions that favored flight disappear, the theory struggles to say the wing still has a function at all. The causal-role theory doesn't have this problem, since it can just point to whatever the wing causally contributes to now, such as balance or mating display, so there's always something for it to call a function."
