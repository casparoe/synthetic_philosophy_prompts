Quick version in case its useful — panpsychists think consciousness (or some baby version of it) goes all the way down, like maybe electrons have a tiny flicker of experience, and the combination problem is the question of how a bunch of those seperate tiny experiences are ever supposed to add up into one single unified experience, like the one I'm having right now typing this sentence. Turns out William James was already worrying about basically this exact thing in Principles of Psychology back in 1890 (theres a whole chapter on what he calls the "mind-stuff theory"), and his take was kind of brutal: he argues that no matter how you shuffle a hundred little feelings together, each one just stays exactly what it always was, shut up in itself, so if a genuinely new, unified feeling ever showed up on top of them, that would have to be a totally separate 101st thing that the hundred original feelings didn't actually add up to or become. Basically he thought real combination might just be impossible, which is a wild thing to land on in 1890 and also, unfortunately, kind of my exact problem right now.

Here's why I want to actually use it and not just drop it as a fun fact: I want to tell my class that we really did become one thing over four years, an actual "class," not just three hundred people who happened to share a building, but I don't want to just say the cheesy "we are one" line if the combination problem means that might not even be a coherent thing to say. And I can't dodge it either, because Mr. Alvarez, who ran the philosophy club and is basically the reason I know any of this, is going to be sitting right in the front row and will 100% clock it if I fake my way through. Meanwhile literally everyone else there is just parents, grandparents, and little siblings who do not want five minutes of philosophy of mind at graduation.

So could you actually write the speech (aim for like five minutes out loud) using the combination problem as a real load-bearing thing in it, not decoration — it should genuinely sit with the possibility that our "unity" is just an aggregate we're telling ourselves a nice story about, and maybe also with the flipped version some panpsychists go for now (I think its called cosmopsychism?) where you start from one big thing and individual people are more like what's left over when it's carved up, instead of us being pieces that got added together.
