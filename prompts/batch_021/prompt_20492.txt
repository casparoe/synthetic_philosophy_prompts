I cover artificial intelligence and formal epistemology for a science-and-ideas magazine, and I've spent the last few weeks reporting on "logical induction," the framework that researchers at the Machine Intelligence Research Institute introduced around 2016 as an answer to the problem of logical uncertainty. Roughly, the puzzle is this: what credence should a reasoner assign to a mathematical or logical claim -- a hard case of the Riemann hypothesis, say, or the parity of some enormous but computable number -- before it has been proved or refuted, given that classical probability theory simply assumes logical omniscience (probability 1 on every theorem, perfectly coherent credences across the whole deductively closed algebra) and so has nothing to say about that interval? In interviews, several researchers sympathetic to the framework describe its central result -- that no efficiently computable "trader" can indefinitely exploit the sequence of prices it assigns to sentences -- as a genuinely principled solution, structurally analogous to a Dutch-book argument for ordinary probabilism. A formal epistemologist I also spoke with was much less convinced, arguing that immunity to computable exploitation is a purely pragmatic, market-stability property that doesn't obviously track epistemic justification at all, especially since convergence to sensible credences is only guaranteed eventually, with no computable bound on how long a logical inductor might sit with wildly unreasonable credences first.

I'd like to work out my own view on this before I write the piece, rather than just relaying both camps' talking points. Could you write an essay that lays out the strongest version of each position -- the view that logical induction really does vindicate intermediate credence in undecided mathematical statements as rational, and the view that it is instead an elegant piece of engineering that leaves the philosophical question of what makes such credences rational either unanswered or quietly redefined into something else, such as uncertainty about one's own future computations -- and then actually settles the matter rather than presenting it as a draw? A hedged but substantive verdict (one that concedes real ground on a specific point) is fine; a flat "reasonable people disagree" is not what I'm after.
