I spent about thirty-five years as an evolutionary biologist, most of it studying cichlid fish speciation in the African rift lakes, and now that I'm retired I've finally got time to sit down and read philosophy of science properly instead of just name-dropping "falsifiability" in the intro paragraphs of papers like everyone else in my field does. Naturally I started with Popper, and I was honestly kind of floored to learn that Popper himself apparently didn't think natural selection was fully scientific by his own criteria. From what I can tell he called it something like a "metaphysical research programme" at one point (I think this is in his intellectual autobiography, Unended Quest, but don't hold me to the title) and worried that "survival of the fittest" comes dangerously close to being a tautalogy, since "fitness" so often just gets defined as whoever happens to survive.

Then, apparently, late in his life he gave a lecture — Darwin College, Cambridge, if I remember right — where he said he'd changed his mind and wanted to offer a "recantation." I mentioned all this to an old colleague over email and she was just as puzzled as I was about what exactly got recanted: was he fully taking back the tautology charge, or just softening it while keeping some deeper reservation intact? Neither of us could figure it out from the secondhand summaries we found.

What really has me stuck, though, is how any of this squares with the fact that Popper also built an entire theory of knowledge, "evolutionary epistemology," explicitly modeled on natural selection — conjectures standing in for random variation, criticism and refutation standing in for selection pressure, that kind of thing. It seems bizarre to use a theory as your grand template for how all knowledge grows while simultaneously doubting its own scientific credentials. Could you actually walk me through the real shape of this: why Popper thought natural selection had a testability/tautology problem in the first place, what his later recantation did and didn't concede, and how the whole picture is supposed to hang together with the evolutionary epistemology stuff? I'd like the honest, in-the-weeds version rather than the soundbite that creationists like to trot out.
