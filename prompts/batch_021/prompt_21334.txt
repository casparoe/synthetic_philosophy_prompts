Exam question: In "On Denoting" (1905), Russell presents his theory of definite descriptions partly as a way of resolving two puzzles that he thought embarrassed the accounts of denoting phrases offered by Frege and by Meinong: the apparent violation of the Law of Excluded Middle by sentences such as "The present King of France is bald," and the apparent paradox of true negative existentials such as "The present King of France does not exist." Using Russell's distinction between the primary and secondary occurrence of a denoting phrase, explain how his analysis resolves both puzzles, and indicate briefly why he regarded this as preferable to the alternatives offered by Frege and by Meinong.

Student's answer: On Russell's view, a sentence of the form "The F is G" does not have the simple subject-predicate structure it appears to have. Instead, it says that exactly one thing is F, and that thing is G — formally, something like ∃x(Fx & ∀y(Fy⊃y=x) & Gx). Because "the present King of France" is a description and not a genuine name, the sentence "The present King of France is bald" is perfectly meaningful even though nothing satisfies the description, and it comes out false, since the first conjunct — that something is presently King of France — is false. This is already an improvement on Meinong, who is forced to invent some object, an existent King of France that nonetheless doesn't exist, to serve as the sentence's subject matter, multiplying entities Russell thought we had no good reason to believe in, and on Frege, who had likewise held that the phrase denotes an object that subsists even though it does not exist, which Russell rejects as offending what he calls "a robust sense of reality."

The excluded-middle puzzle is solved by scope. "The present King of France is not bald" is ambiguous. On its primary occurrence, the description has narrow scope: the sentence says that it is not the case that exactly one thing is presently King of France and bald, which is true, since there is no King. On its secondary occurrence, the description has wide scope, so the sentence still asserts that exactly one thing is presently King of France, and that thing is not bald — false, for the same reason the original sentence was false. Since the negation comes out true on one of the two disambiguations, there is no genuine breakdown of excluded middle here, just an unnoticed ambiguity.

The same apparatus dissolves the existence puzzle: "The present King of France does not exist" simply denies that anything satisfies "is presently King of France," so it can be true without there being anything for the sentence to be about.
