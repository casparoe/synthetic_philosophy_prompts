I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, drafting a section of a longer piece on "coherence arguments" in decision theory — the family of arguments holding that any agent whose preferences or beliefs violate certain formal axioms can be exploited for a guaranteed loss, and that avoiding this exploitation effectively forces the agent to act as an expected-utility maximizer. Below is the draft passage. I'd like your comments on three things specifically: whether I've represented the underlying arguments accurately and kept them properly distinct from one another; whether the pushback in the closing paragraph is fair to the strongest version of the skeptical case or is more of a straw man; and whether the piece would land with an intelligent general reader without oversimplifying the philosophy.

Draft passage:

Picture someone who prefers apples to bananas, bananas to cherries, and, completing the circle, cherries to apples. A merchant who knows this can profit indefinitely: hand the person cherries, then offer to swap them for bananas plus a one-cent fee (accepted, since bananas beat cherries); swap the bananas for apples plus another cent (accepted, since apples beat bananas); swap the apples back for cherries plus a third cent (accepted, since cherries beat apples). The person ends up holding cherries again, a few cents poorer, having gone nowhere. Decision theorists call this a "money pump," and it is the standard reason rational preference is assumed to be transitive: intransitive preferences are not merely odd, they are exploitable.

A structurally similar argument, the "Dutch book," targets degrees of belief rather than preferences over goods. Someone who thinks a coin will land heads with probability 0.6 and tails with probability 0.6 has violated the probability axioms, and a bookmaker can sell them a pair of bets, each individually attractive by the person's own lights, that together guarantee a loss whichever way the coin lands. Combine the money-pump and Dutch-book arguments with a representation theorem of the sort proved by von Neumann and Morgenstern, or by Savage, and the result is striking: any agent immune to both kinds of exploitation will behave exactly as though maximizing the expectation of some utility function relative to some coherent probability function.

This package has lately found a second career in writing about advanced AI, where the claim is that optimization pressure of almost any kind should push a sufficiently capable system toward coherence, on pain of leaving exploitable resources on the table, and hence toward acting like an expected-utility maximizer with comparatively stable goals — part of the case for worrying that such a system would resist having those goals corrected later.

I'm not convinced this last step is as forced as it is often made to sound. Every money-pump story depends on the mark being walked through the trades one at a time without noticing or minding where they lead; an agent that simply commits in advance to refuse the third trade escapes the pump while its underlying preferences remain exactly as cyclic as before. And the representation theorems assume that an agent's preferences are already defined, richly and consistently, over a vast space of hypothetical gambles it may never actually face — a strange thing to presuppose about any real, bounded system. Coherence over the situations an agent actually encounters seems like a considerably weaker, and considerably less alarming, conclusion than global expected-utility maximization.

Please tell me, with reference to the specific moves in the argument rather than just the general gist, where this goes wrong.
