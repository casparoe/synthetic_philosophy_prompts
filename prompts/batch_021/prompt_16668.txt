For a piece I'm working on about the rationalist/AI-risk scene, I keep running into an idea I can't quite get a handle on: "acausal trade" -- the notion that two agents who will never causally interact with each other, like a future superintelligence and present-day humans, or civilizations on seperate sides of the cosmic horizon, can still meaningfully cooperate just by each simulating how the other reasons and acting accordingly. I brought this up with two people I interviewed for the story and they went back and forth on it for like twenty minutes without really landing anywhere. One of them, who's big into decision theory, kept insisting this is basically a straightforward extension of why you should one-box on Newcomb's problem, and that if you reject acausal trade you should probably also reject superrationality in the twin prisoner's dilemma. The other thought the whole thing was dressed-up wishful thinking -- his point was that in ordinary trade you can at least in principle verify the other side held up their end, whereas here you never can, so "trade" is just a flattering word for whatever you'd unilaterally decided to do anyway.

Could you write out that conversation properly, as an actual dialogue, where each side has to engage with the other's strongest point instead of just restating their own priors? I'd want it to dig into why a mere correlation between two decision procedures is supposed to carry any real weight, plus the messier follow-up problems it raises -- like how you'd ever narrow down which of the infinitely many hypothetical trading partners to take seriously, and whether the whole framework quietly collapses into something like Roko's basilisk once you notice that "acausal cooperation" and "acausal extortion" look identical from the inside. Neither of them should be a pushover -- I want the strongest version of each side, not a strawman.
