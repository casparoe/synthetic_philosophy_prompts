Over a forty-year career as a theoretical physicist working mainly in general relativity, I grew accustomed to thinking of any persisting object, myself included, as really a four-dimensional worldtube: an extended structure of space-time points, with "change" amounting to nothing more than the variation of properties across the object's successive time-slices. Since retiring, I have joined a small philosophy discussion group run through my old university's continuing-education program, and at our last meeting, devoted to material persistence, I was rather taken aback to learn that this seemingly innocuous four-dimensional picture is exactly what is contested in the metaphysical dispute between endurantism and perdurantism, and that a great deal of the argument turns on something called the "problem of temporary intrinsics."

As I now understand it, the difficulty is roughly this. An ordinary object appears to have incompatible intrinsic properties at different times — bent at noon, straight at one o'clock, say — and perdurantists dissolve the appearance of contradiction by holding that it is strictly speaking distinct temporal parts of a four-dimensional whole that bear these properties, so that no single thing is ever both bent and straight at once. Endurantists reject temporal parts outright and insist that the whole object is wholly present at both times, so they instead try one of two moves: either (a) relativizing the properties themselves to times, so that being bent turns out to be a disguised relation between the object and a time, or (b) leaving the properties alone as genuinely monadic and intrinsic, and instead relativizing the exemplifying of them, so that it is the act of exemplifying itself that is indexed to a time — the object exemplifies-at-noon the perfectly ordinary property of being bent, and exemplifies-at-one the property of being straight. My reading tells me that Lewis dismissed the first move as effectively conceding that shape is not a real intrinsic property after all, while others, Haslanger among them, have pressed the second move as a way of resisting that concession.

What I cannot get clear on, and where I would very much value your help, is what genuinely distinguishes these two "relativizing" strategies from one another at a fundamental level, given that both end up saying, in some sense, that the object is F only relative to a time — and whether the first move does not covertly grant the perdurantist's point anyway by smuggling in something worldline-like through the back door. More broadly, I would appreciate an explanation of why this problem is generally treated as a serious cost specifically for endurantists who also hold that past and future times are just as real as the present, rather than for their presentist counterparts, who I gather sidestep the whole difficulty more cheaply, and your own sense of which of the two relativizing strategies, if either, actually earns its keep.
