My dissertation work concerns how speakers learn to extend general terms to novel particulars, broadly within the Kripke–Putnam tradition on natural-kind terms. A seminar on classical Indian epistemology has just introduced me to a striking parallel: the Nyāya theory of upamāna, illustrated by the stock example of someone told that "a gavaya resembles a cow" who later encounters the animal in a forest and thereby comes to know what the word "gavaya" denotes. Naiyāyikas treat this as evidence for an irreducible fourth pramāṇa, distinct from perception, inference, and testimony, on the grounds that none of these alone yields the specific cognition that this word applies to this object. Opponents who admit only perception and inference as pramāṇas, such as Dignāga and his successors in the Buddhist epistemological tradition, deny that anything is left over once the remembered testimony, the present perception, and an inference from resemblance are accounted for. I'd like to see the strongest version of this dispute laid out as a dialogue between a Naiyāyika and such an opponent, with each pressed on the exact point where their position is weakest and neither allowed to concede prematurely.
