I spent most of my career as an experimental physicist working on low-temperature condensed matter, and it is only since retiring four years ago that I have begun reading philosophy with any seriousness. A former colleague from the same department, who retired around the same time and has always had more patience for the humanities than I did, meets me every other Thursday to work through a book together, and for the past several months that book has been Wittgenstein's On Certainty. The earlier sections, where he describes certain propositions as functioning like hinges on which our doubts and inquiries turn, rather than as things we know or have evidence for, made reasonably good sense to me: it is not so different from the way a working scientist simply does not entertain, while actually taking a measurement, the possibility that the apparatus in front of him has vanished and been replaced, or that his memory of calibrating it five minutes ago is a hallucination. But we have both become stuck on the passage below, and I would be grateful for help thinking it through before Thursday.

The passage is from near the end of the book (On Certainty, §§608–612, translated by Denis Paul and G. E. M. Anscombe):

608. Is it wrong for me to be guided in my actions by the propositions of physics? Am I to say I have no good ground for doing so? Isn't precisely this what we call a 'good ground'?
609. Supposing we met people who did not regard that as a telling reason. Now, how do we imagine this? Instead of the physicist, they consult an oracle. (And for that we consider them primitive.) Is it wrong for them to consult an oracle and be guided by it?—If we call this "wrong" aren't we using our language-game as a base from which to combat theirs?
610. And are we right or wrong to combat it? Of course there are all sorts of slogans which will be used to support our proceedings.
611. Where two principles really do meet which cannot be reconciled with one another, then each man declares the other a fool and heretic.
612. I said I would 'combat' the other man,—but wouldn't I give him reasons? Certainly; but how far do they go? At the end of reasons comes persuasion. (Think what happens when missionaries convert natives.)

What unsettles me about this is the following. If my trust in the propositions of physics and the tribesman's trust in his oracle both ultimately rest on something that is not itself a reason but a kind of groundless bedrock, and if a clash between two such bedrocks can end only in "persuasion" rather than proof, then it looks as though Wittgenstein has quietly made my entire working life epistemically no better off than astrology. That cannot be the whole story, since elsewhere in the same book he is perfectly happy to say that a tribe which believes one can reach the moon in dreams simply knows less than we do, and that our system of knowledge is the richer one by far. My colleague, who did some analytic philosophy decades ago before retraining in law, insists the tension between these two moments in the text is only apparent, but neither of us can actually produce the argument that dissolves it. Could you help me work out, first, whether this passage really does commit Wittgenstein to the relativism about justification that it seems to threaten, and if not, exactly where an inference to that relativism would go wrong; and second, what "persuasion" is doing in his argument, given that he plainly does not mean mere rhetorical manipulation, and how it is supposed to relate back to the picture of hinge propositions from earlier in the book? I have a nagging sense that this overlaps with what Kuhn later called incommensurable paradigms, but I do not know that literature well enough to tell whether the comparison actually helps or just relabels the puzzle.
