A friend and I record a small, fairly amateur podcast on philosophy of science together, and over dinner last week the two of us fell into a long, increasingly heated argument about how much credit Einstein's general relativity really deserves for the precession of Mercury's perihelion. His position was that because the roughly 43-arcseconds-per-century anomaly in Mercury's orbit had already been sitting in the astronomical record for more than half a century before 1915, general relativity's explanation of it is closer to a curve-fit than to a genuine confirmed prediction, and so shouldn't move anyone's confidence in the theory very much. I disagreed on instinct, since physicists have always treated this as one of the great confirmations in the history of science, but when I tried to spell out why in Bayesian terms I got stuck: if I was already certain of the perihelion data before general relativity came along, then conditioning on a proposition I already assign probability 1 can't move my credence in anything, general relativity included.

Neither of us budged, and afterward I discovered that this exact impasse already has a name in formal epistemology: the problem of old evidence. As it happens, next month's guest on the podcast has spent a good part of her career defending one particular way out of it. Her view is that the mistake lies in treating Bayesian agents as logically omniscient, since real scientists don't automatically know everything their theories entail; what actually confirms general relativity, on her account, is not the old perihelion data itself but the later, genuinely uncertain discovery that the theory entails that data, and it is that discovery, not the data, that a rational agent updates on. I don't want the episode to turn into forty minutes of her simply restating that thesis, so I would like help drafting eight interview questions, each with a short follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give.

Please make sure the set includes: one question that uses the Mercury case to state the naive version of the paradox as sharply as possible before she gets to respond to it; one that presses her on how she can assign anything other than probability 0 or 1 to a claim about what a theory logically entails without quietly reintroducing the very omniscience she is trying to avoid; one that asks her to defend the seemingly awkward consequence that a theory's degree of confirmation ends up depending on the contingent, almost biographical timing of when some particular scientist happened to notice an entailment, rather than on any timeless relation between theory and evidence; one that has her weigh her diagnosis against rival proposals that measure old evidence's degree of support by appeal to the credence a scientist would have had counterfactually, had that scientist never learned the evidence in the first place; and a closing question asking what finding or argument, if any, she would accept as evidence that her logical-omniscience diagnosis is not, after all, the right explanation of why old evidence can confirm a theory.
