I work as a software engineer and have no formal background in philosophy, but a friend who is finishing a PhD in East Asian Studies recently lent me a translation of the Zhuangzi and insisted that its passages on death are the closest thing to genuine "advice" she has ever found in the Chinese philosophical canon. I went down a bit of a rabbit hole after that, and what I noticed is that almost every discussion of "Zhuangzi and death" I could find online converges on the same two episodes: the dream of being a butterfly, and the scene where he is discovered drumming on a basin and singing instead of mourning after his wife's death. Both are wonderful, but after the fourth or fifth summary making exactly the same two points, I went looking for the rest of the argument, and it turns out there is quite a lot more there. There is a passage, in the chapter usually rendered as "The Great and Venerable Teacher," where a dying man describes the universe as a kind of "Great Clod" that loads him with a body, wears him out with living, eases him in old age, and finally lets him rest in death -- right before he jokes that if his arm turns into a rooster he will use it to crow at dawn, and if his backside becomes a wheel and his spirit a horse, he will never need to buy a carriage again. There is also a much stranger scene later in the book where Zhuangzi comes across an old skull lying by the road, interrogates it about how it ended up there, uses it as a pillow, and that night dreams that it tells him being dead is more pleasant than being a king.

I would like you to write a short story of roughly 900 to 1400 words that takes the actual philosophical content of one or more of those less-quoted passages -- not the butterfly, and not the wife's funeral, please, I think I've read enough retellings of those two -- and dramatizes it in a setting of your own choosing, as long as it is not ancient China. I am not looking for a generic story about "coming to terms with mortality"; I want the specific moves Zhuangzi is making to be doing real work in the plot -- for instance, the idea that dread of death is really just a kind of provincialism about one's current vantage point, the refusal to assume that the living are in any position to know better than the dead, or the picture of the self as one temporary shape taken by a single ongoing process of transformation rather than a fixed thing that gets destroyed. Ideally none of this should be delivered as a lecture by some wise character; it should be doing its work in how the story is built and in what happens to whoever is at the center of it. If you can also carry over some of the original's tone, which struck me as considerably more playful, ironic, and comfortable leaving things unresolved than most Western writing about death, rather than tying everything up into a tidy moral, I would count that as a success.
