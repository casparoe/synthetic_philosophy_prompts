I spent nearly forty years as a physiologist studying homeostatic regulation, and since retiring I have been working my way through the bioethics literature on how death itself gets defined -- a question I once took entirely for granted. One dispute keeps pulling me back in: whether the destruction of the whole brain is itself the organism's death, or whether death properly occurs only with the permanent loss of circulatory and respiratory function. Please write a dialogue, set at the bedside of a ventilated patient who has just been declared whole-brain dead and is awaiting organ procurement, between two equally well-informed bioethicists who take opposing sides of this dispute. The first should defend whole-brain death using its strongest available rationale, such as some version of the "integrative unity" argument or one of its more recent successors. The second should press the Shewmon-style empirical challenge -- that ventilator-supported "brain-dead" bodies can go on regulating their temperature, healing wounds, resisting infection, and occasionally sustaining a pregnancy for weeks or months -- and should argue that this forces the first speaker to trade a genuinely biological criterion for a covert higher-brain or personhood criterion. Have the first speaker revise their rationale at least once under this pressure, and have the second test whether the revision still avoids implying that patients in a persistent vegetative state are already dead. Don't let the two resolve the disagreement artificially; end where the real disagreement bites.
