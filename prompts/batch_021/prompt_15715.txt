I am several drafts into a novel about an art forger -- there is more than a little Han van Meegeren in him -- and background reading has pulled me into Nelson Goodman's distinction, in Languages of Art, between autographic and allographic arts: a painting is autographic, its identity tied to the artist's own act of production, so that even an undetectable copy still counts as a forgery, while a novel is allographic, identified only by compliance with a notational scheme -- correct spelling, in Goodman's own phrase -- so that any faithful copy is as genuine an instance of the work as any other. Two things about this deserve more than the capsule summary I keep running into. First, walk me through Goodman's own argument for why history of production keeps mattering aesthetically even against a hypothetically perfect, permanently undetectable forgery -- I understand it leans on some claim about the future discriminability of copy from original, but I have never seen the actual steps, nor the case for preferring it to Alfred Lessing's rival view that the wrong in forgery is simply the forger's stolen credit for an achievement, with no bearing on any property of the object itself. Second, since the novel is allographic on Goodman's own scheme, there seems to be no real sense left in which a text can be forged the way a painting can, and yet we ordinarily treat a fabricated manuscript of a genuine novel, an invented story falsely attributed to a dead author, and plain plagiarism as versions of one offense. Reasoning first from Goodman's terms and then from Lessing's, tell me whether those three really share a single kind of wrong, or whether the autographic/allographic line shows that they do not.
