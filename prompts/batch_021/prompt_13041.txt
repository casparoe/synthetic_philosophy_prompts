I retired several years ago after a long career in experimental condensed-matter physics, and I have lately been filling the time with a long-deferred interest in philosophy, mainly through a small reading group that meets monthly at our local library. We recently spent a session on Elizabeth Anscombe, and I was struck by how different her 1956 pamphlet opposing Oxford's decision to award Harry Truman an honorary degree is from the way I had always heard the ethics of Hiroshima discussed among physicists themselves. The physicists who debated the bomb beforehand -- I have in mind things like the Franck Report and Szilard's petition to Truman -- argued almost entirely in the idiom of consequences: whether a demonstration in an uninhabited area would end the war as quickly as a strike on a city, whether a surprise attack on a population center would save more lives on net than it cost. Anscombe will have none of this. Her case turns on the claim that the bombing intentionally killed civilians as a means of breaking Japan's will to fight, rather than merely foreseeing their deaths as a side effect of striking some legitimate military target, and that this is what makes it murder, irrespective of how many lives the bombing may in fact have saved.

For our next meeting I have sketched a small argument that I would value your comments on before I put it in front of the group. My thought is this: the fact that even the physicists' own consequence-driven debate kept circling back to essentially the same question that occupies Anscombe -- was a given target chosen partly for its demoralizing effect on a civilian population, as opposed to its narrowly military value? -- suggests that her intended/foreseen distinction is tracking something real, rather than being a philosopher's convenient bit of hair-splitting invented after the fact to reach a predetermined verdict. But I suspect this may be too quick, since the reading group has also introduced me to the "closeness" objection to the doctrine of double effect: the worry that any act can be redescribed so that a given harm looks either "intended as a means" or "merely foreseen," depending on how finely one carves up the description of what was done, which would make the distinction unable to do the settling work Anscombe needs from it. I would be grateful if you could tell me, first, whether I have represented Anscombe's actual argument accurately; second, whether the parallel I want to draw with the physicists' target-selection debates is really doing philosophical work or whether it quietly conflates strategic reasoning about effectiveness with her quite different claims about intention; and third, whether the closeness problem is as damaging to her position in this particular case as I have come to fear, or whether double-effect theorists have a standard way of blocking it here.
