While reporting on a small workshop on multi-agent AI safety, I watched two researchers spend a coffee break arguing about "program equilibrium" -- the idea, going back to Moshe Tennenholtz, that letting two agents in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma condition their move on each other's actual source code, rather than on a fixed action, can make mutual cooperation a Nash equilibrium of the resulting program game. They were arguing specifically about the best-known formal version of this, built from agents that reason about each other via provability logic: FairBot cooperates with an opponent just when it can prove that opponent cooperates with it, while PrudentBot cooperates with an opponent just when it can prove that opponent cooperates with it and can also prove that the opponent does not cooperate with DefectBot. (DefectBot defects unconditionally; CooperateBot, for reference, cooperates unconditionally.)

Here's roughly how the exchange went between the two of them, whom I'll call A and B, paraphrased:

(1) A: The whole framework oversells itself. Mutual DefectBot is just as much a program equilibrium as mutual FairBot, so all this machinery does is relocate the equilibrium-selection problem, not solve it.

(2) B: True, but beside the point. In the classical one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, defection isn't merely an equilibrium, it's the unique rational outcome -- so showing that cooperation can be stable and reachable at all is real progress, whatever else also happens to be an equilibrium.

(3) A: Even the cooperative result is less impressive up close. FairBot cooperates with CooperateBot, a program that cooperates with literally anything, leaving free utility on the table. PrudentBot gets the same mutual cooperation with FairBot and with itself while defecting against CooperateBot -- so FairBot simply isn't the rational program to run.

(4) B: "Does at least as well against everything" only holds against the fixed cast of bots the papers happen to compare. An opponent that conditions its own cooperation on whether you'd exploit a naive cooperator will defect against PrudentBot's opportunism while still cooperating with FairBot. There's no dominant strategy once the roster of opponents isn't fixed in advance.

(5) A: That concedes my point. If a bot's ranking depends entirely on which other programs happen to be in the room, calling any of them "the rational way to cooperate" oversells a result that really just hands you a menu of equilibria.

(6) B: That's not a failure, it's just what rationality looks like once you admit it's dispositional and relational rather than context-free -- no more damning than the fact that the rational price to pay for something depends on the market you're in.
