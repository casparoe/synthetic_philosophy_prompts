Our student council has spent two meetings arguing over whether to replace plurality voting (most votes wins, majority or not) for student body president, after a race last spring where two candidates with nearly identical platforms split the vote and a third candidate won despite being almost nobody's first choice. At the most recent meeting, two members, Theo and Nadia, had the following exchange while the rest of us just listened:

(1) Theo argued we should switch to ranked-choice (instant-runoff) voting, since it eliminates candidates one at a time and transfers their votes until someone ends up with a majority of what's left.

(2) Nadia said that doesn't actually make it fair: she'd read that Arrow's impossibility theorem proves no ranked voting method, with three or more candidates, can satisfy a handful of genuinely reasonable fairness conditions all at once, so ranked-choice is guaranteed to fail somewhere too.

(3) Theo replied that this sounded like a contrived mathematical curiosity, not something that would really come up in a race with three or four actual candidates.

(4) Nadia said it isn't hypothetical: ranked-choice can eliminate the one candidate who would have beaten every other candidate head-to-head, purely for lacking enough first-choice votes early on — and, if she's remembering the article right, that's essentially what happened in Burlington, Vermont's 2009 mayoral election, part of why the city later repealed it.

(5) Theo then proposed switching straight to a "Condorcet method," built specifically to elect whoever wins every head-to-head matchup, which seemed to directly fix Nadia's objection.

(6) Nadia answered that this doesn't get them out of it either, since preferences can cycle so that no candidate beats everyone else at all; any Condorcet method still needs its own tie-breaking rule for that case, and Arrow's theorem guarantees that whichever rule gets built in will itself violate one of those same conditions for some possible set of ballots.

(7) Theo, sounding a bit defeated, concluded that if literally no system can ever be made fair, there's no principled reason to prefer any reform over plurality, so the council might as well keep what it has.

(8) Nadia disagreed with that last step specifically: "no system is perfect" isn't the same claim as "all systems are equally flawed," she said, adding that some non-ranked systems, like approval voting, aren't even covered by Arrow's theorem to begin with, though she admitted she wasn't sure that actually got them off the hook.

Could you go through Theo and Nadia's exchange move by move, (1) through (8), and say who has the stronger position at each step and why, including anywhere you think they're both missing something important? Please close by addressing (8) head-on: is Nadia right that approval voting escapes Arrow's theorem, and if so, does that actually help a group like ours trying to pick a fairer system?
