In my philosophy of biology seminar this term, we've spent the last couple of weeks on the species problem, and what's been sticking with me is how it stops looking like a mere definitional headache once you consider ring species. My professor's example was a chain of neighboring populations, each able to interbreed with the next, that wraps around some geographic barrier until the two ends meet again -- at which point the terminal populations can no longer interbreed with one another. Under the biological species concept this seems to produce an outright contradiction rather than just a fuzzy boundary: "can interbreed with" behaves as though it ought to be transitive, so the whole connected chain should count as one species, yet at the closure point the very same lineage seems to be simultaneously one species and two. It's less that we lack enough data to classify these organisms correctly, and more that the concept of a species itself seems to buckle under the case.

I'd like you to write a short story (roughly 1000-1500 words) that dramatizes this problem rather than explaining it -- something closer in spirit to how a novel might dramatize the ship-of-Theseus puzzle through a character's situation than to an essay that states the paradox outright. Center it on a field biologist, graduate student, or museum taxonomist working through a real or invented ring-species case -- salamanders circling a valley or warblers circling a plateau, along the lines of the real Ensatina or greenish warbler cases, or a scenario entirely of your own devising -- who is forced by some concrete professional stake, such as a conservation listing, a dissertation chapter, or a legal dispute over a protected population, to actually commit to an answer about where the species boundary lies. Please don't have a character simply lecture about the philosophy out loud; let the tension between what the data show and the need to draw a line emerge through the plot and the character's choices, and feel free to end on an unresolved or ambiguous note rather than neatly settling whether species carve nature at its joints or are just a useful fiction we impose on a continuum.
