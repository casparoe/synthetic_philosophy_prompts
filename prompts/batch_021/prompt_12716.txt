I was at a dinner party last week and got roped into refereeing an argument between two writer friends that I can't stop thinking about. Dan had just turned in a big nonfiction manuscript and said, kind of offhand, "I believe every fact in this book, but with a few thousand claims in it, I'd bet money at least one is wrong somewhere." (1) Noor said thats incoherent -- you can't believe every fact individually yet also believe the book as a whole has a mistake; if he can't say which claim he doubts, he's treating each one as certain, and a few thousand certains should add up to certainty, not doubt -- he's helping himself to a doubt he won't localize. (2) Dan said that's just ordinary intellectual humility, same as being sure a lottery has a winner without being able to name the ticket beforehand; you don't need to localize a flaw to be justified in believing one exists. (3) Noor said the lottery comparison fails, because a lottery guarantees exactly one winner by design, while Dan's belief that his book contains an error is just an inductive guess based on how often books like it turn out to have mistakes, so its on much shakier ground. (4) Dan replied that this only changes how confident he should be, not the structure of the problem, and that almost everything we believe rests on inductive guesses rather than logical guarantees anyway. Neither budged.

This is basically a real-life version of the preface paradox, and the side argument between them is really about whether the lottery paradox is even a fair comparison to bring in. Can you rate each numbered move from -2 (clearly wrong) to +2 (clearly right) with a quick justification, then say whether the lottery/preface analogy holds up or Noor's disanalogy in move 3 is decisive -- and whether either of them is missing a consideration that actually matters?
