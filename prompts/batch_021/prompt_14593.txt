For my seminar on 19th-century ethics we've spent the last two weeks slowly working through Sidgwick's Methods of Ethics, and this week our professor dropped this aside that Sidgwick was also the founding president of the Society for Psychical Research back in 1882 and spent basically the rest of his life investigating mediums, telepathy experiments, haunted houses, etc, mostly getting null or fraudulent results (like the whole Creery sisters mess) -- which is definately not what I expected from the guy who wrote maybe the most painstaking ethics book of the century. What's been bugging me since is how this squares with the "dualism of practical reason" he agonizes over at the end of the Methods -- basically that egoism and utilitarianism might both be rationally binding even though they can conflict, and that this dualism used to get patched up by religious assumptions about an afterlife that rewards virtue, assumptions he doesn't think he can just help himself to anymore. Half my seminar thinks the psychical research is basically him trying to empirically rebuild that missing premise, so its actually continuous with the argument of the book; the other half thinks that's way too charitable and that it's more of a personal, Victorian-crisis-of-faith type thing that sits pretty awkward next to how careful he is everywhere else.

Instead of just picking whichever side sounds nicer, could you write me a dialogue between two Sidgwick scholars who hold these two opposing readings, where they actually respond to each other's specific arguments instead of just restating their own take, and neither one is obviously winning from the start -- I want both of them genuinely sharp, landing real points against each other.
