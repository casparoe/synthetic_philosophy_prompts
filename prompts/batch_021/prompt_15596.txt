I'm a software engineer who got interested in Gottlob Frege because his 1879 Begriffsschrift is usually credited as the ancestor of the predicate logic used throughout CS. That curiosity led me to learn that Frege kept a private diary in 1924, the year before he died, which surfaced only decades later and turned out to be full of harsh antisemitic and antidemocratic opinions -- he wanted Jews and other groups excluded from political life and had nothing but contempt for Weimar parliamentary democracy.

I mentioned this in a reading group I'm part of, and two people who've actually studied some Frege got into an argument I can't referee myself.

Elena said the diary is simply irrelevant to evaluating his philosophy: the sense/reference distinction, the treatment of quantifiers, the case against psychologism in arithmetic stand or fall on their logical merits, and judging them by his private prejudices is a textbook genetic fallacy -- nobody re-derives Newton's mechanics differently because of his personality.

Marcus agreed that no specific proof becomes invalid, but argued the diary isn't just irrelevant biography: Frege's philosophical writing is shot through with a demand for "purity" -- purging arithmetic of psychological contamination, insisting concepts have perfectly sharp boundaries -- and the diary shows that same purifying, boundary-policing instinct turned on the nation and its citizens. He wasn't claiming the logic is wrong, just that this should change how we read the rhetoric of rigor running through the philosophy.

Elena replied that this proves too little: plenty of equally rigor-obsessed logicians of that generation -- Russell, Hilbert, later Tarski -- wanted exactly the same sharpness and were nothing like Frege politically, so "he valued purity" can't be doing any real explanatory work; it's an unfalsifiable just-so story dressed up as intellectual history.

Marcus came back with the point that he wasn't claiming purity-in-logic caused or entailed the antisemitism, only that knowing about the diary should color how we read specific passages -- his contempt for "the mob," his insistence that real understanding is only for the few -- differently than we otherwise would. That's a claim about interpretation, not validity, so Elena's counterexamples don't really touch it.

Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each point, rather than just who wins overall? Please also flag anything you think both of them are missing.
