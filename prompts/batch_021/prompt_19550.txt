I'm putting together a session on scientific realism and verisimilitude for my graduate philosophy of science seminar, and I keep hitting the same pedagogical wall when we get to Popper's original account. I can present his qualitative definition cleanly enough: theories are ranked by comparing their truth-content and falsity-content classes, and I can walk the class through the Miller–Tichý result from 1974, which shows that on this definition no two theories that both fall short of the whole truth can ever be ranked against one another — the ordering only ever manages to place a fully true theory above a false one, never one false theory above another. What I have not managed to do is give students an intuitive sense of why this had to happen, rather than simply presenting the proof as a clever trick that happens to work.

Could you help me build that intuition? Specifically, I would like an explanation of what it is about defining verisimilitude purely in terms of a theory's deductive consequence classes — as opposed to, say, some notion of similarity or distance to the truth — that makes this sort of collapse more or less inevitable once ordinary classical logical closure is in play. I would also value your own assessment of whether the subsequent move made by Tichý himself, and later by Oddie and by Niiniluoto (who draws on Hintikka's notion of "constituents"), toward likeness-based measures over the space of possible states of affairs genuinely escapes the underlying problem or merely relocates it — for instance, into a dependence of the resulting ordering on the choice of descriptive vocabulary. A colleague of mine thinks this language-dependence worry is fatal to the whole verisimilitude project; I am less certain, but would rather hear your own considered opinion than a diplomatic survey of the positions.
