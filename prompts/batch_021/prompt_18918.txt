I'm a few chapters into a novel narrated partly through the eyes of a government communications director, and while reading Hannah Arendt's essays on political lying for research, I found myself refereeing, without meaning to, an argument between two friends at a literary festival dinner. I was seated between Ines, who teaches political theory and is finishing a book on propaganda, and Callum, who spent a decade as a communications adviser to a health minister before moving into corporate PR. What set them off was a case Callum offered from his own past, lightly disguised: a minister's flagship rural hospital upgrade program was quietly falling behind and running over budget, but the communications director kept telling journalists it was "on track and delivering," and, according to internal memos that later leaked, was still saying so in private to her own staff many months after she had personally been shown figures proving otherwise. It is exactly the kind of scene I want to get right on the page, so I would like the philosophy underneath it settled first.

Here is roughly how the argument went, as best I can reconstruct it. (1) Ines said this was a textbook case of what Arendt means by distinctively modern political lying: the director was not merely hiding one embarrassing fact, she was substituting a whole preferred image of the program for the factual record, which is precisely the more dangerous, wholesale falsification Arendt distinguishes from an old-fashioned, contained lie. (2) Callum objected that if the director had genuinely come to believe her own line by the time she repeated it to her staff, then by Arendt's own criteria she was no longer lying at all. A liar, he said, has to keep the truth in view in order to hide it, and Arendt treats self-deception as a distinct phenomenon rather than simply an advanced stage of lying. (3) Ines answered that this concedes too much. Arendt wrote an entire essay on the Pentagon Papers about officials who lost track of the truth in precisely this way, and never suggests that this lets them out of the category: the essay is called "Lying in Politics," and it covers the self-deceived "problem-solvers" right alongside the deliberate deceivers, because what worried her was the damage done to the shared factual world, not the tidiness of any one person's inner state. (4) Callum replied that this blurs two different questions: whether an act damages the shared world, and whether a given person counts as a liar. Arendt, he said, was if anything careful to keep them apart, since an organized lie can in principle be refuted because someone, somewhere, still remembers the truth being hidden, whereas the self-deceived spin doctor has erased even that fixed point. That, to him, is a difference in kind rather than degree, and arguably makes "liar" the wrong word for her even though the damage she does is worse. (5) Ines then shifted ground, arguing that Arendt's deeper point is that the liar and the sincere-but-mistaken image-maker draw on the very same human capacity: the freedom to act, to introduce into the world a state of affairs that was not simply given. Arendt explicitly ties lying to this freedom, Ines said, so Callum's tidy conceptual line cannot really be doing the work he wants it to do. (6) Callum granted the link Arendt draws between lying and the freedom of action, but said she uses it to explain why lying so tempts people who act in the world, not to dissolve her own distinction between the deliberate liar and the self-deceived. Collapsing the two, he added, hands every dishonest press officer a ready-made excuse: simply claim, after the fact, to have believed it.

Neither of them budged, and both had clearly read the essays more recently than I have.
