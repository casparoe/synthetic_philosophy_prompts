And here's the answer from one of the exams (typos theirs, not mine):

"In the Chinese Room, a man who doesn't speak any Chinese sits in a room and recieves batches of Chinese writing through a slot. He has an English rulebook telling him how to match the incoming symbols to outgoing ones, purely by their shape, without ever grasping what any of it means. Since he's just manipulating shapes according to formal rules, he's doing syntax, not semantics. Because a digital computer running a program is doing exactly the same thing the man is -- just shuffling symbols per formal rules -- Searle concludes that no program, however sophisticated, could ever produce responses good enough to actually fool a native Chinese speaker; the appearance of fluency would always give itself away as mechanical. This is how he argues that syntax isn't sufficient for semantics, and that strong AI -- the claim that a suitably programmed computer would thereby have real understanding -- is false. The Systems Reply objects that it's unfair to ask whether the man understands Chinese, since the real question is whether the whole system (man plus rulebook plus paper) understands. Searle actually accepts this move: he agrees that although the man doesn't understand Chinese, the system as a whole does, since understanding can be a property of a whole system without being a property of any of its parts individually. I think that's the right place for him to land, since it lets him keep the intuition that the room produces intelligent-seeming output while still leaving room for a further story about how semantics emerges from syntax."
