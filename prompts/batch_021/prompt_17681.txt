I'm taking a seminar this term on practical reason that has spent several weeks on Ruth Chang's work on hard choices — in particular her argument that what makes certain choices genuinely hard is not that the alternatives are incommensurable, lacking any common scale, but that they stand in a distinct fourth relation, being "on a par": fully comparable, yet neither better, worse, nor precisely equal. My older brother is getting married in two weeks, and I'm giving the best man speech. He spent nearly three years torn between finishing his PhD in marine biology and taking over our late uncle's boatbuilding workshop, before finally leaving the program, taking the workshop, and getting engaged to the labmate who left with him. Relatives still ask him, half-joking, whether he "wasted" his training, and I don't want to answer that by insisting he actually made the objectively better call — on Chang's view that would misdescribe the situation as badly as calling it a coin flip. I want the speech to use her distinction correctly, including her idea that resolving a hard choice is an act of self-commitment rather than the discovery of a pre-existing right answer, so that it puts the question to rest without turning the toast into a seminar recap. About half the guests are his old lab colleagues who will recognize exactly what I'm doing; the rest are boatyard friends and family who have never taken a philosophy class. Please write the speech.
