I write about science for a living, and after several years spent reporting on the reproducibility crisis in psychology and medicine, a puzzle from inside my own newsroom has started to bother me more than a mere editorial quibble should. We have a house rule that we only state something as flat fact, rather than hedging it with "according to" or "allegedly," once it clears about 99 percent confidence after fact-checking. I'm currently closing a long investigative piece built on roughly 500 individually verified claims — court filings, leaked documents, quotes corroborated against recordings — and every single one clears that bar on its own, so the rule entitles me to assert all 500 outright. And yet, from having covered enough retractions and corrections at outlets far more careful than mine, I know that a project of this size is very unlikely to be perfectly free of errors; if you asked me to bet, I'd put real money on there being a mistake somewhere in the piece. So I seem to believe each of the 500 claims individually while also believing that the piece as a whole probably isn't entirely true — precisely the tension our standard closing line, "any errors that remain are ours alone," is designed to smooth over without anyone examining it too closely.

A colleague on the data desk shrugged this off by comparing it to a 500-entry office raffle: I'm confident my own entry will lose, and equally confident that somebody's entry will win, and nobody finds that pairing of beliefs troubling. The comparison feels close but not quite honest to me, since each of my 500 claims was earned through separate, independent reporting, whereas one raffle entry is interchangeable with any other and backed by nothing except the total number sold. I'd like to know whether my situation and the raffle really are the same problem wearing different clothes, or whether the disanalogy I'm sensing is philosophically real; whether the 99 percent confidence rule is itself what's generating the trouble, rather than any failure of reporting; and whether there's an honest way for a newsroom to keep saying "this piece might contain an error" without that line quietly undercutting every claim the piece makes.
