I'm putting together a small collection of short fiction in which each story is built around a real philosopher's ideas being tested by a single evening of their own life, and I want to write the entry on A. J. Ayer. The obvious material is his brush with death in 1988: while in hospital being treated for pneumonia, he choked on a piece of smoked salmon, went into cardiac arrest, and was without a pulse for several minutes before being revived. He later wrote up the episode himself, describing a painful red light and a sense of being in the presence of beings charged with the administration of space, who seemed to have made rather a hash of the job, and remarked afterward, in characteristically dry fashion, that the experience had somewhat loosened, though not overturned, his conviction that his own death would be the end of him. A friend who teaches a course on philosophy of religion mentioned this to me as the standard cautionary tale trotted out whenever near-death experiences come up in discussions of verificationism, and it has stuck with me since, mostly because it seems so hard to write well: play it as straightforward comeuppance for the great debunker and it's cheap, play it as secret vindication of an afterlife and it's dishonest to what actually happened.

What I want to draw on, though, is more specific than "he didn't believe in ghosts." Ayer's verification principle holds that a sentence has genuine cognitive content only if some possible observation would count for or against it; this is why he treated talk of a soul surviving the body not so much as false as strictly unclear (on his view, we don't even know what would make it true), and why he was equally insistent that whatever we mean by "the same person" has to be cashed out in terms of some continuity that bodily death simply removes. I'd like a story, close third person, somewhere in the 1200-1800 word range, set in the hours just after such a man is revived, in which that specific apparatus - not generic empiricist skepticism, but the actual shape of the verification principle and his views on personal identity - is what's genuinely at stake for him as he tries to decide how to describe, to a nurse, a former student, or just to himself, what has just happened. Please don't let characters simply recite arguments at each other; the ideas should surface through gesture, interior monologue, and the small comedy of other people's reactions rather than through lecture. Fictionalize freely - a different name, an invented visitor, whatever serves the story - as long as the philosophical commitments underneath remain recognizably his, and aim for an ending whose ambiguity is earned philosophically rather than merely emotionally, so that a reader who knows Ayer's actual arguments could say exactly which of his commitments are, and are not, threatened by what he has just been through.
