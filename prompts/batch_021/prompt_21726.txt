Draft is below:

Quick recap in case you don't know it: there's two urns, each with 100 balls. Urn A has exactly 50 red and 50 black, you know this for a fact. Urn B also has 100 red-or-black balls but you have no idea what the split is, could be 90-10, could be 1-99, whatever. You win $100 if you draw the color you bet on. Almost everybody, given the choice, bets red on urn A over red on urn B, AND ALSO bets black on urn A over black on urn B. That's the paradox part: there's no probability you can privately assign to urn B that makes both of those choices consistent with expected-utility maximization. Preferring red-A over red-B means you must think red-in-B is less likely than 50%. But urn B only has two colors, so if red-in-B is under 50%, black-in-B has to be over 50% — which should make black-in-B the more attractive bet, not less. Yet people prefer black-in-A too. There's no single probability split for urn B that fits both preferences at once; people's actual choices just don't correspond to any coherent probability estimate.

My take: this is basically the aleatoric vs epistemic uncertainty split we use in ML. Urn A is pure aleatoric risk, the randomness is real but the generating process is fully known and won't budge. Urn B is epistemic uncertainty about the process itself, like a model that hasn't seen enough data yet. And I don't think it's irrational to treat "I don't know the process" as worse than "I know the process is a coinflip" - if you don't know the process, you can't rule out that its stacked against you somehow. It's less "violating the axioms of rationality" and more doing a minimax move: you're optimizing against the worst distribution consistent with what you know, instead of against one best-guess distribution like standard expected utility theory assumes. I looked it up after writing this part and apparently this is pretty much what Gilboa and Schmeidler's "maxmin expected utility" formalizes back in the 80s, so I'm probably not the first to notice, but nobody explained it to me in ML terms before and it just clicked.

The wrinkle: I vaguely remember reading that this ambiguity aversion flips into ambiguity-SEEKING once the same bets get reframed as losses instead of gains, like people start preferring the ambiguous urn when the "prize" is avoiding a loss. If my minimax story is right that shouldn't happen — the worst case in the ambiguous urn is still the worst case whether you dress the outcome up as a gain or a loss, so a true minimaxer should never flip. So either my whole "it's just robust control" story is too simple, or I'm misremembering the loss-framing result, or there's some fix to the minimax story that handles this and I'm just not seeing it. Not sure which, and it's kind of bugging me, would love your take.
