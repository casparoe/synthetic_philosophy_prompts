I co-host a small podcast that grew out of a local rationalist meetup; we mostly interview researchers whose work gets discussed on LessWrong and the Alignment Forum but rarely gets a plain-language treatment anywhere else. Next week's guest is a game theorist who works on "safe Pareto improvements": the idea that when two principals delegate play of some game to agents acting on their behalf, the principals can sometimes agree beforehand to modify the game the delegates will actually face, such that whichever reasonable strategy the delegates end up using, both principals come out at least as well off as under some baseline — without either side having to trust that the delegates will coordinate on any particular efficient equilibrium. A few of us at the meetup have spent the last couple of sessions arguing about whether this framework is doing more work than ordinary bargaining-and-renegotiation theory, or whether "safe" is quietly smuggling back in the same trust assumptions it claims to avoid, just one level removed.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that don't let the guest get away with a bare restatement of the definition? I'd like at least one question that forces a comparison with the more familiar case of a trusted mediator implementing a correlated equilibrium, so we can pin down exactly what the "safety" is safe against, if not the same coordination failures a mediator already handles. I'd like one question on the tradeoff between how permissive an assumption you make about delegate rationality — must the delegates play a Nash equilibrium of the modified game, or merely avoid strategies that are strictly dominated? — and how large a Pareto improvement can still be guaranteed once that assumption is weakened. I'd like one question connecting the framework to the "commitment races" worry, asking whether adopting safe Pareto improvements actually removes an AI system's incentive to commit early and unilaterally, or just displaces the race onto the question of who gets to propose the modification first. And I'd like one question that uses surrogate goals — adopting a substitute target for threats to land on — as a worked example, pressing on what stops the surrogate itself from becoming the thing that gets threatened once it's common knowledge that it's a surrogate. For each of these four, add a short follow-up question keyed to the hedge or deflection you'd expect the guest to reach for first. Close the sequence with one question asking the guest to describe a concrete bargaining situation in which no nontrivial safe Pareto improvement exists at all, so the episode doesn't leave listeners thinking the technique is available for free in every negotiation.
