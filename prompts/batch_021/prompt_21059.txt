I work as a software engineer, and I spend a fair amount of my free time reading popular books on evolutionary biology, partly because I've experimented with genetic algorithms at my job and got curious about the real theory behind the metaphor. A few months ago I read Richard Dawkins' "The Selfish Gene," where he argues that the gene, rather than the individual organism or the group, is the fundamental unit on which natural selection acts, and where he's fairly dismissive of "group selection" as a conceptually sloppy holdover from before biologists reasoned carefully about the underlying math. I came away thinking this was a more or less closed question, decisively settled in the gene's favor sometime in the 1970s.

Recently, though, a friend finishing a biology PhD told me it isn't so settled after all. She mentioned that David Sloan Wilson has spent decades rehabilitating a version of group selection under the name "multilevel selection," and, more surprisingly to me, that E. O. Wilson -- who made his early reputation partly on gene-level, kin-selection accounts of social insects -- reversed himself later in his career and co-authored a widely discussed paper arguing against kin-selection theory in favor of group-level explanations, prompting a public rebuttal signed by a large number of other evolutionary biologists.

Given that Dawkins' side seemed to me like it had basically won the argument, I'm struggling to make sense of a "defeated" position getting revived decades later by serious researchers, including someone who used to argue the other side. Does that mean Dawkins was simply wrong, or that the field has just been going back and forth on what's ultimately a matter of intellectual fashion rather than settled fact? Or is there a more precise sense in which the two camps aren't actually making competing claims about the same thing, so that asking who "won" is itself the wrong way to frame the situation? I'd like to understand what's really going on before I repeat any confident-sounding version of this story to other people.
