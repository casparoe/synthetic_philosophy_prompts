Ok so this is randomly relevant to my life right now. My great-aunt died a few weeks ago, and cleaning out her house we found letters making it pretty clear her brother (my great-uncle, long dead) had been part of a small resistance cell in occupied Belgium that helped plan and carry out the killing of the regional administrator the occupying government had installed, before most of the cell was caught and executed in '43. It turned into a whole arguement at the funeral lunch between my uncle Mark and my cousin Elena, who are both weirdly into Aquinas (Mark almost went to seminary and still brings it up, Elena did a philosophy minor and won't let anyone forget it).

Mark's position: Aquinas would definately not have approved. In De Regno he's actually really wary of private citizens deciding on their own that a ruler's a tyrant and killing him -- that should go through some public/legitimate authority instead, because ordinary people are bad judges of when a ruler's crossed the line, and letting anyone act on their own judgment invites abuse. A few guys in a barn plotting a hit is about as "private" as it gets.

Elena's comeback: that caution is about a ruler who came to power legitimately and then governs badly. A regime installed by military occupation, with zero consent from anyone it rules over, is a usurper rather than that kind of tyrant, and Aquinas is way more permissive about removing usurpers, since they never had legitimate authority to begin with -- so there's no legitimate authority for the cell to be undermining.

Mark: the usurper category, as he remembers it, is about someone seizing power inside an existing political community (basically a coup), not a foreign occupation, which he thinks is a different question entirely (more just-war territory than sedition) -- so Elena's stretching a category to cover something it wasn't built for.

Elena: fine, maybe it's technically a different category, but the underlying logic -- no legitimate title, no sedition in resisting it -- should apply at least as well to a foreign occupier as to a home-grown usurper, probably better.

Mark's closer: even granting all that, Aquinas also says resistance isn't justified if it causes more disorder than it prevents, and reprisal killings of civilians after this kind of operation were common and predictable back then, so the cell might fail Aquinas's own test regardless of which flavor of tyrant applies.

Nobody backed down and my grandmother got kind of upset. Can you go through this move by move and say who's got Aquinas right at each step, and whether there's something in his actual view that both of them are steamrolling past?
