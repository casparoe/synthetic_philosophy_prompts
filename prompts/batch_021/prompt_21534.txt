Hey, so I'm about two-thirds through drafting my next novel and I've hit a snag that's more philosophical then literary, so bear with me. The book follows two infantrymen on opposite sides of a war (loosely inspired by a real 20th century conflict, though I've fictionalized the countries involved) who don't actually meet each other until the final chapters. One of them, Anton, is fighting for the side that started the war under flimsy, self-serving pretexts, and whose commanders are separately responsible for real atrocities elsewhere in the war; the other, Marek, is defending his own country from that invasion. Both of them are ordinary conscripted teenagers who didn't choose this war, and both stick to the same rules of engagement, and in the confrontation I've drafted, which is meant to be the emotional core of the book, Anton kills two of Marek's friends in a pretty "clean" firefight - no war crimes, just soldiering.

My critique group got into a real argument about this scene last week. Half of them said Anton is basically a murderer even if he personally never does anything atrocious, because the cause he's fighting for is unjust, so he never had the right to kill anyone in the first place. The other half said that's nuts - he's a conscripted 19 year old who'd be shot for desertion, he's following the laws of war exactly like Marek is, and holding individual soldiers morally responsible for the justice of a war they didn't start and can't opt out of seems to prove way too much (it would make basically every ordinary soldier on the losing side of any war of aggression, in all of history, a murderer). I want to actually get this right in how I write Anton rather than just picking whichever answer is more dramatically convenient, and I dont know enough moral philosophy to adjudicate it myself.

So here's my actual question for you: when a soldier is conscripted to fight for a cause that's unjust, and he fights strictly "by the book," is he morally equivalent to a soldier on the just side doing the literal same things on the battlefield, or is there a real moral asymmetry between them even though their individual conduct is identical? I'd love the strongest version of both sides of this, plus which one you actually find more persuasive and why, since I want Anton to be a genuinely three dimensional character rather than a strawman for whichever position turns out to be wrong.
