I've been reading LW/ACX-adjacent stuff for like a decade now but always skated past the actual math-y epistemology posts, until a reading group I'm in started slow-crawling through material on Jeffrey conditionalization (it's basically a LW-meetup offshoot, we got into it after someone got obsessed with Abram Demski's radical-probabilism posts). Last week one member mentioned she'd taken a home pregnancy test with a very faint second line -- the kind where you genuinely can't tell if it's there or your just pattern-matching on nothing -- and was thinking out loud about how much to update on it. That set off an actual argument between two other regulars, Dev and Sasha, that ran long enough I wrote most of it down afterward. Neither of them is a professional philosopher (Dev does something in ML, Sasha's a lawyer) but they clearly both know the material cold.

(1) Dev's opening move was that you don't need Jeffrey conditionalization for this at all -- just let E be "I am having exactly this visual experience of a faint pinkish line," which you ARE certain of, and run ordinary strict conditionalization on that instead. Jeffrey's whole apparatus for updating on uncertain propositions is solving a problem that dissolves once you push things down to the level of raw experience. (2) Sasha said that move doesn't stop the regress, it just relocates it: you're not actually certain you're having "exactly" that experience either, since introspecting your own perceptual state is fallible and shifts the longer you stare at the test, so you'd need a still-more-certain proposition underneath that one, and so on -- which is precisely why Jeffrey invented probability kinematics instead of just adding one more layer of conditioning. (3) Dev conceded that point but came back with a more technical objection: Jeffrey conditionalization is provably non-commutative, meaning updating on E and then F can leave you with a different final credence than updating on F and then E, and he said that's a real mark against calling it rational updating, since the order you happen to learn two things in shouldn't affect what you believe once you've learned both. (4) Sasha's response was that the non-commutativity is a known, already-discussed result (she brought up Diaconis and Zabell) rather than a defect: strict conditionalization only looks order-independent because it smuggles in certainty as an idealization, and once updates are allowed to fall short of certainty, which conditional probabilities you're even entitled to hold fixed depends on what you've already learned -- so some order-sensitivity is just what honest partial updating looks like, not something to be embarrassed about.
