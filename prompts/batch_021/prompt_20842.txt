Question: "Frederick Douglass is often remembered simply as a fierce critic of slavery, but his view of the U.S. Constitution itself changed dramatically over the course of his career. Describe the two main positions Douglass held regarding whether the Constitution was a pro-slavery or an anti-slavery document, explain what specifically caused him to move from the first position to the second, and cite at least one piece of textual evidence he pointed to for his later view."

Jake's answer: "Frederick Douglass didn't always think about the Constitution the same way. When he first became famous as an abolitionist speaker in the early 1840s, he was working closely with William Lloyd Garrison, and he agreed with the Garrisonian position that the Constitution was fundamentally a pro-slavery document. Garrison called it 'a covenant with death and an agreement with hell,' and Douglass used similar language himself, because the Constitution protected slavery through things like the three-fifths clause and the fugitive slave clause, and because it let the slave trade continue until 1808. Garrisonians thought the North should actually leave the Union entirely rather than stay tied to a document that supported slavery, and Douglass agreed with this for years.

Then Douglass changed his mind completely, and it caused a permanent split between him and Garrison. The final straw was the Supreme Court's 1857 Dred Scott decision, which is when Douglass first started arguing that if you look at the actual text of the Constitution, the words 'slave' and 'slavery' never appear anywhere in it, so it can't really be a pro-slavery document on its own terms — any pro-slavery meaning has to be read into it, not found in it. Once he accepted that, he started calling the Constitution, in his own words, a 'glorious liberty document,' and said it should be interpreted according to its stated purpose in the Preamble of securing liberty and justice, not according to whatever people assumed the framers secretly meant.

Douglass's shift is basically a case study in the difference between reading a legal document literally versus reading it based on assumptions about what its authors wanted, and it shows how the exact same text could be used to argue for totally opposite conclusions about slavery."
