I'm at work on a historical novel, and in preparation I've been rereading Tolstoy's War and Peace, partly for the philosophical epilogue where he argues that 'great men' like Napoleon don't really cause historical events in any meaningful sense. Over dinner recently this turned into a real argument between two friends of mine, Sabine (a historian) and Theo (also a novelist), and I've been turning it over ever since.

Sabine took Tolstoy's side: calling the invasion of Russia an expression of 'Napoleon's will' is a convenient fiction, since the campaign's actual course -- the delays, the looting, the disastrous retreat -- was the product of millions of individual choices that Napoleon neither intended nor controlled; he's just the name historians hang the story on. Theo objected that this proves too much, since any event is the sum of finer-grained causes and we don't conclude from that that no one ever causes anything; Napoleon's position at the top of the chain of command gave his choices disproportionate leverage, whatever the execution beneath him looked like. He also offered a counterfactual test: if Napoleon had died in 1805, it's doubtful that whichever marshal inherited his army would have pushed on to Moscow all the same in 1812.

Sabine replied that we overrate that kind of leverage in hindsight, since the historical record preserves mainly the orders that happened to be obeyed and to 'work,' while the far larger number that circumstance simply ignored leaves no trace -- the sense that great men steer events is a retrospective illusion, not a fact about their causal weight at the time. She doubted the counterfactual too, since France and Russia were arguably on a collision course regardless of who led France, given how much strain Napoleon's blockade against Britain was already putting on relations between the two empires. Theo granted the hindsight point as a caution about assigning credit, but not as support for Tolstoy's stronger claim that individual will is basically epiphenomenal in history -- and he pressed further: if Napoleon merely channeled impersonal forces, in what sense is he responsible for what happened to his own army, or to Russia? Sabine answered that it needn't undercut responsibility at all, since we can hold someone answerable for their choices as expressions of character without crediting those same choices as the primary cause of a continent-wide catastrophe -- moral responsibility for choosing and causal responsibility for an outcome of that scale, she said, are simply different questions, and Tolstoy, on her reading, only ever denied the second.

Neither of them budged, and it matters to me beyond curiosity, since it bears on how much causal weight I let my own protagonist carry against the historical backdrop of my book. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each stage, rather than just declaring an overall winner, and flag anything important that both of them are overlooking?
