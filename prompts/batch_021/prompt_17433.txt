I'm taking a philosophy of biology seminar this term, and this week's unit covers Amotz Zahavi's handicap principle and its extensions to human behavior -- which is why a disagreement I overheard over the weekend felt oddly relevant to the reading. At my cousin's engagement party, two of the groom's friends got into an increasingly serious argument about whether the ring he'd bought, clearly well beyond what he could comfortably afford, was a rational thing to do. I ended up transcribing the exchange in my notes app afterward because I wanted to bring it to section, but I'd rather get an outside philosophical opinion on it first.

(1) Friend A opened by calling the purchase irrational: the money has negative expected value for the couple's actual welfare, since it could have gone toward rent or the honeymoon instead, and the ring is basically theater with no practical return.

(2) Friend B said this misses the whole point: the "theater" is doing real work. On Zahavi's account, she said, a costly, seemingly wasteful ornament -- like a peacock's tail -- is worth having precisely because it's wasteful, since only someone with real resources and real intent can afford to squander that much; the expense is what makes the gesture a credible signal of commitment rather than empty talk.

(3) Friend A replied that a genuine handicap has to be differentially costly -- harder for a low-quality signaler to fake than for a high-quality one, the way a heavy tail burdens a sickly bird more than a healthy one. A diamond costs the same number of dollars no matter who's buying it, and anyone, sincere or not, can put it on a credit card, so the purchase doesn't actually discriminate between a devoted partner and a manipulative one. It's just expensive, not a handicap in Zahavi's technical sense, and calling it one is a category error.

(4) Friend B answered that debt isn't evenly costly at all: overspending is a much bigger gamble for someone who doesn't actually have the follow-through to make the relationship last, since for them the expense is far more likely to end in financial default or in a breakup that leaves the cost stranded with nothing to show for it. That asymmetric risk, she said, is quietly doing the condition-dependent work that the sticker price alone doesn't.

(5) Friend A's last word was that this just relocates the signal from the ring to whatever gives someone the safety net to spend without much risk -- savings, family money, good credit -- so what's actually being tracked is financial security, not devotion, and a genuinely devoted partner without that cushion ends up looking, unfairly, like the less committed one. He said that's the exact bait-and-switch people fall for when "costly signaling" quietly becomes "expensive is honest," which isn't the same claim at all.

Neither of them budged before the toasts started, and I didn't want to referee on the spot. Could you go through the exchange move by move? I'd like a verdict on each numbered point specifically, not just an overall winner -- ideally a short JSON object scoring moves 1 through 5 from -2 (clearly mistaken) to 2 (clearly right), followed by a couple of sentences justifying each score, plus a note on anything about the handicap principle itself that both of them seem to be overlooking.
