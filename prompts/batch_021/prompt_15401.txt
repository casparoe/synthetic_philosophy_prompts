I spent the better part of four decades as a reactor physicist, working latterly on fuel-cladding integrity and radiation-damage modeling for a national laboratory, and it is only since retiring two years ago that I have had the leisure to read philosophy properly rather than in stray evenings. Perhaps because of that career, I have gravitated toward the ethics of nuclear weapons in particular, and I am currently a few weeks into a continuing-education seminar on the ethics of war offered online through a university philosophy department. The course runs on a peer-marking system: after we submit our own short written answers each week, we are each assigned a classmate's answer to mark against a rubric the instructor circulates, and the instructor later spot-checks a sample of our marks against her own. Before I commit myself to a mark, I would like an independent, disinterested opinion on the answer I have been assigned, since I am not yet confident I would catch every error a trained philosopher would catch.

This week's question, worth 30 points, reads:

"In 1956, G.E.M. Anscombe circulated the pamphlet 'Mr. Truman's Degree,' opposing Oxford's decision to award former President Harry Truman an honorary doctorate. In 400-600 words, reconstruct the structure of her objection to the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. Your answer should (a) state the moral principle underlying her objection and explain why she thinks the case cannot be settled by weighing consequences; (b) explain the doctrine of double effect and why Anscombe denies that it can be invoked to justify the bombings; (c) explain how her position differs from pacifism; and (d) situate the pamphlet in relation to her later critique of consequentialism in 'Modern Moral Philosophy' (1958)."

And here is the answer I have been asked to mark:

"Anscombe's pamphlet is a furious response to what she saw as Oxford's complicity in honoring a man she regarded as a mass murderer. Her central principle is that choosing to kill innocent people as a means to some further end is murder, and that this judgment does not change no matter how good the further end is or how many lives are supposedly saved on net. On her view, the fact that the bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki probably shortened the war and saved American, and perhaps even Japanese, lives in the long run is simply irrelevant to whether Truman committed murder in ordering it; to think otherwise is to accept a kind of moral reasoning on which literally anything, including the judicial killing of an innocent person, could be justified by a sufficiently favorable balance of consequences.

This is where the doctrine of double effect comes in. Anscombe originated this doctrine to distinguish between effects that are intended, either as ends or as means, and effects that are merely foreseen as side effects of what one does. On this view, it can sometimes be permissible to perform an action that you know will cause the deaths of innocent people, provided that you do not intend their deaths, that their deaths are not your means to your goal, and that the harm is proportionate to the good you are pursuing. Anscombe denies that the bombings can be defended along these lines, because the raids were not aimed at military installations with civilian deaths as an unfortunate side effect: the whole point of bombing the two cities was to kill and terrorize the civilian population directly, so as to break Japan's will to keep fighting. The civilian deaths were the means, not merely a foreseen side effect, so double-effect reasoning simply does not apply.

It is sometimes said that Anscombe was not a pacifist, and in a narrow sense that is right: she does not condemn all killing in warfare whatsoever. But the objection to the bombing in this pamphlet is, at bottom, a pacifist's objection: the Japanese civilians were wronged because their deaths were used as an instrument of policy, not principally because of where they fell relative to the line between combatants and non-combatants. On this reading, the distinctively just-war apparatus of proportionality and discrimination is not doing much of the argumentative work for Anscombe; the crucial premise is simply that using someone's death as a lever for your own purposes is murder, independent of who that person is.

Finally, the episode anticipates the argument of her later, more famous essay 'Modern Moral Philosophy' (1958), in which she coins the term 'consequentialism' for exactly the kind of thinking she is attacking in the Truman pamphlet: the view that no act is intrinsically forbidden and that its rightness is settled entirely by its consequences. The Truman pamphlet can then be read as an early, applied instance of the complaint she would later make at the level of moral theory in general."
