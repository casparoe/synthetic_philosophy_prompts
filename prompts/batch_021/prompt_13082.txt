I spent my working life as an analytical chemist and only got serious about philosophy after I retired a couple of years back -- better late than never. My reading group (mostly other retired scientists, plus one very patient retired English teacher who keeps us honest about the texts) has been going through the classics in roughly historical order, and we just got through the bits of Aristotle's Physics where he takes on Zeno. His way out, if I've got it right, turns on the distinction between a "potential" infinity (a line you can always divide further, a count you can always add one more to) and an "actual" infinity (a completed, all-at-once totality of infinitely many things), the latter of which he thinks just can't exist in nature. On his view, Zeno's runner never actually completes infinitely many sub-tasks -- he's only ever potentially subdividing a finite stretch over and over, so the paradox never really gets off the ground.

Next week the group is doing Thomsons lamp, and one member who read ahead described it to me like this: you switch a lamp on, then off a minute later, on again after another half-minute, off after another quarter-minute, and so on forever, so that after two minutes total the switch has been flipped infinitely many times -- is the lamp then on or off? Thomson's own answer, apparantly, is that it can't coherently be either, so the whole "supertask" must be impossible to begin with. Someone also mentioned that a later philosopher, Benacerraf, pushed back and said Thomson had only shown that the switching rule doesn't fix a final state, not that completing the process itself is impossible, so for all Thomson actually proved, the lamp might still end up on or off for reasons the rule just doesn't capture. (I might be mangling that last part, it was relayed to me secondhand over coffee.) What I'd really like help thinking through before next week is where Aristotle himself, with his actual/potential infinity distinction, would come down on all this: does his machinery let him dodge Thomson's puzzle the same way it let him dodge Zeno's, does it actually commit him to Thomson's own contradiction, or does it land him somewhere closer to Benacerraf, or somewhere different from all three?
