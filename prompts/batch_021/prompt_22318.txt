I'm a PhD student in history and philosophy of science (HPS) and this whole thing kicked off in our philosophy of biology reading group a couple weeks ago, when we finally got to the Popper material that's on my quals list. Two people in the group, Malik and Corinne, ended up arguing about it over email afterward and its still bugging me because I genuinely can't tell who's right, or if they're both partly right.

(1) Malik's opening move was that Popper spent decades — Objective Knowledge, Unended Quest, the essay in the Schilpp volume — insisting that the theory of natural selection is "almost tautological" and "a metaphysical research programme" rather than a genuinely testable scientific theory, so treating him as some late-career convert to orthodox Darwinism is a misreading. (2) Corinne's reply was the 1978 Dialectica lecture, "Natural Selection and the Emergence of Mind," where Popper says outright that he's changed his mind about "the testability and the logical status of the theory of natural selection" and is glad to "make a recantation" — so, she said, quoting the old tautology line as his considered view is just out of date, full stop, that's the whole point of a recantation. (3) Malik's comeback was that she's being too generous: in that exact same lecture, right after recanting, Popper adds that he "still believe[s] that natural selection works in this way as a research programme," and the version he says is now testable is one he's had to modify; and by the 1986 Medawar lecture at the Royal Society he's still denying that "blind" chance and selection are creative on their own, insisting organisms' own problem-solving activity has to do real work too — so on Malik's view the recantation was narrow, and Popper's underlying discomfort with textbook neo-Darwinism never really went away, it just relocated. (4) Corinne's comeback was that Malik is stacking two unrelated complaints to avoid conceding the point: the testability charge is exactly what he gave up in '78, full stop, while the "organisms as problem-solvers" stuff isn't some face-saving addition from later in life — it's the same evolutionary epistemology (the trial-and-error, problem-shift picture) he'd been running since the 60s, totally independent of whether selection is falsifiable — so its reappearing in '86 doesn't show the recantation was narrow, it just shows he always had two separate things going on, and only one of them is the one he actually recanted.

Neither of them budged and the thread just died. Could you go through this move by move (1 through 4) and say who has the better of it at each one, rather than just declaring an overall winner? And since I can't shake the feeling they're both onto something real, I'd also love your best stab at stating what Popper's actual, considered position on Darwinism ended up being — one that actually respects both of their points instead of just picking a side.
