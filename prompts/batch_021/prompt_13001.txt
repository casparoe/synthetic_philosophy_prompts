I'm a backend engineer at a mid-sized algorithmic trading firm, and I was recently rotated onto our internal Autonomous Agent Governance Board — a small committee that has to sign off on any change to the decision logic running in our market-making bots before it ships to production. We have a genuinely contested case going to a vote this Thursday, and since I'm the newest member — and by a wide margin the one with the least philosophy or game-theory background on the board — I've ended up drafting the recommendation memo the rest of the board will vote on.

Here is the situation. Our bots belong to a loose, informal network that includes several unaffiliated firms' bots; membership requires publishing a cryptographically signed snapshot of your bot's decision code to a shared registry every quarter, specifically so other members can simulate how you would behave in situations you haven't yet faced. The point is a mutual-aid arrangement: a firm whose bot is about to be forcibly liquidated in a sudden liquidity crunch can request short-notice emergency credit from another member's bot. Repayment of principal is automatic and contractually enforced, so that part isn't in dispute; but by long-standing informal norm, a rescued bot is also expected to wire back a discretionary goodwill payment once it's solvent again, and that payment is never legally enforceable. Extending emergency credit isn't free for the rescuer — it ties up capital and carries some residual counterparty risk — so a prospective rescuer only agrees to it when simulating the requester's published code predicts a goodwill payment large enough to justify that risk. Because the network spans dozens of firms and any two of them interact only rarely, nobody can wave this off as ordinary repeated-game reputation-building; on any given day, a firm is effectively being judged on a single shot.

Last month our own bot was the one rescued, and its current logic, which evaluates every decision purely on its own forward-looking costs and benefits, declined to send anything back afterward, on the reasoning that the rescue was by then a sunk event and paying would be pure cost with no further causal upside. Two counterparties have since quietly stopped extending us credit. One faction on the board now wants to patch the bot so that it always sends the goodwill payment once rescued, no exceptions; another faction thinks that gives away shareholder money for nothing, and worries that a bot known to always pay up becomes a standing target — other firms would have every incentive to extend us marginal, barely-necessary "rescues," or to quietly inflate how dangerous our position looked, precisely because they'd know the payment is now guaranteed regardless of how real the emergency was. One of our engineers keeps citing something he half-remembers from a decision-theory blog, called Parfit's Hitchhiker, as though it obviously settles the matter, though I don't think anyone on the board, including me, actually understands it well enough to lean on it with any confidence. Could you draft the memo I'll circulate before Thursday's vote: a concrete recommendation for how the bot's logic should handle this going forward, a specific trigger or time frame for revisiting it, and an honest treatment of the exploitability worry — one that either shows a principled way to get the benefits of being rescuable without becoming a standing target, or admits that there isn't one?
