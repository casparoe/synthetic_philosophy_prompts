My first editor at the Chronicle is retiring after thirty-five years, the last twenty of them as the paper's ombudsman -- the reporter whose job is to hold the paper's own journalism to account -- and the newsroom has asked me to give the toast at her send-off dinner. For as long as anyone can remember, she has kept a battered copy of Kierkegaard's "The Present Age" (the second half of his Two Ages) on her desk, and she would invoke his argument about the press, "the public," and the leveling effect of anonymous chatter at any reporter who filed something glib. Half the room will be colleagues who know that argument is genuinely damning for people in our line of work, not just erudite garnish, and they will notice if I sand its edges off; the other half is family who have never heard of him. Could you write me a five-minute toast that takes Kierkegaard's critique of the press seriously on its own terms -- including the way it implicates a career spent, however self-critically, inside that very machine -- and that still lands as something warm, funny, and true rather than either a lecture or an empty compliment?
