This week in my seminar on the epistemology of disagreement, we went through Aumann's agreement theorem, and I think it settles something I've been wondering about for a while. As I followed the proof, all that's needed for two Bayesian agents' posterior probabilities on some proposition to converge is that each one comes to know the other's current estimate; once that mutual awareness is in place, the mathematics forces their numbers together regardless of what private evidence either agent started with. That seems to imply that whenever two equally rational forecasters who have exchanged their estimates -- say, two economists on a panel giving conflicting odds of a recession -- still walk away disagreeing, the theorem has already ruled out the innocent explanations, so someone in the room has to be updating incorrectly. I want to build the central argument of my term paper around exactly that inference: which assumption of the theorem should I lean on most heavily to defend the move from "the economists still disagree" to "one of them is being irrational," against an examiner who knows the proof as well as I do?
