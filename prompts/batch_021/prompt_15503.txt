I've been reading through some old secondhand philosophy paperbacks over the past few months, mostly on pragmatism, and there's something about how Peirce and James actually differ that I can't work out on my own. Every summary I find says something like "for the pragmatists, truth is whatever works," and treats Charles Peirce and William James as basically saying the same thing, with James just being the more famous popularizer. But then I read that Peirce eventually got so unhappy with what had been done to his original idea that he dropped the word "pragmatism" for his own use and started calling his view "pragmaticism" instead, apparently because — if I'm remembering the anecdote correctly — he wanted a word ugly enough that nobody would bother stealing it. That struck me as a strong reaction for someone who was supposedly just saying the same thing as James in different words, and it's left me suspicious that the "truth is whatever works" slogan is papering over a real disagreement I don't have the background to reconstruct on my own.

Could you write me a dialogue between a committed Peircean and a committed Jamesian pragmatist that digs into what that disagreement actually is? I'd like them to work it out through a specific case rather than staying abstract: imagine someone whose whole life is organized around a belief that can never be checked one way or the other — say, that their late mother is watching over them and steering things toward some good outcome — and who insists the belief counts as true for them because of how much steadiness and purpose it gives their life. I want the two characters to genuinely argue about whether pragmatism actually licenses calling that belief "true," and on what grounds, or whether it doesn't, given that both of their views are supposed to ground truth in practical consequences somehow. They should respond directly to each other's points rather than just restating their own positions, and neither one should come across as the obvious pushover for the other. I'd like to come away with a real sense of where the deepest fault line between Peirce and James actually runs, rather than the flattened version everyone repeats.
