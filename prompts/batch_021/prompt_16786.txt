I've been working on a piece about multiverse theories for the science section, and to get some conceptual ballast before I get into Everett branches and the string landscape, I called up a metaphysician who works on modality for a living. She veered off almost right away into an older, purely philosophical version of the "multiple worlds" idea — David Lewis's counterpart theory versus what she called the "transworld identity" view — basically, whether one and the same person can literally exist in more than one possible world, or whether each world just contains its own seperate individual who merely resembles the others closely enough to count.

The bit that stuck with me: she said Lewis rejected transworld identity partly because of the "problem of accidental intrinsics" (I think this is from his book On the Plurality of Worlds) — if Humphrey himself, the actual guy, is thin in one world and fat in another, and he's supposed to be wholly and literally present in both, then one and the same thing ends up with two incompatible shapes at once, which looks like a real contradiction unless you do something ad hoc like say properties are only ever had "relative to a world." Counterpart theory sidesteps this by saying it's never literally the same individual across worlds, just similar enough counterparts, so nothing ever has contradictory properties. But then she brought up Kripke's old objection to counterpart theory (a footnote in Naming and Necessity, I believe) that this gets the whole thing backwards: when we say Humphrey could have won the election, we're not remotely interested in what happens to some other guy who merely resembles him in a different world — we care about Humphrey himself. She also mentioned, kind of in passing, that Kripke maybe isn't even defending transworld identity so much as rejecting the question altogether, since on his view you just stipulate what happens to Humphrey in a counterfactual scenario rather than going out and identifying him among a bunch of world-inhabitants — but by that point, honestly, my eyes glazed over a bit and I loose the thread.

Could you actually sort this out properly for me: explain the accidental intrinsics argument and the Humphrey objection in a way that does justice to both, say whether either one is really decisive against the other side, and then give me your honest verdict on who comes out ahead — Lewis, Kripke, or neither, if the whole dispute just dissolves once you stop assuming there has to be one correct way of matching individuals up across worlds? I want a paragraph on this in the piece and would rather get it right than oversimplify it.
