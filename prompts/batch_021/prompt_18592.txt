I'm a PhD student in philosophy, currently writing a dissertation chapter on Henry Sidgwick's "dualism of practical reason" — the conflict, left unresolved at the end of The Methods of Ethics, between the standpoints of rational egoism and of universal (utilitarian) benevolence, which Sidgwick thought reason alone could not fully adjudicate without some further guarantee — a moral order to the universe, say, or personal immortality — that duty and self-interest ultimately coincide. Reading around the topic, I've become increasingly preoccupied with how this connects to the other, less philosophically respectable-seeming project that occupied so much of his adult life: from the 1870s until his death in 1900, Sidgwick co-founded and served as president of the Society for Psychical Research, spending decades investigating mediums, apparent telepathy, and haunted houses, all while maintaining a level of scrupulous, self-undermining honesty that led him to publicly retract promising early results from his own circle — like the Creery sisters' apparent telepathy — once they turned out to involve nothing more than a signalling code.

My reading group has a running joke that whoever presents on a philosopher that term has to close the session with an actual joke about them; this term, that's me, and my philosopher is Sidgwick. I'd like you to write me one: something that connects the psychical research to the dualism of practical reason, so that the humor depends on knowing why an ethicist this rigorous would spend thirty years chasing ghosts, and why his own investigative honesty arguably made things come out worse for him, philosophically, than if he had never looked at all. It doesn't need to be side-splitting, but it should fail as a joke for someone who thinks Sidgwick was just a Victorian eccentric with a taste for séances — a generic "philosopher believes in ghosts" joke that could be told about any of his contemporaries is exactly what I don't want.
