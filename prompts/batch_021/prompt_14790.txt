I've spent the last few weeks reporting a piece on L.A. Paul's work on transformative experience for a magazine's philosophy column, and made the mistake of mentioning it at dinner with two old friends, Ana and Ben, both of whom argue with exasperating precision. The conversation turned to our mutual friend Clara, who has spent over a year weighing whether to have a child and keeps insisting she just needs to "think it through properly." Ana and Ben ended up in a genuine disagreement about whether that project even makes sense, and neither would concede an inch to the other.

Ana's opening claim was that Clara cannot reason her way to this decision at all, because becoming a parent is what Paul calls an epistemically transformative experience: it will hand Clara concepts and a kind of first-person knowledge she structurally cannot possess beforehand, however many parents she interviews or novels about parenthood she reads, so any expected-value calculation she runs is being fed a number that doesn't yet exist. Ben objected that this proves too much, since we routinely commit to huge, hard-to-preview choices — a demanding degree, emigrating, a risky surgery — by leaning on other people's testimony, discounting for how much people vary, and simply proceeding, and nobody says that isn't real deliberation; parenthood, he said, just looks like an unusually large version of the same problem, not a different kind of problem. Ana replied that the difference is not one of degree: in Ben's examples, Clara already possesses the raw experiential concepts involved (she knows what exhaustion, fear, or homesickness feel like, so testimony can update a model she already has), whereas Paul's whole point is that some experiences hand you concepts you don't yet have the equipment to represent, the way no description of color gives a color-blind person the concept of red — so testimony there isn't weak evidence, it's the wrong kind of evidence. Ben's rejoinder was that granting the distinction doesn't save the deliberation: he pointed out that on Paul's own account, having a child is not merely epistemically transformative but personally transformative too, meaning it can rewrite Clara's underlying preferences and not just her knowledge, so the self who would later judge whether "finding out" had been worth it isn't the self doing the deciding now. Telling Clara to decide based on whether she values discovery for its own sake, he said, just smuggles back in a preference that the experience itself might overhaul, which relocates the problem rather than solving it.

Both of them appealed to me afterward as the one who'd actually read the book, and I honestly don't think either is simply wrong. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say, for each of the four moves, who has the stronger position and why — including whether either side is representing Paul's argument accurately where they invoke it? Please close with your own judgment on whether Ana's original claim, that ordinary rational deliberation is the wrong tool for a decision like Clara's, survives Ben's objections.
