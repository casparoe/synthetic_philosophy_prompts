Over the past year I've been working my way through Victorian intellectual history as a hobby, with no coursework or degree behind it, and Henry Sidgwick has become something of a fixation. He is usually introduced as the last rigorous systematic utilitarian, author of The Methods of Ethics, but almost every account mentions in passing that he also spent nearly three decades as a founder and repeat president of the Society for Psychical Research, sitting through séances, cross-examining mediums, and poring over testimony about apparitions, without ever quite letting himself declare the case proven. I run a very small history podcast out of my spare room, and next month's episode has a Sidgwick scholar as my guest, someone who has spent years going through the SPR archives and Sidgwick's private letters and diaries.

I'd like help drafting eight to ten interview questions that won't let her simply narrate the biography I already know. At least one question should press on the connection between the psychical research and the unresolved "dualism of practical reason" at the close of The Methods of Ethics, since some scholars read his search for evidence of personal survival or a larger moral order as an attempt to supply, empirically, the missing premise that would reconcile self-interest with duty. At least one should focus on a concrete case where his own circle's early confidence collapsed under closer scrutiny, such as the Creery sisters' thought-transference experiments, where suspicion of a signalling code eventually undid what had looked like striking results, or the Cambridge sittings with the medium Eusapia Palladino, where members of his group ended up catching her using a limb they had believed was secured. And at least one should raise the "cross-correspondences," the automatic-writing scripts produced by several mediums after Sidgwick's own death that some of his former colleagues took as his circle's last, ironic attempt to meet its own evidentiary standards from beyond the grave. For each of these, please add a natural follow-up question keyed to whatever answer she is most likely to give, so the sequence builds rather than sitting there as a list of unrelated questions.
