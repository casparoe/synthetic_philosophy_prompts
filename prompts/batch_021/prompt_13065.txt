I work as a machine-learning engineer at a company that has spent the last couple of years shipping "reasoning" models, and lately I can't get away from a very old argument whenever the question of what these systems can and cannot do comes up at work. People keep invoking the line associated with J.R. Lucas and Roger Penrose: that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show no fixed computational system can ever capture what a human mathematician does, because a mathematician can always recognize the truth of that system's own Gödel sentence, something the system itself can never prove. I had filed this away as a curiosity I didn't need to resolve until two friends of mine, Elena, who is finishing a philosophy PhD on the foundations of mathematics, and Marcus, who works in theoretical computer science, spent most of a dinner arguing about it, and I've been unable to settle for myself who came out ahead.

It went roughly like this. (1) Elena opened with the core move: for any consistent, sufficiently expressive formal system F that someone proposes as a model of a mathematician's reasoning, Gödel's construction yields a sentence G_F that is true if F is consistent but unprovable within F; since we can run this construction and recognize G_F as true for literally any F we're handed, no single F can be the whole of what we are, and minds therefore cannot simply be fixed formal or computational systems. (2) Marcus answered that this is exactly where the argument smuggles in its real premise: by Gödel's second theorem, no consistent F can prove the statement of its own consistency, Con(F), so Elena's "recognizing" G_F as true already amounts to assuming Con(F) rather than deriving it, and nothing guarantees that assumption is even true of her own reasoning — working mathematicians have held outright inconsistent theories for years at a stretch before noticing. At most, he said, this shows a mind willing to keep adding Con(F) for any F it's handed isn't reducible to one static system, not that it escapes mechanism altogether. (3) Elena conceded the point about proof versus assumption but pushed back that this was her point sharpened rather than refuted: for any F a computationalist names in advance as an exhaustive model of her, she can, once shown that specific F, always take the further step of affirming Con(F), and this holds no matter which F is proposed — whereas a concrete machine, by construction, has to already be some one particular, advance-specifiable procedure. (4) Marcus replied that this "keep transcending whatever F is named" strategy is neither new nor obviously non-mechanical: it's close to the territory Turing explored in his 1938 thesis on ordinal logics, mechanically extending systems by successively adjoining consistency statements, and the extending procedure itself — given any F, produce F-plus-Con(F), and iterate — can be specified as a single rule. What the argument actually needs, and has never gotten, is proof that a human can reliably supply the right consistency affirmation for a system disclosed with no advance warning, under a soundness guarantee that isn't simply assumed, and centuries of retracted proofs and slowly-discovered contradictions are not obviously evidence in favor of that guarantee.

Neither of them budged, and this bothers me more than it probably should, because both sides of it keep resurfacing in the debates I sit in at work about whether any model I help build could ever really understand mathematics rather than pattern-match its way through it. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of each specific step, rather than just declaring an overall winner — and flag anything you think both of them are taking for granted that they shouldn't be?
