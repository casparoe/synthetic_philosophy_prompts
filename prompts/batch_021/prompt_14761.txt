I spent thirty-five years as an experimental physicist doing Bayesian parameter estimation on detector data, and in all that time the only uncertainty I ever formalized was uncertainty about the world: some measurement not yet taken, some parameter not yet pinned down. Since retiring I have joined a small philosophy-of-science reading group that meets monthly, and at our last session we worked through the 2016 paper on logical induction, which unsettled me in a way I am still turning over. As I understand it, the paper builds a single computable procedure for assigning probabilities not to future observations but to mathematical statements themselves -- whether some enormous number is prime, say, or what a long-running program eventually outputs -- well before anyone has the resources to prove or refute them outright. I find the trader-and-market construction, with its echo of the no-Dutch-book arguments behind ordinary Bayesian coherence, genuinely elegant, but I cannot decide whether it actually dissolves my discomfort with the phrase "probability of a necessary truth" or merely relocates it one level up.

Our group has invited a guest for next month's session: a formal epistemologist who has defended logical induction as essentially the correct account of rational belief under logical uncertainty. Since the topic was my proposal, I have been asked to draft the questions, and I would like eight to ten that do not simply let her re-explain the market picture for newcomers but press on where I suspect the view is most exposed. Please include at least one question on why exploitability by efficiently computable, polynomial-time traders is the right standard of irrationality for a logically uncertain reasoner, rather than a modeling choice that quietly smuggles economic assumptions into what is supposed to be a purely epistemic criterion; at least one on the fact that, even by its own developers' admission, the procedure is computable but inefficient and its guarantees hold only in the limit, and what that implies for the claim that logical induction describes how a bounded reasoner ought to reason moment to moment, rather than merely what it converges to eventually; and at least one on the Troll Bridge problem, where a Löbian argument apparently makes a logical-inductor-based agent refuse to cross a perfectly safe bridge purely because of how it reasons about its own consistency, in seeming tension with the theory's advertised gift of self-trust -- and whether that tension is a fixable edge case or a sign that grounding decisions in provability is the wrong move generally. For each of these three, add a short follow-up question keyed to the answer she is likeliest to give. Close the list with one further question asking what, if anything, would count as evidence that treating logical uncertainty probabilistically is the wrong approach altogether, rather than a research program still awaiting its patches.
