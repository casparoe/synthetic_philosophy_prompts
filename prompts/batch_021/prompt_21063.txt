Something that happened at a family reunion last month has been nagging at me, and I'd like an outside take on it. My aunt Carol told my cousin Nate, in front of everyone, that he'd been inconsiderate for going silent on the family group chat for weeks at a stretch. My uncle Doug cut in and said Carol had no right to say that to Nate, since she'd done the very same thing herself last year, disappearing from the chat for months while she was going through her divorce. What followed was a back-and-forth I haven't been able to stop turning over since:

(1) Doug said Carol wasn't in a position to call Nate inconsiderate, given that she'd been just as absent herself.
(2) Carol answered that whether she'd also gone quiet has no bearing on whether Nate's silence counts as inconsiderate -- bringing up her own record doesn't make his behavior fine, it just changes the subject.
(3) Doug said he wasn't disputing that Nate's silence was inconsiderate; he agreed with that much. His point was narrower: given her history, Carol specifically wasn't the one who got to say so, and that's a different question from whether the charge itself is true.
(4) Carol said that if lacking a spotless record on something disqualifies you from ever pointing it out in someone else, nobody would be allowed to criticize anything, since everybody has fallen short of something at some point.
(5) Doug said he didn't mean any old lapse -- Carol had gone quiet for the same reason Nate did, not wanting to deal with people, and had never acknowledged that or said anything about it afterward; if she'd owned up to it at the time, he wouldn't be bringing it up now.
(6) Carol said the two situations weren't even comparable to begin with, since she'd had a divorce to deal with and Nate has no such excuse, so the charge doesn't get off the ground if the underlying behavior isn't really the same fault.

Nobody at the table could agree on who'd gotten the better of it, and honestly, neither can I. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the stronger position at each step, rather than just picking an overall winner? I'm especially interested in whether the distinction Doug draws in (3), between a charge being true and the person making it being the right one to make it, actually survives the pressure Carol puts on it in (4) and (6).
