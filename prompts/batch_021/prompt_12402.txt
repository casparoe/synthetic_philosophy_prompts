I've been reading Plato's Laws on my own time (no philosophy background, I just got kind of obsessed after finishing the Republic a while back), and theres a bit early on, I'm pretty sure still Book 1, that's been rattling around in my head for days. The Athenian Stranger is going back and forth with the Spartan and Cretan guys about wine, and he argues that drinking parties, if they're supervised right, basically work like military courage-training: just like you toughen up a soldier's nerve by exposing him to controlled amounts of scary stuff instead of shielding him from fear his whole life, you can toughen up someone's self-control by exposing them to controlled amounts of wine instead of just clamping down on it the way Sparta and Crete do. Same move both times according to him — a cheap, low-stakes way to both reveal what someone's really like underneath their manners and train them to hold it together anyway. He even builds in a fix for the obvious objection, by having whoever runs the party stay completely sober himself, like a general who keeps his head while everyone under him is the one being tested.

Here's the part I can't get past, and what I ended up arguing about with a friend over dinner last week: when you train courage with fear, the soldier's own judgement is fully switched on this whole time, thats the very thing getting stronger. Wine just doesn't work like that. It's not like it tests your self-control while leaving your reason alone to do the resisting — it dissolves the very faculty that would be doing the resisting in the first place. And sticking a sober president over the party doesn't rescue this, because now its his sober judgement keeping things from going off the rails, not the drunk guy's, which is a totally different setup from the scared soldier, who has to keep his own head straight without anyone else doing it for him.

Could you actually argue this out properly for me? I want the strongest possible version of the claim that Plato's fear/wine analogy breaks down for exactly this reason, really engaging with how he sets the comparison up himself (the "cheap test" framing, the sober-supervisor fix, all of it) rather than just a quick "well, alcohol impairs judgement, case closed." If you genuinely think there's a good Platonic comeback that saves the analogy, feel free to end with it, but I want the main event to be the case against him.
