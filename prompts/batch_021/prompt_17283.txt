I'm a PhD student in philosophy finishing a dissertation on the history of utilitarianism, and for the past two years I've also served, without a vote, as the resident Bentham specialist on the small editorial committee that maintains my department's open-access sourcebook for the introductory ethics sequence — a set of excerpts that three of our own sections, and now a couple of nearby community colleges, assign instead of a commercial anthology. We're building a module on classical utilitarianism, and the committee is stuck on whether, and in what form, to excerpt Bentham's unpublished manuscripts on sexual "irregularities" (the general eighteenth-century term for non-procreative or otherwise non-conforming sexual acts) — specifically the essay modern editors have titled "Offences Against One's Self," first brought to scholarly attention by Louis Crompton in the 1970s and now printed in full in the Collected Works volume Of Sexual Irregularities, and Other Writings on Sexual Morality. In it, Bentham argues that the death penalty and lesser punishments then attached to consensual homosexual acts cannot survive the principle of utility, since — unlike rape or public indecency — the acts injure no one, and that the law's actual engine is what he elsewhere (in the published Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation) names the "principle of sympathy and antipathy": felt disgust doing the work of a reason. He never published the essay himself.

Two members of the committee want to feature a substantial excerpt as a startling primary source and pair it with a headnote spelling out the antipathy argument for students who have just finished the chapter on the hedonic calculus. Two others want it trimmed to almost nothing, not out of squeamishness about the topic but because the same manuscripts run the identical no-harm, no-mere-antipathy argument across every "irregularity" the eighteenth century bracketed together — bestiality, for instance, was prosecuted under the same buggery statute — and Bentham's reasoning does not obviously stop where a modern reader would want it to. They worry that trimming the excerpt to end at the comfortable conclusion would misrepresent both Bentham's actual argument and the real difficulty it exposes in any sexual ethics built solely on harm-to-others and suspicion of disgust. Both factions have asked me, as the group's Bentham specialist, to draft the recommendation memo that will open next week's meeting, so I need something that states plainly what to include and in what form (full excerpt, trimmed excerpt, or omitted but cited), specifies what the accompanying headnote should and should not claim on Bentham's behalf, and takes the second pair's worry seriously as a philosophical objection rather than dismissing it as mere discomfort.
