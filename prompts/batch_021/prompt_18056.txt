Question: "Money-pump arguments are often invoked to show that a rational agent's preferences must be both transitive and complete, on pain of being exploited through a sequence of trades. (a) Explain how the money-pump argument works for an agent with cyclic (intransitive) preferences. (b) Assess whether an exactly analogous argument shows that preferences must also be complete, or whether the two cases are relevantly different. Relate your discussion to the axioms behind the VNM expected utility theorem."

Student's answer: "An agent with cyclic preferences A ≻ B ≻ C ≻ A can be turned into a money pump. Suppose it currently holds C: since B ≻ C it will pay a small fee to trade up to B, since A ≻ B it will pay a fee to trade up to A, and since C ≻ A it will pay a fee to trade up to C again. It's now back where it started but strictly poorer, and a bookie can repeat the cycle forever. This is essentially the argument von Neumann and Morgenstern used to justify the transitivity axiom in their expected utility theorem, and it shows that any agent that wants to avoid being exploited like this must have transitive (or at least acyclic) preferences over outcomes.

The same reasoning carries over to completeness. Suppose the agent has no preference at all between two options X and Y, i.e. it can't rank them. Then if it's holding X it will be willing to pay a cent to swap for Y, and once it's holding Y it will be willing to pay a cent to swap back to X, and so on forever - so incompleteness is exploitable in exactly the same way intransitivity is. This is why the VNM theorem needs completeness as well as transitivity: both axioms can be independently defended by appeal to the threat of a money pump, and an agent that satisfies both (plus continuity and independence) is guaranteed to be representable as an expected utility maximiser."
