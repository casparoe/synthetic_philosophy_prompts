This is a slightly random one but it's a real thing I now have to deal with, so bear with me. I've covered the Scottish wildcat reintroduction stuff (the "Saving Wildcat" project releasing captive-bred cats into the Cairngorms) for my science desk for a few years now, and at some point the comms team decided that made me qualified to sit on their external advisory panel — mostly geneticists, vets and land managers, plus a couple of "outside" voices like me for optics. Turns out the breeding programme has used a combined score for years, something like Andrew Kitchener's old pelage-scoring criteria plus genetic admixture testing, to sort individual cats into "wildcat enough for the breeding pool / release cohort" versus "too hybridised with domestic cats to count." Nobody outside the genetics subcommittee seems to know the exact cutoff number, but this year's founder cats include four animals whose scores are all within a whisker of each other, and the line happens to fall right in the middle of the four, so two get bred from and two get quietly dropped, even though on paper they're barely distinguishable from one another. Half the panel wants to nudge the cutoff down just this once, since the founder population is so small we can't really afford to lose two of the most genetically useful cats over what's basically noise; the other half says that if the line moves whenever it's inconvenient, you've admitted there was never a principled line in the first place, which is a bad thing to admit for a program whose entire public and legal case rests on "these are real wildcats, not hybrids."

They want a one-page recomendation memo out of the advisory panel before spring pairing decisions get locked in, and because apparently my job makes me "good with words," I've somehow been the one asked to draft it. Could you write that memo for me? I need it to actually land on a recommendation — hold the current cutoff, move it this cycle, or drop the single-number approach for something else entirely — reasons the geneticists on the panel can't just shrug off, and a rough timeframe for acting on it. Please don't let the "move it once and you'll have to move it forever" objection off easy, either; it's honestly the strongest card the hold-the-line side has, and I'd like the memo to earn its conclusion against it rather than just assuming genetic diversity automatically wins because the stakes feel bigger.
