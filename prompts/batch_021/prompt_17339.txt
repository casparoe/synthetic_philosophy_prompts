In his unpublished manuscript "Offences Against One's Self" (c. 1785), Bentham argues against the legal punishment of consensual acts between adult men, which he discusses under the classical label of "paederasty." Reconstruct the structure of this argument in terms of the felicific calculus, explaining how Bentham handles the objections that such acts are "unnatural" and that they provoke antipathy in the public. Then assess whether his conclusion rests on denying that the acts in question are vicious, or on a distinct claim about the proper limits of legislation.

Here is the student's answer:

Bentham's argument is best understood as a direct application of the felicific calculus to a question of legislation. He starts from the observation that the acts he groups under "paederasty" produce genuine pleasure for the parties involved, while causing no tangible injury to any third party. The only harm typically alleged is the offence such conduct gives to observers, and this Bentham treats as an imaginary rather than a real injury: it rests on antipathy and prejudice rather than on any actual loss of interest, and it can in any case be avoided simply by not inquiring into other people's private conduct. Against this slight and avoidable pain he sets the enormous suffering produced by the criminal law itself — not only the punishment of those convicted, but the terror spread through everyone who shares the relevant disposition, and the opportunities for blackmail and false accusation that the law's very existence creates. On the charge that such acts are "unnatural," Bentham argues that the term is either empty or, if it is cashed out as "infrequent" or "non-procreative," applies equally to many pleasures nobody thinks to punish, so it cannot by itself justify criminal sanction. In effect, Bentham is applying Mill's harm principle avant la lettre: the state has no business punishing a class of acts merely because most people find them distasteful, only where real harm to a non-consenting party can be shown. This is a remarkably consistent extension of his utilitarianism, and he later folded a condensed version of the argument into the Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation once the political climate had grown slightly more permissive. The essay thus shows Bentham distinguishing the question of vice from the question of legislation: even granting, for the sake of argument, that these acts are vicious indulgences, that alone gives the legislator no warrant to punish them.
