Been down a weird rabbit hole this week for a piece I'm writing on predictive AI and how good algorithms are getting at guessing what people will do before they've even consciously decided anything. That led me to Newcomb's Problem — the old thought experiment where a near-infallible Predictor fills two boxes based on what it foresees you'll pick, and you have to decide whether to trust dominance reasoning (grab both boxes) or the fact that the Predictor is almost never wrong (take just the one). Digging around for background on why philosophers still argue about this, I landed on Luis de Molina and his idea of "middle knowledge" — the 16th century Jesuit attempt to explain how God can know with total certainty what every possible free creature would do in absolutely any circumstance, logically prior to even deciding to create the world, without that foreknowledge making the choice any less free. Its basically the same worry the Reformation was having about predestination, just fought out inside Catholic theology instead, with Molina trying to hold onto real libertarian freedom against both Luther/Calvin on one side and the stricter Dominicans on his own side.

What I can't stop thinking about is that these feel like the same puzzle wearing different metaphysical clothes — an agent whose choice is flawlessly known in advance by something outside (or almost outside) the normal flow of time, plus the nagging suspicion that this foreknowledge is secretly doing causal work it shouldn't be allowed to do. So: if you sat Molina down in front of the Newcomb setup, what do you think he'd actually say, using his own framework rather than ours? Would his "counterfactuals of freedom" machinery give him a principled reason to insist the Predictor can know your choice with total accuracy without it ceasing to be free — and does that framework even give him grounds to say whether the rational move is to one-box or two-box? Or does the whole apparatus only hold together with an infinite, atemporal God doing the knowing, so that swapping in a finite (if extremely good) predictor makes it collapse — e.g., does the "grounding objection" people raise against middle knowledge bite even harder here, given there's obviously no act of divine creation for the relevant counterfactuals to be grounded in? Curious how far you think the analogy actually holds before it snaps.
