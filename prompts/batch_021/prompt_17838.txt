I teach a graduate seminar that works through the Leibniz–Clarke correspondence before turning to the metaphysics literature that grew out of Max Black's 1952 two-spheres thought experiment, and next month I'm recording a short interview for the seminar's companion podcast. My guest is a metaphysician who has built a research program around rescuing the identity of indiscernibles from Black's counterexample: her position is that the two spheres, though indiscernible by any purely monadic qualitative predicate, are nonetheless discerned in Quine's "weak" sense by the irreflexive symmetric relation each bears to the other -- being some fixed distance from a sphere of exactly the same intrinsic character -- since nothing can bear that relation to itself. Whenever a student objects that this looks like it settles the spheres' numerical distinctness by fiat, her stock reply is some version of "an irreflexive relation is still a qualitative relation, and qualitative relations are exactly what the principle was always meant to range over," a line that tends to end the discussion rather than advance it.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that don't just invite her to restate the weak-discernibility move? I want at least one question that puts the circularity worry to her directly -- that citing an irreflexive relation to secure numerical distinctness is uninformative, since irreflexivity already presupposes the very distinctness supposedly being explained -- one that asks how this rescue is even available to a genuinely Leibnizian defender of PII, given Leibniz's own insistence that spatial relations are ideal and that every true predication must ultimately be grounded in a substance's intrinsic, monadic complete concept, and one that tests whether the move generalizes past toy cases like the spheres to quantum-statistical ones, where it is far less obvious that indistinguishable bosons, as opposed to fermions, are even weakly discernible. For each of those three, add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give -- if, say, she retreats to calling the weak version "PII enough," the follow-up should make her specify what a world would have to look like for even that weakened principle to come out false. Close the sequence with one question asking her to say, concretely, what discovery or argument would actually convince her to abandon weak discernibility rather than patch it further.
