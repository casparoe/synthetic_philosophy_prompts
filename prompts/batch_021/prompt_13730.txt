I've been teaching a graduate seminar this term on Bernard Williams' internalism about reasons -- the view that "A has a reason to X" is only true if A could reach the motivation to X by a sound deliberative route from what's already in his "subjective motivational set" (desires, projects, cares, commitments, etc.), as against external reasons theorists who want reasons that hold regardless of what the agent happens to be motivated by. After one session two of my students, Femi and Sana, ended up arguing about it in the hallway for like 40 minutes and then emailed me their reconstruction of it afterward because neither would let it go. The case that set them off wasn't from the readings -- it was about someone Sana knows slightly, a guy named Warren whose father was neglectful and occasionally violent when Warren was a kid. They haven't spoken in almost twenty years, and now the father is dying and has asked, through an intermediary, to see him. Warren says, and Sana believes him, that he feels genuinely nothing about it either way, not anger, not love, no pull at all, and has no interest in feeling otherwise. Does Warren have a reason to go?

Here's the exchange, near as I can reconstruct it:

(1) Femi: On Williams' view, strictly speaking Warren has no reason to go. If there's really nothing in his motivational set that a sound deliberative route could connect to this, then "Warren has a reason to visit his father" is just false, however much we might wish it weren't.

(2) Sana: That can't be right, because it means the very people who most need to hear "you have a reason to do the decent thing" are exactly the people for whom it comes out false. The worse someone's motivational set, the more internalism protects them from ever having failed to do what they had reason to do.

(3) Femi: You're conflating "has a reason" with "ought to become someone who has a reason." Williams has room for a second-order reason to fix a defective S, so internalism isn't as protective as it looks. And even absent any first-order reason, we can still say the visit would be good, or that Warren's indifference is a moral failing -- internalism is a thesis about reasons for action, not about all evaluation of persons.

(4) Sana: That second-order reason would itself have to already be sitting in Warren's S by your own lights, so you've just pushed the regress back a step without giving him any actual foothold. And calling him a bad person while denying he has any reason to do the one thing that would fix it isnt modest, its just incoherent.

(5) Femi: No regress -- sound deliberation doesn't require Warren to already want self-improvement, only that the process would produce the motivation if he deliberated soundly on what's actually there (correcting false beliefs, imagining things vividly, whatever). Whether that would actually work on Warren specifically is an empirical question about him, not a hole in the theory.

(6) Sana: Fine, but then "you have a reason" becomes unfalsifiable and useless in exactly the hard cases where we need it, since no one can verify what idealized deliberation would produce in someone else's head. Meanwhile the external reasons theorist just says: anyone has reason to see a dying parent, full stop, no psychological hostage-taking required.

Neither of them would budge, and honestly I think they're each onto something real.
