In the incident postmortems we run at work, someone always argues that a monitoring gap or a missed alert "caused" the outage, while someone else insists it only failed to stop damage that something else caused. I looked into why this is so hard to settle and found that philosophers of causation have a genuine, unresolved dispute over whether absences and failures to act can be causes at all, and I'd like to see that argument actually played out rather than summarized. Write a dialogue between a counterfactual theorist of causation, who holds that an omission is a perfectly good cause of some outcome whenever that outcome counterfactually depends on it, and a physical-process theorist, who holds that genuine causation requires some continuous process — a transfer of energy or momentum, say — linking cause to effect, so that omissions can never be causes in the strict sense. Have them argue through a single shared case, a lifeguard who never notices a drowning swimmer, with each pressing the other on their view's hardest problem: the counterfactual theorist has to explain why every other person on earth who didn't jump in isn't equally a cause of the drowning, and the process theorist has to explain why we're so confident the lifeguard's inattention was a real cause of death when there's no physical process to point to. Each should respond directly to the objection just raised rather than restate their opening position, and the dialogue should end without either side conceding.
