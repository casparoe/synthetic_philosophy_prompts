I've spent enough years reading LessWrong, Astral Codex Ten, and similar blogs that I've developed a reflexive suspicion of any famous debate that gets compressed into a single-sentence summary, and the Hart–Fuller exchange is a prime example of one that suffers for it. The standard gloss is that positivists say law and morality are conceptually separate while natural lawyers say they are necessarily connected, but working back through the original 1958 Harvard Law Review papers for a jurisprudence seminar, I keep noticing that both men end up conceding something that sounds like it belongs to the other side. Hart, about as committed a positivist as they come, argues in The Concept of Law that there is a "minimum content of natural law": given some very general truisms about the human condition — our vulnerability to violence, rough equality of strength, limited altruism, scarce resources, and limited understanding and willpower — any viable legal system will end up containing rules against violence, something like stable property, something like promise-keeping, and sanctions to secure compliance. Fuller, from the other direction, argues that legality itself has an "inner morality" made up of eight desiderata — generality, promulgation, prospectivity, clarity, non-contradiction, possibility of compliance, constancy through time, and congruence between announced rules and official action — such that a regime that fails badly enough on these counts (his implicit target throughout is the Nazi legal order) has not produced law at all, whatever the content of its rules happens to be.

I'd like you to compare these two concessions rather than just restating the textbook "positivism versus natural law" line. First, what exactly is each one a necessary condition for: a social order surviving at all, in Hart's case, versus something counting as law in the first place, in Fuller's, versus — a further and stronger claim Fuller sometimes seems to want — a legal system actually being good? Second, take on Hart's well-known rejoinder that Fuller's eight principles are really just principles of efficacy, the kind of craft-norms you would need to rule effectively at all and no different in kind from the norms for effective poisoning, so that they do not earn the name "morality" in any sense a positivist needs to worry about. Does that objection actually dissolve Fuller's position, or does it generalize in a way that equally trivializes Hart's own minimum content? I would rather you stake out and defend an actual verdict on whether either man's concession quietly undercuts his own side of the debate than give me a balanced survey of both.
