So I sit on the incident-response steering committee at the company I work for (we sell scheduling and records software to a couple hundred small medical clinics, so when we go down, they go down too), and Monday night we got hit with ransomware. Everything client-facing is encrypted, the attackers want something like $900k in crypto by Friday or they start leaking clinic data, and our security policy has had a "we do not pay ransomware demands" line in it for two years, mostly lifted wholesale from a CISA advisory that nobody on the board ever really argued about, because it cost nothing to agree with in the abstract. Now that it's not abstract, half the executives want a one-time carve-out, because rebuilding from backup plus the contractual penalties we owe clinics for downtime will probably cost more than the ransom itself -- so on a "what's the best move from where we're sitting right now" basis, they might genuinely be right.

I've been reading rationalist blogs long enough that this whole thing feels like Parfit's hitchhiker wearing a blazer. The entire point of a credible no-pay policy is that it's supposed to make you a worse target, or get you gentler treatment, precisely because it's predictable in advance that caving is off the table -- and if the one time it's actually tested you fold anyway, you haven't just made one payment, you've revealed there was never really a policy, which seems worse then never adopting one. But I keep getting stuck on a disanalogy: Parfit's driver is stipulated to actually be reading the hitchhiker specifically, whereas our attacker is running some automated numbers game across thousands of victims with no fine-grained read on our particular "type." So I'm honestly not sure the deterrence story survives once the predictor is a statistical aggregate instead of an accurate predictor of us in particular. I also vaguely remember this is the exact case where causal decision theory is supposed to embarrassingly fail, and you need something like functional or updateless decision theory to get the "right" answer, but I dont know how you'd translate any of that into something you could actually say to a room of executives and outside counsel.

Could you draft the memo I need to bring to Thursday's emergency board call? I want one clear recommendation -- pay, hold the line, or something conditional -- tied concretely to Friday's deadline, and I want it to take the "the deterrence logic doesn't obviously apply to a one-off, statistically-driven attacker who'll never specifically know or care what we decided" objection seriously, rather than just restating the standard no-negotiation talking points.
