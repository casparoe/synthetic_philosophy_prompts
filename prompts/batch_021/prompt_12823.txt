I've spent a fair amount of time on LessWrong and the EA Forum reading value-of-information arguments, and I keep running into an appeal to what people call Good's theorem: I. J. Good's decision-theoretic result that, for an idealized Bayesian expected-utility maximizer, the option to observe a free, accurate signal before choosing an action can never lower your expected utility relative to acting without it, since you always retain the fallback of ignoring the signal and doing whatever you would have done in its absence. It's routinely cited as the formal backbone behind the idea that true information can never leave a rational agent worse off in expectation, and it does real work in things like forecasting practice and cause-prioritization write-ups. But whenever the claim comes up, the same three counterexamples get trotted out: Bostrom's information hazards, the economics-of-information point, going back to Hirshleifer, that letting people learn their own risk category can unravel an insurance market, and the Schelling-style observation that staying ignorant of your own reservation price can function as a useful commitment device in bargaining. I suspect any model has simply memorized the standard resolution to each of those three rather than actually understanding the underlying logic of the theorem well enough to generate a fresh case.

So here is what I'd like from you: construct a new decision scenario -- not a repackaging of any of the three cases above, and not the genetic-testing-for-an-untreatable-disease chestnut either -- in which a single Bayesian-rational agent facing one decision seems, intuitively, to end up worse off purely because she costlessly and correctly learned something true before acting. Spell the scenario out in enough concrete detail that the expected utilities could actually be tallied: the relevant states of the world, the action set, the payoffs, and exactly what the signal tells her, rather than just gesturing at the shape of a paradox. Then use your own example to pin down precisely which of the theorem's idealizing assumptions is doing the work of dissolving the apparent counterexample -- is it that the information isn't really free once you count some hidden cost, that her utility function itself shifts upon updating rather than staying fixed over a stable set of outcomes, that some other agent is reacting to what she now knows (or to the mere fact that she looked), or that she never actually goes on to implement the Bayes-optimal action once informed?
