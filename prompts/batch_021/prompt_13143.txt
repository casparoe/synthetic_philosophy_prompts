I'm putting together the lineup for a small philosophy podcast on collective decision-making — I got interested in the topic after sitting on a hiring committee that somehow reached a recommendation none of us individually endorsed, and went down a rabbit hole trying to understand why. Next month's guest is a philosopher of collective agency who defends what I'll call "group agent realism": building on List and Pettit's work on judgment aggregation, he argues that group agents such as courts, legislatures, and corporations can hold genuine judgments and beliefs of their own, not reducible to any simple aggregate of their members' individual attitudes. He grounds this partly in the discursive dilemma — the fact that a three-judge panel ruling on a tort claim requiring both a breach of duty and a causal link to the harm can reach opposite verdicts depending on whether it votes proposition-by-proposition on the premises or directly on the conclusion — and partly in the associated impossibility results: no aggregation procedure, it turns out, can satisfy a small set of jointly attractive conditions, among them anonymity and a requirement, often called systematicity, that every proposition be handled by the same rule independently of the others, while still guaranteeing a fully consistent collective judgment.

He has given versions of this talk many times and has smooth answers ready, so I want questions that don't let him simply restate the framework. Please draft eight to ten interview questions that push past exposition. I want at least one question that presses on the inferential leap from a formal impossibility result to a substantive metaphysical claim about group-level mentality, one that presses on whether giving up systematicity specifically, rather than, say, anonymity, is a principled philosophical choice or just the least costly item to sacrifice, and one that asks him to explain why talk of "the court believes" or "the board has decided" isn't better read as convenient instrumentalist shorthand, a Dennett-style intentional stance applied to committees, rather than evidence of a literal group mind. For each of these three, add a follow-up question keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. Close the list with a question asking him to specify what finding, formal or empirical, would actually count as evidence against group agent realism, so that he cannot simply treat the position as unfalsifiable.
