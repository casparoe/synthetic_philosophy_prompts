A friend and I spent an entire dinner last week arguing about whether biological "function" talk is covertly historical -- fixed by what a trait was shaped by selection to do -- or simply picks out whatever a part currently contributes to some capacity of the system it's in. Neither of us budged. I sit on my professional association's committee revising the general definition of "mental disorder" in our diagnostic manual, where the wording will decide which conditions count as disorders for treatment and insurance purposes, and it runs straight into that dinner argument. The current text takes the etiological line: a mechanism is "dysfunctional" only if it fails to do what natural selection shaped it to do, so a fear response to a stimulus that poses no genuine threat counts as dysfunction, since the mechanism is failing to track the threats it evolved to detect, even though, causally, it's doing exactly what fear mechanisms do. A vocal faction on the committee wants to drop the evolutionary language entirely and define dysfunction in terms of a mechanism's current causal contribution to the person's psychological functioning, since we can almost never establish with any rigor what a given psychological trait was actually selected for. Please draft the short memo I'll bring to the committee before we vote on the wording: recommend that we keep the etiological criterion, adopt the causal-role alternative, or take some third option, and make sure the losing side's strongest objection gets a real answer rather than a dismissal.
