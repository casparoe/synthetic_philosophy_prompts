I'm a PhD student in political philosophy, currently drafting a dissertation chapter on the "equality of what?" debate, and I've run into a puzzle my advisor brushed aside in our last meeting but that I can't quite let go of. Most survey articles treat G.A. Cohen's "equality of access to advantage" as little more than a friendly amendment to Amartya Sen's capability approach — a slight broadening of the metric that leaves Sen's basic architecture intact. But when I go back to Cohen's own texts, especially "On the Currency of Egalitarian Justice" and the later essay "Equality of What? On Welfare, Goods and Capabilities," he clearly thinks he's doing something more substantial: introducing "midfare" as a distinct category sitting between resources/goods and utility, and arguing that Sen's exclusive focus on functionings and capabilities leaves out welfare shortfalls that don't reduce to any specifiable failure of functioning.

I'd like your help working out exactly how much daylight there really is between the two views, along two axes in particular. First, how does each framework handle the contrast between an involuntary expensive need (say, a costly medical condition) and a voluntarily cultivated expensive taste? Second, does Sen's own distinction between "capability to function" and "achieved functioning" already give him the resources to accommodate Cohen's worry about pure welfare shortfalls, without having to import anything like midfare? Having worked through both, I'd also like your considered verdict: does Cohen identify a real gap in Sen's account that "access to advantage" actually closes, or does his proposal just smuggle back in a welfarist element that Sen had good philosophical reason to exclude? Please defend whichever verdict you reach rather than simply asserting it.
