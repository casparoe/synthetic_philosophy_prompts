I'm a senior engineer at a mid-sized startup, and I've been asked to chair a small working group reviewing our engineering hiring pipeline ahead of next quarter's hiring push. The sticking point is our take-home project: candidates who clear the phone screen are given an open-ended coding exercise -- unpaid, roughly 15-20 hours of work, due within a week -- and how well they do on it has historically carried a lot of weight in the final decision. Two people on the committee want to cut it entirely. Their case is partly moral (it's exclusionary toward candidates with kids, a second job, or unstable housing) and partly empirical (our own retrospective data shows only a weak correlation between take-home scores and performance a year in). The other two want to keep it essentially as-is, and their argument isn't really about the exercise's content at all -- it's that voluntarily absorbing a large, unpaid cost is itself the informative part, and that if we swap it for something cheaper (a shorter exercise, more weight on the resume, a live pairing round) we'll just get flooded with applicants who look identical on paper, with no way left to tell them apart.

A friend of mine who did a PhD in evolutionary biology before moving into industry told me the second argument is basically Zahavi's handicap principle from animal signaling theory -- the logic behind why a peacock's tail or a gazelle's stotting can be trusted as an honest signal -- transplanted into a hiring pipeline, and that economists have their own version of the same idea in job-market signaling models. That reframing has stuck with me, but I don't have the background to tell whether it actually survives contact with our specific situation or is just a plausible-sounding analogy -- for instance, whether the take-home's costs really do fall differently on strong and weak candidates the way the theory needs them to, or whether it's just a uniformly expensive filter that happens to track things like free time rather than engineering ability. Could you draft the short memo I'll circulate to the group before we meet? It should come down on a concrete recommendation -- keep, cut, or modify the take-home project, and if it's cut or modified, what (if anything) should replace it -- and it should take the signaling argument seriously enough to say where it holds up and where it might not, rather than either deferring to the equity argument by default or waving the signaling argument through because it sounds rigorous.
