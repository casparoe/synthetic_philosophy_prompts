I spent just over thirty years as a field ecologist, mostly studying predator-prey and pathogen dynamics in temperate forest systems, and it is only since retiring that I have started reading philosophy in any serious way, largely through a small discussion evening I now run with two or three former colleagues. It struck me early on that ecologists talk constantly in terms philosophers apparently find suspect: we say that a pathogen's disappearance from a watershed caused an insect population to crash, or that removing wolves from a landscape caused a deer irruption, without ever pausing to ask whether a mere absence can do any causing at all. So when I discovered how contested "causation by omission" still is in the metaphysics literature, I felt as though I had been speaking loosely for thirty years without knowing it. I have been reading around the topic since -- Lewis on counterfactual dependence, Hart and Honoré's old normal/abnormal-conditions strategy from the philosophy of law, and, out of old habit, some of the computer-science literature on causal models, where a very similar move shows up under the heading of "default values" or "normality."

Next month a philosopher who has worked out her own version of this normalcy-based rescue of omissive causation is giving a talk to our group, and I will be moderating the Q&A afterward. Her view, roughly, is that among the indefinitely many things that did not happen and on which some effect counterfactually depends, only the omission that departs from an operative baseline -- moral, statistical, or otherwise -- gets to count as the cause; that is how she blocks the well-known multiplication of causes, so that it is the negligent gardener who caused the flowers to die of thirst, and not every other person on Earth who also failed to water them. Whenever someone presses her on whether this quietly smuggles our interests and norms into the metaphysics of causation, she has a ready-made reply: that ordinary "positive" causation already trades on exactly the same normal/abnormal contrast, so omissions are no worse off than anything else.

I would like help drafting eight to ten questions for this Q&A that do not simply let her restate the view or retreat to that line, ideally building so that the deflection gets harder to use as the sequence goes on. I want at least one question on the multiplication-of-causes problem and whether her baseline genuinely solves it or just relocates it one level up; one that presses the worry that she has the explanatory order backwards, since we ordinarily think moral responsibility is grounded in prior causal facts rather than the reverse; one on what happens when the statistical baseline and the prescriptive baseline point in different directions, and which one she lets win; one that tests the account against a genuinely non-agential case -- I can offer my own pathogen-and-weevil story, or the standard wolves-and-elk one, whichever she prefers -- to see whether "norm" means the same thing once no moral agent is anywhere in sight; and a final question asking her to state plainly what would count as evidence against her account, or else concede the point to those who deny that omissions are ever real causes at all, calling them at best "quasi-causes." For each question, please add a short follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give.
