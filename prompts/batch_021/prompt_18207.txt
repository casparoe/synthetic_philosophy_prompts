I've been a longtime reader of LessWrong and the adjacent rationalist blogosphere, and acausal trade has always struck me as one of the more elegant ideas to come out of that world — right up until I try to explain to someone why it actually works, at which point I run into a snag that I raised at my local rationalist meetup last week without getting a satisfying answer. The setup, as I understand it, runs roughly as follows: two agents — civilizations, say, or the superintelligences running them — occupy regions of the universe, or branches of the multiverse, with no causal contact whatsoever: no shared light cone, no signal, no way of ever observing, or being observed by, the other. Nonetheless, each is capable of modelling the other well enough to predict what it would do under various circumstances, and each acts so as to benefit the other on the strength of that prediction: "I'll bear this cost for your sake, because I've inferred that you, in your causally sealed corner of reality, will bear a comparable cost for mine." It's usually presented as descending from Hofstadter's superrationality by way of Gary Drescher's "acausal subjunctive morality," and illustrated with dressed-up versions of the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma.

What I can't get past is the reciprocity half of the story. Suppose civilization A goes first, in whatever sense "first" even means here, and actually pays up: it diverts resources, or forgoes some locally optimal policy, for B's benefit. Once that's done, what possible reason does B have to hold up its end? There is, by hypothesis, no causal channel back to A, so B can't be detected, punished, or embarrassed for reneging; from B's own self-interested standpoint at the moment of choice, defection is strictly costless. Every account I've come across of why B complies anyway seems to quietly presuppose that B already has some independent disposition toward honesty that the "trade" framing itself contributes nothing to — in which case "acausal trade" looks less like a genuine discovery about rational agency than a redescription of "the universe happens to contain agents who are nice to each other" in fancier language. Am I missing some feature of the setup that actually forces B's hand, or does the whole apparatus quietly depend on the cooperative dispositions it's supposed to be deriving from first principles?
