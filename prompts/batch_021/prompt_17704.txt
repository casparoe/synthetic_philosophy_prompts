I don't have any medical or academic background — I run a small landscaping business — but two years ago I joined my hospital's Patient and Family Advisory Council, and last year the council put me forward as its representative on the hospital's newly formed Diagnostic Safety Committee, which reviews cases where a diagnosis was delayed or missed. There's a case coming up for a vote this week that I can't stop turning over, and the more I sit with it the more it feels like a logic problem dressed up as a medical one.

A patient came to our emergency department three times over about two weeks with worsening back pain. On the first two visits she was examined, given a common and entirely reasonable diagnosis (first a muscle strain, then possibly a mild disc issue), and sent home with pain medication; on the third visit she had new weakness in one leg, an urgent MRI found a spinal epidural abscess, and by the time it was drained she was left with permanent nerve damage. Looking back, there were a few soft warning signs at the earlier visits — a slightly raised temperature, pain that wasn't settling with rest, a skin infection a few weeks before — but each of those, on its own, is common in people who turn out to have nothing serious at all, and scanning everyone who shows up with one or two of them would mean an enormous number of MRIs to catch a single rare case. What strikes me most is that the abscess was never on the list of possibilities the doctors were weighing at those first two visits; it wasn't considered and set aside, it just wasn't a candidate at all. Trying to work out why this bothers me as much as it does, I did some reading and found that philosophers have a name for the general style of reasoning doctors are using here — inference to the best explanation — and that there's a long-running argument over whether picking the best of the explanations you happened to think of tells you anything about the truth, when the truth was never among the candidates to begin with. That is more or less the puzzle this case has turned into for me.

The committee is split roughly along those lines. About half think this should be classed as no error: the doctors reasoned soundly from what was in front of them at each visit, nobody can order scans on every patient with a mild fever and unsettled back pain on the off chance of a disease this rare, and calling it a failure now is hindsight bias dressed up as quality improvement. The other half think that misses the point: if the "best available explanation" is only ever drawn from a shortlist, and the shortlist was built in a way that left out a well-recognized, high-stakes diagnosis despite known red flags being present, then the failure was in constructing the list, not in ranking it, and no amount of careful reasoning afterward could fix that. Our charter defines three possible findings — error, no error, or a system-level contributing factor that stops short of faulting the individual physicians — but gives no guidance on which one fits a case like this, where the physicians may have reasoned perfectly well from an incomplete list. I need to circulate the short written opinion before Thursday's vote. Could you draft it? It should come down clearly on one of the three classifications, spell out concrete follow-up action with a timeframe, and take the hindsight-bias objection seriously as a real argument rather than something to be waved off — ideally in terms the committee could reuse as a rough standard the next time a case like this comes up, since I doubt it will be the last one.
