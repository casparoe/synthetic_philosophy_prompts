A friend and I spent much of last night arguing about what Plato actually thinks of drunkenness, and we never managed to settle it. I argued that the Symposium gives Plato's real verdict on the matter: Socrates is the one guest who can drink all night without ever losing his composure, and Alcibiades' speech treats this immunity to wine as living proof of a self-mastery that everyone else in the room conspicuously lacks, so that the dialogue's whole dramatic arc quietly ranks sobriety above intoxication as a mark of the philosophical soul. My friend insisted this is a one-sided reading, since in Books 1 and 2 of the Laws Plato has the Athenian Stranger go out of his way to defend regulated drinking parties against Spartan and Cretan objections, treating carefully supervised drunkenness as a genuinely useful device for testing and toughening a citizen's self-control, and even as a necessary aid to the choral education that lets older citizens sing and dance without shame. Neither of us could agree on whether the two texts are really in tension, or whether the apparent shift simply reflects Plato adapting his view to a different context: private philosophical friendship in one case, the design of a whole city's laws in the other. Could you compare Plato's treatment of drunkenness in the Symposium with his discussion of wine in the Laws, and say whether the two views strike you as ultimately consistent? I am especially interested in whether Socrates' personal imperviousness to wine in the Symposium can be squared with the Laws' idea that deliberately induced, properly supervised drunkenness is a valuable diagnostic and pedagogical tool, rather than something a genuinely virtuous person would simply avoid.
