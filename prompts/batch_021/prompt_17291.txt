I recently moved from a software engineering role into a newly created People Analytics position at my company (roughly 900 employees, split mainly across Engineering, Sales, Customer Support, and Marketing), and I've run into a problem I don't yet have the statistical vocabulary to handle with full confidence. Last year we piloted a voluntary mentorship program, the "Career Accelerator," and when I pulled eighteen-month promotion outcomes, participants had been promoted at a noticeably higher rate than non-participants company-wide (about 35% versus 28%). Leadership is ready to roll the program out to everyone on the strength of that headline number. When a friend from my old statistics program asked me to break the same numbers down by department, though, the comparison reversed in all four: within Engineering, Sales, Support, and Marketing alike, non-participants were promoted at an equal or higher rate than participants. Sales, which happens to be both our largest department and by far the most enthusiastic adopter of the program, also has the fastest promotion cycle of the four for reasons that have nothing to do with the program itself, and that combination alone seems to be enough to flip the company-wide comparison even though the program looks neutral-to-harmful within every department taken on its own.

My friend tells me this is a textbook instance of Simpson's paradox, and I've since read enough about its classic illustrations (the 1973 Berkeley admissions data, the kidney-stone treatment study) to follow the mechanics of the reversal itself. What I haven't found is guidance aimed at someone in my actual situation: our analytics team is three people, none of us formally trained statisticians, we cannot realistically randomize most of the programs we're asked to evaluate, and leadership wants a clear recommendation rather than a statistics lecture. Could you describe the most important implications of Simpson's paradox for how a team like ours should design, report on, and interpret evaluations such as this one going forward? I'd especially value your thoughts on how we might decide which variables are even safe to stratify by in the first place, since it occurs to me that slicing the data along every dimension we can think of might introduce its own distortions rather than resolve the one we've already found.
