And here's the student's answer, copied word for word:

"This puzzle comes out of Robert Nozick's work on predictors, the same paper that gave us Newcomb's Problem. A man meets Death in Damascus, who tells him he's coming for him tomorrow. The man can stay in Damascus or flee to Aleppo, and Death is an excellent predictor of where the man will actually be, so wherever the two locations match is where the man dies.

Ratifiability, in the relevant sense, says a decision to perform an act is rational only if the act still comes out best once you update your credences on the supposition that you've settled on doing it. Here, if the man becomes confident he'll stay in Damascus, that confidence is itself evidence that Death will be waiting there, so relative to that updated credence, fleeing now looks better - staying isn't ratifiable. Exactly symmetric reasoning shows fleeing isn't ratifiable either: whichever act you settle on, the resulting update makes the other act look better. So neither pure strategy is a stable, self-endorsing choice, and only a mixed strategy - flipping a coin, assuming Death can't predict coin flips - turns out to be self-ratifying, as later work on the case showed.

This is really the same phenomenon as Newcomb's Problem: there, EDT (evidential decision theory) recommends one-boxing and CDT (causal decision theory) recommends two-boxing, and ratifiability is just the technical notion that explains why the two theories end up disagreeing about which box to pick. Since Death in Damascus has the identical predictor structure as Newcomb's Problem, it generates the identical EDT/CDT split, just dressed up with a scarier premise."
