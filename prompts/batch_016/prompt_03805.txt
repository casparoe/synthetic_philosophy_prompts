So the reading group I'm part of (a bunch of us who used to do the SSC meetup before it fizzled out) just spent two sessions on evidential cooperation in large worlds — we went through Oesterheld's original multiverse-wide-superrationality writeup and the newer CLR FAQ on it — and it kicked off an argument between two of the regulars, Jonah and Renata, that's now like three separate group-chat threads long. Quick gloss in case this isn't fresh for you: ECL says that if the universe (or multiverse) is large enough, it contains tons of agents whose decision procedures correlate with ours even though we can never causally interact with them, so under something like evidential decision theory, choosing to act cooperatively is evidence that those correlated agents act cooperatively too — same logic as cooperating with your psychological twin in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, just scaled up to a whole multiverse of near-twins who might have very different values.

Jonah's opening claim was that this is genuinely action-guiding: we should put real weight on broadly cooperative, widely-endorsed actions (the kind of thing lots of different value systems would sign off on) because doing so is evidence that our distant correlated counterparts are doing the same, and the payoff sums over an enormous population. Renata called this a Pascal's mugging with extra steps (the classic worry about tiny odds of vast payoffs warping your decisions): we have zero empirical handle on the correlation strength, so the whole thing is just a made-up small probability times a made-up huge number, dressed up in decision-theory language. Jonah pushed back with the "blue balls in a box" move — if your odds of acausally influencing any single agent are low but roughly independent across a huge population, you can still be highly confident you're influencing plenty of them in total, which is structurally different from real Pascal's mugging (that requires thinking you probably influence either all the relevant agents or none of them). He also brought up the "evidentialist's wager": even a modest credence in noncausal decision theory, combined with how much higher the stakes are if it's right, can be enough to make ECL worth acting on. Renata conceded that this beats the naive mugging charge, but said stacking a wager on decision theory on top of a wager on population size on top of total vagueness about who even counts as being in your "reference class" of correlated cooperators is the same move wearing a trenchcoat — and pointed out that even ECL's own proponents admit the concrete upshot is something as hand-wavy as "commit to benefiting the aggregate values of whoever you're cooperating with," with no real account of who that includes or what it cashes out to, which makes it flexible enough to rationalize whatever priorities you already had.

Neither of them budged and I dont think either one landed a clean knockout blow, but I can't tell if that's because they're evenly matched or because I'm missing something myself.
