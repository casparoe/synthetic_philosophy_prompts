I teach a graduate seminar on rational choice and the philosophy of commitment, and this term we worked through the familiar Schelling-style paradoxes: the strategic value of ostentatiously throwing away your steering wheel in a game of chicken, Kavka's toxin puzzle, the deterrent logic of a doomsday machine that only functions if you announce it before you've finished building it. Lately, a few students who also work in AI safety pushed the discussion toward a bleaker descendant of these puzzles that circulates under the name "commitment races": the worry that once two sufficiently sophisticated bargaining agents—nations, corporations, or artificial superintelligences—both understand that committing early and irrevocably to a hard-line strategy confers bargaining leverage, each acquires an incentive to lock itself in before the other does, and before either has had time to think the commitment through. On this picture, the agents' very rationality drives them to truncate their own deliberation, racing each other not toward some prize but toward the first available act of self-binding, however reckless; and because both sides reason this way, the outcome can end up worse for everyone than if neither had been in such a hurry to bind themselves.

None of my payoff matrices have made this feel real to my students the way a piece of fiction might. I'd like you to write a short story, something in the range of 1,000 to 1,500 words, that places two parties on exactly this collision course—they might be rival states, warring corporations, estranged spacefaring civilizations meeting for the first time, or two artificial agents negotiating on behalf of humans who no longer have much say in the matter. I want the story to dwell inside the self-undermining logic of the race itself: each side dimly aware that its rival is rushing to commit, each tempted to commit faster and more irreversibly still, and each unsettled by the sense that this very awareness is what is destroying its own capacity to think clearly. Please resist the temptation to write a straightforward cautionary tale about AI or nuclear brinkmanship; I would rather you leave the underlying paradox—that acting rationally can require disabling one's own future rationality—essentially unresolved at the story's end.
