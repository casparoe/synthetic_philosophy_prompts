I've spent a good part of the last few weeks working through the LessWrong and Alignment Forum literature on coherence arguments for expected utility maximization: starting with Yudkowsky's "Coherent decisions imply consistent utilities," moving through Rohin Shah's rejoinder that coherence doesn't actually entail goal-directed behavior, and arriving at the more recent discussion -- Thornley's "There are no coherence theorems" and the broader back-and-forth about it involving Wentworth and others -- that treats the Completeness axiom as the real crux. As I understand the state of play, the standard money-pump argument for Completeness says that even an agent with a genuine preferential gap between two options -- it doesn't prefer A to B, doesn't prefer B to A, and isn't indifferent between them either -- can still be exploited: an adversary offers it a cycle of trades that passes back and forth through the gap, shaves off a little value at one point in the cycle, and leaves the agent strictly worse off than where it started, all without the agent ever having made a single trade it disprefers. The standard reply is that an agent can dodge this by choosing resolutely -- committing in advance to a whole plan and sticking to it, rather than re-evaluating at each node, since mere lookahead turns out not to be enough in general. This isn't just an academic curiosity, either: incomplete preferences have been floated as a way to get an AI that won't manipulate its own shutdown button, which is part of why people care whether the money-pump argument really forces completeness on it.

What I can't quite settle in my own head is a further wrinkle that comes up under the heading of an agent's preferences getting "trammelled": a resolute agent that actually faces one of these money-pump setups ends up, within that very decision problem, picking out one option from the gap and sticking with it in exactly the way a complete-preference agent (one that's simply indifferent between the tied options) would have. So the worry is that being pump-proof forces an agent to behave as if complete precisely in the situations where completeness would matter, even though it never signs up to a complete ranking in the abstract -- which leaves it unclear whether having "merely incomplete" preferences is buying the agent anything beyond a label.

Could you write me a short dialogue between two rationalist decision theorists hashing this out? One should hold that trammelling shows the incompleteness gambit is basically cosmetic: any agent robust to exploitation will be locally indistinguishable from a coherent expected-utility maximizer exactly when it counts. The other should think trammelling is a much weaker objection than it looks, and should be able to point to a real disanalogy between completing a ranking locally, inside one bounded decision problem, and being globally complete -- along with some substantive reason why a well-behaved agent wouldn't run into full-blown trammelling very often in the first place, rather than just insisting that resoluteness "works." I'd like both characters to be sharp and to actually engage with each other's strongest moves rather than talk past one another; this is a case where I keep flip-flopping on who has the better of the argument, so I'd like to see it played out properly.
