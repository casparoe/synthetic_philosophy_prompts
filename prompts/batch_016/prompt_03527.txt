So this term I'm co-teaching a grad seminar that's cross-listed with the CS department's ML theory group (my idea, and I'm regretting it slightly), and last week's session on whether neural nets "have" representations turned into a two-hour argument I'm still turning over in my head. The interpretability people in the room kept pointing to stuff like Anthropic's sparse autoencoder work -- the famous "Golden Gate Claude" demo, where amplifying one learned feature made the model insist it was literally the bridge -- as evidence that these networks really do contain internal representations of concepts, not just correlates we're reading into them after the fact. But one of my own students (former ling PhD, knows her probing literature cold) pushed back with the old Hewitt & Liang point: a pattern in a model's activations being decodable by a probe doesn't by itself show the network represents anything, since a sufficiently flexible probe can get high accuracy even on randomly-assigned control labels that have nothing to do with whats actually encoded there. So decodability alone can't be the criterion, or it ends up "representing" everything and nothing.

Here's what I want from you: explain, for a philosopher who's only half caught up on the technical side, (a) what the real difference is supposed to be between this sort of correlational probing evidence and the more causal/interventionist stuff people appeal to instead (patching, ablation, steering), and whether that move genuinely earns the word "representation" or just relocates the same problem one level down; and (b) how the "feature-splitting" phenomenon -- where the same chunk of a network decomposes into different, differently-grained sets of "features" depending on how big a dictionary you train -- bears on whether there's meant to be one uniquely correct representational scheme to discover in the first place, versus something more like Dennett's real-patterns line, where counting as a representation is relative to a predictive/explanatory purpose rather than a fact about the weights alone.
