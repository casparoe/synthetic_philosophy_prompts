I was elected to my local school board this fall, and at my first meeting a disagreement broke out between two longtime members that has stayed with me since. We were debating changes to the district's health-curriculum opt-out policy, and one member, Warren, closed his remarks with a religious justification -- "Scripture is clear that parents, not the state, form a child's conscience" -- then voted no.

Alicia told him afterward that leading with that violates public reason: board members deciding basic policy owe each other reasons anyone could accept regardless of faith. Warren said he'd read his Rawls: the "proviso" lets citizens introduce comprehensive religious doctrines into political debate, provided public reasons are supplied in due course for whatever those doctrines are said to support -- so he was exercising a permission Rawls carved out, not violating one. Alicia replied that "in due course" still means the reasons have to actually show up, and he gave none that night: no child-development or parental-rights argument, just Scripture and a vote. Warren countered that Rawls treated Lincoln's biblical rhetoric against slavery, and the abolitionists generally, as compatible with public reason. Alicia said that precedent cuts against him, not for him: their religious language was paired with public arguments from equality and freedom -- exactly what Warren skipped.

Could you go through their exchange move by move, say who has the better reading of Rawls at each step, and flag anything about how public reason and the proviso actually work that you think both of them are missing?
