Okay so this semester I'm taking a history of economic thought seminar as part of my econ PhD (yes, apparently people still study 18th century political economy for fun, dont judge me), and we just spent two weeks on Hume's economic essays -- Of Money, Of Interest, Of the Balance of Trade, Of the Jealousy of Trade, Of Public Credit, all of it. My study partner and I got into a whole thing about it after class, about how weirdly relevant a lot of this sounds once you swap out the vocabulary, and now I can't stop chewing on it.

Two things in particular. First, Hume has that argument that trade deficits are basically self-correcting through the flow of specie: gold leaves you, your prices fall, your exports get competitive again, so a country can't just bleed money forever without something else going on. He also thought getting worked up over a trading partner's prosperity (his jealousy of trade essay) was borderline deranged, since a rich neighbor is a customer, not a threat. Seperately, he's got this almost apocalyptic line in Of Public Credit about how a nation will either destroy its public debt or the debt will destroy the nation, because a government that can keep borrowing will just keep doing it until it can't.

Here's what's bugging me: all of that is built on a world of metal coins, where the money supply is physically limited and rebalances itself automatically across borders. We live in a world of fiat currency, floating exchange rates, and central banks that can expand the money supply on purpose, plus governments (US, Japan) running huge debt-to-GDP ratios for decades now without the collapse he predicted. So if you dropped Hume into 2024 and handed him a newspaper, would he look at the constant tariff/trade-deficit panic and the size of the national debt and say "yep, exactly what I warned about, it just took longer because you people figured out how to paper over the adjustment mechanism for a while"? Or would he actually have to throw out most of his framework once you take away the specie-flow mechanism that was doing all the actual work in his argument? People love quoting his "debt will destroy the nation" line like it obviously applies today, but I'm not convinced it's that simple once the monetary plumbing underneath it is totally different. Curious what you think he'd actually say.
