I keep returning to Kierkegaard's "The Present Age," the concluding portion of his 1846 book "Two Ages," written in the wake of his own public mauling by the satirical paper The Corsair. There he argues that the modern press had conjured "the public" into existence: a bodiless, placeless phantom of spectators, held together only by idle talk, which therefore never assembles and is answerable to no one. He is careful to distinguish this "public" from an assembled "crowd" — which does act, but which he judged no less corrosive to individual responsibility, hence his 1847 verdict that "the crowd is untruth." Suppose you had to explain to him a modern, algorithmically amplified pile-on, in which thousands of strangers who never meet or coordinate nonetheless converge to get a private citizen fired or harassed off a platform. Working strictly from his own categories — leveling, anonymity, reflection without passion — would this count as an instance of "the public," of "the crowd," or does it demand a third category he never articulated?
