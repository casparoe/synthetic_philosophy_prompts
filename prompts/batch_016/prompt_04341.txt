For my philosophy elective this term, we spent a class period on Leibniz's Monadology, and I haven't been able to stop thinking about his "Mill" argument since. Leibniz asks us to imagine a machine built so that it could think, sense, and perceive, then to imagine it enlarged, keeping the same proportions, until we could walk around inside it the way we would walk into a mill; he insists that all we would ever find there are parts pushing on other parts, never anything resembling a perception, and concludes that perception must belong to a simple, unextended substance (a monad) rather than to any machine or composite. In his letters to Arnauld, he makes a related move: he says that things like woodpiles and machines have only a "unity by aggregation," held together by a mind that chooses to count them as one thing, rather than the genuine, per se unity that requires some single, indivisible principle, which in us is a soul.

I brought this up at my school's philosophy club, and a friend who is really into consciousness research mentioned Giulio Tononi's Integrated Information Theory, which turns out to be obsessed with almost the same problem, just from the opposite direction. IIT claims a physical system is conscious to the extent that it "integrates" information, a quantity it calls phi, and its exclusion postulate exists specifically to explain why mere aggregates, like a heap of sand or a crowd of individually conscious people, end up with negligible phi and no group-level experience, while a sufficiently interconnected system, like a brain, can itself become a single, maximally irreducible, unified whole. So IIT is a rigorously physical and mathematical theory that nonetheless agrees with Leibniz that aggregation alone can never add up to a mind, and that consciousness requires some special, non-aggregative kind of unity.

What I can't decide, and would like your help with, is this: if Leibniz were given a complete technical account of IIT, phi, and the exclusion postulate, would he conclude that it is just a more sophisticated version of the very mill he already dismissed, since phi is still calculated over an extended system built out of separable, interacting parts, or would he recognize in phi's hunt for a "maximally irreducible" system a real, if metaphysically confused, attempt to capture his own criterion for true substancehood? Please commit to one of these readings rather than a wishy-washy "he'd probably see merit in both," and defend your answer by actually working through Leibniz's own arguments, such as the principle of sufficient reason and his reasons for thinking perception could never be found by decomposing a machine into its parts, rather than resting your case on the fact that both theories happen to use the word "unity."
