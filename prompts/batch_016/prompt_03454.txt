Last weekend a friend and I spent a good three hours arguing about anthropic reasoning, starting from Bostrom's 'presumptuous philosopher' thought experiment. In it, physicists have narrowed a theory of everything down to two equally well-supported candidates, one predicting a trillion times more observers in the universe than the other, and a philosopher who accepts the self-indication assumption announces that there's no need to run the deciding experiment: since so many more observers exist under the second theory, we should already be almost certain it's correct, just from the fact that we exist at all. We agreed this is absurd, that no one should be able to settle a question in cosmology from an armchair that way, and that the self-indication assumption has to go.

Where we ended up, though, is that once you throw out the self-indication assumption, you're left with the self-sampling assumption, on which you reason as though you were a random draw from the actual observers in your reference class, and that this commits you to taking the Doomsday Argument seriously: given that something like 100 billion humans have already lived, treating my own birth rank as a random sample among everyone who will ever live is genuine evidence against humanity having a vastly long future still ahead of it. Assuming that's right, I've been wondering how much weight this should actually get in ordinary long-term planning, decisions about career, having children, that sort of thing, relative to the more concrete empirical risks (pandemics, nuclear war, unaligned AI) that people usually cite when they worry about existential risk. Should reasoning like this really move my credences here, or would I be over-weighting it?
