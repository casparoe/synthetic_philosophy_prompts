I've been on a bit of a Frederick Douglass kick lately (read his autobiography, now I'm falling down YouTube/wiki rabbit holes about him), and I ran into something that kind of broke my brain a little. Apparently for like the first decade of his career as an abolitionist he was totally with William Lloyd Garrison that the U.S. Constitution was a pro-slavery document through and through — Garrison actually burned a copy of it in 1854, calling it "a covenant with death, and an agreement with hell." But then around 1850-51 Douglass does a complete 180 and starts arguing the opposite: that if you actually read the plain text, the Constitution is an anti-slavery document, since the words "slave" and "slavery" never once show up in it. He said he got alot of this from reading Lysander Spooner. By 1860 he's giving a whole speech on it in Glasgow where he goes through things like the three-fifths clause and the fugitive slave clause one by one — his argument on the fugitive slave clause is basically that it can't even be about slaves, since a slave "cannot even make a contract," so the clause has to mean something else. Which struck me as either genuinely clever lawyering or a bit of a stretch, honestly can't decide.

I don't have any background in law or history, I just find this stuff fascinating, and its been bugging me for days now, because I can see it both ways. Maybe Douglass was doing real close-reading, good-faith legal work. Or maybe he just needed a more hopeful story once he broke with Garrison and went into electoral politics, and worked backward to a reading that fit. And honestly Garrison's side feels almost more "honest" in a call-it-what-it-is way, even though burning the Constitution and seceding from the union doesn't seem like much of a strategy.
