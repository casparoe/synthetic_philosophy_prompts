In his 1951 Gibbs lecture, Gödel drew only a cautious disjunction from the incompleteness theorems: either the human mind's mathematical power outstrips that of any finite machine, or there exist absolutely unsolvable number-theoretic problems. He declined to assert in print which disjunct holds, though Hao Wang's later reports of conversations with him suggest Gödel privately paired anti-mechanism with what commentators call his "rationalistic optimism" — the denial that any such unsolvable problems exist. Penrose's Gödelian argument in Shadows of the Mind (1994) goes well beyond this disjunction: it requires that a mathematician can know, of herself, that she reasons soundly, a premise that critics such as David Chalmers single out as its key weakness. Suppose Gödel had lived to encounter Penrose's book: predict whether he would have endorsed, rejected, or only partially accepted this argument, and explain what in his own epistemic caution and philosophical commitments would drive that verdict.
