A friend and I spent a long evening last week arguing about Douglas Hofstadter's notion of superrationality, and specifically about whether the "symmetry argument" for mutual cooperation in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma is as airtight as it is usually presented. Her position was essentially Hofstadter's own: since two superrational agents reason identically and know this about one another, their strategies must coincide, and among the outcomes where both play the same way, cooperation simply is the rational choice because it yields the higher payoff. I pushed back that this move is too quick. Symmetry by itself only tells you that the outcome lies on the diagonal of the payoff matrix — both cooperate or both defect — since mutual defection is exactly as "symmetric" an outcome as mutual cooperation. Getting from "somewhere on the diagonal" to "cooperate" requires a further premise: that among strategy profiles consistent with symmetry, the superrational agent selects the Pareto-superior one. That premise sounds obviously correct, but it isn't obviously a piece of decision theory or logic; it looks more like a separate efficiency or fairness norm doing the actual work while riding on the coattails of "rationality." When I raised this, she replied that I was still thinking causally, as if agents were selecting among options the way ordinary game-theoretic players do, whereas superrational agents aren't choosing among a menu at all — they are both running the same deduction and landing on the same single answer, so there is no free-standing tie left to break. We went back and forth on this for a while, dragging in the chicken game and Kant's categorical imperative along the way, and neither of us managed to fully close the gap before we gave up around midnight.

I'd like a more rigorous version of my side than I managed to produce on the spot. Please construct the strongest possible argument for the claim that the standard superrationality argument for cooperation in the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma is an enthymeme: that the inference from "agents reason identically" to "agents cooperate" smuggles in an independent optimality or tie-breaking principle that is not itself entailed by the bare notion of superrationality, and that this gap survives even once you take seriously my friend's "one deduction, not equilibrium selection" reply. As part of the argument, please also address whether an appeal to Kant's categorical imperative, or to the acausal or logical dependence invoked by superrationality's modern descendants (such as timeless or functional decision theory), can close this gap without quietly importing something beyond rationality narrowly construed.
