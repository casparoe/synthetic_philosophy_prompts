So I went down a wikipedia rabbit hole last night (1am, as usual) and got stuck on Hempel's raven paradox. The setup, as I understand it: 'all ravens are black' is logically equivalent to 'everything that's not black is not a raven,' so technically every non-black thing you ever notice - my grey keyboard, a yellow banana on the counter, whatever - counts as a tiny bit of confirming evidence for 'all ravens are black,' since its an instance of that second, equivalent statement. Felt absurd to me at first, but I couldn't actually find the flaw in the logic, and I never took a philosophy class beyond a gen-ed requirement so maybe I'm just missing something obvious.

Here's why it stuck with me though: it reminds me a lot of something that's always bugged me about testing and CI at work. We treat every green checkmark as confirming evidence that 'the code is correct,' even when the test is just another slightly-varied happy-path case that's nowhere near the actual edge cases we're worried about. Running 500 more normal-input tests feels like it should barely move the needle on 'our error handling is solid,' yet in aggregate - code coverage percentage, all-green pipelines, etc - it kind of gets treated that way anyway. Brought this up with a coworker over lunch and she thinks I'm just pattern-matching two unrelated things, but it really feels like the same underlying shape of problem to me.

Could you describe the most important implications of Hempel's paradox for how engineers should actually think about what a passing test suite is (and isn't) evidence of? Specifically I'd love an actual principled reason, not just intuition, for why one nasty edge-case test should move your confidence way more than another happy-path test does, even though on paper they might look like the same 'confirming instance' of the code being correct.
