I spent thirty-five years as an experimental physicist before retiring, and for the past year I have been attending a small monthly reading group on decision theory with some former colleagues; none of us has any formal training in philosophy, so we tend to argue things out for ourselves rather than defer to the literature. At last month's meeting the topic was a thought experiment from the updateless-decision-theory literature usually called the "counterfactual mugging," and two of the members reached an impasse I have not been able to resolve since.

The version we discussed: Omega, a perfect predictor, flips a fair coin out of your view. If it comes up tails, Omega asks you for $100. If it comes up heads, Omega pays you $10,000 — but only if Omega predicted in advance that you would have paid the $100 had the coin come up tails instead. The coin did come up tails, and Omega is now asking for the $100. This is a one-time encounter — you will never meet Omega again, and nothing further hangs on your answer either way. Do you pay?

Here is roughly how the argument went:

1. Mara said you obviously should not pay. The coin has already landed tails; there is no heads-branch left in this run of events to reward you, so handing over $100 is a certain loss with no possible corresponding gain. Refusing costs nothing, and paying only costs money.

2. Julian objected that this reasoning proves too much: because Omega is a perfect predictor, whatever disposition you actually act on is the very disposition Omega would have detected had the coin come up heads instead. Someone who refuses upon hearing "tails" is thereby exactly the sort of agent who would have received nothing had the coin come up heads. Since you don't know in advance which branch you will end up in, rationality should be assessed at the level of the policy rather than the isolated act, and the policy of paying has strictly higher expected value before the coin is flipped.

3. Mara replied that "before the coin is flipped" is exactly the problem: that expected-value calculation describes a decision you are no longer facing. You now know the coin came up tails. Conditioning on new information, rather than sticking to a plan formed in ignorance of it, is what makes an agent rational in every other domain, and refusing to update here looks less like sound policy and more like superstition.

4. Julian answered that updating this way is only harmless when what you are updating on is causally and logically independent of your disposition to update on it, and here it is not: the heads-branch payout is settled by Omega's prediction of exactly the disposition Mara now wants you to exercise. An agent who updates in this situation is systematically the type who fares worse across the scenario as a whole than an agent who does not — the same structural complaint standardly leveled against causal decision theory's advice in Newcomb's problem.
