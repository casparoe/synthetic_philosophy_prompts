I don't have any background in mathematics or philosophy - I've spent most of my working life as an electrician - but for the last couple of years probability puzzles have been a private hobby of mine, something I read about in the evenings instead of watching television. It started with the St. Petersburg paradox: a coin is tossed until it lands heads, and if that first happens on toss number n you're paid 2^n dollars. Since the chance of heads first appearing on toss n is exactly (1/2)^n, every single outcome contributes exactly one dollar to the expected payout, and because there are infinitely many possible outcomes, the total expected value of the game is infinite - even though almost nobody would sensibly pay more than a few dollars to play it. I gather the usual response is that money has diminishing marginal value, or that no real casino could ever actually make good on an unbounded prize, so the infinite expected value is treated as a kind of artifact rather than a serious guide to what the game is worth.

What I hadn't come across until recently is a variant called the Pasadena game, from an old paper by Nover and Hajek, which gets discussed under the heading of the "Pasadena paradox." It's built the same way - toss a coin until it lands heads on toss n, with probability (1/2)^n - but the payout is (-1)^(n-1) times 2^n/n dollars, so wins and losses alternate and grow more slowly than in the St. Petersburg game. When I sat down and worked out the expected value term by term, I realized the sum is just the alternating harmonic series, 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which adds up to roughly 0.69 if you take the terms in that natural order. But apparently, because this series only converges conditionally rather than absolutely, the very same terms can be reshuffled - without changing the underlying game in any way - so as to add up to any number you like, or run off to positive or negative infinity, or never settle down at all. That strikes me as a genuinely different, and stranger, problem than merely being infinite, since it seems to mean the game has no expected value at all, rather than an unusually large one.

I'd like an actual explanation of this rather than a talking point, so could you compare the two paradoxes properly: in what precise sense does an "undefined" expected value pose a different, and perhaps deeper, problem for expected-utility decision theory than an "infinite" one does? And do the standard fixes for the St. Petersburg paradox - bounding utility, invoking diminishing marginal value, restricting attention to realizable finite payouts - actually carry over to defuse the Pasadena game as well, or does it resist them in a way that makes it the more serious puzzle of the two?
