I've been reporting on this little corner of AI-safety-adjacent game theory that keeps coming up whenever people speculate about how future AI's (or AI's negotiating on behalf of companies, states, whatever) might avoid catastrophic conflict, and I honestly can't tell if it's profound or just a neat trick. The idea is called "program equilibrium." It goes back to a Moshe Tennenholtz paper from 2004 (apparently an economist named McAfee had basically the same idea buried in an obscure technical report back in the 80s). The setup: instead of players in a game choosing moves directly, each one submits a little PROGRAM that gets read-access to the other player's source code before it acts. Tennenholtz showed that in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, if both sides submit a program along the lines of "cooperate if and only if you're looking at an exact copy of yourself, otherwise defect" (people call this CliqueBot), that's a genuine Nash equilibrium -- real cooperation, no repeated game, no outside enforcer. There's a fancier descendant from about ten years back that swaps exact code-matching for Löb's theorem and provability logic (so-called FairBot), billed as an improvement because CliqueBot is notoriously brittle -- add one stray whitespace character to your source and the whole thing collapses into mutual defection.

Here's what's bugging me though: apparently the same body of theory also proves a folk theorem to the effect that pretty much any individually-rational payoff -- including good old mutual defection, forever -- is just as sustainable as a program equilibrium. So the framework never actually tells two agents to cooperate, it just adds cooperation to the pile of available equilibria, right next to "everybody defects at each other forever." Which makes me suspect the whole apparatus is smuggling the hard problem back in under a different name (now it's "which program do you submit, and how do you know the other guy's transparency is genuine" instead of "do you cooperate or defect") rather than actually resolving anything.

So here's what I want from you: write me the strongest version of that skeptical argument. Argue that program equilibrium -- including the more sophisticated Löbian/FairBot version, not just the brittle CliqueBot toy case -- does not give us a real solution to the problem of rational cooperation absent external enforcement, but merely relocates that problem one level up. I'd especially like the argument to engage seriously with why FairBot is supposed to be the harder case for a skeptic, rather than just dismissing the whole research program via the easy CliqueBot objection.
