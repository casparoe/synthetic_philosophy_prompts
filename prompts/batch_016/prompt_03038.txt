While reporting on the growing use of autonomous AI agents to negotiate, trade, and draft contracts on our behalf, I find myself returning to Moshe Tennenholtz's 2004 concept of "program equilibrium" — the result that if players submit programs able to read each other's source code before acting, mutual cooperation can be sustained as an equilibrium even in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, with no external enforcer required. Please describe the most important implications of program equilibrium for how we should design, trust, or regulate AI agents (or self-executing smart contracts) that can inspect, or credibly commit to, one another's code before striking a deal. I'm especially interested in where this elegant theoretical fix is most likely to break down once real incentives to misrepresent one's own source code, obfuscate it, or exploit the sheer difficulty of verifying arbitrary programs enter the picture, rather than in a restatement of the textbook cooperative case.
