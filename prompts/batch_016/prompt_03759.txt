I'm teaching a seminar on early modern political philosophy this term, and last week we got completely bogged down on a Pascal fragment I'd always just skimmed past for the famous one-liner without ever actually working out what the argument underneath it is. It's from the cluster his editors title "Justice and the Reason of Effects." In Trotter's translation (fragments 297–298 in the old Brunschvicg numbering, which most English editions still follow), it reads:

"Veri juris.—We have it no more; if we had it, we should take conformity to the customs of a country as the rule of justice. It is here that, not finding justice, we have found force, etc.

Justice, might.—It is right that what is just should be obeyed; it is necessary that what is strongest should be obeyed. Justice without might is helpless; might without justice is tyrannical. Justice without might is gainsaid, because there are always offenders; might without justice is condemned. We must then combine justice and might and, for this end, make what is just strong, or what is strong just. Justice is subject to dispute; might is easily recognised and is not disputed. So we cannot give might to justice, because might has gainsaid justice and has declared that it is she herself who is just. And thus, being unable to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just."

(Pascals own word is actually "force" — Trotter just renders it "might" throughout, for what its worth, though I don't think that changes much.)

Here's my problem: half the seminar read this as basically Hobbesian, maybe even proto-ideology-critique — justice has no independent reality at all, it's just whatever force enforces, full stop. But a few fragments earlier, in the bit about justice flipping at the Pyrenees, Pascal says almost in passing "...there are natural laws; but good reason once corrupted has corrupted all" — which sounds like a genuinely different claim: there IS a real justice, we've just lost reliable access to it, so force rushes in to fill the gap left by our corrupted reason. Those don't seem like the same view to me at all — one is debunking justice-talk, the other is making an epistemic point while leaving justice's reality intact. Can you help me work out which of these the 297–298 passage actually commits him to? And does it matter that the whole cluster is framed around the "reason of effects" — I have a hunch that his usual three-tier move (simple people who accept customs at face value, the "semi-learned" who see through them and turn cynical, and the truly wise who circle back around to endorse them for different reasons) is supposed to tell us how to take the justice fragment, but I couldn't reconstruct the connection well enough to defend it in front of the class.
