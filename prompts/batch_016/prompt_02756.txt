I'm taking a graduate-level seminar on epistemology this term, and last week's discussion section turned into a genuine argument between two classmates that our TA never really settled. Some brief context in case it's useful: Fitch's paradox of knowability starts from the anti-realist idea that every truth is in principle knowable -- formally, for any true p, it is possible for someone to know that p -- together with the very modest assumption that at least one truth is currently unknown by anyone. From just these two premises, Fitch's proof derives the seemingly absurd conclusion that every truth is actually known by someone.

Here is roughly how the exchange went. (1) Marco argued that the paradox is fatal to any anti-realist or verificationist theory of truth: since assuming universal knowability collapses straight into full-blown omniscience, nobody can hold onto the knowability thesis in its original, unrestricted form, so the whole position is simply refuted. (2) Aisha objected that this moves too fast, invoking Neil Tennant's response: the derivation only goes through because it is applied to one very special, self-undermining proposition -- "p is true and p is not known" -- and since knowing that particular conjunction is itself provably inconsistent, no anti-realist needs to say it is knowable in the first place. Restrict the knowability principle to what Tennant calls "Cartesian" statements (roughly, statements it isn't self-defeating to know), and the paradox disappears while every statement anti-realists actually care about is still claimed to be knowable. (3) Marco replied that the restriction looks suspiciously convenient: Aisha has carved out exactly, and only, the proposition that breaks the theory, and unless there is some independent reason to treat "Cartesian" statements as a natural kind rather than as a description of "whatever refutes the theory," this isn't a solution so much as a relabeling of the counterexample. (4) Aisha responded that the distinction isn't ad hoc at all, since propositions that turn out inconsistent the moment they are known belong to a recognizably different logical species than ordinary empirical or mathematical claims -- and, she added, Dorothy Edgington's quite different fix, which doesn't exclude any statements from the principle the way Tennant's restriction does, but instead reformulates knowability itself by inserting an "actually" operator, blocks the very same derivation by a completely different route, which she took as evidence that some repair along these lines has to be correct.

Could you go through this move by move and say who has the better of it at each step, not just who comes out ahead overall, and note anything important both of them are missing? I'm particularly stuck on two things: whether there is a genuinely principled way to defend Tennant's restriction against Marco's ad-hocness charge in move (3) that amounts to more than "well, it blocks the paradox," and whether Aisha is right, in move (4), that the mere existence of Edgington's very differently structured solution actually supports her side, rather than cutting against it.
