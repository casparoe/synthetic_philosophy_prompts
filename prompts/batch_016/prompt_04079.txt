I don't have any formal training in philosophy, but I've spent the last year or so studying it on my own through podcasts and secondhand books, and I recently became rather absorbed in Avicenna's account of the soul. The argument that has stuck with me most is his "flying man" thought experiment from the Book of Healing. He asks the reader to imagine a person created instantaneously as a fully grown adult, suspended in empty air, with his limbs spread apart so that they cannot touch one another or anything else, and with every one of his senses blocked, so that he has no perception even of his own body. Avicenna's claim is that this person would nonetheless affirm his own existence immediately and without any doubt. Nearly every popular account I've come across treats this as essentially the same insight Descartes would reach roughly six hundred years later with "I think, therefore I am," and treats the two thinkers as having arrived at it completely independently of one another, since Descartes, from what I can tell, never had access to Avicenna's work on the subject.

This has stayed with me because of what it seems to imply for the mind-body problem. If two major philosophers, working centuries apart in unconnected traditions, converged on the same proof that the self can be known with certainty apart from any awareness of the body, that looks like unusually strong evidence that the mind or soul really is distinct from the body. It seems like this should count for more than either argument would on its own, since independent convergence is usually a mark of truth rather than coincidence. I raised this with a friend who works in neuroscience, and although she remained unconvinced by dualism, she couldn't quite tell me what was wrong with treating two independent proofs as better than one. So I would like to know: given that Avicenna and Descartes appear to have reached the same argument for dualism from completely different starting points, why do most contemporary philosophers of mind still treat dualism as such a marginal position? Is there a flaw in treating this kind of convergence as strong confirmation, or have philosophers simply not engaged much with Avicenna's version of the argument?
