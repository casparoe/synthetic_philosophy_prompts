I spent my career as a physical chemist, which perhaps explains why questions about causation never troubled me professionally: you trust the rate laws, run the reaction, and do not lose sleep over what "necessary connection" could possibly mean. Since retiring I have had more time for philosophy, and a friend who works on Islamic intellectual history recently pointed me toward al-Ghazālī's discussion of causality in the Tahāfut al-Falāsifa. I was particularly taken with the famous example of the fire and the cotton, where he denies that the fire itself produces the burning and holds instead that the two events are merely conjoined by God's decree, with no necessitating power running from the one to the other, so that God could in principle create the burning without the fire, or the contact without the burning.

What stopped me short was learning how precisely al-Ghazālī seems to define necessity: a connection counts as necessary, on his view, only if it is inconceivable that the two things come apart, and since we can perfectly well conceive of cotton failing to burn on contact with fire, the connection fails to be necessary. That is close enough to Hume's own separability criterion, and to his talk of constant conjunction, that I gather there is genuine scholarship describing Hume's argument as having a kind of occasionalist ancestry running back through medieval theology. This surprised me, since every popular account I had previously encountered treated Hume's skepticism about necessary connection as a wholly novel, thoroughly secular achievement of eighteenth-century empiricism.

What I cannot work out is exactly where the two positions part ways once this surface convergence is granted. Is the real difference simply that al-Ghazālī supplies a substantive answer to the further question of what, if anything, explains the regularities we do observe, namely continuous and direct divine action, whereas Hume declines to answer that question at all and relocates the entire matter into the psychology of the observer? Or is occasionalism committed to something stronger that a Humean regularity theorist need not accept, perhaps concerning whether the "customary course of nature" has any genuine stability as opposed to being liable to change at any single instant without contradiction? I understand, too, that Averroes charged al-Ghazālī with a position incompatible with there being any natural science at all; I have not been able to work out whether an analogous charge was ever pressed against Hume with equal force, or whether there is some asymmetry between the two cases that would make the objection land differently. I would be very grateful for help sorting out what is genuinely shared between these two views on causation and what only appears that way at first glance.
