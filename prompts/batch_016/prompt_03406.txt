Last month I served on a jury for the first time, and something from the deliberations has stayed with me ever since. Once most of us had settled on a reading of the evidence, any testimony that cut against it was dismissed as unreliable, precisely because it conflicted with what we already "knew" from the rest of the record. Looking into why this troubled me, I came across what philosophers call the dogmatism paradox, an argument traceable to Saul Kripke: if I know some proposition h is true, I thereby know that any future evidence against h would be misleading, and so I am entitled to discount it before I have even seen it. I would like a short story, set somewhere other than a courtroom, that walks a character through exactly this chain of reasoning, one locally sound step at a time, until it hardens into something that looks, from the outside, like willful blindness. Please do not make the character an obvious fanatic or straw man; what unsettled me on the jury was precisely how reasonable each step felt from the inside.
