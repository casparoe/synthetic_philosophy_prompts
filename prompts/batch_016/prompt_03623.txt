In my formal epistemology seminar we've covered van Fraassen's Reflection Principle -- that one's current credence in some proposition A, conditional on one's anticipated future credence in A being r, should itself equal r -- and the diachronic Dutch book argument he offers for it, which is explicitly modeled on the standard Dutch book argument for conditionalization: in both cases a bookie is supposed to exploit a mismatch between an agent's credences at two different times to guarantee a net loss. I've since come across the claim that the two arguments aren't actually on a par -- that the bookie's strategy against a Reflection-violator implicitly requires an assumption about the bookie's access to the agent's future credences that the conditionalization argument doesn't need -- but I haven't been able to reconstruct why this asymmetry is supposed to hold. Could you explain what's structurally different between the two Dutch-book setups, and why this extra assumption, if genuine, would undercut Reflection's justification without also undercutting Conditionalization's?
