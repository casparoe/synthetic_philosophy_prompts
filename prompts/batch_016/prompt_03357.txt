Been down a Hume rabbit hole lately (the monetary essays, not the epistemology stuff everyone quotes) and it's messing with my head a bit. His case against mercantilism in "Of the Balance of Trade" rests on the specie-flow mechanism: run a trade surplus, gold flows in, domestic prices rise, your exports get less competitive, gold flows back out again — self correcting, nothing to worry about. But that whole mechanism only works if money is a physically scarce commodity that actually has to move across borders when a country runs a deficit. None of that applies to a fiat reserve currency, where the dollars that leave the US for Chinese exporters mostly just come back as demand for treasuries instead of forcing any price adjustment, which is a big chunk of why the US has run deficits for decades without the correction he predicted ever showing up. And in "Of Interest" he goes out of his way to argue that rates track commerce and frugality, not the sheer quantity of money sloshing around, which sounds a lot like what people said in 2020-21 to explain why "money printer go brr" didn't produce the immediate inflation/rate spike everyone with a quantity-theory prior expected.

So if you sat 1752-Hume down and walked him through the Fed's balance sheet, reserve currency status, and the US-China trade imbalance, does his own framework predict this is unstable and overdue for a correction, or would he just shrug and say its all consistent with what he already argued (money's the oil, not the wheels)? curious what you actually think he'd say here, reasoning from what he actually wrote rather than the "Hume=quantity theory guy" cliff notes version.
