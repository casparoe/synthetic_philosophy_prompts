So this came up at an in-person rationalist-adjacent meetup a couple weeks back and I still can't decide who won. We'd been doing a slow group read of Aaronson's Quantum Computing Since Democritus (we'd just hit the "Penrose" chapter, ch. 11) and it turned into an actual argument between two people in the group, Nate and Sasha, over whether Gödel's incompleteness theorems actually show anything about minds not being machines. I've seen the dumb internet version of this fight a hundred times (I mostly lurk on LW/ACX but this is exactly the kind of thread that gets me to delurk), but this one had more real moves in it than usual and both of them clearly new the material, so I can't just wave it away. Roughly reconstructing it from memory:

(1) Nate opened with the basic Lucas move: for any consistent formal system F strong enough to encode arithmetic, Gödel's theorem hands you a sentence G(F) that's true but unprovable within F, and a mathematician reasoning about F from outside can see that G(F) has to be true given that F is consistent, even though no machine confined to F could ever output it as a theorem; since this works for any F you propose as a model of a mathematician, no fixed formal system or algorithm can capture everything a mind can, in principle, come to know, so minds aren't equivalent to machines. (2) Sasha shot back that this smuggles in exactly what Gödel's second theorem rules out: to even run the argument you have to already know F is consistent, but no sufficiently strong consistent system can prove its own consistency from the inside, so if Nate himself is (or is modeled by) some system H, he's in no better position to certify Con(H) than a machine running H would be — he's just granting himself something for free — and that's before you even ask whether humans are consistent at all, given how comfortably people hold contradictory beliefs. (3) Nate conceded the plain "just-consistency" version has that hole, which is exactly why he pivoted to Penrose's fix from Shadows of the Mind: instead of deriving Con(F) as a theorem from nothing, you grant as a premise that you take your own reasoning to be sound, not merely consistent, meaning whatever F would certify as true really is true; add "I am F" to F as a further axiom to get F', and if you really are F', then since you know you're sound you know F' is sound, so you know its Gödel sentence G(F') is true — but F' itself could never derive G(F') internally, that's just Gödel's theorem again — and since you're supposed to be F', that's a flat contradiction, so the only way out is to deny that any F could have been the whole of you to begin with. (4) Sasha said fine, but now everything rests on "I know I'm sound," which is a much bigger ask than mere consistency, and she doesn't buy that working mathematicians actually have it — she brought up that a "proof" of the four-color theorem was accepted as basically settled for eleven years before anyone found the gap in it, and if the mathematical community can be confidently, unassailably wrong about something for over a decade, then "I know my mathematical convictions are sound" is exactly the premise nobody's entitled to just help themselves to.

They basically ran out of steam rather than resolving anything, and neither one seemed to think the other was dumb (which, given how much weight this argument sometimes has to bear in debates about whether an AI could ever fully match human mathematical insight, felt like a weirdly quiet ending). Could you go through it move by move instead of just declaring an overall winner — for each of the four numbered moves say who's got the better of it, or whether it's genuinely a wash — and then separately give me your own honest read on whether the Gödel argument, in its strongest available form, actually lands a real blow against mechanism, or whether the mechanist always has another escape hatch available.
