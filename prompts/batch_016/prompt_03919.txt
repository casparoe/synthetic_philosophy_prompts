I'm assembling a small cycle of short fiction built around historical thought experiments rather than contemporary premises — closer in spirit to Borges's philosophical parables, or to what Ted Chiang does with a harder conceptual edge, than to conventional literary realism. The piece I keep circling but haven't yet drafted is meant to grow out of Avicenna's "flying man" argument, which a friend who teaches medieval philosophy pressed on me last year and which has quietly stayed with me since.

As I understand it, Avicenna asks us to imagine a man created all at once — an adult, fully rational, but without a single moment of prior sensation. His eyes are veiled; he is suspended in a void, or in perfectly still air, so that nothing buffets him enough to be felt; his limbs are held apart from one another and from the rest of him, so that no part of his body ever touches any other part. He sets the scenario out in his treatment of the soul in The Cure (sometimes translated The Healing; the Kitab al-Shifa), and again, more tersely, in the Remarks and Admonitions. Avicenna's claim is that such a man, the moment he is capable of thought at all, would affirm without hesitation that he exists, and that he would affirm this bare self — he calls it, in different passages, dhat ("essence") or anniyya ("individual existence") — without affirming any hand, organ, heart, or brain, and without attributing to it any length, breadth, or depth. From this he draws a genuinely large conclusion: that self-awareness requires no sensory mediation whatsoever, and therefore that the self is a substance really distinct from the body. Readers ever since have argued about whether this anticipates Descartes' cogito or is doing something else entirely, and a persistent objection — one I find hard to dismiss — is that none of this shows the man's self-awareness isn't simply a property of a nervous system he happens not to know he has; his ignorance of his own brain hardly proves his self-awareness has no need of it. There's also a stray, probably apocryphal detail I can't quite let go of: the story that Avicenna drafted this thought experiment while imprisoned in a fortress, his own body about as immobile and cut off as the flying man's. You're welcome to let that irony inform the story, or to set it aside entirely — I mention it only because it's the detail that first made the argument feel like fiction to me rather than an exercise in logic.

What I'd like from you is a short story, somewhere in the neighborhood of 800 to 1,500 words, that gives this scenario flesh in a modern or near-future setting of your own choosing — an experimental subject in a sensory-deprivation trial engineered to strip away proprioception as well as the five senses, a mind instantiated in code the instant before any sensor is switched on, a patient surfacing from anesthesia with some vanishingly rare total deafferentation, or anything else that earns its place — provided it actually honors Avicenna's specific constraints: instantaneous origin, zero antecedent sensory or bodily history, and limbs and organs kept from all contact, including with each other. I'm not interested in a story that simply re-skins a sensory-deprivation tank for atmosphere; I want the felt certainty of existence and the nagging question of what, if anything, that certainty proves, to both be alive on the page at once — ideally without a single line that reads as though it wandered in from a philosophy seminar.
