I'm putting together a feature for a science magazine on the idea of scientific progress, and one thread of research has led me into deeper water than I expected. Karl Popper introduced the notion of "verisimilitude" specifically to rescue his falsificationism from an awkward consequence: if every theory is eventually shown to be false, in what sense can a later theory be said to be better than the one it replaced? His proposed fix was that a false theory could still have more verisimilitude — be closer to the truth — than a rival false theory. Digging into the history for the piece, I learned that in 1974 David Miller and Pavel Tichý independently proved a theorem showing that, under Popper's own formal definition, no false theory can ever come out as having more verisimilitude than any other false theory, which seems to mean the definition can't perform the one task it was designed for.

I mentioned this to a physicist friend over dinner, and it crystallized into what I now take to be the natural conclusion for my article: that the whole idea of theories "getting closer to the truth" — language scientists reach for constantly, whether comparing Newtonian mechanics to general relativity or early continental drift to modern plate tectonics — was shown, fifty years ago, to be mathematically incoherent, and that anyone still using it is unknowingly leaning on a piece of Popperian philosophy that specialists quietly wrote off as a dead end. Before I commit that framing to print, I'd like a sanity check from someone with a firmer grip on the technical literature than I have: is this an accurate reading of what Miller and Tichý actually established, or am I smuggling in a conflation somewhere? I'd be grateful for a precise account of exactly what the theorem does and doesn't show, and of what happened to the concept of verisimilitude in philosophy of science in the decades after 1974.
