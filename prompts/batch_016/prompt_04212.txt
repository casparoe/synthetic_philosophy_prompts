Jonathan Vogel's case of Roxanne is the classic illustration of what he calls "bootstrapping": she comes to believe that her car's gas gauge is reliable simply by noting, on many occasions, both what the gauge reads and what she consequently believes the tank contains, and then inferring inductively, from this run of matched observations, that the gauge must be accurate — all without ever checking the tank independently of the gauge. Stewart Cohen frames the same structural worry as the "problem of easy knowledge": many attractive epistemological theories seem to make knowledge of a source's reliability far too cheap to acquire. I rely every morning on a wrist-worn fitness tracker's readings of my resting heart rate and blood-oxygen level, and, like Roxanne, I have no way of checking these readings except by consulting the tracker itself on other occasions. Please describe the most important implications of the bootstrapping/easy-knowledge debate for whether, and under what conditions, I could ever become entitled to trust the tracker's reports, engaging with at least one serious attempt in the literature to show that such bootstrapping need not be vicious — for example, Michael Bergmann's distinction between malignant and benign epistemic circularity, or Crispin Wright's account of non-evidential "entitlement."
