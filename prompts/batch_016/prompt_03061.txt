Over dinner last week, a friend of mine who is finishing a doctorate in political theory and I got into a long argument about Rawls's proviso -- the rule, from "The Idea of Public Reason Revisited," that citizens may bring their comprehensive doctrines, religious or otherwise, into political debate at any time, provided that in due course they supply properly public reasons for whatever those doctrines are being used to support. Her view is that the proviso is genuinely even-handed, since it binds Kantians and utilitarians exactly as it binds Catholics and evangelicals; she also invoked Rawls's own discussion of the abolitionists and King, whose openly religious rhetoric is supposed to have satisfied the proviso without any loss of religious substance, as proof that the demand is livable even for full-throated religious speech.

I kept arguing that the abolitionist case actually gives away the point: it worked only because the religious reasons in play and the independently available public values of liberty and equality happened to converge, and that the real test of the proviso is what it demands of citizens whose comprehensive doctrine does not converge with the available public values in that way. There, I think, having to produce a separate public rationale "in due course" is not a symmetric constraint on all citizens alike but a much heavier tax on citizens whose reasons are essentially, and not merely contingently, religious. She maintains that I'm only restating the old complaint that public reason is secular reason wearing a disguise, which she thinks Rawls already defused by insisting that secular comprehensive doctrines face exactly the same requirement. We went back and forth for over an hour without either of us budging, and I still have the nagging feeling that I'm missing some standard rejoinder to my own position.

Could you set out the strongest version of my side of this: that the proviso fails to achieve genuine even-handedness between religious and secular citizens because the asymmetry is structural rather than a mere sociological accident -- not simply that religious reasons happen, as things stand, to be harder to re-express? I'd like the argument to engage directly with the two moves my friend used against me -- the claim that secular comprehensive doctrines are bound in exactly the same way, and the appeal to the abolitionists and King as evidence that the proviso can be satisfied without remainder -- and to work through the actual apparatus Rawls builds for this purpose (the wide view of public political culture, the duty of civility, the idea of an overlapping consensus) rather than resting on the general observation that public reason reflects a broadly liberal, post-Protestant milieu. If, having tried all that, you don't think the position actually survives, say so, but only after you've given it a genuinely serious run.
