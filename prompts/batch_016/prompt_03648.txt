A friend of mine is choosing between a costly nineteenth-century Italian violin and a much cheaper new one that her teacher says sounds just as good. She brought up the Fritz-Curtin blind tests, where soloists who couldn't see the instruments failed to reliably tell old Italian violins (including several Stradivaris) from new ones, and on average preferred the new ones. Two friends at the table began arguing about what this actually shows.

Naomi said this settles it: if experts can't detect a difference blind, paying extra for the 'real thing' over a perceptually indistinguishable copy is paying for signaling, not for any property the object itself has. Ravi said that's too fast -- a perfect forgery of a painting is worth less than the original to almost anyone who has considered it, and that isn't just fear that a difference might surface later; the object's actual history seems to matter independent of how it strikes the senses. Naomi asked what that could mean if the history never affects any experience or ability anyone will have -- isn't naming the history just relabeling the preference rather than explaining it? Ravi answered that we do this elsewhere without embarrassment: we rank a real fossil above an indistinguishable replica, and a clean athletic record above an identical one achieved through doping, even though examining either pair reveals nothing different. Naomi replied that the doping case only has force because a rule was broken, which has no counterpart with the violin, so Ravi still hadn't said what the extra ingredient actually is.

Could you adjudicate this move by move -- who has the better of it at each stage, whether Naomi's rule-violation objection really disposes of both of Ravi's examples or only one of them, and what a good answer to her final challenge would look like?
