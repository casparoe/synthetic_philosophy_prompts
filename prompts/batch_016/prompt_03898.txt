ok so this weekend I got into a whole thing with my friend Priya about whether my cat actually "knows" anything when she starts meowing at the cupboard where the treats live, or if that's just pavlovian reflex all the way down with nobody home. that kind of spiraled (as these things do) into a bigger argument about how anyone is supposed to figure out how much mind to attribute to a given animal in the first place, and Priya brought up this "law of parsimony" thing from a 19th century comparative psychologist called Lloyd Morgan that apparently the early behaviorists loved to cite. I went and dug up the actual text afterward and now I'm honestly more confused than when we started, because there's a wrinkle in it that most summaries leave out and that we couldn't agree on how to read.

the famous bit, from his 1894 book An Introduction to Comparative Psychology, goes:

"In no case may we interpret an action as the outcome of the exercise of a higher psychical faculty, if it can be interpreted as the outcome of the exercise of one which stands lower in the psychological scale."

fine, sounds like Occam's razor for animal minds, default to the dumber explanation. but in the 1903 revised edition, a bit later in the same discussion, he tacks on a caveat that basically never makes it into the textbook one-liner version of "the canon":

"...it should be added, lest the range of the principle be misunderstood, that the canon by no means excludes the interpretation of a particular activity in terms of the higher processes, if we already have independent evidence of the occurrence of these higher processes in the animal under observation."

Priya's take is that this second bit basically guts the whole principle: "always assume the simpler explanation unless you already have good reason to think the more complicated one is true" is just a description of how evidence works generally, so the canon isn't adding any actual content once you tack that bit on. I argued there's still something substantive left, namely a claim about where the default sits, i.e. what you're entitled to assume before you have that independent evidence. we went back and forth for like an hour on her porch and got nowhere, and neither of us are philosophers so we probably butchered something. can you actually help settle this: does Morgan's own caveat reduce his canon to an empty truism, or is there a real reading where it still does substantive methodological work? and if there's real content left, can you spell out what "independent evidence" could even mean here that doesn't just quietly smuggle back in the very question (does this animal have the higher faculty) that the canon was supposed to help us answer in the first place?
