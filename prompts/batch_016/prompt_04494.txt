Over the past few months I have been volunteering for a small nonprofit that supplies Afghan girls with phones, laptops, and data plans so they can keep learning after the Taliban barred girls from school beyond the sixth grade. Around the same time, in a night class on colonial Latin American literature, we spent two sessions on Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz, focusing on her 1691 "Respuesta a Sor Filotea de la Cruz," the letter in which she defended her lifelong pursuit of learning after a bishop, writing under a woman's pseudonym, urged her to concentrate on religious devotion rather than secular study. The parallel struck me immediately: as a child, Sor Juana begged her mother to let her disguise herself as a boy so she could enter the university, was refused, and taught herself instead out of her grandfather's library.

What complicates the parallel, though, is a detail from the Respuesta that I had not fully appreciated before: Sor Juana does not hold up her self-education as proof that books and determination are all a woman really needs. She explicitly laments having studied for so long without a teacher to guide or correct her, and her mature intellectual life depended heavily on the protection of the vicereines who patronized her and on a circle of learned correspondents with whom she could argue and exchange ideas. This makes me wonder how she would actually judge the situation in Afghanistan today, where large numbers of girls now receive their only schooling through a patchwork of underground classes, online lessons, and self-directed study using smuggled phones and laptops, much of it delivered remotely by volunteers the students may never meet in person, with no institution able to certify what they have learned.

If Sor Juana could see this, do you think she would treat it as a vindication of the case she made in the Respuesta, or would she, of all people, be quickest to point out what it still lacks compared to what she herself fought so hard to obtain? I would like your best reconstruction of how she would actually weigh this, grounded in the specific arguments she made about knowledge, teachers, and protection, rather than in her general reputation as an early defender of women's right to learn.
