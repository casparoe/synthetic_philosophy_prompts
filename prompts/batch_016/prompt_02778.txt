Random question that's been eating away at me while I work on a story about vision-restoration surgeries. I stumbled into this old 17th-century thought experiment called the Molyneux problem — where a person born blind, who's learned to tell a cube from a sphere by touch, suddenly gains sight. Could they tell which shape is which just by looking, before they'd ever touched them? Turns out this isn't just an armchair puzzle anymore: there's a research group, I believe it's called Project Prakash out of MIT, that's actually done cataract surgeries on kids and young adults in India who'd been blind since birth, and tested them on more or less this exact scenario. My editor thinks this is a weird tangent for a piece that's supposed to be about surgical technique, but I can't let it go.

Here's what's bugging me. The historical split was basically: Locke sided with Molyneux and said no, the newly-sighted person wouldn't be able to tell right away — and he was careful to specify "at first sight." Leibniz disagreed and said yes, but with a big caveat: he thought the person needed some time to actually use their new eyes before you could fairly test them, not be quizzed the second the bandages came off. And weirdly, that's pretty much what the real study found — the subjects were basically at chance right after surgery, but a few days later, some of them suddenly could match shapes by sight that they'd only ever known by touch.

So, if you could sit Leibniz down and show him this data, do you think he'd take it as vindication, since the timeline lines up with his own condition almost exactly? Or would that be giving him too much credit, since the fast improvement might be driven by some low-level statistical, associative learning process that has nothing to do with the kind of concious geometric reasoning he actually described? Genuinely torn on this one and curious what you think.
