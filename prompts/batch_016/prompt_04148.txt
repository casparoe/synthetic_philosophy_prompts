Ok so about a month ago I fell into one of those youtube-into-wikipedia-into-random-blog rabbit holes about Kurt Gödel (the incompleteness theorem guy) and found out that late in his life he wrote a bunch of letters to his elderly mom basically trying to logically prove to her that there's an afterlife, no religion needed, just "reason." I got kind of obsessed with this because it seemed like such a weird thing for a hardcore logician to spend his time on, and I ended up writing up my own little version of his argument plus two objections I thought of myself. I don't have any philosophy training (closest I got was an intro to logic gen-ed course I barely passed), so I honestly have no idea if I'm representing him fairly or if my objections are the kind of thing a first-year philosophy student would laugh at. Would love your honest take on both before I annoy my friends with this at dinner again.

Here's my reconstruction: Gödel's core move is that the world is "rationally organized," which he thinks is obvious from how orderly and law-governed everything in nature is (that's basically what science finds everywhere it looks). If the world as a whole is rational, then human lives, being part of that world, should also make rational sense. But they clearly don't, at least not on their own — we're born with this huge amount of potential to grow and learn and become wiser, and then we die having barely scratched the surface of it, kind of like someone pouring a house's foundation and then just walking away forever. Since a truly rational world wouldn't leave that potential permanently wasted, there has to be some further existence where we actually get to finish the job. No afterlife = the world is ultimately pointless, which he's not willing to except, so afterlife it is.

Two things bug me about this though. First, he seems to slide from "rational" meaning "orderly / follows laws" (fine, granted) to "rational" meaning "meaningful / has a point," like those are obviously the same thing, but they don't seem to be to me — plenty of orderly, law-following processes (an avalanche, a dying star) definitly don't have "a point" in the sense he needs. Second, even if you buy that unfulfilled potential demands a sequel, why does that rule only apply to humans? Acorns that get eaten before becoming trees, species that go extinct a few generations after evolving some cool new trait, whole civilizations that collapse mid-project — nature is basically one giant pile of wasted potential. Either the same logic means practically everything gets an afterlife, or he needs some extra reason why humans specifically are owed a sequel, and I don't think "we're able to learn from our mistakes" (which I've seen mentioned as his actual answer to this) really earns that on its own. Curious whether I'm missing something obvious here or whether these are actually the standard objections people raise against this kind of argument.
