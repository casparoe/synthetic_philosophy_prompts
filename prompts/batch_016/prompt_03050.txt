I spent my working life as a physicist, and only since retiring have I begun reading Newton's philosophical and theological writings rather than just the physics he is remembered for. I had always taken his "absolute space" from the Principia to be a bare mathematical postulate, useful for defining true motion and nothing more, so I was unsettled to find him giving it theological content in the Opticks. Two passages from the queries appended to the later editions, in particular, I cannot reconcile.

At the end of Query 28, he concludes from the phenomena that "there is a Being incorporeal, living, intelligent, omnipresent, who in infinite Space, as it were in his Sensory, sees the things themselves intimately, and throughly perceives them, and comprehends them wholly by their immediate presence to himself: Of which things the Images only carried through the Organs of Sense into our little Sensoriums, are there seen and beheld by that which in us perceives and thinks."

In Query 31 he goes further, describing God as "a powerful ever-living Agent, who being in all Places, is more able by his Will to move the Bodies within his boundless uniform Sensorium, and thereby to form and reform the Parts of the Universe, than we are by our Will to move the Parts of our own Bodies" – only to add immediately that "we are not to consider the World as the Body of God ... He is an uniform Being, void of Organs, Members or Parts."

I cannot work out how literally to take this "Sensory"/"sensorium" language. Is Newton claiming that God apprehends bodies through something functionally like a perceptual organ, with infinite space itself serving that role, or is this only a figure of speech for omnipresence, with no further content than saying God is everywhere? And if it is merely figurative, why does he take such evident care to block one anthropomorphic reading – God as the world's soul – while apparently leaving in place another, no less strange: that God literally perceives bodies "by their immediate presence to himself"? I would welcome help situating this passage within his wider theological commitments.
