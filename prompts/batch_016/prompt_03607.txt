I'm taking a research seminar this term that's devoted to a slow, section-by-section reading of Nietzsche's *On the Genealogy of Morality*, and each week we submit a short reflection paper on a passage of our choosing before the seminar meets. I've picked the opening of the Second Essay for next week, but I've run into an interpretive snag I can't resolve on my own, and my professor's office hours are booked solid until after the paper is due, so I'm hoping you can help me think it through.

The passage is Nietzsche's description of the "sovereign individual" in Second Essay, section 2. In the Horace Samuel translation we've been assigned (I know Kaufmann phrases a few of the key terms slightly differently, but we've been told to cite Samuel for consistency), it reads:

"This is simply the long history of the origin of responsibility. That task of breeding an animal which can make promises, includes, as we have already grasped, as its condition and preliminary, the more immediate task of first making man to a certain extent, necessitated, uniform, like among his like, regular, and consequently calculable. The immense work of what I have called, 'morality of custom' (cp. Dawn of Day, Aphs. 9, 14, and 16), the actual work of man on himself during the longest period of the human race, his whole prehistoric work, finds its meaning, its great justification (in spite of all its innate hardness, despotism, stupidity, and idiocy) in this fact: man, with the help of the morality of customs and of social strait-waistcoats, was made genuinely calculable. If, however, we place ourselves at the end of this colossal process, at the point where the tree finally matures its fruits, when society and its morality of custom finally bring to light that to which it was only the means, then do we find as the ripest fruit on its tree the sovereign individual, that resembles only himself, that has got loose from the morality of custom, the autonomous 'super-moral' individual (for 'autonomous' and 'moral' are mutually-exclusive terms),—in short, the man of the personal, long, and independent will, competent to promise, and we find in him a proud consciousness (vibrating in every fibre), of what has been at last achieved and become vivified in him, a genuine consciousness of power and freedom, a feeling of human perfection in general."

Taken on its own, this reads like an unambiguous ideal: a figure who has broken free of custom and now stands "autonomous" and "super-moral" above it. What's bothering me is that this sits uneasily next to something Nietzsche says only a few dozen pages earlier, in First Essay, section 13, where he mocks the very idea of a free, self-identical agent standing behind its deeds, insisting that "there is no such substratum, there is no 'being' behind doing, working, becoming; 'the doer' is a mere appanage to the action. The action is everything." There, the belief that a strong man could just as well have acted weakly—that the bird of prey could have chosen to be a lamb—is treated as precisely the fiction slave morality needs in order to hold the strong responsible, and therefore blamable, for their strength. So how does the same book, a few sections later, turn around and praise a figure defined by exactly this kind of durable, self-possessed, promise-keeping will? I've come across secondary readings that split on this: some treat the sovereign individual as Nietzsche's genuine picture of freedom achieved on the far side of morality, while others argue the figure is really just the most refined and internalized product of the disciplinary "morality of custom" the passage has just finished describing, and so is continuous with, rather than opposed to, the bad conscience the essay goes on to diagnose two sections later. Could you help me work through the actual wording of section 2 (rather than general summaries of master/slave morality, which I already have a handle on) and tell me which of these two readings the text itself better supports, or whether the tension is doing something more deliberately unresolved?
