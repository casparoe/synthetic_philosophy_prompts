I'm about a third of the way into a novel built around a decades-long rivalry between two siblings who jointly inherited their late father's cattle ranch. Mara left home years ago to become an environmental and animal-welfare lawyer; her brother Joel stayed on and has built his life around the ranch, including a small but profitable rodeo circuit that Mara regards as genuinely cruel — more so, in her eyes, than the ordinary business of ranching cattle, which she has largely made peace with. Their father's will requires the two of them to agree jointly on the ranch's operating plan every year, which has made for some ugly holidays. In the next act, I want them to stumble, almost by accident, into a private bargain: Joel permanently retires the rodeo, something he never much cared about financially, in exchange for Mara quietly using her legal contacts to help the ranch fight off a grazing-rights reclassification that would otherwise put him out of business. Neither sibling budges an inch on the underlying disagreement about ranching or animal welfare, yet each comes away believing, by their own lights, that the arrangement has left the world a little better than it was.

When I described this plan to a friend who does philosophy for a living, she told me it's a fictional instance of something the philosopher Toby Ord discusses in a 2015 paper simply titled "Moral Trade": trade that's made possible not by differing tastes or resources but by differing moral convictions, so that two people who will never agree about ethics can each still get more of what they separately think matters. She walked me through a couple of complications the paper itself raises — whether a good deed done for the sake of a bargain, rather than for its own sake, still carries its usual moral weight, and the awkward fact that neither party can ever quite verify that the other wouldn't have done their part anyway, which would seem to make the "trade" at least partly illusory — and then added, more as her own opinion than anything she attributed to Ord, that if arrangements like this were ever scaled up from favors between people who already trust each other into an organized marketplace, they would start to resemble a secular trade in indulgences, in which whoever has the most money or leverage simply buys relief from whatever happens to offend their conscience.

Before I decide whether Mara and Joel's bargain should read, in the finished book, as a small moral achievement or as something the plot ought to complicate or quietly punish, I'd like to think the underlying question through properly rather than just trust my instincts as a storyteller. Could you write me an essay that weighs the strongest considerations for and against treating moral trade of this kind as something a scrupulous, reflective person should actually be willing to do, rather than a practice to regard with suspicion? Please don't leave it as an even-handed survey — reach an actual verdict, even a qualified one (something like "legitimate between parties who already trust each other, but not once it's institutionalized into a market" would count as a real answer), and defend it as the position you think the arguments genuinely support.
