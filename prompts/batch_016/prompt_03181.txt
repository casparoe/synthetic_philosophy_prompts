So over the last couple months I've gone a bit down a rabbit hole reading about Jain philosophy - started with a chapter in a comparative religion book from the library and now I just keep googling stuff. Everyone always explains anekantavada with that blind-men-and-an-elephant story (one guy grabs the leg and says it's a pillar, another grabs the ear and says it's a fan, etc), which is a nice image but I found out its way more formal than that - there's a whole logical structure behind it called syadvada, where every statement is supposed to get qualified with "syat" (roughly "in a certain respect"), building up to this list of seven possible statements about anything - it is, it isn't, it is-and-isn't, it's flat out indescribable, plus combos of those - which apparently is called saptabhangi. Kind of blew my mind that a 2500-year-old tradition worked out something that looks almost like a truth table.

But then I ran into an objection to the whole thing that's apparently pretty old (some Buddhist and Vedanta writers were throwing versions of it at the Jains a thousand-plus years ago, and I've seen it repeated in modern write-ups too): if the whole point of anekantavada is that no single statement is ever the absolute, unqualified truth, then what happens when you apply that to the doctrine itself? Either "anekantavada is true" is itself only true "in a certain respect" - in which case fine, but then maybe from some other respect a claim CAN be flat-out, unqualified true, so the doctrine hasn't actually ruled that out - or it's being asserted as the plain unqualified truth, in which case it's contradicting its own content the second it opens its mouth. Reminds me a bit of that "this sentence is false" liar paradox thing, except stretched out over "in some respect." I brought this up with a Jain coworker once and she just said I was overthinking the logic-chopping side of it, but I can't let it go. I've since seen two different escape routes people offer: some say the qualification only ever applies to claims about how physical substances exist across space/time/mode, not to the abstract doctrine describing them, so there's no real self-application problem; others (more the modern logician crowd) basically say fine, it IS a genuine contradiction, and that's okay because reality (or at least Jain logic) can tolerate some true contradictions, paraconsistent-logic style.

Can you write me an essay that actually digs into this and comes down somewhere definite - either "anekantavada survives the self-refutation charge, and here's the move that actually does the work" or "no, it doesn't, and here's why both of the escape routes above fail"? I don't want a wishy-washy "well, both sides raise good points" essay, I've read plenty of those - I want you to actually argue for a verdict, nuanced is fine, wishy-washy isn't.
