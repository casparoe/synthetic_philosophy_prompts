Three weeks ago I reported my employer to our industry's data-protection regulator over a privacy violation that my director had been quietly sitting on for months. A couple of friends who know what happened have called it an act of real integrity. Privately, I'm less sure it deserves the label: that same director had just blocked a reorganization I'd spent months fighting for, and if I'm honest, what finally got me to pick up the phone was fury at her as much as it was concern for the customers whose data had been exposed — though I did, and still do, think the exposure was a real harm and that she was clearly wrong to sit on it.

This has left me stuck on a question I hadn't expected to find so hard: what, beyond an act's simply being right, makes it morally worthy? Kant's answer is that full moral worth requires acting from the motive of duty, which makes me suspicious of my own case. But I've also come across Julia Markovits's rival account, the Coincident Reasons Thesis, on which moral worth requires only that one's motivating reasons coincide with the reasons that actually make the act right, whether or not one was thinking in terms of duty at all.

Could you spell out precisely where these two views diverge — not just in emphasis, but in the actual verdicts they would hand down — and then say which of them gives the more defensible verdict on a case like mine, where a right-making reason and a merely self-interested one were both doing real motivational work?
