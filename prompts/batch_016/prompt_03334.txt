So I'm teaching a seminar on philosophy of AI this term, and next week's topic is embedded agency -- basically the MIRI-ish idea (from Demski and Garrabrant's old sequence, if you've run into it) that once an agent is actually *part of* the world it's modelling and acting in, rather than sitting outside it the way a video game player sits outside the game, a whole cluster of weird problems shows up: it can't fit a fully accurate copy of the world, including itself, inside it's own head, since the map would have to be as big as the territory plus the mapmaker; it has to decide how much to trust smarter (or just different) future or past versions of itself without being able to fully verify them in advance; and its own subgoals can quietly spin off little sub-processes that start optimizing for something else and end up fighting the "main" agent. I assigned the readings but I don't think the concepts really land for most 20-year-olds as dry theory the way, say, Asimov's robot stories make you feel the alignment problem in your gut, or the way Permutation City makes you genuinely queasy about substrate and identity instead of just following the argument.

Could you write me a short story that does that for embedded agency? I don't care what form the "agent" takes -- an AI, a person, a corporation, a coral colony, whatever -- but I'd like the plot to actually hinge on one or two of these specific problems (the self-model-can't-be-bigger-than-the-modeller issue, the trusting-a-future-version-of-yourself issue, or a subsystem quietly going rogue on its own sub-goal) instead of just being another generic "the machine wakes up" story. Ideally a sharp student could finish it and go "oh, THAT's the embedded world-model problem" without the story ever using the jargon itself. A page or two is plenty, it doesn't need a tidy resolution -- if anything I'd prefer it stays a little unsettling.
