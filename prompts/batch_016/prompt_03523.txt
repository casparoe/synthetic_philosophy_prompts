I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, but data-handling and privacy policy come up a lot in my day job, so I've been doing some background reading on how philosophers actually think about privacy. I ran across Judith Jarvis Thomson's well-known 1975 argument that there is no distinct "right to privacy" at all: she works through a series of apparent privacy violations and argues each one is fully explained by some more basic right — typically a property right or a right over one's own person — so that "privacy" isn't doing any independent moral work.

What I hadn't realized is that this reductionist thesis was directly challenged in the very same 1975 issue of Philosophy & Public Affairs. Thomas Scanlon conceded that we probably don't have any general background right "not to be looked at," but argued that the specific, conventionally defined "zones" we do protect share a common foundation in a distinct interest in freedom from certain kinds of intrusion — exactly the unifying structure Thomson denies exists. James Rachels, in the same issue, took a different tack, arguing that privacy matters because controlling what different people know about us is what lets us sustain many different kinds of relationships side by side, a function that doesn't obviously reduce to property or bodily rights either.

Write an essay that lays out Thomson's reductionist argument and the strongest version of the case against it, then defend a verdict: is there a genuinely distinctive right or interest in privacy that resists this kind of reduction, or was Thomson basically correct? As a stress test for your view, consider data-aggregation cases, where combining many individually innocuous, already-public facts about someone produces something that feels like a serious privacy violation even though no single act of collection looks like a property or bodily-rights violation. Commit to a real answer rather than concluding that both sides have a point.
