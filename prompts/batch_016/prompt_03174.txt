At my school there's an argument that started at a student council meeting last month and hasn't really died down since, and I keep turning it over in my head. Every month, on the first Friday, we have a fire drill: everyone stops what they're doing, files outside, stands around for a few minutes no matter the weather, and then loses a few more minutes settling back into class once we're let back in. A couple of us on the council did some rough math for a proposal (I'm in AP Statistics, so I couldn't resist): with about 1,800 students, ten minutes lost per drill, and nine drills a year, four years of high school adds up to something like 650,000 student-minutes spent on drills that prepare for a scenario — an actual fire breaking out during the school day — that hasn't happened here in over thirty years and that, according to the fire marshal himself, is extremely unlikely in any given year. We proposed cutting the drills to twice a year and made the case plainly: a small, mildly annoying cost, multiplied across enough people and enough years, is still a real cost, and if it clearly outweighs the sliver of risk we'd be giving up, we should be willing to make that trade.

Our assistant principal wouldn't budge, and the reason she gave has stuck with me more than I expected. She said that no matter how many student-minutes we pile up, being bored and mildly inconvenienced is simply not the same kind of thing as a student getting seriously hurt or killed, and that no quantity of the first can ever add up to justify accepting more risk of the second — the size of the pile doesn't change what it's made of. One of my friends on the council thinks that's a cop-out, since we accept exactly this kind of trade-off (lots of small, spread-out costs set against one rare but severe harm) whenever we set speed limits, design playground equipment, or decide how much to spend on guardrails, so it seems inconsistent to suddenly treat fire drills as immune to that logic. I'd like to understand the actual disagreement here rather than get a verdict specifically about fire drills: is there a real, defensible principle behind the idea that no number of small harms can be added together and weighed against one severe harm, or is that just a comforting instinct people reach for once the arithmetic starts pointing somewhere they don't like?
