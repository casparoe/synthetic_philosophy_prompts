Ariel Rubinstein's "electronic mail game" involves two players who would prefer to coordinate on a risky joint action over a safe one, provided the state of the world favors it. An automated system relays confirmation messages between them, but since each message has a small, independent chance of being lost, no finite exchange of confirmations ever produces true common knowledge that the favorable state obtains — only ever-higher, but always finite, orders of mutual knowledge. Strikingly, in the unique equilibrium, both players play safe no matter how many confirmations have passed. The "global games" literature (Carlsson and van Damme; Morris and Shin) reaches the opposite conclusion from a structurally similar perturbation: giving each player a noisy private signal of an underlying continuous parameter, rather than undermining coordination, collapses what would otherwise be multiple equilibria into a single one, uniquely selected by iterated dominance, without any appeal to common knowledge.

Write a dialogue between two epistemic game theorists who agree that both results are mathematically correct but draw opposite morals from them. One holds that the email game reveals something real: common knowledge is a discontinuous, all-or-nothing achievement, and its absence is exactly why rational, mutually well-informed people can fail to coordinate. The other holds that the global games result shows this "paradox" is an artifact of how the uncertainty was modeled, and that a more realistic treatment of higher-order belief makes such coordination failure evaporate. Each should press the other on why the two treatments of "almost common knowledge" pull apart, rather than simply restating their own preferred result.
