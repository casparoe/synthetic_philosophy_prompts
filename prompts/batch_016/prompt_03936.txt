I've spent a long time reading LessWrong and the Alignment Forum, and one move that keeps recurring whenever the "problem of the priors" comes up is an appeal to Kolmogorov complexity: assign a hypothesis a prior probability that shrinks exponentially with the length of its shortest description on some fixed universal Turing machine, and Occam's razor falls out as a theorem rather than a brute assumption. People in that tradition often treat this as also handling Nelson Goodman's grue paradox: "grue" (an object is grue if it is green and examined before some time t, or blue and examined after t) only looks like a serious rival to "green" if you assume every predicate is equally natural, but once "simple" is cashed out via minimum description length, the grue-based hypothesis needs extra machinery to encode the switching condition, comes out longer, and gets a correspondingly lower prior. A Solomonoff-style reasoner, on this story, predicts green rather than grue without ever making Goodman's own brute appeal to which predicates are more "entrenched" in our language.

A friend from my old philosophy-of-science reading group raised a wrinkle in this that I haven't been able to argue my way out of. Kolmogorov complexity is only ever defined relative to a choice of universal Turing machine (equivalently, a reference description language), and while any two choices can disagree about a given string's complexity by no more than some bounded additive constant, nothing guarantees that constant is small relative to the minuscule complexity difference at stake in a toy case like "all emeralds observed so far are green" versus "...are grue." Build a machine whose instruction set treats "stay one color until step t, then switch" as cheap and primitive, rather than treating "stay the same color forever" that way, and the grue hypothesis becomes the short one while green becomes the contortion. That looks uncomfortably like Goodman's original point wearing a lab coat: we've traded "which predicate is more entrenched in our language" for "which Turing machine is more natural," without any obvious language-independent way to answer the second question that doesn't just smuggle back in our pre-theoretic sense that colors aren't supposed to switch at arbitrary dates.

I'd like an essay that takes a real position on this. Explain, clearly enough for someone who has never heard of Solomonoff induction to follow, why the Kolmogorov-complexity move looks like a genuine answer to Goodman; then present the "gruesome universal Turing machine" objection in its strongest form; then take seriously, rather than briefly gesturing at, the standard reply that predictions based on different machines converge as more data comes in, and say whether that reply actually rescues the approach or merely postpones the problem. Don't retreat to "both sides have a point" — tell me whether you think algorithmic information theory gives the problem of induction a genuine, non-circular solution that Goodman's own entrenchment-based answer lacked, a solution that only works in some qualified sense (say, in the infinite-data limit but not for any evidence we could actually collect), or no real solution at all, and defend whichever verdict you reach against the strongest objection you can think of.
