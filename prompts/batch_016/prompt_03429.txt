I'd like your comments on a short essay I wrote for extra credit in my AP European History class, on Galileo's 1615 Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina. I'm mainly unsure about the objection I raise in the final paragraph. Here it is:

Galileo's Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina is usually taught as the moment science broke decisively from biblical literalism, but the argument he actually makes is more careful than that summary suggests. He never claims Scripture is wrong. Instead, he argues that since Scripture and nature both come from God, they cannot truly conflict, so an apparent contradiction must come from a bad reading of one or the other. Because the Bible's purpose is salvation rather than astronomy, he says it was written to "accommodate them to the capacities of the common people, who are rude and unlearned," describing sunrises and sunsets because that is how things look, not because that is how they are. He backs this up with Cardinal Baronius's line that the Holy Spirit intended "to teach us how to go to heaven, not how the heavens go."

Crucially, though, Galileo does not treat this license to reinterpret Scripture as unconditional. He insists throughout the letter that a passage should only be read figuratively once the contrary natural claim has been necessarily demonstrated, not merely made probable; where there is only conjecture, the literal words should stand.

This is where I think the argument runs into trouble on its own terms. What had actually been necessarily demonstrated by 1615? The telescope had shown the phases of Venus and the moons of Jupiter, which did refute the strict Ptolemaic system. But those same observations were equally compatible with Tycho Brahe's geo-heliocentric model, in which the planets circle the sun while the sun and stars still circle a stationary earth. Direct evidence that the earth itself moves — stellar parallax, the Foucault pendulum — was still centuries away. So by Galileo's own standard, the "necessary demonstration" required before Scripture could be reinterpreted did not actually exist yet in 1615. He seems to be asking the Church to accept his hermeneutic principle on credit, for a conclusion he had not yet earned.

Could you check three things: (1) whether I've represented Galileo's argument and his standard of proof accurately, (2) whether my closing objection is a fair criticism or whether there's an obvious response on Galileo's behalf I'm missing, and (3) whether the essay holds together as a piece of writing or gets lost in the history-of-science weeds?
