I'm writing a historical novel set in seventeenth-century London with a subplot dramatizing Thomas Hobbes's long feud with John Wallis over geometry -- the quarrel that ran from Hobbes's De Corpore (1655) until his death, fought largely over his claimed quadrature of the circle and kindred problems. Having read Douglas Jesseph's Squaring the Circle as background, I want to settle something before I put speeches in Hobbes's mouth. His insistence that a point, line, or surface be understood as generated by a moving body, rather than as Euclid's dimensionless and breadthless abstractions, followed naturally from his broader materialist rejection of incorporeal entities -- yet he also accepted Cavalieri's method of indivisibles while dismissing Wallis's arithmetic of infinites as illegitimate. Write an essay weighing whether Hobbes's geometrical materialism amounts to a coherent philosophical program that happened to produce bad mathematics, or whether it was essentially motivated reasoning in service of his mathematical vanity. Weigh the strongest considerations on each side, but don't leave the question open: commit to a specific verdict (a split verdict, such as 'his premises were respectable but his conduct in the dispute was not,' is acceptable, provided you argue for it clearly) that I can use to decide how sympathetically to write him.
