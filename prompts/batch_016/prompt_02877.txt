So this discount-rate argument keeps coming back around whenever longtermism/x-risk stuff gets discussed in rationalist-adjacent spaces (there was a whole subthread about it in an Astral Codex Ten open thread a while back), and it always eventually traces back to the same source: Frank Ramsey's 1928 paper "A Mathematical Theory of Saving." I finally sat down and read the actual thing instead of just absorbing the one line everyone quotes, which is where he says that discounting future generations welfare just because it's later is "a practice which is ethically indefensible and arises merely from the weakness of the imagination" (p. 543, for anyone keeping score). Great sentence, very quotable, and I basically already knew it going in from seeing it recycled a hundred times.

What I hadn't picked up on is what happens right around that line. As best I can reconstruct it: Ramsey first sets up the "pure" version of his position, maximizing the plain undiscounted integral of utility from now to infinity. But that integral diverges for basically any reasonable consumption path, so "maximize total undiscounted utility" isn't even a coherent thing to ask for. His fix is to invent "Bliss" — an upper bound the utility stream can approach but never actually hit — and instead maximize the integral of the *shortfall* from Bliss, which can converge even when the raw sum doesn't. And then, practically in the same breath as the "ethically indefensible" line, he mentions, without much elaboration, that he's still going to redo the whole exercise "in Section II" using an honest-to-god positive discount rate anyway.

That's the part I can't untangle myself: is the Bliss/shortfall manuever just a technical patch to make the integral well-behaved, with no real conceptual overlap with discounting — or is it quietly doing the same *job* as a discount rate, just implemented as "distance from a ceiling" instead of "distance in time" (which still ends up privileging some consumption paths over others in a way that smells a lot like time preference to me)? And how seriously should I take "ethically indefensible" as a philosophical claim, given he's clearly fine using the discounted version a few pages later when it's convenient? I don't have a strong econ background so I might be missing something dumb here, but I'd really appreciate someone walking through how these pieces are actually supposed to fit together, ideally before I cite Ramsey wrong in some Stern-vs-Nordhaus argument down the line.
