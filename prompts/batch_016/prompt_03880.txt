Last winter a friend, tired of hearing me complain about how hollow party politics has become, handed me a slim little book: Simone Weil's essay "On the Abolition of All Political Parties," in the edition translated and introduced by Simon Leys, with a short essay on Weil by Czesław Miłosz tacked on at the end. I don't have any formal background in philosophy or political theory — I mostly read on the train — but the essay got under my skin, and I've gone back to it several times since, along with a couple of things written about it online. From what I gather she wrote it in London in 1943, only months before she died, at a moment when de Gaulle's Free French were actually debating whether political parties should be allowed to operate again once France was liberated.

What I keep getting stuck on is how much Weil's whole case leans on Rousseau. She defines a party by three features — it exists to manufacture collective passion, it's built to pressure every member's private thinking into line, and whatever it claims to stand for, its real and only long-term goal is its own unlimited growth — and she says this is intolerable because individual reason, left to itself, tends to converge on the truth, while passions just multiply disagreement. That sounded to me like a straight borrowing of Rousseau's idea of the general will, so I went and actually read the relevant bit of the Social Contract (Book II, the short chapter asking whether the general will can be mistaken), and it turned out to be more complicated than I expected. Rousseau says the genuinely ideal arrangement is that there be no factions, or "partial societies," at all, and he credits the legendary Spartan lawgiver Lycurgus with actually having pulled this off in practice — but he immediately adds that if partial societies do exist anyway, the next-best thing is to multiply them and keep them roughly equal in size, the way he claims Solon, Numa, and Servius once arranged things. He never really treats outright abolition as a live option for an ordinary modern state, only as a kind of unreachable limiting case.

So here's what I'd like help thinking through: is Weil, writing almost two centuries later, essentially just taking Rousseau's throwaway ideal case more seriously than he took it himself, and turning it into an actual policy proposal for postwar France? Or is her argument doing something that Rousseau's own institutional discussion doesn't really cover — resting on extra assumptions of her own, for instance about truth being single and undivided, or about what belonging to any sufficiently large collective body necessarily does to a person's conscience, regardless of how many rival factions exist or how evenly balanced they are? I'd also be curious how her separate distinction between "Anglo-Saxon" parties, which she treats as a fairly harmless, game-like affair, and continental ones, which she treats as potentially totalitarian, fits into all of this — does it line up naturally with Rousseau's remedy of numerous, evenly balanced factions, or does it suggest she is actually diagnosing a different disease altogether? I'd like an actual comparison of the two arguments, not just a summary of each one on its own.
