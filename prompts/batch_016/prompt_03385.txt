So a few of us run a little reading group (grew out of the local ACX meetup, extremely original naming, i know: we just call it "the Bostrom group") and we've been slowly working through *Superintelligence*. A couple weeks ago we hit the chapter on the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence, and it turned into a real argument between two of the regulars, Marcus and Yuki, that never actually got resolved because we ran out of time and just moved on to the next chapter. It's been bugging me since, so I wanted to lay out the exchange and get an outside read on it.

(1) Marcus opened by saying the two theses are actually in tension: orthogonality says intelligence tells you nothing about final goals, a superintelligent paperclip-maximizer is just as coherent as a superintelligent saint. But then instrumental convergence turns around and predicts, pretty confidently, that almost any sufficiently smart agent will do the same handful of things: protect itself, grab resources, resist having it's goals edited. If literally any goal can go with high intelligence, how are we also entitled to such a specific, narrow prediction about behavior? (2) Yuki said there's no real tension, he's just conflating two levels: orthogonality is a claim about terminal goals, convergence is a claim about instrumental sub-goals that happen to serve almost any terminal goal, the same way nearly everyone wants money regardless of what they actually want it for. (3) Marcus said okay, but convergence specifically predicts agents will resist edits to their own goal content, and resisting edits only makes sense if the terminal goal is already fixed. By orthogonality's own logic, an agent could just as easily have "update my goals whenever I hear a sufficiently good argument" as its terminal goal, and then it wouldn't resist anything. So convergence quietly assumes a narrow, fixed-goal type of mind and talks as if that followed from being intelligent as such.

(4) Yuki answered that Bostrom only ever claims convergence for a "wide range" of goals and agent types, not all of them; self-revising agents are a real but unusual corner of mind-space, so there's no inconsistency, just a scope restriction that was baked in from the start. (5) Marcus said that concession is bigger then it sounds: if convergence only kicks in for the subset of minds we happen to expect to actually build, it's really an empirical bet about likely AI architectures, wearing the same "thesis" costume as orthogonality, which is a much cleaner logical/conceptual claim about possible minds in general -- so the two aren't really peers, even though they always get presented as a matched pair. (6) Yuki said fine, they don't have to be peers: one is a claim about the shape of mind-space, the other a conditional claim about what happens if, as currently looks likely, we keep building advanced AI via reward- or objective-maximizing optimization, and Bostrom is explicit enough about that condition that nothing's being smuggled in.

Neither of them budged, and honestly I don't know this corner of the AI-risk literature well enough to referee it myself.
