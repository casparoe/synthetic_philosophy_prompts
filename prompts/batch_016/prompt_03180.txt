I've drafted the following short piece for a small philosophy discussion group I recently joined (mostly retired academics and interested amateurs like myself), and I'd be grateful for comments before I circulate it further.

Since retiring from a career in experimental condensed-matter physics, I've been spending my mornings reading philosophy of physics, and I keep returning to Bell's theorem. I believe I have the core argument straight: the CHSH version of the inequality bounds any theory satisfying locality together with the usual "free choice" (measurement-independence) assumption at |S| ≤ 2, whereas entangled quantum states permit violations up to 2√2, consistent with every experiment run since Aspect's in the early 1980s. Something in the classical package — locality, realism, or free choice — has to give.

What I've only recently appreciated, and can't yet get a comfortable grip on, is a further wrinkle. If we're already prepared to abandon locality (in Bell's sense) to accommodate these correlations, it isn't obvious why nature should stop at 2√2 rather than going further. Popescu and Rohrlich noted that one can define hypothetical correlations ("PR boxes") that reach the full algebraic maximum of 4 while still forbidding faster-than-light signalling; nothing in relativity as usually stated excludes them. Yet no physical system realizes them, and quantum mechanics never does better than 2√2. Various information-theoretic principles — "information causality" is apparently the best developed — recover the quantum bound from axioms about communication rather than from Hilbert-space machinery, but as far as I can tell none is regarded as a complete derivation.

Here is my amateur's conjecture, offered for correction. Popular treatments of Bell's theorem present the choice as binary: the world is local, or it isn't. The existence of an unexplained ceiling strictly between the classical bound and the maximally nonlocal one makes me suspect that framing hides the more interesting question, which is not whether nature is nonlocal but why it is nonlocal to exactly this degree and no further. If that's right, "locality" may not be the fundamental notion in this neighbourhood at all; it may be a coarse stand-in for some sharper informational constraint that hasn't yet been properly identified — or perhaps has been, and I simply haven't come across it.

I'd value comments on three things: whether I have the physics above right; whether the inference from "there is an unexplained bound" to "locality isn't the fundamental concept here" is defensible or simply confused; and whether this line of thought is already familiar territory (and if so, where it leads) in the foundations-of-physics literature.
