I've been prepping a grad seminar on Sidgwick's Methods of Ethics and got sidetracked down a rabbit hole I can't quite climb out of, so I'm hoping you can help me think it though. We spent a whole class period on Sidgwick's argument for temporal neutrality -- his claim that "the mere difference of priority and posteriority in time" gives us no rational basis for caring more about a nearer moment of good than a more distant one. Then one of my students, who's deep into the effective altruism scene, pointed out that this is basically the exact premise longtermists lean on when they argue for a "zero rate of pure time preference" -- Greaves and MacAskill actually cite Sidgwick by name for it in their Case for Strong Longtermism paper. That set off a whole argument with a colleague of mine over lunch afterward.

Here's what we couldn't settle. If you put the full argument for strong longtermism in front of Sidgwick -- not just "don't discount the future" but the whole package, i.e. that given the astronomical number of people who could exist over the coming millions of years, far-future effects should dominate basically all of our major practical decisions, so that funding existential risk research beats funding anti-malarial bednets by a landslide -- would he actually have signed on? Or would other parts of his outlook have made him hold back? I'm thinking of his repeated worry that hedonistic calculation is only reliable at short range, his defense of leaning on common-sense moral rules for exactly that reason (the move Bernard Williams later mocked as "Government House utilitarianism"), and his general wariness of conclusions that outrun ordinary moral intuition too badly. Though there's also the fact that his impartiality principle -- not discounting anyone's good "from the point of view of the universe" -- cuts kind of both ways here, which matters alot for how you read him.

Could you write me something essay-length working through this? I don't want a wishy-washy "on the one hand, on the other hand" -- actually reconstruct the relevant strands of Sidgwick's own reasoning and land on a real verdict: would he, by his own lights, have ended up a strong longtermist or not?
