Last week I had a long, and honestly somewhat unsettling, argument with a close friend who is finishing a PhD in cognitive neuroscience. It started over dinner, when I mentioned that I had finally decided to leave my stable job at a large firm to start a small business of my own, and that I had reached this decision only after months of consciously weighing my growing dissatisfaction with corporate life against my desire for more creative independence. My friend didn't dispute that this is how the decision felt to me from the inside, but she argued that, strictly speaking, my reasons couldn't have done any actual causal work. Since my decision, like any decision, can in principle be traced back entirely to a prior sequence of neurons firing in my brain, and since that neural sequence already counts as a complete and sufficient cause of what I did, she insisted there simply isn't any causal room left over for my reasons, as such, to have contributed anything further. On her view, the whole story about weighing values and options is really just a kind of narrative gloss laid over a process that was already settled at the physical level.

We tried early on to set aside the old free-will-versus-determinism debate, since neither of us much cares whether those underlying brain states were themselves predetermined; what we got stuck on was something narrower. It strikes me as absurd that I would have made exactly the same decision regardless of what my reasons had been, or whether I had any reasons at all — surely my actually weighing the pros and cons made some difference to the outcome. But I can also feel the force of my friend's argument: if the physical story really is complete on its own, it is hard to see how my reasons could be doing any independent causal work of their own without the decision ending up strangely double-caused, in the way it would be strange to say that a single death was brought about both by a heart attack and, quite independently, by a bullet arriving at exactly the same instant, each alone being enough to do the job. Neither of us wanted to accept that kind of picture, but neither of us could see how to avoid it either. I would appreciate help thinking through who has the better of this: is my friend right that my reasons, however real they may have felt, did no genuine causal work of their own, or is there some coherent way for both the psychological explanation and the neural one to be fully and simultaneously true of my decision, without this amounting to that kind of implausible redundancy?
