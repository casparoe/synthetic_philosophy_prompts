I've noticed that most treatments of the Sleeping Beauty problem stop at the thirder/halfer scorecard and skip past what I find the more interesting wrinkle: Adam Elga's use of the case against van Fraassen's Reflection Principle. Reflection says, roughly, that if you're already certain your credence in some proposition will equal r at a later time — with no new evidence and nothing like memory loss or brainwashing expected in the interim — your current credence should already equal r; van Fraassen motivated it with a diachronic Dutch book argument in "Belief and the Will." Elga pointed out that on Sunday night, before the experiment (fair coin; Beauty woken only on Monday if heads, on Monday and Tuesday with an intervening memory wipe if tails), Beauty's credence in heads is 1/2 — yet she's already certain that on Monday, which happens regardless of the outcome and before which she gains no new evidence and suffers no cognitive mishap, her credence will be 1/3. Since nothing has yet been forgotten by Monday, this looks like a clean counterexample to Reflection.

Write a dialogue between two equally sharp Bayesian epistemologists hashing this out over drinks after a conference talk. One holds that Elga's case sinks Reflection as a genuine rationality constraint. The other wants to save it by arguing, in the spirit of Bradley Monton's reply to Elga, that the amnesia built into the tails-branch of the experiment means Beauty's Monday credence can't be treated as a clean, forgetting-free update from Sunday after all. Have them press each other on whether that move is a principled use of van Fraassen's own resources or an ad hoc rescue, and don't let either side concede too easily or talk past the other.
