In my AP Language and Composition class, we just finished reading the section of Benjamin Franklin's *Autobiography* where he lays out his "bold and arduous project of arriving at moral perfection." I was struck by how deliberate his method was: rather than simply resolving to be a better person in general, he drew up a list of thirteen specific virtues, kept a little book with a page ruled off for each one, and worked through them one at a time in weekly rotation, since he judged it best "not to distract his attention by attempting the whole at once." He marked a dot every time he slipped up, and once he felt a habit was under control he moved on to the next virtue, cycling through all thirteen several times a year.

What really stayed with me, though, were the moments where Franklin seems to gently mock his own project. There's the story of the man who brings an axe to a blacksmith wanting the whole head polished bright, gets tired of turning the grindstone, and decides he actually prefers a "speckled axe" — which Franklin uses to explain why he never fully conquered the virtue of Order. And Humility only made the list at all because a Quaker friend informed him that he had a reputation for being proud and overbearing in conversation; Franklin admits that pride is almost impossible to kill off completely, joking that even if he ever did subdue it, he would "probably be proud of my humility."

This all came to mind recently because several of my friends have gotten really into habit-tracking apps, Duolingo-style streaks, and things like the 75 Hard challenge, and there's a whole world of productivity influencers who post their morning routines and color-coded habit trackers online for followers. Given how specific and self-aware Franklin was about his own method, I'm curious what you think he would make of all this if he could see it today. Would he think apps that have people tracking a dozen habits simultaneously undermine the whole point of his approach, which was built around focusing on a single virtue at a time so as not to overwhelm yourself? Would he view streaks and badges as a reasonable substitute for the kind of reasoned justification he tried to give each virtue, or as a shallow gimmick? And given how candidly he wrote about his own struggle with pride, what do you think he'd say about influencers who turn personal moral improvement into content for an audience?
