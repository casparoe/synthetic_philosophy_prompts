In my AP Biology class, we spent a class period on animal communication and covered Amotz Zahavi's handicap principle: the idea that showy, costly traits like a peacock's tail or a gazelle stotting in front of a predator function as honest signals precisely because they are so wasteful that only a genuinely strong or healthy animal could afford to produce them. A weaker animal attempting the same display would pay a proportionally higher cost and eventually get selected against, which is supposedly what keeps such signals from being faked. Almost as an aside, our teacher mentioned that economists and sociologists apply the same logic to people, citing things like going into debt for an engagement ring or throwing an extravagant wedding as costly, and therefore credible, signals of commitment.

That offhand comment is what has been bothering me ever since. It seems to me that the principle, if true, actually proves something much bigger than what we covered in class. If honesty only survives, biologically or socially, because a signal is too expensive for a liar to fake, then any cheap, effortless signal should be essentially worthless as evidence, since a dishonest sender could produce the exact same signal at the exact same, zero, cost as an honest one. That would seem to cover a huge amount of ordinary human communication, including a friend telling you "I've got your back," a company's plain claim that its product is safe, or a stranger giving you directions. When I brought this up with my older brother, who is majoring in economics, he told me I was missing something and mentioned "cheap talk" models, in which communication is apparently stable and honest in equilibrium without any cost attached at all, but he couldn't really explain why that doesn't just flatly contradict the handicap principle.

So which of us is closer to right? Is the handicap principle simply false as a general theory of honest signaling, is there some condition on when it's actually supposed to apply that our class glossed over, or can the handicap principle and this "cheap talk" idea both be true at once?
