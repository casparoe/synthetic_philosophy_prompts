Random thought that's been eating at me since a lunch argument with a colleague from the history-of-philosophy side of the department. You know how "Humean supervenience" -- Lewis's name for the view that laws of nature are nothing but the axioms of the best systematization of the total, non-modal arrangement of particular matters of fact (the "mosaic") -- is literally named after Hume, on the assumption that he's the guy who denied necessary connections in nature altogether? That's as opposed to the anti-Humean picture, where laws involve some further governing ingredient -- Armstrong's necessitation relation between universals, say, or Bird-style dispositional essences. My colleague was pushing back on that naming assumption though, bringing up the whole "New Hume" business -- Galen Strawson's line (and Wright's before him) that Hume was actually a skeptical realist: he thought there probably IS some real necessitating oomph out there connecting causes to effects, we just can't ever form a clear idea of it given his own empiricist strictures on concept formation, so the skepticism is about our knowledge of causal powers, not their existence. Thats obviously the minority view against the "Old Hume" reading (Winkler's rebuttal, Blackburn, more or less the textbook consensus), but its a real live scholarly debate, not a joke position.

So here's what I want your take on, and I want an actual answer, not a "we can't know" shrug: if you resurrected Hume and handed him the current literature on laws -- Lewis, Armstrong, Bird, all of it -- which side of the Humean/anti-Humean debate would he land on? Does that depend entirely on whether Old Hume or New Hume is the historically accurate reading, or is there a stance he'd take that cuts across both interpretations? And for a bonus complication: quantum entanglement gets raised a lot these days (Maudlin's argument, mainly) as a specific problem for Humean supervenience, since entangled states don't seem to supervene on the local, point-by-point qualities Lewis's mosaic is built out of. Would 18th-century Hume, dropped into the present, see that as a real dent in "his own" view, or would his general skepticism about ever getting at the deep nature of things make him shrug it off as more of the same old story? Give me a real position on all of this and argue for it -- "it depends on X" is a fine shape for an answer as long as you tell me what X is and why.
