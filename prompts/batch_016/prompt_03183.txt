I am writing a novel set in China's Warring States period, and one of its central figures is a travelling rhetorician in the tradition of the "School of Names," modeled loosely on the historical Gongsun Long. I want to give him a set-piece scene in which he defends his notorious claim that a white horse is not a horse, and I would like the exchange to read as genuine argument rather than a hollow pun -- but the reasoning has proven harder to reconstruct than I expected. Below is my own working rendering of the core exchange, drawn from the "White Horse" chapter of the Gongsun Longzi, in which he answers a skeptical questioner with three separate replies:

Q: Can it be maintained that a white horse is not a horse?
A: It can.
Q: On what grounds?
A: "Horse" is the name for a shape. "White" is the name for a color. Naming a color is not the same as naming a shape. Hence a white horse is not a horse.
Q: But if a white horse exists, surely one cannot say that no horse exists -- and if one cannot say that no horse exists, then a horse must exist. To have a white horse just is to have a horse. How, then, can the white one fail to be a horse?
A: If a man wants a horse, a yellow horse or a black horse will each satisfy him. If he wants a white horse, neither will satisfy him. Were "a white horse" nothing more than "a horse," what he wanted in the two cases would be one and the same thing, and a yellow or a black horse would answer to both requests equally. Since it plainly does not, a yellow horse and a black horse are alike only in this: each may count as a horse, yet neither may count as a white horse. That is why a white horse is not a horse.
Q: Yet no horse in the world is without some color. If a horse's color already disqualifies it from being "horse" pure and simple, must we conclude that there is no such thing as a horse in the world at all?
A: A horse must indeed have some color, which is exactly why a white horse can be picked out to begin with; if "horse" named a colorless kind and nothing more, no white horse could ever be found. So "white horse" is "white" and "horse" taken together, and "white" and "horse" taken together are not the same as "horse" alone.

Could you help me reconstruct how these three replies are meant to work together as a single case, and tell me whether "is not" means the same thing throughout -- denying that "white horse" and "horse" are identical, or denying that a white horse belongs to the class of horses -- or whether the speaker quietly shifts between these senses as it suits him? I would also like to know whether a genuinely serious philosophical reading is available here, one involving names, classes, and composition rather than mere wordplay, and if so, which of the three replies is strongest and which is most vulnerable, so that I can decide where his rival ought to land a real blow rather than a cheap one.
