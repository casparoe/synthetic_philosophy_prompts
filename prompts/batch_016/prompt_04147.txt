I retired five years ago after a career in non-equilibrium statistical mechanics, and I have spent much of my retirement working through the philosophy of probability, partly in hopes of finally settling something that nagged at me throughout my working life: what physicists actually mean when we call something a "chance." That reading eventually led me to David Lewis's Principal Principle and his Humean account of law and chance, and from there to the difficulty he himself called, rather theatrically, the "big bad bug" — the undermining-futures problem that runs through "A Subjectivist's Guide to Objective Chance" (1980) and "Humean Supervenience Debugged" (1994). Below is a short argument I have drafted that I believe defuses the bug by way of an analogy from my own field. I would like an honest assessment of it before I present it to my local philosophy discussion group next month, since I suspect a specialist will spot the trouble with it faster than I have been able to.

The setup, compressed as far as I can manage: Lewis holds that chances, like laws, are not primitive but supervene on the entire pattern of events in history, past and future alike, via a best-system analysis that rewards simplicity, strength, and fit to the actual outcomes. Because the future is part of that pattern, a theory of chance Tw that is true today is hostage to how the future goes: there is some "undermining" future F to which Tw itself assigns a positive chance, but whose occurrence would make a rival system true instead of Tw. The Principal Principle then requires that credence in F, conditional on Tw and the present history, equal Tw's own positive chance of F, while the fact that Tw's truth strictly precludes F forces that same conditional credence to be exactly zero by the ordinary axioms of probability. Hence the bug: not mere tension, but outright contradiction.

My proposed dissolution is that this is structurally identical to something physicists tolerate without any anxiety at all. The fluctuation theorems of non-equilibrium statistical mechanics assign a spontaneous, macroscopic entropy-decreasing episode some fantastically small but strictly positive probability, and nobody thinks this "undermines" the Second Law or breeds a contradiction; we simply say the law is statistical, and an atypical trajectory is still a permitted realization of the very statistics that mark it as atypical. An undermining future, on my reading, is nothing more than this kind of rare fluctuation wearing modal dress. If a statistical law and a nonzero chance of its "violation" can coexist peacefully in physics, I do not see why they cannot coexist just as peacefully for the Humean about chance, and Lewis's alarm starts to look like a dramatization of an entirely ordinary feature of statistical regularities.

I would welcome comments on three things specifically: whether my summary of Lewis's problem above is accurate; whether the thermodynamic analogy actually engages the logical structure of the bug or merely relabels it while changing the subject underneath me; and, if the latter, what precisely it is about how chance is supposed to be grounded on a Humean view that an ordinary statistical-mechanical probability distribution does not share. I gather Lewis credits Michael Thau, and independently Ned Hall, with the eventual fix, but I have deliberately held off reading their papers until I had tested my own instincts, so please do not feel obliged to go easy on me for their sake.
