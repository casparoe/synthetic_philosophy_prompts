I retired eight months ago after thirty years as a corporate tax attorney, and free time has been harder to fill than expected. Mostly it has gone into rebuilding a derelict wooden sailboat in my garage — no client, no deadline, nothing beyond getting the joints right. A friend sent me Moritz Schlick's 1927 essay "On the Meaning of Life," and it has needled at me since. Schlick argues that meaning can never lie in "work," activity done for some further end (ultimately the perpetuation of life), since that chain of purposes either never closes or ends in something too thin to justify it; meaning belongs instead to "play," whose point is wholly internal to the activity. He offers the scientist's research and the artist's labor as paradigm cases of such play, then narrows the claim further, identifying meaning not with play as such but with "youth" — the period when play predominates — so that even a childhood cut short counts as fully meaningful. I can't tell whether this last move saves the theory or just cheats: the disciplined, years-long apprenticeship behind any scientist's or artist's "play" looks nothing like a child's. Please argue that Schlick's shift from play to youth is no harmless restatement of his view, but a maneuver that lets him quietly readmit the very purposive, effortful striving his work/play distinction was meant to exclude from a meaningful life.
