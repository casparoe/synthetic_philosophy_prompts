I've been reading Douglas Jesseph's account of the long, strange war between Thomas Hobbes and the mathematician John Wallis for a piece I'm working on about the history of mathematical certainty, and I keep getting stuck on the philosophical logic behind Hobbes's side of it. The bare narrative is almost comic: in "De Corpore" (1655), Hobbes announced that he had squared the circle; Wallis tore the proof apart within months in his "Elenchus Geometriae Hobbianae"; and the two of them then spent the better part of twenty-five years, right up until Hobbes's death, trading increasingly contemptuous pamphlets, with Hobbes never once conceding that his geometry was wrong. It apparently cost him dearly: Wallis was a founding member of the Royal Society, and Hobbes, for all his stature, was never admitted to it.

What I haven't seen explained well anywhere is why Hobbes found it so impossible to simply back down once his errors were pointed out. My understanding, from what I've read so far, is that this wasn't ordinary stubbornness or vanity: Hobbes seems to have held that geometric figures must be defined "genetically," by specifying the motion that generates them -- a circle, say, as the path swept out by a line rotating about one fixed end -- and to have treated this as required by his materialism, on the grounds that we can have perfectly certain, demonstrative knowledge of geometry only because we ourselves construct the very figures we reason about. Wallis, by contrast, was quite comfortable working with more abstract algebraic treatments of quantity that owed nothing to any such physical construction.

Could you help me understand what was really at stake philosophically in that disagreement? Specifically, I'd like to understand how Hobbes's causal, motion-based theory of geometric definition and his so-called "maker's knowledge" conception of certainty connect to his broader materialist system, and why that particular combination of commitments made it so hard for him to treat a mathematical mistake as just a mistake, rather than a position he had to defend to the end.
