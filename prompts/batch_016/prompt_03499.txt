I'm writing a novel whose protagonist, a theoretical physicist, is preoccupied with the history of "action at a distance," which she treats as a private metaphor for her own estrangement from the people she loves. While researching her backstory, I came across this passage from Newton's third letter to Richard Bentley, dated 25 February 1692/3:

"It is inconceivable that inanimate brute matter should, without the mediation of something else which is not material, operate upon and affect other matter without mutual contact... That gravity should be innate, inherent, and essential to matter, so that one body may act upon another at a distance through a vacuum, without the mediation of anything else, by and through which their action and force may be conveyed from one to another, is to me so great an absurdity that I believe no man who has in philosophical matters a competent faculty of thinking can ever fall into it. Gravity must be caused by an agent acting constantly according to certain laws; but whether this agent be material or immaterial, I have left open to the consideration of my readers."

In one scene I want her to tell a younger colleague that Bell's theorem essentially vindicated Newton's unease, proving that nature does, after all, traffic in exactly the sort of unmediated action at a distance he found absurd, only in the quantum domain rather than the gravitational one. Before I commit that line to the page, I'd like to know whether it's something a careful physicist could honestly say. If it's an oversimplification, could you explain precisely what Bell's theorem does and does not establish about locality, and how it actually bears on Newton's original worry?
