I'm a PhD student in financial economics, and while drafting a paper on mandatory disclosure rules I've run into a tension I can't fully dissolve. Good's theorem guarantees that a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer should never, in expectation, prefer to remain ignorant of cost-free evidence before acting. Yet Hirshleifer's classic result shows that publicly releasing information before agents can trade may destroy opportunities for mutually beneficial risk-sharing, leaving every party worse off in expectation than if the information had never been revealed. Please describe the most important implications of Good's theorem for this puzzle: specifically, which of the theorem's background assumptions Hirshleifer's scenario actually violates, and what that implies about how much weight regulators should place on arguments that "more disclosure is always weakly welfare-improving" when justifying mandatory disclosure rules.
