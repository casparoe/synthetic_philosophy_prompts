I'm drafting a scene for a novel in which two adult siblings — one a classicist who has spent her career on Epicurus and Lucretius, the other a moral philosopher working on the ethics of death and dying — end up alone in the kitchen after their father's funeral reception and fall into an old argument: whether their father's death was actually bad for him, as distinct from being bad for those who loved him.

Please write their dialogue. The classicist should press the genuinely Epicurean line — that harm requires a subject to undergo it, so death itself, as opposed to the process of dying or the grief of survivors, cannot be bad for the person who dies — and should back her brother into Lucretius's symmetry argument: if the eternity of nonexistence after death is nothing to fear, why does no one mourn the eternity of nonexistence before birth? Her brother should defend a deprivation account of death's badness, in the spirit of Nagel, and needs a genuine answer to the symmetry challenge rather than a dodge. Give me several real exchanges on each side — interruption and direct rebuttal, not parallel speeches — and don't feel obliged to resolve it neatly.
