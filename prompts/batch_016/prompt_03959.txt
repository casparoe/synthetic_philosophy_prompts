So I was at a decision theory conference last week for a piece I'm working on, and after the talks wrapped up two of the speakers (one's a decision theorist, the other's a mathematician) ended up arguing at the hotel bar about the Pasadena game -- apparently named that because the guys who invented it teach at Caltech. Basic setup: you flip a coin til it first lands heads, and if that happens on flip n, you get 2^n/n dollars if n is odd, or you owe 2^n/n dollars if n is even -- so the longer it takes, the bigger the swings, and they alternate between you winning and you owing. Roughly the argument went:

1. He said the expected value isn't just infinite, its actually undefined: the sum (1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ...) only converges conditionally, and Riemann's rearrangement theorem says you can reorder those exact same outcomes to make the total come out to any number you like, or to plus or minus infinity. So there's no fact of the matter about what the game is worth, and the rational move is to just refuse to price it.

2. She said that's overthinking it -- the natural order to add the terms in is by which flip the heads first shows up on, 1st, 2nd, 3rd, and so on, and that gives you ln(2). That's not an arbitrary bookkeeping choice, she said, it's the order the actual mechanism hands you, so ln(2) is the fair price, end of story.

3. He said the game doesn't care how the outcome gets revealed to you -- standard decision theory says only the payoff/probability pairs matter, not the story of how you learn which one occurred -- and that you can re-index those very same pairs (say, counting two 'win' outcomes for every one 'owe' outcome instead of going straight through) and get 3/2 times ln(2) instead, without touching a single payoff or probability.

4. She said fine, but decision theory has to output an answer eventually, because somebody actually has to decide whether to sit down and play; announcing 'the value is undefined' and walking away isn't more rational, it's just refusing to answer the question.

Neither one budged, and it's been bugging me since. Can you go through this move by move, say who's got the better of it at each step (not just an overall winner), and flag anything you think both of them are missing?
