I'm taking a seminar this term called "Philosophy of Induction," and we recently finished a unit on algorithmic information theory as a candidate formalization of Occam's razor: Solomonoff induction, Kolmogorov complexity, the invariance theorem, and so on, working through excerpts from Li and Vitányi's textbook and from Rathmanner and Hutter's survey "A Philosophical Treatise of Universal Induction." After the seminar broke up, two people in the class, Marcus and Elena, ended up arguing about it in the hallway for a good twenty minutes, and I haven't been able to settle in my own mind who came out ahead.

(1) Marcus opened by claiming that Kolmogorov complexity finally gives Occam's razor real teeth: because the invariance theorem guarantees that the complexity of any string is the same across universal machines up to an additive constant, which of two hypotheses counts as "simpler" stops being a matter of taste and becomes an objective, machine-independent fact. (2) Elena pushed back that the additive constant is exactly where the whole problem is hidden: nothing stops you from building a universal machine with, say, Ptolemaic astronomy pre-compiled into its compiler, so that geocentric epicycles come out short and Copernican astronomy comes out long, and since the invariance theorem places no bound on how large that constant can be, for any two rival hypotheses of realistic size you can flip which one looks "simpler" just by choosing your reference machine—so calling the result "objective" is false advertising. (3) Marcus conceded that the constant is unbounded in principle but said this only bites for short strings and toy examples: Solomonoff proved that predictions made from the universal prior converge to the true, computable data-generating distribution no matter which universal machine defines the prior, so given enough data any two reasonable machines end up agreeing. (4) Elena replied that "given enough data" is doing all the work: the whole reason anyone reaches for Occam's razor is to adjudicate between hypotheses now, with whatever finite evidence is already in hand, and the very convergence theorem Marcus is citing bounds the total error in terms of the complexity of the truth under whichever machine you happened to pick—so a "rigged" machine doesn't stop convergence, it just makes you pay for a bad prior in how much data you need first, and that amount could differ astronomically between machines. She added that Kolmogorov complexity itself is not computable, only approximable from above, and neither is the universal prior, so no one can actually run any of this; every practical stand-in, such as minimum description length, has to fix one specific description language by hand, reintroducing exactly the choice the theorem was supposed to have dissolved. (5) Marcus's last word was that uncomputability doesn't sink the framework any more than the uncomputability of Chaitin's halting probability sinks probability theory: what matters is that Solomonoff induction proves at least one coherent, convergent inductive method exists at all, which already puts it ahead of centuries of philosophers, including Popper, who tied simplicity to testability but never made the notion formally precise.
