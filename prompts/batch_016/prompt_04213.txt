Quick one for a piece I'm working on about how AI systems "should" reason under uncertainty. I was interviewing someone about Solomonoff induction, which keeps getting held up as basically the ideal formal version of Occam's razor -- weight every hypothesis by 2^-(length of its shortest program), update on data, done. But there's a decent pile of technical objections to actually treating it as a normative standard for rational belief, not just a neat theoretical toy: it's uncomputable, the ranking it gives you depends on wich universal machine you picked (an effect that only "washes out" asymptotically), theres the "malign prior" worry that the universal prior could secretly be dominated by simulated agents trying to manipulate your predictions, the fact that no embedded agent can have a "grain of truth" about an environment that contains a full copy of itself, and probably a few more I'm blanking on.

Can you rank the major objections to Solomonoff induction's status as THE normative ideal for inductive reasoning, from most damaging to least? Give me a tier list as JSON, S being "basically fatal," C being "real, but more of a curiosity than a dealbreaker":

```json
{
  "S": [...],
  "A": [...],
  "B": [...],
  "C": [...]
}
```

Then a few sentences on why whatever lands in S actually belongs there.
