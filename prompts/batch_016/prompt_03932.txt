I'm a PhD student in the history of political thought, and I've spent the last few weeks working through Part I of Hobbes's De Corpore alongside his far more polemical Six Lessons to the Professors of the Mathematiques (1656) — a tract he wrote in the middle of his long and increasingly humiliating dispute with John Wallis over his repeated, botched attempts to square the circle. What strikes me is that Hobbes uses that very work to make one of his boldest methodological claims: that geometry and civil philosophy are the only two sciences capable of full demonstration, because in both cases we ourselves are the makers of the objects we reason about, and so possess the causal knowledge that is simply unavailable to us in physics. As he puts it there, "Geometry therefore is demonstrable, for the lines and figures from which we reason are drawn and described by ourselves; and civil philosophy is demonstrable, because we make the commonwealth ourselves." Amusingly, in that same tract he also insists that Euclid's own definitions don't really deserve their foundational status, since they omit the generative causes of the figures they define — which already makes "as certain as geometry" a slipperier benchmark than it sounds.

For a seminar paper, I want to get past the usual gloss — "Hobbes wanted a geometrical political science" — and pin down what this specific "maker's-knowledge" doctrine commits him to, as opposed to the more familiar rival reading on which a law of nature simply functions as a Euclidean-style axiom from which the other laws are "derived." Two things about the maker's-knowledge move bother me. First, a geometer's construction is general in exactly the way that licenses a universal theorem: I draw one triangle, but because the construction procedure itself carries the demonstration, the result holds for every triangle. A commonwealth, by contrast, is made exactly once, by a particular, historically contingent, often coercive act — so it's unclear what entitles the resulting "science" to hold for commonwealths in general rather than just the one actually made. Second, Hobbes's definitions of geometric objects are explicitly generative — a line, he says elsewhere, is made by the motion of a point — and some commentators (Marcus Adams, for instance) read the laws of nature as playing an analogous generative role: instructions for "making" peace, rather than commands "derived" from an axiom, which is meant to explain how they can be normative and demonstrable at once. But following a generative procedure in geometry cannot fail to produce the figure it defines, whereas following, or trying to follow, the laws of nature plainly can fail to produce peace, since the outcome also depends on what everyone else does. That disanalogy seems to bite exactly where it matters most for the claim that civil philosophy is demonstrable in Hobbes's own sense of the word.

Could you lay out what you take to be the most important implications of reading Hobbes through this maker's-knowledge conception of certainty, rather than through the axiomatic/definitional reading, for whether his civil philosophy can really claim the same scientific status he grants geometry? I would especially value your take on how the two worries above — the particular-versus-universal problem, and the fact that geometric construction can't misfire while political "construction" obviously can — bear on that question, and on how much of Hobbes's larger claim to have founded civil philosophy as a science survives them.
