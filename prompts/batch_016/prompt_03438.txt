A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about whether Quine actually earns the asymmetry he wants between ethics and science in "On the Nature of Moral Values" — reportedly the only substantial thing he ever published on moral philosophy, and part of why we had been picking through it so carefully for a reading group on naturalized epistemology. Quine's picture there starts from a broadly Humean, "bipartite" view of motivation, illustrated with a dog that won't touch a turnip, whose inaction could reflect either a failure to notice the turnip (a belief failure) or simple indifference to it (an evaluative one). From there he compares morality in turn with aesthetics, with language, and with science, looking for cross-cultural uniformities in each, and concludes that moral disagreement across societies is comparatively unconstrained next to scientific disagreement: science is disciplined by recalcitrant experience and failed predictions, while ethics, once you dig down far enough, bottoms out in "ultimate ends" that are irreducible, unjustifiable, and really just expressions of our innate likes and aversions — landing him, by his own admission, close to a Stevenson-style emotivism as a fallback.

My friend took this to license treating ethics as methodologically inferior to science. I pushed back with something close to an objection Morton White actually pressed on Quine in print (Quine wrote a short reply that neither of us has read, so I can't speak to what's in it): Quine's own holism already denies that individual scientific sentences face the tribunal of experience one by one — by his own famous formulation, they answer to it only "as a corporate body" — so a good deal of what does real theoretical work in science, like simplicity or conservatism, is no more directly checkable against a raw observation than an ethical principle is, and yet Quine lets it count as answerable to the world anyway. Given that moral sentences are logically and causally entangled with exactly the kind of non-normative facts (about suffering, need, and so on) that Quine's own comparisons keep invoking, why shouldn't that entanglement earn them a comparable kind of holistic answerability? My friend's comeback was that this misses a further disanalogy: science's periphery is causally forced on us by an external nature whether we like it or not, whereas Quine grounds ethics's periphery in nothing but our own tastes, so there is simply no external "moral nature" of the same kind for a moral web to answer to, however holistic its internal organization.

I had no good response to that at the time. Please construct the strongest possible argument that my friend is nonetheless mistaken — that Quine is not actually entitled to this further disanalogy once the rest of his naturalism is taken seriously — rather than resting on the bare assertion that holism alone should erase the distinction.
