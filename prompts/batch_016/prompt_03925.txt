I spend a fair amount of time reading rationalist-adjacent writing on forecasting -- Kahneman and Tversky on the planning fallacy, Tetlock on superforecasting, the whole "outside view" cluster of ideas -- so I couldn't help noticing, when two friends of mine got into it over dinner last week, that they had stumbled into what philosophers of probability call the reference class problem without either of them ever using that phrase.

The occasion was our mutual friend Owen sinking his savings into a new restaurant, and the question at hand was how confident to be that it's still open in five years. (1) Ines opened with the outside view: roughly four in five restaurants fail within five years, Owen's restaurant is a restaurant, and absent some special reason to treat it differently, it should get the base rate, full stop. (2) Marcus objected that this throws away everything distinctive about Owen -- fifteen years as a head chef, a well-capitalized investor, a neighborhood with rising foot traffic -- and that the right comparison class is something like "experienced, funded chefs opening in gentrifying neighborhoods," not "restaurants" in general. (3) Ines's reply was the classic regress: if it's legitimate to narrow "restaurants" down to "restaurants like Owen's" because that's more specific and more relevant, what stops Marcus from narrowing all the way down to "restaurants exactly like Owen's," a class with exactly one member, where the frequency is either 0% or 100% and tells you nothing? He can't just stop narrowing at the point that happens to flatter Owen's odds. (4) Marcus tried to dodge the whole apparatus: he doesn't need a reference class or a frequency at all, just a Bayesian credence that weighs the specific facts he has about Owen directly, without averaging over any class of similar cases. (5) Ines's comeback here was sharper than I expected: the instant Marcus treats fifteen years of experience or a good location as relevant to his credence, he's implicitly comparing Owen to other cases with those features -- he's doing reference-class reasoning too, just tacitly, and arguably worse, since at least her number owns up to which class and which data generated it. (6) Marcus's last move was to concede that there's no uniquely correct reference class, but to deny that this makes every estimate equally arbitrary: traits like culinary experience, funding, and foot traffic are plausible causal contributors to restaurant survival in a way that, say, Owen's astrological sign is not, and that difference should count for something even without a principled cutoff rule. Ines wasn't persuaded -- she suspected "causally relevant" was smuggling in an entire theory of what makes restaurants succeed, one that needed its own defense -- but neither of them could move the other, and the conversation got interrupted before it went any further.

I've kept turning it over since, without landing anywhere definite. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move?
