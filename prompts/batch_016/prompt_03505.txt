For a while now I've been reading on my own — mostly Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy articles and a couple of pop-metaphysics books, since I never studied philosophy formally — about the debate between endurantism and perdurantism: the question of whether persisting objects are "wholly present" at every moment of their existence (endurantism), or whether they are really four-dimensional "space-time worms" of which only a temporal slice, or stage, exists at any given moment (perdurantism). I follow the basic motivations on both sides well enough — things like the problem of temporary intrinsics, and the puzzles about a statue and the lump of clay it happens to be made of — but what I keep getting stuck on is what any of this is actually supposed to mean for ordinary life, rather than being a purely technical dispute among metaphysicians.

What set me off thinking about it in practical terms was a joke a friend made after I mentioned I'd started paying into a retirement fund: she said I was basically "saving up for a stranger." Under endurantism the joke doesn't really land, since it is simply me, full stop, wholly present now and wholly present in thirty years, who benefits later. But if perdurantism is true, my retirement-age self is, strictly speaking, a different temporal part of the four-dimensional whole that includes both of us — related to my current stage by continuity of memory, character, and body, but not literally one and the same "wholly present" thing that is typing this sentence right now. That distinction suddenly seemed to bear on something very concrete: whether there is a principled difference between sacrificing for "my own" future and sacrificing for some entirely different person.

So I would like to understand the most important implications of the endurantism/perdurantism debate for how much rational, self-interested weight I should actually give to my future self's welfare. Is the practical stake here real, or am I overstating what is ultimately a technical dispute in analytic metaphysics that dissolves once you look at it closely? And if perdurantists really are committed to something like my retirement-age self being, in the relevant sense, a distinct entity from me-now, how have they tried to preserve the ordinary, common-sense rationality of prudence and self-sacrifice, without quietly smuggling back in something that looks a lot like the endurantist's robust notion of being one and the same thing over time?
