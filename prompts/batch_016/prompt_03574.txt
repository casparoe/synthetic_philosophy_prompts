As a novelist, I've become preoccupied with a structural problem: my characters live their choices as genuinely open, while I, the author, already hold the book's ending in mind before I've drafted the scene that leads to it. That tension sent me back to Aristotle's sea-battle argument in De Interpretatione 9, and from there to the twentieth-century logicians who tried to make his suggestion precise. Arthur Prior devised two rival branching-time semantics for the puzzle: on the "Peircean" reading he himself preferred, "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is simply false today, since it does not hold on every course that events might take; on the "Ockhamist" reading, the sentence is already true or false today relative to whichever course will in fact unfold, even though nothing now available settles which course that is. Belnap and Green later argued that treating one such course as the privileged "actual" future quietly smuggles back in exactly the determinism Aristotle's argument was meant to block. Which position do you think Aristotle himself would adopt, and how would he defend it against the other two, using the actual argument of chapter 9 rather than the simplified gloss usually given in logic textbooks?
