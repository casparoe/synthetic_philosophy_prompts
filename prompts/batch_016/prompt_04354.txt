Frederick Whelan's "boundary problem" observes that no democratic procedure can, from within itself, tell us who is entitled to take part in it: letting "all those affected" by a decision determine the composition of the demos already presupposes some demos empowered to make that very determination. Whelan takes this circularity to show that the problem is insoluble from within democratic theory itself, and that the boundary must instead be fixed by some external criterion. Two influential candidates disagree sharply about what that criterion should be. Robert Goodin's all-affected-interests (AAI) principle would enfranchise everyone whose interests are causally affected by a decision — a criterion he himself acknowledges tends, for almost any consequential decision, toward enfranchising something close to the whole of humanity. Arash Abizadeh, David Owen, and others instead ground the demos in subjection to a polity's coercive laws: on this all-subjected principle (ASP), only those actually coerced by, or legally subject to, a polity's laws belong to its demos.

Write an essay assessing whether either principle genuinely escapes Whelan's circularity, or merely relocates it — for instance, by requiring an antecedent, non-circular account of who counts as "affected" or "subjected" before it can even be applied. Weigh the strongest considerations for each view, and close by defending a definite verdict on whether the boundary problem admits of a principled solution and, if so, whether AAI, ASP, or some alternative you specify comes closest to providing one.
