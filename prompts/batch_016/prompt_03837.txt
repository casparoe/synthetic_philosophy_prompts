A few months ago my sister and I inherited a small rental property jointly, and working out how to divide things when we eventually sell it turned into a surprisingly thorny negotiation, since neither of us can force the other into a deal and we do not value the same things about keeping versus selling. That got me curious, in a general way, about whether there is any actual theory of how two parties "ought" to divide the gains from an agreement when there is no referee around to impose a particular split, so I have been reading about bargaining theory online in my spare time (I never studied mathematics or economics past secondary school, so it has been slow going). I mentioned this to a friend who did study economics, and she said something like "oh, that's just the Edgeworth box thing," which sent me further down the rabbit hole than she probably intended. Several sources trace the whole problem back, before Nash and the other names people usually mention, to a nineteenth-century writer named Francis Ysidro Edgeworth. Since his 1881 book, Mathematical Psychics, turned out to be old enough to be freely available online, I decided to look at the actual passage myself instead of relying on other people's summaries of it. His Victorian prose, mixed with stray literary allusions and undefined technical terms, is rough going for someone without training, though, and I was hoping you could help me with it.

Here is the passage, lightly trimmed but otherwise in his own words:

"'Is it peace or war?' asks the lover of 'Maud,' of economic competition, and answers hastily: It is both, pax or pact between contractors during contract, war, when some of the contractors without the consent of others recontract. Thus an auctioneer having been in contact with the last bidder (to sell at such a price if no higher bid) recontracts with a higher bidder. So a landlord on expiry of lease recontracts, it may be, with a new tenant.

"The field of competition with reference to a contract, or contracts, under consideration consists of all the individuals who are willing and able to recontract about the articles under consideration. [...]

"A settlement is a contract which cannot be varied with the consent of all the parties to it. A final settlement is a settlement which cannot be varied by recontract within the field of competition. Contract is indeterminate when there are an indefinite number of final settlements. [...]

"Demonstrations.—The general answer is—(α) Contract without competition is indeterminate, (β) Contract with perfect competition is perfectly determinate, (γ) Contract with more or less perfect competition is less or more indeterminate."

Two things about this puzzle me. First, just on the level of following the argument: why does Edgeworth need two separate notions, "settlement" and then "final settlement," rather than simply saying that a deal only really counts once nobody anywhere can improve on it by "recontracting"? What exactly does "recontract within the field of competition" add on top of plain mutual consent between the two original parties, and why does that extra layer matter enough for him to build the whole theory around it?

Second, and this is really the part I most want to understand: in the bare two-person case, with no wider market to discipline anybody (Edgeworth's own illustration a bit later is two people bargaining directly over wages and labor, with nobody else to compete with), his conclusion is simply that the outcome is indeterminate, full stop, with nothing in the mathematics that picks out one deal rather than another. I gather that economists have since proposed various ways of pinning down a single point regardless, and I have come across one of these called the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution, which apparently rests on some kind of "monotonicity" idea rather than the "independence" condition that the more famous Nash solution relies on. Before I read further, I would like to understand what that solution actually does to Edgeworth's problem: does it resolve the indeterminacy in some principled way that tracks something we would genuinely want to call fair, or is it, underneath the axioms, really just a dressed-up tie-breaking convention, not so different in spirit from simply splitting the difference along his "contract-curve"? And since the contrast with Nash's solution is supposed to come down to that one monotonicity idea, could you explain concretely what it amounts to, and why someone might find it more, or less, compelling than what Nash's approach requires instead?
