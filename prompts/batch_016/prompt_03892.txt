My high school's Ethics Bowl team has been prepping this year's case on organ donation, and two of my teammates, Jonah and Talia, got stuck in a long argument about whether "whole-brain death" is really death. I wrote down their exchange as closely as I could:

(1) Jonah argued that the whole-brain standard tracks real death, not just a convenient legal fiction: the brainstem and hypothalamus are what integrate the body into a single functioning organism, so once that function is irreversibly gone, a ventilated body is just organs being run by machines, not a living thing.

(2) Talia countered with something she'd read about a neurologist named Shewmon, who documented roughly 175 cases of correctly diagnosed brain-dead patients surviving weeks to decades on ventilators, some continuing to heal wounds, fight off infections, and go through puberty; she also mentioned brain-dead pregnant women occasionally maintained long enough for a fetus to reach viability. If a body with no functioning brain can still do all that, she said, the brain can't be what's integrating it.

(3) Jonah replied that none of this is real integration, just local processes being propped up by the ICU: brain-dead patients typically develop diabetes insipidus (they stop retaining water on their own) once the pituitary fails, and need synthetic hormones to correct it — the machines and drugs are doing the integrating, not the patient.

(4) Talia said that proves too much: patients on ventilators after high spinal cord injuries also need constant drug support to keep their blood pressure stable, and nobody considers them dead, so needing life support can't be what draws the line.

(5) Jonah refined his claim: spinal-injury patients still generate their own drive to breathe from an intact brainstem, even though the signal can't reach the diaphragm — which is exactly what the apnea test checks for — while brain-dead patients generate no drive at all. The line, he said, isn't "needs a machine" but "is anything still trying, from the inside."

(6) Talia granted that this is a genuine neurological difference, but said it only shows brain death is the end of consciousness and of the drive to breathe, not of the organism as a whole. She added that the "cleaner" circulatory standard has a parallel problem: doctors declare circulatory death just minutes after the heart stops, which counts as irreversible only because everyone involved has already agreed not to attempt resuscitation. Both halves of the legal definition of death, she concluded, look less like biological facts we've discovered than lines drawn to fit what we need death to mean.

They never really settled it. Could you go through the exchange move by move, saying who has the better of it at each step rather than just declaring an overall winner, and then note anything important you think both of them are missing?
