This term I am taking a seminar on Frederick Douglass and constitutional thought, and we have spent real time on his postwar insistence — first in an 1865 address on the Thirteenth Amendment, then again in an 1867 speech — that full citizenship for Black Americans required not only the ballot and jury service but also practical access to what he called the "cartridge box": the ability to keep and bear arms against the violence of the Black Codes and the nightriders that followed emancipation. This is not merely of antiquarian interest: Justice Thomas's concurrence in McDonald v. City of Chicago (2010) draws directly on this history to help justify applying the Second Amendment against the states. I would be curious how you think Douglass would react to where that doctrine has since gone: in Heller and then Bruen, the Court has come to treat the right to bear arms as a general, historically fixed individual entitlement, tested mainly by whether a modern regulation has a close founding-era or Reconstruction-era analogue, rather than as a protection whose weight tracks a particular group's actual exposure to violence. Would Douglass most plausibly see the Court's current approach as the fulfillment of his own argument, or as having quietly dropped the premise that made his argument work in the first place?
