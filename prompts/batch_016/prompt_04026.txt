I've been rereading some old Overcoming Bias and LessWrong threads on backward induction, and I keep getting stuck on how genuinely absurd the centipede game becomes once you take the "common knowledge of rationality" premise completely seriously. Rosenthal's original 1981 setup has a unique subgame-perfect equilibrium in which the first player grabs the small pile on the very first move, even though both players end up far richer if they cooperate for a few rounds — and when McKelvey and Palfrey actually ran the game in the lab, only 37 of the 662 games they recorded ended with the first player defecting immediately. What I find genuinely funny, in a bleak way, is the second-order paradox that Bicchieri and Binmore raised against Aumann's defense of backward induction: if the players are common-knowledge-rational, then a player who watches their opponent pass at a node that "should never be reached" is stuck trying to rationally update on a counterfactual the theory itself ruled out, and it is genuinely unclear what a rational agent is supposed to conclude about the game they now find themselves in. I brought this up with a friend who does mechanism design for a living, and she was thoroughly unimpressed, which has only made me more determined to find a sharper way to make the point.

So: I would like you to turn that specific tension into an actual joke — a real setup and punchline, not an essay with a punchline bolted on. The humor should depend on the actual mechanics of the paradox (the unraveling logic of backward induction, the common-knowledge-of-rationality assumption, and/or the awkwardness of updating on a move the theory says cannot happen) rather than being a generic "economists are heartless calculators" bit that could be reskinned onto any game theory concept. Someone who has never heard of the centipede game should still be able to tell it is a real joke with a setup and a payoff, but someone who actually knows what a subgame-perfect equilibrium is should get an extra "oh, that's exactly right" layer that the first listener misses entirely. Set it wherever it lands best — two game theorists on a first date, a pair of trading algorithms, siblings dividing an inheritance, whatever serves the joke — just don't let the setting do the conceptual work that the logic of the paradox should be doing.
