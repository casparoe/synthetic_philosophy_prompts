For my Augustine seminar this semester we just spent two weeks on his lying material (De Mendacio, then Contra Mendacium), and we've got a short 3-4 page position paper due Friday on some angle of it. I got kind of fixated on the backstory to Contra Mendacium -- this guy Consentius writes to Augustine asking whether its okay for Catholics to pretend to be Priscillianists so they can infiltrate them and expose the heresy, and Augustine spends the whole letter explaining, at length, why absolutely not, not even for that. I drafted an argument about it that I want you to stress-test before I commit, since I've only got a day left to change course if this is a bad reading.

The standard line on Augustine we got in lecture is that lying is always wrong because a lie is speech that contradicts your own mind, and keeping your words lined up with your thoughts is a basic condition of the soul's integrity -- so lying damages the liar regardless of outcome, and no external good (even a life saved) can outweigh that internal damage. That's true as far as it goes, but when I actually read the Consentius letter, I don't think that's carrying most of the argumentative weight. What Augustine keeps hammering on instead is that if you grant Catholics may lie their way into the Priscillianists' confidence, you've lost your standing to condemn the Priscillianists on principle, since their whole heresy already includes the claim that hiding your real belief behind a false statement is fine when it serves the faith -- so entrapment-by-lying doesn't refute that claim, it enacts it. That reads less like "lying corrupts your soul" and more like "you can't beat your opponent's principle by relying on it," and it creates a problem I don't think he resolves: he's not nearly this cautious about killing, and he's fine with soldiers killing in a just war even though "it's justified this one time" is exactly the precedent-opening move he refuses to allow for lying. Is there something in his view of speech/signification specifically that would make this asymmetry principled, or is it just a real gap in his thought?

Could you poke holes in this for me? Specifically: (1) is the "two strands" reading actually supported by the letter, or am I inventing a tension that isn't really there, (2) is the lying-vs-killing asymmetry a known problem for Augustine with some standard response I'm just not aware of (would be kind of embarassing to present it as my own discovery if it's textbook stuff), and (3) is this even a stronger paper topic than just writing the more standard soul-purity version everyone else in the seminar is probably doing anyway.
