A friend and I spent an evening arguing about evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL) -- the idea, discussed in some effective-altruist and rationalist circles, that in a sufficiently large or infinite universe you should act somewhat cooperatively toward the values of other agents whose reasoning correlates with yours, despite having no causal contact with them. Neither of us gave ground. Her position: ECL is just Hofstadter's superrationality dressed up in cosmology; she pointed out that Caspar Oesterheld's original 2017 paper literally called the idea 'multiverse-wide superrationality,' so the later rebranding as 'ECL' is a change in marketing, not in substance. My position: the rebranding tracks something real, since ECL is meant to justify cooperation among agents with divergent, even conflicting, terminal values who never negotiate or communicate, whereas Hofstadter's argument was built for symmetric games between agents with the same payoff structure. She replied that extending superrational reasoning to asymmetric, differing-value cases is just applying the same logic to a wider domain, not a different kind of argument. I want a real verdict, not a diplomatic split of the difference. Please compare ECL with superrationality in enough technical detail to identify exactly where, if anywhere, ECL's case for cooperation relies on assumptions Hofstadter's argument doesn't need -- for instance, regarding correlation across dissimilar agents, compromise across value systems, and the role of evidential versus causal decision theory -- and then state plainly whose reading of the relationship holds up better, noting anything you think we're both overlooking.
