For the past few weeks, my graduate seminar on formal epistemology has been wrestling with higher-order evidence and the puzzle of epistemic akrasia — roughly, the question of whether it can ever be rational to believe P while also believing that one's evidence doesn't support P. The puzzle arises because evidence that your reasoning is compromised seems to call for two different responses at once: revise the first-order belief itself, or merely note, off to one side, that you currently look like an unreliable judge of the very question you're confident about. Neither option sits comfortably, and the students have taken the matter far more personally than I expected.

We spent a session on Christensen's well-worn "DRUG" case: a student works out a logic puzzle, feels entirely confident in her answer, and then learns that the coffee she drank beforehand contained a substance that leaves about half the people who take it just as confident in the wrong answer to problems exactly like this one. Horowitz wants to say that stable "P, but my evidence doesn't support P" states — violations of what's often called the Enkratic Principle — can never be rational, on pain of licensing absurd bootstrapping to a track record of success; Lasonen-Aarnio is willing to bite the bullet and hold that a belief can remain justified even while its holder has what looks like decisive evidence against that very justification. Both positions have committed defenders in the room, and each side is convinced the other is simply refusing to feel the force of the cases.

One student tried to lighten the mood with a joke about akratics being people who know they shouldn't order dessert and order it anyway — mildly amusing, but it has nothing to do with anything specific to higher-order evidence; it would work just as well against Aristotle's incontinent man. I'd like to send the class off this term with something sharper. Could you write me a short joke — a one-liner or a brief exchange, nothing belabored — whose punchline actually turns on the structure of this puzzle: the self-undermining character of higher-order evidence, the awkward position of the committed steadfaster or level-splitter, or the peculiar shape of akratic belief itself? I'd like something that makes a working epistemologist wince with recognition, rather than a generic joke about philosophers being indecisive.
