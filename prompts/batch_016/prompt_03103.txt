I'm about two-thirds through a novel whose narrator used to be an actuary before she quit to play poker for a living, and I've taken to crashing my partner's philosophy reading group every couple of weeks to steal real arguments for her interior monologue -- turns out formal epistemology people argue exactly like my narrator would at a table. Last session the group was on van Fraassen's Reflection Principle, which as I understand it (I might be slightly mangling the technical statement, but this is the gist) says that if you're already certain what your own credence in some claim will be at a later time, then your current credence in that claim, conditional on that anticipated future credence, has to match it right now -- otherwise a bookie can string together bets across the two times that guarentees you a loss no matter how things turn out. Everyone else cleared out around midnight except my partner Mara and her officemate Devon, who kept going at it, and I wrote down as much as I could because it's exactly the kind of tangent my narrator would get stuck on mid-hand.

Mara's opening move was that this is basically forced on you the second you notice it: if you're sure that tomorrow, after sleeping on it, you'll be 95% confident your book's ending works, and today you're sitting at 40%, something's already off, since by your own lights you know where the evidence is headed, and refusing to budge yet just leaves you exploitable in the meantime. Devon's comeback was the classic worry, though he didn't call it classic, he just called it "the confidence-pill case": suppose instead you know that tomorrow you'll take something that leaves your memory untouched but makes you wildly overconfident about your ability to pull off something reckless. Nobody thinks you should start raising your credence today just because you're certain that's what you'll believe by tomorrow night, so being certain of your future credence clearly isn't enough on its own. Mara agreed the pill case sinks the principle stated that bluntly, but said the only version worth defending restricts it to cases where you have no specific reason to think the future shift comes from anything besides picking up genuine evidence -- sleeping on a draft counts, getting dosed obviously doesn't. Devon wasn't having it: he said the whole job of a principle like this is to tell you in advance how to treat a future opinion you can't yet justify to yourself, and if you already have to privately decide which anticipated updates count as "legitimate" before the principle even applies, it isn't doing any work anymore -- it's just relabeling a judgment you'd already made on your own. Then Mara paused and admitted something that seemed to cut against her own fix: the actual argument for the principle, the bookie argument, never mentions why your future self holds the credence it holds, it only needs that you're currently sure you'll hold it. So if that argument is what's supposed to establish the principle in the first place, it doesn't obviously come with a built-in exception for pills, which means the same reasoning that's meant to vindicate the manuscript case also seems to vindicate the pill case. Devon's last word was that this leaves the principle in a strange spot: obviously fine for something like the manuscript, obviously not fine for the pill, and the one rigorous argument anyone's given for it can't tell those two cases apart.

It was 1am and everyone had work in the morning, so they just left it hanging there, but its been bugging me since. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who's got the better of it at each stage, rather than just declaring an overall winner? And separately, I'm curious: is there an actual principled reason the manuscript case and the confidence-pill case come apart that neither of them landed on, or is Devon's closing question basically still sitting there unanswered?
