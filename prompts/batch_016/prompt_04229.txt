I've been slowly working through this old public-domain English translation of the Zhuangzi (the James Legge one from 1891 — found it sitting on one of those ancient single-page HTML sites, not the nicer Burton Watson version everyone actually recommends). Got into this because after my dad's health scare earlier this year I started reading about how different traditions deal with death instead of just doomscrolling about it at 1am, and a friend who minored in philosophy pointed me at Zhuangzi as maybe the least grim option out there. I'm now in chapter 6, "The Great and Most Honoured Master," and hit this passage about a dying man and his friend that has a metalworking metaphor in it I keep re-reading without fully getting. (Small note: a couple of the names have accent marks in the original that just show up as garbled boxes in my copy, so I've dropped those.)

Here's the bit:

"Before long Tsze-lai fell ill, and lay gasping at the point of death, while his wife and children stood around him wailing. Tsze-li went to ask for him, and said to them, 'Hush! Get out of the way! Do not disturb him as he is passing through his change.' Then, leaning against the door, he said (to the dying man), 'Great indeed is the Creator! What will He now make you to become? Where will He take you to? Will He make you the liver of a rat, or the arm of an insect?

Tsze-lai replied, 'Wherever a parent tells a son to go, east, west, south, or north, he simply follows the command. The Yin and Yang are more to a man than his parents are. If they are hastening my death, and I do not quietly submit to them, I shall be obstinate and rebellious. There is the great Mass (of nature); I find the support of my body in it; my life is spent in toil on it; my old age seeks ease on it; at death I find rest on it: what has made my life a good will make my death also a good.

'Here now is a great founder, casting his metal. If the metal were to leap up (in the pot), and say, "I must be made into a (sword like the) Mo-yeh," the great founder would be sure to regard it as uncanny. So, again, when a form is being fashioned in the mould of the womb, if it were to say, "I must become a man; I must become a man," the Creator would be sure to regard it as uncanny. When we once understand that heaven and earth are a great melting-pot, and the Creator a great founder, where can we have to go to that shall not be right for us? We are born as from a quiet sleep, and we die to a calm awaking.'"

I already get the headline takeaway everyone points to with this stuff, that parts pretty much unavoidable — death is just another transformation, don't be scared of it. What's actually bugging me is the sword-metal bit specifically. It sounds like Zhuangzi is saying that wanting to become any particular thing, even something impressive like a legendary sword, is "uncanny," basically an act of arrogance against the natural process. But doesn't that logic apply just as much to becoming skilled or virtuous while you're alive, not only to what happens to you after you die? There's that much more famous bit later in the book about the cook who's carved up oxen for nineteen years and is so good at it he barely ever has to sharpen his knife, and Zhuangzi clearly wants us to admire the guy. But wasn't he, earlier in life, "metal" that wanted to become a Mo-yeh sword instead of just staying whatever dull blade he started out as? Is there an actual principled line in Zhuangzi's thinking between "fine, acceptable" cultivation of skill and this "uncanny" presumption of insisting on a specific outcome, or is he just not bothered by the tension? Bonus round if your up for it: this "don't have preferences about what you become, just accept whatever the process makes of you" idea keeps reminding me of the Stoic amor fati stuff, and I can't tell if that's actually the same underlying argument wearing a different metaphor or if the reasons for accepting your fate are genuinely different once you dig into it.
