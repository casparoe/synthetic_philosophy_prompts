David Lewis — the modal realist metaphysician, not to be confused with C. S. Lewis — argued in "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" (1997) that we are all ambivalent about penal substitution: we find it senseless for a devoted friend to serve a burglar's prison term or a murderer's death sentence, yet most of us see nothing much wrong with that same friend quietly paying the offender's fine, provided the offender consents. He considers and rejects the easy explanation that we tolerate fine-substitution merely because policing gifts of money is impractical, noting that if this were the real reason, we would routinely list it as a drawback of fines as a punishment — and we never do.

Lewis died in 2001, well before online crowdfunding made it commonplace for thousands of strangers who have never met the offender to pool small sums and pay off the resulting fine, often quite openly as a gesture of solidarity or protest against the law or conviction, rather than as a private favor between friends.

Using Lewis's own tools — his survey of what punishment is meant to achieve (retribution, deterrence, expressive condemnation, compensation to victims) and his diagnosis of why fines alone seem exempt from our rejection of substitution — how do you think he would judge this practice? Does making the substitution public, large-scale, and sometimes deliberately adversarial toward the law change his analysis, or is it just the same "de facto substitution" he thought we already tolerated?
