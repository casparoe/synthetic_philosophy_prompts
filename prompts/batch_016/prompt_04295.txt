In his Autobiography, Mill describes the mature political outlook he shared with Harriet Taylor as a qualified form of socialism. Two colleagues of mine, in a reading group on nineteenth-century political economy, have spent several sessions now arguing about how much weight belongs on "qualified" and how much on "socialism" -- in effect, about whether the late Mill should count as a socialist at all. I've reconstructed their exchange as fairly as I can below; both of them know the texts well, and I don't think either is simply confused.

(1) Elena opened with the passage in which Mill reports that his and Harriet's ideal of ultimate improvement went well beyond ordinary democratic reform and would have classed them, in the vocabulary of their day, among the socialists. She took this as about as direct a self-classification as a major Victorian liberal ever gave, and said that reading Mill as a lifelong classical liberal who made only late rhetorical gestures toward socialism refuses to take him at his word.

(2) Marcus replied that the very same passage immediately glosses this future arrangement as one uniting common ownership of the raw materials of the globe with the greatest possible individual liberty of action -- a proviso that most avowed socialists of the day, from Owen's cooperative communities to continental revolutionaries, would have regarded with real suspicion. A socialism whose defining condition is exactly what socialism was supposed to override, he said, is doing the work of his own liberal reading, not hers.

(3) Elena answered that this makes too much of the label and too little of the content: in the Principles of Political Economy, Mill does not merely gesture at a distant harmony but forecasts, with evident approval, that the relation between capitalist employer and hired laborer will eventually be superseded by associations of workers who collectively own their own capital and elect their own managers. That is a prediction that the capitalist firm itself will disappear, not a proposal to soften it.

(4) Marcus responded that cooperatives of exactly that kind -- formed through voluntary saving and free contract, still competing with each other and with ordinary firms for capital and customers -- are just what freedom of association under a market economy predicts and permits, not a departure from it. He pointed out that in the Chapters on Socialism Mill goes out of his way to defend competition against socialists' one-sided complaints about it, and concludes that only small, voluntary experiments on the Owenite or Fourierist model have actually been shown workable, while replacing a nation's whole economy with one central directing authority remains entirely untested. That, he said, is the reasoning of a cautious market liberal, not a socialist.

(5) Elena pointed to a detail she thought cut the other way: when Mill takes up the standard objection that common ownership would remove whatever check private property places on population growth, he does not give the standard liberal answer. He instead allows that this supposed weak point of socialism might, on examination, prove to be one of its strengths, since a community answerable to itself for its own numbers is not obviously worse placed than a market of separate wage-earners to manage the problem. Granting your opponents' best point, she said, is not what a merely sympathetic outsider does.

(6) Marcus's rejoinder was that this concession sits inside a larger argument whose conclusion is that the wholesale renovation of society socialism calls for presupposes moral and intellectual qualities that still need to be tested across the board and, in most people, created from scratch -- so that private property will remain the dominant arrangement for a long time to come, whatever a revolutionary government might decree. A thinker whose considered verdict is that existing arrangements will and should persist for the foreseeable future, Marcus said, is best described as a gradualist liberal with a socialist horizon, not a socialist with liberal scruples -- and it's that overall structure, not the isolated concessions, that dominates Mill's mature economic writing.

They went another round after this without really getting anywhere new. Please work through the six moves and say, for each one, who has the better of it and why -- not just an overall winner. Then say what you think both of them are missing or getting wrong about the terms of the dispute itself.
