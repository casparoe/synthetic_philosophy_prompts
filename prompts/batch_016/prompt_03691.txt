I'm reporting a feature on the current wave of clinical research into psychedelic-assisted therapy, and something a friend said to me in passing has stuck with me more than anything I've collected for the article itself. The empirical picture here is fairly well established: going back to Roland Griffiths' original Johns Hopkins studies, roughly two-thirds of participants in guided psilocybin sessions rate the experience as among the most meaningful of their lives, and more recent survey work has found that these sessions tend to produce a lasting shift in people's metaphysical outlook specifically, away from strict physicalism and toward the sense that consciousness is somehow fundamental to reality, or that the boundary between self and world was never as solid as it felt. Where these shifts occur, they tend to persist for months afterward, not just for the duration of the session itself.

My friend, who has been a committed, faintly evangelical physicalist since her undergraduate philosophy days, has nonetheless booked a guided retreat for later this year, mostly out of curiosity about the experience rather than any philosophical motive. Half-joking, but only half, she put it to me this way: "Statistically, I already know how this is going to go. I'll come home halfway to some sort of panpsychism. So doesn't intellectual honesty actually require me to start shifting my views now, instead of just waiting around for a mushroom to do the work for me?"

I didn't have a good answer, and it's been nagging at me since. Telling her to simply adopt, in advance, a view she currently finds unsupported and slightly embarrassing seems like giving up on having reasons for one's beliefs at all. But telling her that her future conviction is simply irrelevant to what she should believe today feels evasive too, given how confident she is that the change is coming, and given that she thinks of the retreat not as something that will short-circuit her rational faculties, but as something that will reveal things her ordinary state of mind normally screens out. Is there a principled way of deciding when a predictable future change of mind ought to move your present opinion, and when it's rationally safe to ignore? Does the answer turn on whether the retreat counts as a rational, evidence-producing process rather than "mere" chemistry doing the work, or is that distinction carrying more weight than it can bear? And is whatever principle is at stake here specific to drug-induced conviction, or should it also cover other predictable-but-not-yet-earned changes of mind, like the political leanings people are statistically likely to settle into by retirement, or the religious convictions that supposedly become more common on deathbeds?
