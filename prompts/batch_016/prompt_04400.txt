In my AP Statistics class, we recently covered Simpson's Paradox using the classic 1973 UC Berkeley graduate admissions data, where the university looked like it was admitting men at a noticeably higher rate overall, even though women were admitted at an equal or higher rate within most individual departments. Out of curiosity I looked into it further and found that the original authors, Bickel, Hammel, and O'Connell, actually reported a small but statistically significant bias in favor of women once department was properly accounted for, which is not quite the tidy "no bias at all" version of the story that gets repeated in a lot of textbooks. While reading around this, I also came across some writing by Judea Pearl arguing that you cannot just always trust the broken-apart, department-by-department numbers over the combined ones: whether the stratified or the aggregated figure is the one that reflects the real causal story depends on whether the variable you are splitting on is a confounder, a mediator, a collider, or something else again. That distinction has been bugging me, since in class we were basically taught to always disaggregate, no exceptions, and I would like to check whether I have actually understood it by testing it against some other well-known cases.
