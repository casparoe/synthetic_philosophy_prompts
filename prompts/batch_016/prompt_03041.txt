I'm a retired physiologist (spent about thirty-five years poking at cardiovascular and gill function in fish, of all things), and since retiring I've gotten pretty absorbed in philosophy of biology — feels like a nice continuation of the "okay, but what's this thing actually FOR" questions I used to chase in the lab, just one level more abstract. Lately I've been reading around the debate between "etiological" accounts of function, where a trait's function is whatever effect it was selected for and hence explains why it's still around (that's Wright's line, later sharpened up by people like Neander), versus "causal-role" accounts, where a function is just whatever a part presently contributes to some capacity of the containing system, full stop, no history required (that's Cummins). A couple of the survey articles I've read mention, almost in passing, that this whole framework has an ancestor in Kant's discussion of organisms as "natural purposes" in the third Critique — so, being retired and with time on my hands, I dug up a free old translation (Bernard's, 1892, off Gutenberg, since I wasn't about to pay for the fancy Cambridge edition) and tried reading sections 64-65 myself.

Predictably I only half followed it. Here's the chunk that's got me stuck:

"...a thing exists as a natural purpose, if it is [although in a double sense] both cause and effect of itself. For herein lies a causality the like of which cannot be combined with the mere concept of a nature without attributing to it a purpose; it can certainly be thought without contradiction, but cannot be comprehended.

[...] it is requisite, secondly, that its parts should so combine in the unity of a whole that they are reciprocally cause and effect of each other's form. Only in this way can the Idea of the whole conversely (reciprocally) determine the form and combination of all the parts; not indeed as cause -- for then it would be an artificial product -- but as the ground of cognition, for him who is judging it, of the systematic unity of all the manifold contained in the given material. In such a product of nature every part not only exists by means of the other parts, but is thought as existing for the sake of the others and the whole... its parts are all organs reciprocally producing each other. This can never be the case with artificial instruments. Only a product of such a kind can be called a natural purpose, and this because it is an organised and self-organising being.

In a watch one part is the instrument for moving the other parts, but the wheel is not the effective cause of the production of the others; no doubt one part is for the sake of the others, but it does not exist by their means... Hence a watch wheel does not produce other wheels, still less does one watch produce other watches... it does not replace of itself parts of which it has been deprived, nor if it has gone out of order does it repair itself -- all of which, on the contrary, we may expect from organised nature. An organised being is then not a mere machine, for that has merely moving power, but it possesses in itself formative power of a self-propagating kind which it communicates to its materials though they have it not of themselves."

Could you help me with two things? First, in plain english, what is Kant actually requiring of something for it to count as a "natural purpose" — and why does that rule out the watch, even though a watch's parts obviously depend on each other too? There seems to be real work being done by the difference between a part "existing by means of" another part versus just being "for the sake of" it, but I cant quite pin down what that difference amounts to. Second, and this is the bit I really want your take on: does Kant's picture here line up better with the etiological camp, the causal-role camp, or does it actually not sit comfortably in either box — maybe it's doing something the modern debate doesn't have room for? I keep flip-flopping on this myself, so don't just hand me a verdict, walk me through the reasoning.
