My team has a monthly "paper club" where we take turns picking an old CS paper and argue about it over lunch, and this month someone picked Turing's 1950 paper, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" -- the one that invents the imitation game. Most people only ever quote the opening pages, but he actually runs through a numbered list of objections to his own idea, and objection 9 is literally titled "The Argument from Extra-Sensory Perception" (he even breaks ESP into telepathy, clairvoyance, precognition, and psycho-kinesis, like that's a normal thing to define in a computer science paper). He flat out says the statistical evidence for telepathy is "overwhelming," works through an example where a telepathic guy correctly names the suit of a card 130 out of 400 tries against a machine that's just guessing randomly and lands on 104 (chance alone is 100), and floats sticking both contestants in a "telepathy-proof room" so a psychic interrogator or witness cant cheat. This derailed lunch into a side argument between two guys on my team, Devesh and Marco, that never got resolved, so I'm bringing it here instead of picking a side myself.

Roughly how it went: (1) Devesh said this is an actual crack in the imitation game's foundation, not a joke aside -- the test only works because the interrogator's info is restricted to one sealed channel (the teleprinter), and Turing is conceding that if ESP is real, that channel isn't sealed, knocking out a load-bearing assumption of the setup. (2) Marco said Turing patches exactly that with the telepathy-proof room -- it's an added control, like shielding a physics experiment, not a hole in the test's logic, so the definition doesn't actually depend on whether ESP is real. (3) Devesh said the patch isn't the point -- proposing a telepathy-proof room at all shows Turing genuinely believed the "overwhelming" evidence rather than just being agnostic for argument's sake, and that should lower our trust in his other confident one-liners in the same paper, like the bit about interrogators being fooled often enough within fifty years. (4) Marco called that a non-sequitur, since Turing frames the fifty-year prediction explicitly as his own conjecture rather than something backed by cited statistics, so being wrong about one narrow empirical claim doesnt automatically contaminate a differently-argued one. (5) Devesh brought up a stats blog post he half-remembered, comparing this exact move to Kahneman confidently declaring the evidence for social priming basically undeniable in Thinking Fast and Slow, right before that literature got hollowed out by the replication crisis -- his point being that when quantitatively serious people's bar for "overwhelming" is this low more than once in the history of science, that's informative about how much weight to put on any one confident claim from a smart author, Turing included. (6) Marco said the comparison backfires: most of Kahneman's other claims held up fine despite the priming chapter cratering, so one compartmentalized error doesn't generalize to everything else a person writes, and by that logic ESP shouldn't tank our trust in Turing's other claims either.
