This term I'm teaching a graduate seminar on twentieth-century moral philosophy, and last week's session paired Elizabeth Anscombe's 1956 speech opposing Oxford's decision to grant Harry Truman an honorary degree (later published as the pamphlet "Mr Truman's Degree") with the broader literature on the doctrine of double effect. Anscombe's central charge is not merely that a great many innocent people were killed at Hiroshima and Nagasaki, but that they were killed as a means: on her reading, the point of the bombs was to produce a civilian catastrophe large enough to shock the Japanese government into unconditional surrender — which, for her, is precisely what makes Truman's decision murder rather than the sort of foreseen-but-unintended side-effect killing that double effect is supposed to excuse.

A few students pushed back with what's now called the "closeness problem" for double effect: the worry, familiar from the terror-bomber/tactical-bomber pair and from cases like craniotomy versus hysterectomy, that almost any intention can be redescribed finely enough to push an apparently intended harm back onto the merely-foreseen side of the line. Nothing, on the face of it, stops a defender of Truman from insisting that what was actually intended was only a demonstration of overwhelming destructive power sufficient to convince Japan's leadership that further resistance was hopeless, with the deaths of the two cities' inhabitants merely foreseen as the regrettable means by which that demonstration had to be staged — exactly the sort of move critics use elsewhere to show that the intention/foresight line tracks nothing morally deep.

I'd like you to argue for the position that Anscombe's verdict survives this maneuver: that there is some principled, non-ad-hoc way of showing that the killing at Hiroshima and Nagasaki counts as using civilian deaths as a means in a sense that this kind of redescription cannot get around, whether by developing something like Warren Quinn's distinction between direct and indirect agency, a "mere means" or use principle, or some other resource you find more compelling. Please give me a real, first-person argument rather than a balanced survey: work out the strongest version of the redescription challenge before you try to answer it, and if you become convinced along the way that no rescue of this kind is actually available, say so plainly and explain exactly where the argument gives out, rather than hedging throughout.
