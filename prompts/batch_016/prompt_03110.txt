Rather than have him spout the watered-down "control what you can control" version of Stoicism that everyone already knows, I went digging into actual Chrysippus, and got kind of obsessed with his answer to the old "Lazy Argument": the objection that if your recovery from an illness is already fated, calling a doctor is pointless, since you'll get better (or not) regardless. Chrysippus's move, from what I can tell, was to say some facts are merely "simple" while others are "co-fated" (apparently he has an actual term for this, confatalia) -- his stock example being that it's fated both that Laius sleeps with his wife and that Oedipus is born, so the two are yoked together even though neither happens "no matter what."

While I was reading about this, it kept reminding me of a decision theory puzzle called "Death in Damascus" that I half remember from a philosophy podcast: a man runs into Death in Damascus, who looks startled and mentions he's already got the man booked for an appointment in Aleppo tomorrow. That night the man flees to Aleppo to try to dodge his fate, and of course Death is waiting there right on schedule. Decision theorists apparently use this to show that if Death is a near-perfect predictor, fleeing to Aleppo is itself evidence that Deaths book says Aleppo, and staying put is evidence it says Damascus -- so the reasoning never settles, it just chases itself in circles.

Would his confatalia move give the man any genuine reason to think fleeing could work, the way calling the doctor supposedly helps in the original argument? Or is there something about the symmetry of the Damascus case -- both options equally "fated together" with death -- that breaks his solution entirely? And if you had to place him on the map, would Chrysippus land closer to what we'd now call evidential or causal decision theory, or would he think that whole distinction is a category error from his vantage point?
