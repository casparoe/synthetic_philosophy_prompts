This term I'm taking a seminar on the philosophy of language that has spent the last few weeks on conditionals, and I keep getting stuck on a tension that our professor mentioned only in passing and that none of the assigned readings quite resolved. David Lewis gave us the canonical possible-worlds semantics for counterfactuals, on which "if A were the case, C would be the case" comes out true when C holds at the closest antecedent-worlds; we also read enough of Robert Stalnaker's rival selection-function version of that same idea to see that the two disagree on some genuinely technical points, such as whether there is always a unique closest world (Stalnaker's uniqueness assumption, which Lewis rejects) and whether a closest world need exist at all (the limit assumption, which Lewis also declines to assume). Almost in the same breath, though, we then read Ernest Adams and Dorothy Edgington on indicative conditionals, where the standard view is that the assertability of "if A, C" simply tracks the conditional probability P(C|A), and where conditionals are often denied truth conditions altogether -- a move that Lewis himself all but forced by proving, in his 1976 triviality results, that no interpretation of the conditional can make its probability match that conditional probability across the board without the whole system collapsing into something trivial.

For my term paper, I'd like to work through a real comparison of these two treatments of "if." Walk me through how the Stalnaker and Lewis semantics for counterfactuals actually diverge from one another, and then explain how the Adams/Edgington probabilistic approach to indicatives is supposed to escape, or simply sidestep, the demand for a matching truth-conditional semantics. It would help to see the contrast made concrete via Adams's well-known Oswald/Kennedy pair -- "If Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, someone else did" against "If Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have."

Finally, I'd like your actual opinion rather than just a survey of the field: is there a principled basis for treating counterfactuals truth-conditionally while denying, or merely bracketing, truth conditions for indicatives, given that ordinary English uses the very same word "if" for both constructions -- or is this asymmetry, on reflection, more an artifact of how the debate happened to unfold than something well motivated? Please defend one specific answer rather than leaving the matter open.
