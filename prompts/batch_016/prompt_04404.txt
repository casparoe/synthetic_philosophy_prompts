In the graduate seminar on early modern natural philosophy that I teach each spring, we recently spent two sessions working through excerpts from Kepler's Tertius Interveniens and book four of the Harmonices Mundi in translation. The discussion produced a disagreement between two of my students, Ingrid and Marcus, that the rest of the seminar ended up taking sides on without anyone settling it, and which apparently continued by email for most of the following week. Since I deliberately stayed out of the argument at the time, I would like a considered outside view on who actually had the better of it.

The exchange went roughly as follows. (1) Ingrid argued that Kepler's astrology is best understood as a mercenary performance rather than a serious piece of natural philosophy: he depended on income from yearly prognostications and cast nativities to supplement a salary as imperial mathematician that was often paid late, he was openly contemptuous of ordinary astrologers and their credulous clients, and his own quip about astrology being astronomy's foolish daughter, who nonetheless has to feed her mother, shows that he regarded the whole enterprise instrumentally rather than as something he was defending because he believed it true. (2) Marcus objected that this can't explain the actual content of Kepler's reform. If he had only wanted paying customers, he had every reason to keep selling the traditional package -- houses, planetary rulerships, triplicities, the zodiac's twelve equal divisions -- since that is what the market for horoscopes expected. Instead, as he told the English mathematician Thomas Harriot in a 1606 letter, he had already stripped nearly all of that away years earlier, keeping only the aspects and re-deriving even those from the harmonic ratios of his music theory, while proposing an elaborate physical story according to which the earth itself has a dim, non-rational "sense" of geometry that responds to the angles formed by incoming planetary light. That is a strange, commercially unnecessary thing to invent if the goal is simply to keep the horoscope trade alive. (3) Ingrid replied that this cuts the other way: an unusually capable operator who noticed that unreformed astrology was becoming an embarrassment among educated patrons would be expected to dress the very same trade up in exactly this kind of physically respectable, harmonics-flavored theory, precisely in order to protect the income it generated; she took his willingness to mock ordinary astrologers' methods while continuing to publish calendars full of similar predictions as evidence of exactly this kind of bad faith. (4) Marcus answered that mocking the crude, popular version of a practice is perfectly compatible with sincere commitment to a reformed core of it -- we don't say a physician who scorns folk remedies is thereby cynical about medicine as such -- and pointed out that Kepler drew the very same restricted, aspects-only distinction in private letters, not just in works written to sell: he would attribute a bad mood to Mars in a letter to a friend, while insisting, of the very discovery that had first made his scientific reputation, that no astrologer could have read it off his birth chart.

Could you go through Ingrid and Marcus's exchange move by move and say, for each of the four moves, who has the stronger argument and why? I would also like to know what you think both of them are missing in their picture of Kepler's actual position, and I would like you to close with a short overall verdict on whose reading -- sincere reformer or sophisticated mercenary -- comes closer to the truth, or whether that opposition is itself too crude to capture what he was doing.
