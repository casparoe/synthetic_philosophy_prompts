About two months ago, during what I had expected to be a routine progress meeting, my two closest collaborators on an empirical project told me—independently, but within the same week—that they believed a key step in the identification strategy I had been defending for months was mistaken. Going back through the argument afterward, line by line, it still strikes me as exactly as sound as it did before; I have not been able to locate any actual flaw. Trying to work out how I ought to respond, I started reading in epistemology and came across the literature on "higher-order evidence" (evidence bearing on the reliability of one's own first-order reasoning), a term David Christensen did a great deal to establish, together with the associated notion of "epistemic akrasia": roughly, believing some claim P while also believing that your evidence doesn't actually support P.

Once I had that definition in hand, the whole issue seemed to dissolve for me. Believing P while also believing that your evidence doesn't support P looks like just another way of believing P and not-P at once, so it seems to me that it must be trivially, definitionally irrational, no different in kind from any garden-variety contradiction. That is what makes it puzzling to find an entire, still-active philosophical literature asking whether epistemic akrasia can ever be rational, as though this were a genuinely open question rather than something already settled by elementary logic. Am I missing some way in which the "akratic" combination isn't really a contradiction of that sort? And, relatedly, given how confident I still feel in my own reasoning, does rationality actually require me to defer completely to my collaborators and revise my first-order belief until it matches my higher-order doubt, simply in order to avoid landing in this supposedly contradictory state?
