I'm about two-thirds into a novel where a minor but important character is a constitutional court judge in a country that's sliding into a slow-motion coup — the president keeps issuing "emergency" decrees and she keeps finding technically-plausible reasons to uphold them, even though she privately knows the legal reasoning is garbage. I wanted her to have a pet piece of philosophy she recites to herself (and eventually to her clerk) to justify this habit, and a friend who's way more into 17th century stuff than me pointed me at Pascal's Pensées. I dug up a free copy of the old Trotter translation and found what I think is exactly the right passage, except I've now reread it about six times and I honestly can't tell if Pascal is endorsing this view or quietly demolishing it, which matters a lot for how my judge would actually use it. Its two fragments back to back, in the section called "Justice and the Reason of Effects":

"Custom creates the whole of equity, for the simple reason that it is accepted. It is the mystical foundation of its authority; whoever carries it back to first principles destroys it... The art of opposition and of revolution is to unsettle established customs, sounding them even to their source, to point out their want of authority and justice... That is why the wisest of legislators said that it was necessary to deceive men for their own good... We must not see the fact of usurpation; law was once introduced without reason, and has become reasonable. We must make it regarded as authoritative, eternal, and conceal its origin, if we do not wish that it should soon come to an end."

"Justice, might.—It is right that what is just should be obeyed; it is necessary that what is strongest should be obeyed. Justice without might is helpless; might without justice is tyrannical... We must then combine justice and might, and for this end make what is just strong, or what is strong just. Justice is subject to dispute; might is easily recognised and is not disputed. So we cannot give might to justice, because might has gainsaid justice, and has declared that it is she herself who is just. And thus being unable to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just."

Can you actually walk me through what's happening across these two bits? Like, is "conceal its origin" sincere advice Pascal thinks rulers (and judges) should follow, or is he diagnosing something he finds corrupt and a little tragic about human justice generally? And does the second fragment, where strength basically ends up calling itself justice, continue the same thought or is it more of a complaint against the whole arrangement he just laid out? I don't want my judge to sound like she's misreading her own favorite philosopher, so I'd like a real close reading of the logic here before I decide what she does with it.
