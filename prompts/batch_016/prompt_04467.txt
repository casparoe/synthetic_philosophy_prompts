I'm writing a piece on medieval antecedents to contemporary debates in philosophy of mind, and I want to push past the usual gloss on Avicenna's "flying man" thought experiment. Avicenna asks us to imagine a person created instantaneously as a mature adult, suspended in air and deprived of all sensation — unable to see, hear, or feel anything, including his own limbs, which are held apart so that none touches another or anything external — who would nonetheless affirm the existence of his own self while affirming nothing about the existence of any bodily organ. One influential reading, associated with Michael Marmura, treats this as continuous with Avicenna's broader case that the rational soul is an immaterial substance really distinct from the body; a more deflationary reading, defended by Jari Kaukua, holds that the argument only isolates a primitive, pre-reflective self-awareness that remains neutral on questions of substance — a far more modest result than a proto-Cartesian proof of dualism.

Please make the strongest case for the first, more ambitious reading. Argue that the flying man argument, properly reconstructed, licenses the inference from "I am aware of myself while aware of none of my body" to "my self is really distinct from my body," and defend whatever bridge principle that inference requires against the charge that it merely reproduces the conceivability-to-possibility gap that makes Descartes's structurally similar argument in the Sixth Meditation controversial.
