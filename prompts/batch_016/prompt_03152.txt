Frederick Douglass and Garrison's big fight over the Constitution was really a fight about method, not just conclusions. Garrison's side said: look at what the framers actually meant (they loved citing Madison's convention notes) and how the document's actually been applied for decades -- that settles it, its pro-slavery. Douglass said none of that counts: you go strictly by the words on the page, you don't get to peek at secret intentions nobody actually wrote down, and if the text is genuinely ambiguous you're required to pick whichever reading favors liberty. He's also very explicit that everyone applying the Constitution the same way for generations doesn't prove that's what it actually means -- he flat out admits the practice was uniformly pro-slavery and says that's beside the point.

So: what do you think Douglass would make of the "history and tradition" test the Supreme Court leans on now (see Dobbs, Bruen), where whether a right counts as constitutionally protected depends heavily on whether it was historically/traditionally recognized? Is that basically his own method, or the exact thing he was arguing against? And would he even count as an originalist by today's standards, or does his rule just not map onto either camp?
