I've been on the wellness-and-supplements beat for a story lately, so these days I notice every time someone reaches for the word "true" in a way that would probably make a philosopher wince. Case in point: at a dinner last week, two friends of mine, Nadia and Colin, got into it over Nadia's uncle, who's convinced a copper bracelet is fixing his arthritis (the pain's genuinely better since he started wearing it, never mind that the actual trials on these things show nothing beyond placebo). Nadia's position was basically: if believing it is actually helping him function and feel better, then it's true for him, full stop, there's no extra fact floating above that. Colin shot back that this is exactly how psychics and con men stay in business, and that "true for him" is just "comforting to him" wearing a disguise.

Nadia didn't budge -- she said she wasn't just improvising, that this is basically William James's theory of truth: an idea is true if acting on it has good, workable consequences in someone's actual life, no further metaphysical fact required. Colin countered that Peirce -- the guy who'd actually coined "pragmatism" in the first place, before James ran with it -- thought that move was a cop-out, which is exactly why he eventually ditched the word himself and rebranded his own view with a name he deliberately chose to be ugly, so people would stop lumping it in with James's. Peirce, Colin said, wanted truth tied to whatever the whole community of investigators would converge on after open-ended, honest inquiry, not whatever happens to make one guy's shoulder feel better this month. Nadia's comeback was that this just relocates the problem: nobody ever actually reaches "the end of inquiry," so in practice, right now, all any of us has access to is what currently works for the people believing it -- which is her uncle and his bracelet. Colin said that's confusing "we can never fully verify where inquiry would end up" with "there's no fact about where it would end up," which are pretty different claims. Then the food showed up and everyone moved on, but its been bugging me since.

I know enough to know it's not just "Nadia wrong, Colin right," but I can't fully work out where each of them is standing on solid pragmatist ground versus quietly inventing a version of James or Peirce that neither man would recognize. Could you go through the back-and-forth move by move, say who's got the better of it at each point, and flag anything important that both of them are missing -- including whether either one is basically strawmanning the philosopher they're invoking?
