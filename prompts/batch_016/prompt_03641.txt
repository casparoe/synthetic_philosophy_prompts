I'm a PhD student in economics working in the history of economic thought, with a dissertation focus on monetary theory, and I've spent the last few weeks working through David Hume's 1752 essay "Of Money" alongside Richard Cantillon's Essai sur la Nature du Commerce en Général for a chapter on the prehistory of monetary non-neutrality. Both texts get cited as early anticipations of the idea that money isn't neutral in the short run, but the more closely I read them the less sure I am that they're describing the same mechanism. Hume's official position is that money is merely, as he puts it, "the oil which renders the motion of the wheels more smooth and easy," so that in the long run a change in its quantity just rescales prices proportionally; yet he concedes that during the "interval" between an increase in the money stock and the eventual rise in prices, industry is stimulated, because the new money reaches merchants and manufacturers first and they put it to work before wages and other prices have caught up. Cantillon, writing roughly two decades earlier but only published posthumously in 1755, three years after Hume's essay, seems to be making a different kind of claim: that new money permanently reshapes relative prices and the distribution of real income, since it always enters the economy at particular points—a mine owner, a landlord, a given set of tradesmen—and diffuses outward unevenly, so that who receives the money first matters in a way that doesn't simply wash out once the interval is over.

I noticed that Hayek, in his essay on Cantillon, found the parallel close enough to suspect that Hume must have known Cantillon's work directly, whereas other historians of economic thought I've since read argue the two men arrived at their positions independently. Given that Cantillon's manuscript had reportedly been circulating in France for roughly a quarter century before its 1755 publication, and Hume did spend several years there in the 1730s, I don't think the influence question is settled, which is partly why I'd rather set it aside and get your read on the conceptual comparison instead. Could you spell out what is actually doing the causal work in each account—a lag in aggregate price adjustment for Hume, versus a lasting distributional effect keyed to point of entry for Cantillon—and say how each squares with its author's broader commitment to long-run quantity-theoretic neutrality? I'd also value your judgment on which of the two offers the more rigorous or complete theory of short-run non-neutrality, and how much interpretive weight you think the influence debate should actually carry, given how similar the two mechanisms can look once they're paraphrased loosely enough to blur the difference.
