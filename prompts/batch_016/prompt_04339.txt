A friend and I rewatched The Prestige last week, and the second half of the film ended up derailing the rest of our evening. Angier climbs into Tesla's machine night after night, lets the "original" version of himself drop through a trapdoor into a locked tank to drown, while an identical man takes his bow in the balcony as the trick's "prestige." We spent a good two hours arguing about whether he was quietly murdering himself every night or just paying a very strange price for travel, and neither of us managed to talk the other out of their view. Since then I've gone back to Parfit's teletransporter case in Reasons and Persons and to Bernard Williams's "The Self and the Future," hoping to find better tools than we had that night, and mostly I've just succeeded in making the disagreement sharper rather than resolving it.

What would actually help at this point is a dialogue rather than another summary of the two positions. Picture two philosophically sophisticated people standing in front of a teletransporter that scans and destroys a person on Earth and instantaneously assembles a molecule-for-molecule duplicate on Mars, with one of them about to step in, insisting that this is simply a very fast way of travelling. I'd like their exchange written out: one character should defend something close to Parfit's view, that what matters in survival is psychological continuity and connectedness (what Parfit calls Relation R) rather than some further fact about identity, so that entering the machine is not relevantly different from surviving an ordinary night's sleep. The other should defend something closer to Williams's view, that our own first-personal, forward-looking fear of pain and annihilation tracks the persistence of this very body, in a way that no amount of psychological continuity can substitute for.

I'd like them to genuinely argue rather than trade prepared speeches. The Williams-style character should have to face Parfit's Branch Line Case, in which the earthly original survives the scan only to learn he has been fatally injured and has just days left to live while his replica on Mars carries on quite happily, and the Combined Spectrum argument for thinking identity is a matter of degree rather than all-or-nothing. The Parfit-style character, in turn, should have to say something substantive about why our apparently rational fear of tomorrow's pain doesn't lose its grip even once we're told our memories and character will be replaced beforehand, which is roughly the challenge Williams raises against purely psychological criteria of identity. Ideally each side concedes at least one real point over the course of the exchange, the way two people who take each other seriously, and are each a little unsettled by the problem, tend to when they actually argue it out.
