I cover science and ideas for a living, and I have spent the past few weeks re-reading Stephen Jay Gould and Richard Lewontin's 1979 paper "The Spandrels of San Marco and the Panglossian Paradigm" for a piece on how the adaptationist debate in evolutionary biology has aged. Their central claim was that biologists too readily assume every trait exists because natural selection favored it directly, when some features are really just necessary byproducts of other design decisions elsewhere in the organism. To illustrate the idea, they pointed to the curved triangular spaces beneath the domes of the Basilica di San Marco in Venice, which they described as "necessary byproducts of another decision in design" rather than structures built for a purpose of their own.

What I had not appreciated until I got into the secondary literature is how much trouble that specific example has caused. Architectural critics noted that those triangles are technically pendentives, not spandrels; more seriously, the Princeton structural engineer and architectural historian Robert Mark showed, in a 1996 American Scientist article, that pendentives are not incidental filler at all but load-bearing elements that carry the weight of the dome down to the supporting piers. Daniel Dennett pushed further, arguing that Venetian builders had genuine structural alternatives, such as squinches, and chose pendentives partly for how they looked — though Mark separately disputed that particular claim, on the grounds that squinches would not have worked at San Marco's scale. When Gould finally answered his critics in a 1997 paper in PNAS, he engaged with both objections but concluded that neither one threatens the underlying concept, then spent much of the rest of the paper reinforcing it with other biological examples, such as the masculinized genitalia of female spotted hyenas.

That conclusion has always struck me as a bit quick, and I would like to see it pressure-tested. Could you build the strongest possible case that getting the architecture right is not merely embarrassing for Gould and Lewontin's choice of illustration, but actually undermines the spandrel/adaptation distinction their whole argument rests on? I would like the argument to directly confront the obvious rescue: that exaptation, the concept Gould later developed with Elisabeth Vrba for byproducts that get co-opted into a new function, lets you simply reclassify the pendentives rather than count them against the theory. Please explain why that move does, or does not, work in this case, given that a pendentive's load-bearing role seems to be present from the moment it is built, without the historical gap between an accidental byproduct and its later use that the concept of exaptation seems to presuppose.
