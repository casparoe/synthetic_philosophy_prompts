I'm a PhD student in political theory with a focus on the history of political thought, and this semester I'm taking a graduate seminar that pairs Hegel's Philosophy of Right with Popper's The Open Society and Its Enemies. We recently finished the sections on the state's external sovereignty (roughly §321 to §340), where Hegel argues that war performs a genuine ethical function: left to a prolonged peace, the particular, private interests that structure civil society harden and become self-subsistent, and citizens gradually lose their sense that their substantial identity is bound up with the state rather than with property or personal life, so periodic war is what reasserts the priority of this ethical whole. Popper treats this doctrine, together with Hegel's organicist theory of the state more generally, as more or less confirming his larger thesis: that Hegel's dialectic is just a sophisticated way of collapsing right into might, and that this particular argument about war handed the Prussian state, and later fascist movements, a ready-made philosophical glorification of violence and of the individual's subordination to it.

Over coffee after class, a few of us started sketching out how this genealogy might anchor a dissertation chapter on Hegel's afterlife in twentieth-century realist international-relations theory, which treats states as quasi-organic units locked in a competitive, sometimes violent struggle for survival. Since Popper has already established that Hegel's philosophy of war is proto-totalitarian at its root, my instinct is to frame the whole chapter around the claim that any realist IR theory borrowing this organicist picture of the state is, whether its proponents realize it or not, smuggling in that same discredited premise. What I'm unsure about is just how far to press this: should I treat the Hegelian genealogy as a decisive objection to realist IR theory as such, or is it enough to note it as an uncomfortable intellectual debt that realists have generally failed to reckon with?
