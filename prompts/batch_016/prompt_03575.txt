I'm reporting on the rise of individualized risk scores in precision medicine: genetic tests and AI-driven diagnostic tools that tell a specific patient their personal probability of developing a disease or responding to a given treatment. This runs directly into what philosophers of probability call the reference class problem, since any single patient belongs simultaneously to countless overlapping reference classes (by age, genotype, lifestyle, tumor subtype, and so on), each of which can license a different 'objective' risk figure for that same person. As Alan Hájek has argued, this problem doesn't disappear just by trading frequentism for some other theory of probability. Please describe the most important implications of the reference class problem for how clinicians, patients, and regulators should interpret and communicate these individualized risk estimates.
