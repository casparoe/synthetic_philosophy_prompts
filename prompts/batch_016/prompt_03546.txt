I'm a retired experimental physicist (spent most of my career doing low-temperature stuff, a lot of it fiddly oscillating circuits) and since retiring I've been reading my way through the philosophy of infinity, which is turning out to be way more fun then I expected, and more maddening too. I already knew the classic Thomson's lamp puzzle: you switch a lamp on, then off a minute later, then on again after another half minute, then off after another quarter minute, and so on, so that infinitely many flips get crammed into two minutes flat. The "paradox" is that when you ask whether the lamp is on or off once all that's done, there's no good answer — it can't be on, since you never turned it on without immediately turning it off again, and it can't be off by the mirror-image reasoning. Having spent decades around oscillators, my gut reaction was "well sure, a system with no steady state doesn't have one," and I filed it away as a cute point about a divergent sequence rather than anything deep.

Then a friend of mine (a stats guy) told me about what I gather is called the Ross-Littlewood paradox, the "balls in a vase" one, and now I'm less confident I actually understood the lamp. Same basic shape — infinitely many steps crammed into a finite stretch of time — but at each step you drop 10 numbered balls into a vase and pull one back out, so the vase strictly gains 9 balls every step, forever. You'd think it obviously ends up with infinitely many balls in it. But if the rule is "always remove whichever ball has the lowest number still in there," every single ball turns out to get pulled at some finite step, so the vase is actually empty at the end. If instead the rule is "always remove the highest-numbered ball you just put in," you're left with infinitely many balls forever. My friend also swears you can rig the removal order to leave exactly whatever finite number you like — 12 balls, 4000, whatever — and, weirder still, that even removing a ball completely at random each time still empties the vase almost surely. All this despite the plain running count of balls in the vase, at every finite step along the way, being exactly the same in every version.

So here's what I'm actually curious about: unlike the lamp, which just dead-ends in contradiction, the vase spits out a perfectly crisp, well-defined answer every time — it's just an answer that's completely hostage to bookkeeping details that seemingly have nothing to do with the "rate" at which the vase is filling up. Can you compare these two supertasks properly for me? What's structurally different about a case that collapses into "no fact of the matter" versus one that hands you a totally definite fact that's this sensitive to irrelevant-seeming procedure? And which of the two do you think makes the worse trouble for anyone who wants to say completing an infinite sequence of tasks is even coherent — the vase, because it suggests the whole setup is so underdetermined you can get literally any answer out of it, or the lamp, because at least the vase's weirdness stays confined to which balls you're tracking rather than infecting a single physical fact like on-versus-off? Whichever way you land, I'd like your reasons, not just a verdict — and feel free to say whether you think either puzzle tells us something about physically completing infinite processes, or whether it's really just a dispute about the mathematics of limits wearing a physics costume.
