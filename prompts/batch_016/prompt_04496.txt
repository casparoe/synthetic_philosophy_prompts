A friend and I spent most of a dinner arguing about Michael Dummett's short book On Immigration and Refugees, and neither of us really won. Afterward I tried to get my side of the argument into better shape by writing the following, and I'd like your comments on it — particularly on whether I've represented Dummett's own argument fairly, and on whether the move I make in the third paragraph actually works or just trades on an equivocation.

Dummett spends much of the opening pages of the book demolishing the political rhetoric of politicians who warn that a country risks being "swamped" by immigrants; he calls Margaret Thatcher's use of that word "utterly ridiculous." Yet having thrown out the word, he keeps something like the thought: he proposes "submerged" as a soberer replacement, and argues that a country really can be submerged by immigration, so that a nation has what he calls a "right not to be submerged." He then qualifies this claim almost out of existence — the right is "of extremely limited application," Britain was nowhere near triggering it when he wrote, and in any case, having itself flooded Malaya with Chinese labor and Fiji with Indian labor under colonial administration, Britain would be "in no position to complain" even if it were. My friend's objection was broadly political: that this is a needless concession, since it grants the premise behind the very rhetoric Dummett has just dismissed — that there is some bounded "indigenous population" with a stake in relative numbers — and quietly treats the receiving country's point of view as the natural one to adopt, when any act of immigration is just as much an act of emigration.

I want to try a different, more technical version of the same worry, borrowed from Dummett's own philosophical work rather than from politics. Dummett is famous for arguing, against realists, that a statement only has determinate content if we can say what would count as evidence for or against it — that meaning cannot outrun what we are able, in principle, to recognize. Turn that standard on his own claim: what would make it true that a country has been "submerged," as opposed to merely changed? He never says, and I suspect he cannot say without reintroducing exactly the majoritarian assumptions his qualifications are supposed to disown. If that's right, "submerged" isn't a vague-but-contentful predicate on the model of "bald," where there remain plenty of clear cases either way; it behaves more like a predicate with no assertibility conditions at all, and so, on Dummett's own account of meaning, no determinate content. On that reading, the "right not to be submerged" isn't a modest, heavily hedged concession — it's an empty vessel that will simply take the shape of whatever the majority happens to be anxious about at the time.

Does that argument actually hold up, or am I illegitimately sliding from "vague" to "meaningless" in a way Dummett would have had a ready reply to? And separately, have I got the structure of his actual position right? I would rather find out from you than from my friend at the next dinner.
