In a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of infinity, we recently spent a week on supertasks, and I've come to suspect that Thomson's lamp isn't really a distinct puzzle from Zeno's paradoxes at all, just a more dramatic-sounding version of the same thing. The setup: a lamp that starts on is switched off after one minute, on again after another half minute, off after another quarter minute, on again after another eighth of a minute, and so on, each waiting interval exactly half the previous one. Since 1 + 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + ... converges to exactly 2, the entire infinite sequence of flicks is completed within two minutes flat. This looks like exactly the same convergent-series move used to dissolve Zeno's dichotomy paradox, where a runner covers infinitely many shrinking sub-distances (1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + ... = 1, for a unit distance) in finite time.

Given that everyone accepts Zeno's paradox as resolved once the relevant series is shown to converge, it seems to follow immediately that the same convergence resolves Thomson's lamp, and moreover fixes the lamp's state at the two-minute mark as whatever the series converges to. So I'm puzzled by how much continued philosophical attention Thomson's lamp still receives, as though it raised some further difficulty about supertasks beyond the one Zeno already raised and that convergent series already settle. Is there some way in which the lamp case is genuinely harder than Zeno's, or is it merely Zeno's paradox restated with a light switch?
