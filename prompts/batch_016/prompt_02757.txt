I've spent the past few weeks reporting a longread on how philosophers of probability reason about humanity's long-term prospects, and I keep getting stuck on the doomsday argument -- the claim, originating with Brandon Carter and developed at length by John Leslie, that reasoning via the Self-Sampling Assumption (treating yourself as a random draw from everyone in your reference class) gives you Bayesian grounds to expect humanity won't last vastly longer than it already has, since a birth-rank like yours is more probable if only a limited number of humans will ever live than if there will be many more. I found this unsettling until I read about the Self-Indication Assumption, which Dennis Dieks first used, and Ken Olum later refined, to argue that once you correctly condition on the fact that you exist at all, the doomsday update washes out. Except then I learned that Nick Bostrom, who gave both principles their standard names and explored their consequences most rigorously, thinks accepting the Self-Indication Assumption is worse than the disease it cures, because it commits you to the "presumptuous philosopher": someone who, handed two equally well-tested cosmological theories that differ only in how many observers each implies exist, must award the theory with more observers near-certainty before any actual evidence comes in. Every philosopher I've interviewed for the piece is confident the other side's fix is the real embarrassment, and I can't tell whether that's because the matter is genuinely open or because I'm missing some synthesis that dissolves it.

Rather than write another summary paragraph, I'd like to try dramatizing the dispute -- partly to think it through myself, partly because it might become a device in the final piece. Could you write a substantial dialogue between two equally sharp philosophers of probability, cornered together at a conference bar: one who accepts the Self-Sampling Assumption and bites the doomsday bullet, the other who blocks it with the Self-Indication Assumption? I'd like each to be genuinely trying to talk the other out of their view rather than just restating it, so have the self-sampler press the presumptuous-philosopher objection as hard as it deserves, have the self-indicator push back on where the doomsday argument's "reference class" is even supposed to come from, and have both respond to whatever the other actually raises -- including if one of them tries a move like Bostrom's own retreat from observers to observer-moments. I'd rather you leave the disagreement narrowed to its sharpest remaining form than manufacture a tidy handshake at the end.
