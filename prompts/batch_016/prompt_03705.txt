I am preparing supplementary materials for a graduate seminar on modal metaphysics, and this week's session sets David Lewis's counterpart theory against the rival view that ordinary particulars are literally one and the same individual across the possible worlds in which they exist -- a view presupposed throughout Kripke's treatment of proper names and developed more explicitly by philosophers such as Plantinga. In my experience, students tend to come away from the primary readings with only the cartoon version of the dispute ("Lewis denies that anything exists in two worlds at once; his opponents think it obviously does") without much feel for why intelligent philosophers have found each position compelling, or for how the exchange of objections actually proceeds once the initial statements are on the table.

Could you write me a dialogue, roughly a page long, between two equally sharp metaphysicians who happen to run into each other at the wine reception after a colloquium talk and end up arguing the point over drinks? I would like the counterpart theorist to be pressed with Kripke's well-known complaint that talk of "counterparts" quietly changes the subject, since presumably Humphrey himself couldn't care less how some merely similar person fares in another world; and I would like the transworld identity theorist, in turn, to be pressed with Lewis's own argument that treating an individual as one and the same across worlds saddles it with incompatible intrinsic properties -- different shapes, different numbers of fingers, and so on -- unless one relativizes property-having to worlds, a move Lewis thinks illegitimately treats a plainly intrinsic property like shape as though it were a disguised relation. If there is room, I would also love the exchange to touch on the awkward fact that a single individual can have more than one equally good counterpart in the very same world, and what that does to the idea that counterpart theory secures determinate answers about what would have happened to someone. Please do not let either side win too easily: I want each character to have a genuine rejoinder ready for every objection raised against them, since I intend to use whichever exchange feels least resolved as a discussion-starter in seminar.
