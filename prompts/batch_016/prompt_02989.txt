I don't have any background in philosophy, but a disagreement with some friends about climate policy sent me down a rabbit hole a few months ago that I haven't been able to shake. Someone mentioned that the philosopher Derek Parfit had already shown, decades ago, that "future generations" can't really be harmed by our choices in the way everyone assumes, so I tracked down a secondhand copy of his book and read the relevant chapter a couple of times, trying to work out for myself whether what people call "the non-identity problem" was actually as devastating as it sounded.

As best I can reconstruct it, the argument runs like this: exactly which people get born, generations from now, depends on the precise timing of when their parents (and grandparents, and so on) happen to conceive, right down to which particular sperm fertilizes which particular egg. Any large policy choice we make today — how much carbon we burn, how we store nuclear waste, how fast we deplete fisheries or topsoil — will ripple through millions of lives and shift that timing everywhere. So the actual people who get born under a careless policy are simply different people from the ones who would have been born under a careful one, and since those future people, whoever they turn out to be, will presumably still regard their own lives as worth living even on a more depleted planet, none of them can truly say they would have been better off had we chosen differently, given that under the "better" choice they specifically would never have existed at all. It seems to follow that nobody is ever harmed by even the most reckless, short-sighted policy, since there is no single person who exists in both scenarios for us to compare.

If that reasoning is sound, it seems to pull the rug out from under the entire idea of "obligations to future generations" that gets invoked constantly in climate debates, since that idea only makes sense if our choices can leave some specific person worse off than they would otherwise have been. So am I right that, at least on the harm-based version of the argument, this whole notion of owing something to people who don't exist yet rests on a mistake — and if the logic is really this airtight, why do philosophers, environmentalists, and policymakers keep talking as though such obligations obviously still hold?
