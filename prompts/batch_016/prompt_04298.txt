I'm a philosophy PhD student currently TAing a graduate seminar on Kant's ethics, and I'm leading discussion section next week on the First Section of the *Groundwork*. I've read the "moral worth" examples a dozen times now, but the more I stare at them the less sure I am that I understand what Kant is actually claiming, as opposed to what I've absorbed secondhand from intro textbooks ("act from duty, not inclination, full stop"). Rather than paraphrase, let me just give you the relevant stretch of text so we're working from the same words — this is Abbott's translation, from the run of examples around Ak 4:397–399, starting with the shopkeeper case for context before the passage I actually want help with:

"For example, it is always a matter of duty that a dealer should not over charge an inexperienced purchaser... his own advantage required it; it is out of the question in this case to suppose that he might besides have a direct inclination in favour of the buyers... Accordingly the action was done neither from duty nor from direct inclination, but merely with a selfish view. […] To be beneficent when we can is a duty; and besides this, there are many minds so sympathetically constituted that, without any other motive of vanity or self-interest, they find a pleasure in spreading joy around them and can take delight in the satisfaction of others so far as it is their own work. But I maintain that in such a case an action of this kind, however proper, however amiable it may be, has nevertheless no true moral worth... For the maxim lacks the moral import, namely, that such actions be done from duty, not from inclination. Put the case that the mind of that philanthropist were clouded by sorrow of his own, extinguishing all sympathy with the lot of others, and that, while he still has the power to benefit others in distress, he is not touched by their trouble because he is absorbed with his own; and now suppose that he tears himself out of this dead insensibility, and performs the action without any inclination to it, but simply from duty, then first has his action its genuine moral worth. Further still; if nature has put little sympathy in the heart of this or that man; if he, supposed to be an upright man, is by temperament cold and indifferent to the sufferings of others, perhaps because in respect of his own he is provided with the special gift of patience and fortitude... would he not still find in himself a source from whence to give himself a far higher worth than that of a good-natured temperament could be? Unquestionably. It is just in this that the moral worth of the character is brought out which is incomparably the highest of all, namely, that he is beneficent, not from inclination, but from duty."

Here's my trouble. On what I'll call the *purity* reading, Kant is making a claim about the actual psychological state required for moral worth: sympathetic inclination has to be absent, or silenced, at the moment of acting — which is presumably why he can't just describe an ordinary warm-hearted philanthropist and be done with it, but instead needs the elaborate stage-setting where grief has extinguished the man's fellow-feeling first. On what I'll call the *isolation* reading, none of that stage-setting is metaphysically load-bearing; Kant is just constructing a clean thought experiment to let us see the motive of duty operating on its own, and an agent whose sympathy was fully intact could act from duty and receive exactly the same moral credit, provided duty was doing the actual work — the sorrow is there for our epistemic benefit, not because co-present inclination would taint the action. What stops me from just adopting the isolation reading is the very next move to the naturally cold-tempered man, where Kant doesn't just say his dutiful action has worth, but that it has the "incomparably highest" worth of all — which sounds like the complete absence of sympathy is doing some positive work, not merely failing to get in the way. I vaguely recall there being a whole debate turning on more or less this point, something involving Henson distinguishing a "fitness report" model of moral worth from a "battle citation" model, with Herman pushing back on him, though I've never actually traced through the argument myself. Could you help me work out, from the text itself, which reading (or some third option) it actually supports, and whether the sorrow-clouded philanthropist and the cold-tempered man are really doing the same argumentative work, or something subtly different?
