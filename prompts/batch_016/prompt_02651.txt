I've run Ubuntu as my main Linux distro for years, and it took an offhand "wait, why is it called that?" from a coworker to send me down a genuine rabbit hole on where the name comes from. Past the usual "I am because we are" soundbite, I ended up reading a Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry that lays out a much more specific, and frankly more unsettling, claim from the philosopher Ifeanyi Menkiti — Nigerian, as it happens, rather than Southern African, which surprised me a little — whose work turns out to be one of the main sources behind the relational, communitarian picture of personhood that "ubuntu" has become shorthand for. On Menkiti's view, personhood isn't something you simply have by being born human; it's a status acquired gradually, in direct proportion to how fully you take up the obligations attached to your stations in life, with early childhood explicitly described as a kind of "it-status" that precedes real personhood.

Apparently this "radical" reading is contested inside African philosophy itself. Kwame Gyekye's "moderate communitarianism" is explicitly framed as a reaction against it, on the grounds that tying personhood that tightly to communal achievement leaves the moral status of infants, people with severe cognitive disabilities, and people who simply can't (or won't) complete a community's expected rites of passage uncomfortably up for grabs.

I don't have any formal background in philosophy, so I may be flattening a subtler debate than it deserves, but I'd like an essay that actually takes a side here instead of just narrating the back-and-forth. The question: can Menkiti's core idea — that persons are made through relationship and participation, not simply given at birth — survive being paired with the conviction that infants, the severely cognitively disabled, and communal nonconformists are still owed full, non-negotiable moral protection? Please work through the strongest version of whatever resources the radical view itself has for answering this (including anything that separates the metaphysical claim about degrees of personhood from a normative claim about who is owed protection), the strongest version of Gyekye's objection, and — if you think it's the better fix — the option of just decoupling "personhood" from "moral status" altogether (I think the name Thaddeus Metz came up somewhere in what I was reading, but don't quote me on that). Please don't leave it as a draw, though: commit to whichever resolution actually works, and say plainly what, if anything, a relational view of personhood has to give up in order to make it work.
