So this came up at a dinner with some old college friends a couple weeks back and I still can't decide who actually had the better of it. Quick backstory: our friend Owen found out, kind of by accident, that his coworker Dan had been fudging small amounts on his expense reports for about a year -- nothing crazy, maybe a few hundred bucks total, but against policy and not nothing either. Owen went back and forth for ages on whether to report it, especially since he and Dan are actually good mates outside of work too. Eventually he just asked our friend Priti -- who did a masters in philosophy and basically gets treated as the group's resident ethicist -- what she thought. She talked it through with him and landed on: don't report it, since it's disproportionate to how minor the offense is and would torch the friendship for basically no upside. Owen said fine, didn't report it, and told us at dinner that he "basically just went with what Priti said" because she'd obviously thought it through more rigorously than he had.

That's when Marcus and Livia, two other people at the table, got into it over whether what Owen did -- handing the actual moral call to Priti instead of working it out himself -- was okay. It went something like this: (1) Marcus said there's something off about just adopting someone else's moral verdict wholesale like that. With facts, sure, defer all you want, that's what expertise is for -- but a moral judgment only really reflects well on you if you get there by grasping the actual reasons, otherwise you've got the right answer for the wrong kind of reason, like a kid copying down a proof's conclusion without following the steps. (2) Livia said that's a nice distinction on paper, but we defer to other people's moral judgment constantly -- we trust doctors on whether a side effect is "worth it," we trust lawyers on what counts as a fair settlement -- and nobody thinks that's corrupting. Owen was also too close to Dan to see straight, so leaning on someone less entangled was arguably the responsible move here, not a cop-out. (3) Marcus said the doctor case is actually different: there you're deferring on a factual sub-question (how bad is this side effect, really) and still rendering the moral verdict yourself. Owen handed over the whole verdict -- report him or don't -- not just an input into his own reasoning, and that's the part that actually matters. (4) Livia said that line won't hold up either, because pretty much nobody derives their moral views from scratch -- we get them from parents, teachers, culture, which is itself a kind of testimony -- so if secondhand moral views don't disqualify ordinary people from being decent moral agents generally, she wasn't seeing why Owen deferring on this one decision should be disqualifying either.

Neither of them gave any ground and then the food showed up and it kind of died. Can you go through the exchange move by move, (1) through (4), and say who's got the better of it at each point rather than just declaring an overall winner -- and also flag anything you think both of them are missing entirely? I'd rather have an actual verdict than a diplomatic "both sides have a point."
