My sister and her husband have a running habit of guessing a suitcase's weight before it is checked at the airport, and over the years they have been right, within half a kilogram, about equally often, so neither has ever shown a real edge over the other. On our last trip their guesses for one bag diverged unusually far -- she said 18 kilograms, he said 24 -- which mattered, since their airline charged a steep fee for anything over 23 kilograms. She argued that, since neither had ever proven more reliable than the other at this game, the sensible move for both was to shift toward something like the midpoint, around 21, since that figure was a better bet than either original guess on its own. He replied that this time was different: rather than eyeballing the bag as usual, he had mentally tallied the weight of everything inside it, and this distinct method gave him a specific reason to trust his own number more on this occasion. She answered that this could not be enough, since anyone in the middle of a disagreement tends to feel their own reasoning was unusually careful, and if that feeling alone justified holding firm, their years of matched results would count for nothing. He responded that this proves too much: if a specific, describable difference in method never counts for anything, then no one could ever be entitled to trust even their own careful calculation over a casual guess.

I have no formal training in this kind of question, but the exchange has stayed with me, and I have not been able to decide for myself who is right. Could you go through it move by move, saying who has the better of it at each point rather than simply declaring an overall winner, and note whether there is some important consideration that neither of them raised?
