I've been a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, and one thing that has always struck me about that world is how confidently it treats death as an engineering problem to be solved rather than a fact of life to be reconciled with: cryonics, anti-aging research, and the broader project of defeating senescence all rest on the assumption that wanting to keep existing is simply correct, and that anyone who claims otherwise either hasn't thought it through or is rationalizing. I've mostly shared that assumption myself. But a friend from my local meetup recently got me reading the Zhuangzi, and two passages in it have been nagging at me since. In the first, Zhuangzi's wife dies, and his friend Huizi arrives to offer condolences only to find Zhuangzi sitting with his legs sprawled out, banging on a basin and singing; when pressed, Zhuangzi explains that his wife's death is just one more transformation in the same process that first turned formlessness into vital breath, breath into form, and form into life, no different in kind from the turning of the four seasons, so that wailing over her would only show he hadn't understood fate. In the second, Zhuangzi is dying himself, and when his disciples propose an elaborate funeral he tells them heaven and earth will serve as his coffin, the sun and moon as his jade discs, the stars as his pearls; and when they worry that crows and kites will eat his exposed corpse, he asks why that is worse than being eaten by mole-crickets and ants underground, since either way he ends up as somebody's meal.

What I haven't been able to settle is whether Zhuangzi is offering a genuine alternative to the "fight death at all costs" stance -- one that takes impermanence more seriously than either grim resignation or transhumanist optimism do -- or whether he has just dressed up an ordinary coping mechanism in cleverer imagery. So here is what I would like from you: a short story, something in the range of 1000 to 2000 words (though feel free to use your judgment), that brings these two outlooks into genuine contact rather than merely gesturing at both. You might, for instance, place a character shaped by something like Zhuangzi's outlook at the bedside of someone who has signed up for cryopreservation and fully expects it to work, or set the whole story at some later point where radical life extension has actually succeeded and one character still finds herself drawn to the idea that whatever burdened her with a particular form and labored her with a particular life is equally entitled to rest her in death. I would rather not see either side simply win the argument by the story's end; what I am hoping for is a story willing to sit with the possibility that Zhuangzi's equanimity is both genuinely alien to the instinct to keep existing at any cost and not obviously mistaken.
