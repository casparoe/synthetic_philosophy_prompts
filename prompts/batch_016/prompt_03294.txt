I spent a couple of years earlier in my career building planning and scheduling systems for warehouse robots on fairly classical STRIPS-style foundations, and back then "the frame problem" meant something narrow and concrete to me: you don't want to write an explicit axiom stating that, say, moving a pallet leaves the color of the walls unchanged, for every combination of fact and action, so you reach for closed-world assumptions, add/delete lists, or successor-state axioms, and consider the matter closed. Recently, a conversation with a former colleague who is now finishing a philosophy PhD turned into a long-running argument about whether large language models have essentially "solved" the frame problem through sheer statistical competence at commonsense reasoning. That sent me back to the original sources: I read through McCarthy and Hayes's 1969 paper and then Dennett's essay "Cognitive Wheels." I was surprised to find Dennett stating quite explicitly that McCarthy and Hayes had coined the term for a comparatively narrow representational puzzle, while he and other researchers apply the same name to a much larger problem — how any real-time, finite reasoner manages to ignore almost everything it knows in order to isolate what's relevant to a given action — a point he dramatizes with a sequence of increasingly sophisticated robots that all get blown up in different ways.

Digging further, I found that Fodor treats what looks like this same broader difficulty as central to his case against a fully modular, computational theory of mind, describing central belief-fixation as "isotropic" and "Quinean" in a way that seems to connect directly to the frame problem literature, though I don't have a firm grip on why those particular properties are supposed to be so damaging. At this point I genuinely can't tell whether I'm looking at one problem described at different levels of abstraction, or several distinct problems that happen to share a name because they all involve some notion of "relevance." I'd appreciate help sorting this out. Concretely: (1) How does the narrow, representational frame problem that McCarthy and Hayes posed differ from the broader epistemological problem Dennett has in mind, and does progress on the former — non-monotonic logic, circumscription, successor-state axioms, and so on — constitute any real progress on the latter, or does it just relocate the difficulty? (2) In plain terms, what is Fodor's argument that isotropic, Quinean belief fixation poses an in-principle problem for computational theories of central cognition, rather than merely a very hard engineering problem? And (3) does it even make sense, given what these various "frame problems" are actually about, to ask whether a large language model has solved any of them — or is that framing already confused? I'd like to come away with a clearer sense of what would actually count as progress here, and on which problem.
