A few of us who follow the rationalist/EA blogosphere run a small reading group, and two members got into a real argument after we finished Bart Schultz's biography of Henry Sidgwick -- specifically about the twenty-plus years he spent running the Society for Psychical Research, which he co-founded. Roughly reconstructed:

(1) Ravi said the psychical research is a tell for motivated reasoning: Sidgwick's ethics ends in an unresolved "dualism of practical reason" between egoism and utilitarianism -- a gap he thought only some further guarantee, such as evidence of personal survival after death, could close. So he spent decades hunting for exactly what his theory needed. In someone that careful, Ravi said, motivated reasoning doesn't look like gullibility; it looks like a scrupulous, well-instrumented search for a predetermined conclusion.

(2) Simone objected that the research itself was rigorous: the Census of Hallucinations tried to establish real base rates before concluding anything, and when the celebrated Creery-sisters telepathy results turned out to rest on the girls signaling each other, Sidgwick and his colleague Gurney caught it and retracted the results themselves, over the objections of William Barrett, one of the Society's own founders. People who need a conclusion don't do that.

(3) Ravi replied that pruning the easy fraud is exactly what lets the harder-to-explain residue feel more trustworthy than it should -- and that staying stuck for decades on a question the base rates should have settled quickly is itself telling, especially since after Sidgwick died, the "cross-correspondences" kept turning up just the evidence the group was hoping for, eventually persuading even his wife Eleanor, who'd built her name debunking fraudulent mediums.

(4) Simone answered that this proves too much: it treats both confirmation and self-correction as evidence of the same bias, which makes the charge unfalsifiable. She also pointed out that Sidgwick died in 1900 still openly undecided -- an odd resting point for a wish-driven search -- and that trained fraud-hunters failing to find the trick, despite every incentive to find one, is itself some evidence, however weak.
