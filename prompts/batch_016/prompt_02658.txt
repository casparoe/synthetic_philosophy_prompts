At a dinner party, someone told a story about her uncle, who has a standing arrangement with a local watch dealer: whenever the dealer offers to swap the vintage watch he currently owns for one he prefers more, he pays a small trade-in fee and takes the deal. His preferences among his three watches — a Rolex, an Omega, and a Seiko — are stable but cyclical: he prefers the Rolex to the Omega, the Omega to the Seiko, and the Seiko to the Rolex. Over the past year this arrangement has cost him several hundred dollars in fees, while, watch for watch, he has ended up right back where he started. Two other guests fell into an argument about whether this is irrational.

Nora said the cyclical preferences are themselves the problem: rationality requires transitivity, and this is a textbook money pump, proof that he can be exploited indefinitely by anyone willing to charge a fee for each swap.

Milo said the cycle isn't obviously irrational on its own — maybe the uncle really does prefer a scratched Seiko to a mint Rolex, a mint Rolex to a worn Omega, and a worn Omega to a scratched Seiko, for coherent-enough aesthetic reasons that just don't collapse into one master ranking. If anything is irrational here, Milo said, it's failing to notice the pattern and pre-commit to stop trading, not the underlying preferences.

Nora replied that this concedes her point: if a more far-sighted version of the uncle could foresee the pattern and commit to refuse the next trade, that shows rationality demands exactly the kind of foresight the actual uncle lacks. And since real preferences are always elicited one pairwise choice at a time, the "myopic" chooser who gets pumped isn't some exotic edge case — he's the normal case.

Milo answered that this proves too much: almost any instance of simply acting on the preference you actually have, rather than overriding it by reference to some longer-term plan, could be redescribed as a "failure of foresight." Unless Nora can explain why forward planning should take precedence over the preference you hold at the moment of choice, she's asserting that resoluteness is required, not showing it.
