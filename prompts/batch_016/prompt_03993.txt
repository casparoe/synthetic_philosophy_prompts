I'm several chapters into a novel set aboard a research vessel that has lost power in a storm in the Southern Ocean, and I've stalled on a scene because I'd rather get the philosophy right than fake it. Two of my characters are the only ones left who can operate the ship's single working rescue winch, and they have time to free either the six-person survey team trapped in the flooding lower hold or the one engineer trapped alone as the engine room goes under, but not both. Renata, who taught triage ethics before she went to sea, wants to say the numbers simply settle it, but she thinks of herself as a contractualist rather than a utilitarian, so she can't just say the six outweigh the one on some shared ledger of suffering -- she needs a way to reach the same answer without that kind of interpersonal weighing. Yannick, who has spent the crossing reading Taurek, thinks this is exactly the sleight of hand Taurek diagnosed: that talk of "the numbers" quietly treats six deaths as addable into one larger loss, when a death is only ever a loss to the person who dies, and there is no one for whom the loss of six is six times as bad. His own fallback, arrived at with some visible discomfort, is that the only fair thing left to do is toss a coin.

Could you draft the exchange between them -- four or five exchanges, a couple of lines each -- written so that every line is a genuine response to what the other has just said, and not a restatement of first principles? Don't let either of them get away with the easy version of their own view: Renata should have to say something sharper than "obviously six matters more than one" to explain how a contractualist earns the right to let numbers decide without quietly reintroducing the very kind of aggregation she's supposed to reject, and Yannick should have to face whether he'd still reach for the coin if it were six people against six thousand, and what that commitment would cost him. I'd like the scene to end with each of them having landed a real blow on the other rather than one side quietly winning, since that tension is what the chapter actually needs.
