Hey - I'm working on a long-form piece about the history of animal sacrifice across religious traditions (it started as a conversation with an anthropologist friend about "costly signaling" theories of ritual, then completely derailed into medieval Jewish philosophy, as these things do). A rabbi I interviewed for background kept steering me back to Maimonides's Guide for the Perplexed, Part III, chapter 32, and now I've read the same two paragraphs about six times because I dont trust myself to have the argument straight before I put anything in print.

Here's the first chunk: "Many precepts in our Law are the result of a similar course adopted by the same Supreme Being. It is, namely, impossible to go suddenly from one extreme to the other: it is therefore according to the nature of man impossible for him suddenly to discontinue everything to which he has been accustomed... the custom which was in those days general among all men, and the general mode of worship in which the Israelites were brought up, consisted in sacrificing animals in those temples which contained certain images... It was in accordance with the wisdom and plan of God... that He did not command us to give up and to discontinue all these manners of service; for to obey such a commandment it would have been contrary to the nature of man, who generally cleaves to that to which he is used. For this reason God allowed these kinds of service to continue; He transferred to His service that which had formerly served as a worship of created beings, and of things imaginary and unreal, and commanded us to serve Him in the same manner." And then, a bit later, dealing with Jeremiah 7:22-23 ("I spake not unto your fathers... concerning burnt-offering or sacrifices"): "This passage has been found difficult in the opinion of all those whose words I read or heard; they ask, How can Jeremiah say that God did not command us about burnt-offering and sacrifice, seeing so many precepts refer to sacrifice?... Jeremiah says [in the name of God] the primary object of the precepts is this, Know me, and serve no other being... the commandment that sacrifices shall be brought... has for its object the success of that principle among you; and for its sake I have transferred these modes of worship to my name."

What I can't untangle is this: Maimonides seems to be saying sacrifice was never really the point, it was scaffolding to wean a psychologically primitive audience off idol worship on the way to something purer, like prayer. Ok, fine - but then he turns around and treats the sacrificial laws as every bit as binding, permanent and divinely legislated as anything else in the Torah (which family gets to be priests, which animals, which days, all of it), and he's elsewhere completely adamant that the Law is perfect and can never be revised or superseded. So if sacrifice is basically training wheels for an ancient audience's psychology, in what sense does it stay eternally commanded instead of getting quietly retired once "the training" has worked - and doesn't that get even weirder once you remember he also codifies the full restoration of animal sacrifice as part of the Messianic future? Is there an actual logical move here that resolves the tension, or is this a genuine soft spot in his account (I have a vague memory of Nachmanides going after him on more or less this point)? I don't need a full essay back, just help me see clearly what's actually going on argument-wise in these two paragraphs before I write about it.
