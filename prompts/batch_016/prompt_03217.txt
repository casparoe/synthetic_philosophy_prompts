I've been sitting in on a philosophy of psychiatry seminar this term, and after last week's session on Ian Hacking's work on classification, a friend from the seminar and I ended up arguing about it for nearly two hours over coffee afterward, without reaching anything like a resolution. The point of contention was how much weight to put on what Hacking calls the looping effect. His distinction is between indifferent kinds — things like water or sulphur, which are entirely unaffected by how we classify them — and interactive kinds, such as multiple personality disorder (what the DSM now calls dissociative identity disorder), where the classification changes the behavior and self-understanding of the people classified, which can in turn change the diagnostic category itself, in a loop with no natural stopping point. Hacking offers many examples along these lines, including, memorably, a child diagnosed with ADHD who is then placed in a "stimulant-free schoolroom": the label reshapes the very environment it was supposed only to describe, and the resulting behavior feeds back into how the category gets used clinically. My friend's position was that this is fatal to treating categories like ADHD or PTSD as natural kinds in any serious sense: if part of what fixes a category's membership is the classified individuals' own uptake of the label, there is no stable, mind-independent cluster of properties left to track, only a moving target that we partly create by naming it.

I argued that this inference is too quick, because it assumes natural kinds must have an essence, or at least a fixed set of necessary and sufficient conditions — exactly the assumption that Richard Boyd's homeostatic-property-cluster (HPC) account of natural kinds was designed to reject. On Boyd's view, a kind can be perfectly real and mind-independent, with fuzzy boundaries and properties that drift over time, so long as its typical properties are held together by some causal mechanism; biological species are the standard case. It seemed to me that a looping process is itself just such a mechanism, so that looping effects should count as evidence of one particular way a homeostatic cluster can be sustained, rather than as evidence against natural-kindhood as such. The best reply my friend had, which I couldn't fully answer at the time, was that this glosses over a real disanalogy: the mechanisms Boyd had in mind — interbreeding, shared selection pressures, and so on — operate whether or not the organisms involved understand anything about them, whereas Hacking's looping works specifically through the classified person's own awareness of, and reaction to, the label. That, she said, makes it a fundamentally different and much less stable kind of mechanism than anything in Boyd's original picture, and quite possibly no kind-sustaining mechanism at all.

I'd like you to make the strongest possible case for my side, since I couldn't manage it on the spot. Please argue that the presence of looping effects in a category like ADHD or PTSD does not, by itself, show that the category cannot be a homeostatic-property-cluster natural kind — and hence that Hacking's interactive/indifferent distinction should not simply be equated with the natural-kind/human-kind distinction it is often taken to track. I would especially like you to engage directly with my friend's disanalogy objection rather than set it aside: if a mechanism that runs through the classified person's own self-understanding really is different in kind from the mechanisms Boyd describes, say exactly what would have to be true of such a mechanism for it to still play the same kind-sustaining role.
