So my philosophy seminar this semester is basically a deep dive on promising -- why they bind, conventionalist vs. expectation vs. authority-based theories, all that -- and something that happened with my friend Dana turned into a real argument at dinner last week between two people from that class. Backstory: Dana's grandma passed away a couple months ago, and right before she died she got Dana to promise, out loud, hand-holding, the whole deathbed thing, that Dana would never sell her late grandfather's record collection and would keep it in the family forever. Thing is, Dana doesn't even like the records, has nowhere to put them, and could really use the cash (grad school, need I say more). Nobody else in the family knows the promise happened, and nobody would ever find out if the records just quietly ended up at a used vinyl shop. Dana brought it up half as a joke and it turned into a whole fight between Mara and Theo that honestly would not die.

Here's roughly how it went. (1) Mara's opening move was that promises bind because they create expectations (or invite reliance/trust, pick your flavor) in a living person who can actually be let down -- basically the assurance-view stuff we did on Scanlon. Grandma's dead, she has zero mental states left to disappoint, so theres no one left to wrong and Dana's off the hook. (2) Theo answered that this gets the order of explanation backwards: the obligation was already created the second Dana knowingly and voluntarily gave her word, which is more like the normative-power/authority picture (he kept bringing up Owens) -- you bind yourself by the act of promising, and that kind of self-binding doesn't just evaporate because the other party's mental life later changes or disappears. He added that we obviously think promises to the dead can matter generally, like honoring a will. (3) Mara's comeback was that the authority view has basically the identical problem it's accusing hers of: if what ultimately matters is the promisee's standing to release you or enforce against you, a dead person can't do that any more than she can be disappointed -- so if anything the authority view makes the promise weirdly permanent and uncancellable, which seems too strong in the other direction. (4) Theo pivoted here, saying maybe the actual wrong, if there is one, is to the living relatives who'd be hurt or lose trust in Dana if they somehow found out -- not to grandma herself. (5) Mara's closer was that this move basically forfeits the argument, since it makes the promise-to-grandma do zero independent work, which contradicts how everyone at the table actually reacted -- we all felt Dana would be betraying grandma specifically, not just risking some future gossip.
