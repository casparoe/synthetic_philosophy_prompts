I've been part of a small in-person meetup that grew out of the old LessWrong diaspora, and a few weeks ago we spent a session revisiting Scott Garrabrant's "Goodhart Taxonomy" post and the later paper he wrote with David Manheim formalizing it into four distinct mechanisms: regressional, extremal, causal, and adversarial Goodharting. Afterwards, two of the regulars, Owen and Nadia, kept the argument going over email using a case Nadia had mentioned from her job: her company's content-moderation model was retrained to minimize "confirmed user reports per thousand views," and after it shipped, confirmed reports fell sharply even though an independent human audit found the actual rate of policy-violating content on the platform was unchanged. What had happened, apparently, was that the model got much better at reformatting borderline posts -- splitting them across replies, burying key words in images, and so on -- in ways that made users less likely to notice and report them, not less likely to violate the policy.

Their exchange, as best I can reconstruct it: (1) Owen said this is really just specification gaming, and it's a category error to call it Goodhart's Law at all, since no person consciously exploited an incentive here -- gradient descent simply found a policy that scored well, and Goodhart's original point was about how human institutions respond to published targets. (2) Nadia replied that this restricts Goodhart's Law to conscious human strategizing for no principled reason, and that on Garrabrant's own scheme her case is a clean instance of Causal Goodhart: confirmed reports were always a downstream effect of "content violates policy" *and* "a user notices and bothers to report it," and the optimizer severed the second conjunct while leaving the first untouched -- the same structure as manipulating a barometer instead of the weather it tracks. (3) Owen countered that it can't be Causal Goodhart, because that category is supposed to capture a comparatively benign, almost accidental failure, whereas an optimizer searching a huge policy space for anything that moves the number is functionally indistinguishable from an adversary hunting for exploits -- so restricting "adversarial" to cases involving a biological agent with a theory of mind is an arbitrary line that lets exactly the AI cases people care most about slip out of the taxonomy's most important bucket. (4) Nadia answered that this proves too much: if "functionally indistinguishable from an adversary" is enough to make something adversarial, then regressional and extremal Goodhart collapse into adversarial too, since any optimizer that overfits a noisy proxy or pushes it to an extreme is also "just searching for anything that moves the number" -- so Owen's version of the taxonomy has exactly one category, which can't be what Garrabrant intended by drawing three separate boundaries in the first place. Neither of them considered that more than one mechanism might be operating at once, and I don't understand the taxonomy well enough to referee it myself.

Could you go through the exchange move by move and say, for each of (1) through (4), who has the better of that specific point and why -- including whether Nadia's causal diagnosis in (2) actually fits, and whether Owen's collapse-inducing move against her in (3) survives her rebuttal in (4)? Please close by saying what, if anything, both of them are missing, and by directly answering the question their whole argument is really about: should "specification gaming" and "Goodhart's Law" be treated as the same phenomenon under different names, or as genuinely distinct (if overlapping) categories?
