In my Latin class, we are reading Cicero's De Senectute in the Loeb translation, and I have come across something in Cato's argument against fearing death that I cannot quite reconcile. In Chapter 19, addressing the complaint that old age is too close to death, Cato offers what looks like a complete argument on its own: "For clearly death is negligible, if it utterly annihilates the soul, or even desirable, if it conducts the soul to some place where it is to live for ever. Surely no other alternative can be found." This dilemma seems designed to make the truth about the soul irrelevant to whether death should be feared. Yet in the closing chapters (sections 77–85), Cato spends a great deal of time doing exactly what that dilemma implied was unnecessary: building a detailed, one-sided case that the soul is definitely immortal, invoking Pythagoras, Plato's view that learning is a form of recollection, and Cyrus the Elder's deathbed speech from Xenophon's Cyropaedia. If the earlier either-or argument already covers both possible outcomes, why does he need this later argument at all? Is this a genuine inconsistency on Cicero's part, or is there a philosophical reason the dilemma alone would not have been satisfying?
