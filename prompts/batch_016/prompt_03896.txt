I fell down kind of a rabbit hole a few weeks ago after a WW2 documentary mentioned a case where a German woman, in 1944, denounced her own husband to the Nazi authorities for badmouthing Hitler while he was home on leave from the army - he got sentenced to death for it (ended up being sent to the front instead, but still). After the war, in 1949, they put her on trial for basically causing his imprisonment, and it turns out this exact case became a famous argument between two legal philosophers, H.L.A. Hart and Lon Fuller (I later found out law students actually call it "the grudge informer case").

From what I've pieced together, and from arguing about it with my cousin who's doing a paralegal course and fully defended Hart's side, Hart's position, "legal positivism," says the Nazi statute against insulting Hitler counted as totally valid, real law at the time, no different in kind from any properly passed law, since for positivists a law's validity has nothing to do with whether its content is just. Fuller's side, "natural law," says a law that's unjust enough was never really law to begin with, so the German court was right to just treat it as void and convict her. Since we all agree "I was just following orders" wasn't an acceptable defense at Nuremberg, this seems like an easy call: Fuller's team is obviously the morally serious one here, and Hart's positivism is basically the exact kind of reasoning that let Nazi judges and bureaucrats sleep at night, just dressed up in fancier language.

So here's what I don't get: if that's really what positivism amounts to, why is it apparently still the mainstream, respected position in law schools and among judges, rather then something taught mainly as a cautionary tale? Is there actually something going for Hart's side here, or is the legal profession just squeamish about admitting natural law was right all along?
