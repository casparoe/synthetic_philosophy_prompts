I'm a backend engineer, and as a side project I've been building a small rule-based tool that parses natural-language "if...then..." sentences into formal logical statements, partly as an excuse to brush up on logic and partly because it would be a fun addition to a text-analysis pipeline I maintain at work. While researching how to handle conditionals properly, I ran into the philosophical distinction between "indicative" and "counterfactual" (or "subjunctive") conditionals, which the literature treats as requiring genuinely different formal treatments — something about possible-worlds semantics for one and a probabilistic notion of "assertability" for the other, if I followed the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy article correctly. This puzzled me, because in every programming language I've used, and in the propositional logic I picked up in an undergrad course, a conditional is just a truth-function: "if P then Q" is false exactly when P is true and Q is false, and true otherwise, and nothing in that definition cares whether P is currently believed true, believed false, hypothetical, past, or future. So when I came across the stock philosophical example pairing "If Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, someone else did" with "If Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have," my instinct was that these are just two surface renderings of one underlying claim about Oswald and Kennedy — with the mood signaling only whether the speaker already believes the antecedent is false — and that I could safely normalize both to the same internal representation in my tool without losing anything important.

I ran this by a friend who is finishing a philosophy PhD, and she was adamant that collapsing the two would be a real mistake rather than just an inelegant simplification, but when I pushed her for the underlying mechanism rather than a verdict, she mostly said, in effect, "trust me, they behave differently on the tests philosophers use for this." I would rather actually understand the issue than take it on faith, or get it wrong in my code for reasons I can't articulate. Is there a genuine difference in truth conditions, logic, or meaning between indicative and counterfactual conditionals, or is the philosophical apparatus mostly tracking a fact about English grammar that stops mattering once the antecedent and consequent are fixed? If it is the former, I would appreciate a careful walk-through — using the Oswald/Kennedy pair, or a cleaner example if one exists — of exactly where treating the two as interchangeable would lead my tool, or my thinking, astray.
