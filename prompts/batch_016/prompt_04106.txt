been reading Locke's "Some Thoughts Concerning Education" and this bit from section 61 has me stuck (context: he's arguing kids should be motivated by concern for their reputation rather than by punishment):

"Concerning reputation, I shall only remark this one thing more of it, that though it be not the true principle and measure of virtue, (for that is the knowledge of a man's duty, and the satisfaction it is to obey his maker, in following the dictates of that light God has given him, with the hopes of acceptation and reward) yet it is that which comes nearest to it: and being the testimony and applause that other people's reason, as it were by a common consent, gives to virtuous and well-order'd actions, it is the proper guide and encouragement of children, 'till they grow able to judge for themselves, and to find what is right by their own reason."

what i can't untangle: he flat out says reputation isn't the true measure of virtue (thats knowing your duty and following it through reason), but then says its still the right thing to build a kid's whole moral education around, until they're able to reason for themselves. isn't that kind of in tension with the rest of his project - no innate ideas, everything's supposed to come from reason/experience? is "comes nearest to it" doing some work here that dissolves the tension, or is he just being a bit sloppy? also curious if this "use a proxy motive now, swap in the real one later" move has a name, either in his educational theory specifically or in moral philosophy more generally.
