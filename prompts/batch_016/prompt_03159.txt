I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but I've been slowly working through Nietzsche on my own time, partly because he gets invoked so casually in tech and rationalist circles that I wanted to actually read the primary texts myself. I recently finished Ecce Homo, and I was struck by how much of the "Why I Am So Clever" chapter is devoted to what reads like extremely mundane personal advice: which climates are conducive to thought, oddly specific rules about tea strength and cocoa, his contempt for German and English cuisine alike as culprits behind sluggish national intellect, and repeated warnings against alcohol, down to the claim that even a single daily glass of beer or wine was enough to ruin his mood. It's a strange contrast to the Nietzsche I'd absorbed secondhand beforehand -- eternal recurrence, the Übermensch, master and slave morality -- here he reads less like a metaphysician and more like an eccentric wellness blogger.

What really made me stop and think was going back afterward to the "Four Great Errors" chapter of Twilight of the Idols, where Nietzsche mocks Cornaro's famous diet book for confusing cause and effect: Cornaro credited his frugal diet with producing his long, virtuous life, but Nietzsche argues it was really Cornaro's own exceptionally slow metabolism that made a frugal diet both tolerable and necessary for him specifically -- not a rule anyone else could safely generalize from. That looks like a sharp, basically correct methodological point about mistaking a personal physiological idiosyncrasy for a universal causal law. But then Nietzsche turns around in Ecce Homo and does something that looks, on its face, awfully similar: generalizing confidently from his own body and temperament into claims about cuisine, climate, and stimulants that read as prescriptive rather than merely autobiographical. I can't tell whether that's a genuine inconsistency or whether there's some principled reason his own case is supposed to be different from Cornaro's.

Could you write me a dialogue between two Nietzsche scholars, equally sharp and equally well-versed in the texts, who disagree about exactly this question? One should argue that the physiological material is not a lapse into the very error Nietzsche diagnoses in Cornaro, but a serious and consistent extension of his naturalism -- that treating the body as a "guiding thread," and offering his own regimen as a first-person case study in self-experimentation rather than a universal prescription, is precisely what distinguishes his post-metaphysical method from the body-despising philosophy he criticizes elsewhere. The other should argue that this defense is too generous, and that the Cornaro passage actually counts as evidence against Nietzsche's own dietary pronouncements, which look less like disciplined self-experimentation and more like a sick man's idiosyncrasies dressed up as philosophical doctrine. I'd like the two of them to genuinely engage with each other's strongest points rather than talk past one another -- if one side raises a real problem for the other's reading, I want to see an actual attempt at an answer, not just a restatement of the original position.
