So this has been living in my head since a slightly tipsy kitchen conversation at a party a few weeks back, and I cant tell who actually won, which is bugging me. Two friends of mine -- Dana (quant-finance type, thinks Samuelson basically solved the philosophy of economics in '38) and Marcus (philosophy PhD, allergic to anything that smells like behaviorism) -- got into it over whether "revealed preference" can actually do the job economists ask of it, namely, ground the concept of utility. I wrote down the shape of the argument afterward because it kept nagging at me. Roughly, in order: (1) Dana opened with: the whole point of the revealed-preference move was to get rid of any spooky introspective "utility" sitting behind choices -- if you pick A over B whenever both are on the table, that just IS what it means to prefer A to B, and the "utility function" is nothing more than a convenient numerical stand-in for a consistent choice pattern (one obeying the weak/strong axioms). No hidden mental stuff required, and thats a feature, not a bug. (2) Marcus said: sure, but then "people maximize utility" stops being a claim about anything and becomes true by definition -- whatever you picked out is, tautologically, what you "preferred." Which means economists can't have it both ways and also use revealed preference to say some policy made people "better off," since "better off" just got defined as "chose it," and people choose things for all kinds of reasons that have nothing to do with their own good -- addiction, weakness of will, a slick framing effect. (3) Dana pushed back that the axioms aren't empty -- they're genuinely falsifiable, and real people do sometimes violate them (preference reversals under framing, intransitive choices in lab settings), so the theory sticks its neck out in a way a mere tautology couldn't. (4) Marcus said fine, that's a real point about internal consistency, but it's a non-sequitur against his actual objection: you can have a smoker who satisfies every axiom perfectly and still, by his own lights, wishes he'd never started -- the theory just has no vocabulary for the gap between "what I chose" and "what's good for me," which is basically what he thinks Sen was getting at with the "rational fools" stuff. Dana's comeback was that this is really a time-inconsistency problem in disguise -- model the smoker as a sequence of short-lived selves with different utility functions and the puzzle dissolves, no need to smuggle in some extra-choice notion of "true" preference.

Neither of them budged and I had to leave before it got resolved. I've read enough rationalist-blog stuff on money-pumps and consistent utilities (that old Yudkowsky post "Coherent decisions imply consistent utilities" is rattling around in my head) to sense there's something right on both sides, but not enough to actually referee it myself. Can you go through the exchange move by move -- (1) through (4), not just an overall winner -- and say who's got the better of each point? And is there some deeper thing you think both of them are missing that would actually dissolve the disagreement?
