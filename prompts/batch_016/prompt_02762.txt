My friend and I just had a long argument about the strangest section of Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" — the one where, rather than dismissing telepathy outright, he calls the statistical evidence for it "overwhelming" and proposes running the imitation game in a "telepathy-proof room." Historians generally trace that evidence to the card-guessing experiments of British parapsychologists like S. G. Soal, work that was only shown, in 1978, well after Turing's death, to rest on doctored score sheets. What puzzles me is that this is the same Turing who, at Bletchley, devised a rigorous sequential Bayesian method (Banburismus, measured in "bans") specifically to guard against self-deception about ambiguous statistical evidence. My friend thinks this just shows that brilliant people compartmentalize, and that Turing would have shrugged off the fraud as beside the point; I think his own standards of evidence should have made him suspicious well before 1978, and that the exposure would have genuinely unsettled him. Which of us has the more plausible read on how Turing would actually have reacted, and why?
