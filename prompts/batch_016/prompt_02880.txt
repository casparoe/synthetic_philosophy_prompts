I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. A conversation at work about `==` versus `is` in Python led me down a rabbit hole into the metaphysics of identity, which is how I found Max Black's 1952 thought experiment: a universe containing nothing but two qualitatively identical iron spheres, meant as a counterexample to Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles. Black's own dialogue already heads off the obvious fix, that each sphere has the relational property of being two miles from an iron sphere of diameter one mile, by noting that both spheres satisfy this symmetrically.

What I can't get an intuitive handle on is the later "weak discernibility" response, which draws on Quine's distinction between absolute, relative, and weak discernibility and has been pressed by philosophers like Saunders and Muller: on this view, the spheres are discernible after all, via an irreflexive relation each bears to the other but not to itself. Could you explain why this counts as a genuinely different, non-trivial notion of discernibility rather than just a relabeling of the same symmetric relation Black already dismissed, and why some philosophers remain unconvinced that it rescues anything philosophically substantive?
