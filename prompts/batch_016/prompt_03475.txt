A coworker and I spent part of a slow Friday afternoon trading probability brain-teasers, and he brought up the two-envelope paradox: you're holding an envelope containing some amount of money, you're told the other envelope contains either twice or half as much, and the naive argument concludes that switching has an expected value of 1.25 times whatever you're currently holding, so you should always switch — which is absurd, since the setup is perfectly symmetric and you'd immediately want to switch back again. I looked into it afterward and came away fairly convinced that the whole paradox is just a cautionary tale about improper priors: the "always switch" argument implicitly treats every dollar amount as equally likely, and since there's no such thing as a uniform distribution over an unbounded set that actually integrates to 1, the argument is smuggling in a prior that doesn't exist. Being a software engineer, my instinct was to just code the thing up rather than take this on faith, so I wrote a short script modeling the smaller envelope's amount as 2^k dollars with a legitimate, fully normalized geometric prior on k (I tried a couple of decay rates, e.g., P(k) proportional to 0.3^k, and separately to 0.4^k). In both cases, once I conditioned on the specific amount observed, switching never looked better than keeping, no matter what number came up — exactly what I'd expect once an honest, normalizable prior replaces the illegitimate uniform one.

Given that, I'm puzzled as to why the two-envelope paradox still gets treated in decision theory and philosophy as a live, debated problem, rather than a fully closed case of "improper priors generate nonsense; use a real one and the weirdness disappears." Is there some way a fully legitimate, properly normalized prior could still reproduce the "you should always switch, no matter what you see" conclusion, or is the remaining literature on this mostly arguing about something adjacent — terminology, decision theory with unbounded utilities, and so on — rather than this specific mathematical point?
