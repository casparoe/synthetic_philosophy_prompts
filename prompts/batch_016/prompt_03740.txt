I spent about thirty-five years as a statistical physicist before I retired, and these days I mostly annoy my former colleagues by dragging philosophy into every conversation. My daughter's a philosophy grad student and keeps sending me papers, and last week one of them made my old field feel very strange to me. Apparently a bunch of cosmologists and philosophers of physics take seriously the idea of "Boltzmann brains" — the notion that in a universe which just keeps existing forever (like ours might, once dark energy wins out and everything settles into a cold, near-empty de Sitter-ish vacuum), random thermal or quantum fluctuations will, given enough time, spit out isolated, momentarily conscious brains complete with a full set of totally false memories — and that these freak brains vastly, vastly outnumber observers like us who got here the "honest" way, via stars and evolution. So if you reason that you're a typical observer randomly sampled from everyone who will ever exist in such a universe, you should conclude you're almost certainly one of these freak brains about to dissolve back into noise — which is obviously an insane thing to actually believe about yourself.

The paper, by Sean Carroll (I gather he's a well-known physicist-turned-philosopher-of-physics type), tries to get us out of this by arguing that such Boltzmann-brain-dominated theories are "cognitively unstable": if the theory were true, you'd have no business trusting the very brain and memories that led you to accept the theory in the first place, so rather than weighing the observer-counting as ordinary Bayesian evidence, we're entitled to just reject any theory like that from the get-go, practically by fiat. Something about this bugs me, it smells a bit like deciding in advance which conclusions are allowed to count as evidence, which feels like cheating on the Bayesian rules I spent my career trusting. But then again we do something similar to shrug off brain-in-a-vat style skepticism, so maybe its legitimate after all and I'm just not seeing why. Could you build the strongest possible case AGAINST Carroll's move here — i.e. argue that you can't legitimately rule out Boltzmann-brain cosmologies this way, and that a properly careful Bayesian is stuck actually taking the observer-counting seriously as evidence? I'd like real engagement with the underlying epistemology (self-undermining beliefs, priors versus updates, what "typical observer" is even supposed to mean), not just a quick "well, you can never really rule anything out" shrug.
