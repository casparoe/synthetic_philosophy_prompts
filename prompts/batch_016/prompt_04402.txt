Over the past year I've been teaching myself Bayesian probability and formal epistemology from whatever books, lecture notes, and papers I can find online — I never studied philosophy formally, but I've found the subject more absorbing than I expected. A few months ago a friend and I got into a long-running argument about the Sleeping Beauty problem (she is a committed "thirder"), and in trying to settle it I came across van Fraassen's Reflection Principle, which I gather predates the Sleeping Beauty puzzle by some years but keeps getting invoked alongside it. As far as I can piece together, the principle says something like this: if I am currently certain that my own credence in some proposition, at some specific future time, will equal a particular number, then my credence in that proposition right now should already equal that same number — my future self is treated as a kind of epistemic authority that I am not allowed to second-guess in advance. This is apparently backed by a Dutch book argument showing that anyone who sets their credences otherwise can be talked into a series of bets that is guaranteed to lose them money.

Here is what I cannot get past. On Sunday night, before she is put to sleep, Beauty presumably thinks the coin is fair and so sets her credence in heads at 1/2. But if the thirder position is correct, she can also work out, already on Sunday, that no matter how the coin lands and no matter which day it turns out to be, her credence in heads at the moment she wakes up will be 1/3 — that holds in the heads-and-Monday case, the tails-and-Monday case, and the tails-and-Tuesday case alike. It looks, then, as though the Reflection Principle should already force her Sunday credence to be 1/3 rather than 1/2, even though no coin has actually been observed and nothing has happened yet. Something has to give here: maybe thirdism is simply wrong, maybe the Reflection Principle has to be restricted so that it does not apply to cases like this one, or maybe there is some legitimate sense in which merely waking up counts as evidence, so that the principle is not really being violated at all. I would like you to write an essay that sets out this puzzle carefully and then actually takes a side, rather than just listing the options and shrugging — a conditional verdict is fine (something along the lines of "Reflection is valid for ordinary propositions but needs restricting once self-locating beliefs are in play" would count as a real answer), but I would like the essay to end on a definite position, defended on its merits, rather than a conclusion that everyone involved has a point. If you can keep the core of the argument in plain language rather than leaning mostly on notation, I'd appreciate it, since I'm working through all of this without any formal training.
