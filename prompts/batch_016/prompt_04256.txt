I have no formal training in philosophy or decision theory, but I have spent a good deal of time on my own working through the idea of "acausal trade": the claim that two agents who can never make causal contact with one another might still benefit from cooperating, because each can reason about how the other reasons. The one version of this that seems clearly well-founded to me involves two near-identical agents on opposite sides of the same choice, such as two copies of a single decision algorithm facing each other in a Prisoner's Dilemma — since they are built the same way, one's choice is strong evidence of the other's, so neither needs any detailed knowledge of the other as a particular individual. But the more ambitious versions of the idea, the ones that generate the most excitement, involve agents with genuinely different values doing different, tailored favors for one another, such as a distant civilization helping us in exchange for our helping it. That is no longer the twin case: for the "trade" to be worth anything, each side must correctly guess a great deal of fine-grained detail about the other's actual values and reasoning, with no way ever to observe the other party or get feedback on whether the guess was right. Please argue that this second, more ambitious form of acausal trade, between agents whose values genuinely differ rather than near-copies of one another, has no mechanism that could make relying on it rational even in principle, so that only the narrow, twin-like cases warrant any real confidence.
