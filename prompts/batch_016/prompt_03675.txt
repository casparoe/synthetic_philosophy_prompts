I'm about two-thirds through a novel set in a Welsh mining village in the early 1920s. My protagonist survives a pit disaster, helping pull several of the dead from the shaft herself, and is left afterward with nightmares, sudden panics, and a horror of going anywhere near the pit-head. At different points in the manuscript, she tries to explain her state to the village doctor and, separately, to her own husband. My writing group keeps flagging both scenes as the same beat — "she isn't taken seriously" — but I don't think that's quite right, and I've had trouble articulating why. In the doctor's scene, I want him to follow her account perfectly well and simply discount it, since in his view miners' wives are prone to nerves. In the husband's scene, I want something else: he loves her and is genuinely trying, but there is no story anywhere in their world about a woman's mind reacting this way to a shock like that, so neither of them quite has the words for it, including her. Is there an actual distinction between these two failures, and if there is, how should it show up differently on the page, rather than my just asserting it's there?
