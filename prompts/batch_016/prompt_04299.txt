I work as a backend engineer, and early in my career I had to get comfortable with the Two Generals' Problem while building a cross-datacenter reconciliation service: two nodes must agree on whether to commit a transaction, but any acknowledgment can be lost in transit, so no finite exchange of messages ever gives either side certainty that the other will commit too. The lesson every distributed-systems course draws from this is pragmatic: perfect guarantees are impossible, so you engineer around the gap with timeouts, retries, and idempotent operations, and simply accept some small, irreducible probability of disagreement as the cost of doing business.

A friend of mine finishing a PhD in economics recently showed me something called the "electronic mail game," apparently due to the game theorist Ariel Rubinstein, and it's been nagging at me because it seems to say something far stranger than the engineering moral above. Two players need to coordinate their actions; in the rare state of the world where both would be much better off switching from their normal, safe default to a riskier alternative, whichever one switches alone (because the other stuck with the default) is punished badly for it. Player 1 privately learns whenever the rare state obtains, and her computer then automatically emails Player 2, whose computer automatically emails back a receipt, which her computer confirms in turn, and so on — except each email independently has some small chance of being lost, silently ending the exchange, and each side only ever observes how many messages its own computer has sent. Rubinstein's result is that no matter how many rounds of confirmation go through, and no matter how small the failure probability, the only equilibrium has both players playing the safe default forever: behaving exactly as if no email had been sent, even though both privately know the rare state occurred, know that the other knows, and know that the other knows that they know, for as many levels as you like.

That total collapse is what I can't get an intuitive handle on. In distributed systems, more confirmations simply raise your confidence monotonically, and you act once you cross some practical threshold; nothing forces the whole structure to snap back to "ignore the evidence entirely," however many rounds have already gone through. I'd like you to walk me through why the backward-induction argument produces this all-or-nothing unraveling, rather than, say, an equilibrium where players switch once enough confirmations have accumulated. What I'm really trying to pin down is what's doing the work: is it that literal common knowledge (an infinite chain) is discontinuous with even an arbitrarily long finite chain of "I know that you know that...", for reasons that have nothing to do with rationality as such — or is this actually the same kind of common-knowledge-of-rationality unraveling that drives backward induction in something like the centipede game? And, if you'll indulge the engineer in me: does the fact that real distributed systems get by on "accept a small failure probability and move on" suggest they're implicitly relying on the other side not being the kind of fully rational, common-knowledge-respecting agent this model assumes?
