Now that I am retired from a career in condensed-matter physics, mostly working on critical phenomena, I have taken up reading philosophy in earnest for the first time, and I currently find myself immersed in the literature on vagueness and the sorites paradox. I cannot help reaching for an analogy from my own field: a genuine phase transition, with its non-analytic behavior, exists, strictly speaking, only in the thermodynamic limit; any finite system instead shows a smoothed-out, shifted version of the transition -- what we used to call finite-size scaling -- with no principled temperature one could single out as the boundary. Supervaluationist treatments of predicates like "heap" or "bald," going back to Kit Fine, seem to be attempting something structurally similar: locating the sharp cutoff in an idealized limit over the space of admissible precisifications, none of which is individually privileged. As I understand it, though, this approach runs into serious trouble from so-called higher-order vagueness, since it is itself indeterminate which precisifications ought to count as admissible. What I have not been able to get straight from introductory treatments is why this regress is supposed to be a distinctively serious problem for supervaluationism specifically, rather than simply the same difficulty afflicting any theory of vagueness -- including its main rival, epistemicism (associated with Timothy Williamson), which owes its own account of why we cannot know where the borderline of the borderline cases lies. Could you explain, with some technical care, why higher-order vagueness is usually treated as a special embarrassment for supervaluationism, and whether the standard fixes on offer -- relativized or iterated precisification -- actually block the regress, or merely relocate it?
