In my philosophical logic seminar this semester we've spent the last few weeks on temporal logic, and last week's session was devoted to Aristotle's sea-battle puzzle from De Interpretatione 9 and the ways twentieth-century logicians tried to resolve it within branching-time semantics. Our professor presented Arthur Prior's two classic rival formalizations: the Ockhamist system, on which the truth of a future-tensed sentence is relativized to a moment together with one particular history running through it, and the Peircean system, on which a future-tensed sentence counts as true now only if it holds on every history branching off from the present (so "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" comes out false unless a sea battle is already inevitable). What I found genuinely puzzling, from the reading our professor assigned, is that Prior himself preferred the Peircean system he had formalized, even though most of the subsequent literature — including Belnap and his collaborators — ended up building on some version of Ockhamism instead.

I have a short response paper due for this seminar, and I'd like to understand the disagreement more thoroughly than the lecture slides allowed. Could you compare the Ockhamist and Peircean treatments of a sentence like "tomorrow there will be a sea battle," asserted the day before? I'd especially like you to address which of the premises in the standard argument for logical determinism each view has to reject in order to make room for genuine contingency, and what each view gives up in exchange — for instance, anything odd about how the Peircean system handles the relation between "it will be that not-p" and "it is not the case that it will be that p," or about what the Ockhamist can say about the truth-value of a future contingent at a moment simpliciter, independent of any particular history. Once you've laid out the comparison, please say which of the two systems you find the more defensible resolution of Aristotle's original puzzle, and briefly comment on whether patching Ockhamism with something like Belnap and Green's "thin red line" actually earns its keep or just relocates the difficulty.
