A few of us on my engineering team run an informal Thursday lunch reading group -- we started with Gödel, Escher, Bach-adjacent material and have since drifted into old logic and philosophy-of-language texts, because a couple of people are into type theory and keep noticing overlaps with it. Last week's pick, chosen by Devon, our resident type-theory enthusiast, was a short translated excerpt of the 'White Horse Dialogue,' a chapter of the Gongsun Longzi attributed to Gongsun Long, a philosopher of the Warring-States-era Chinese 'School of Names.' In it, an Advocate argues 'a white horse is not a horse': first because 'horse' names a shape and 'white' names a color, so a term naming both differs from one naming only the shape; second because someone who wants 'a horse' can be satisfied with a yellow or black horse, while someone who wants 'a white horse' cannot. The Objector's whole response is essentially 'obviously, if there's a white horse, there's a horse.' This has apparently been argued over for two thousand years, with scholars still disagreeing about whether the Advocate is exploiting a genuine ambiguity in the word 'not' (not-identical-to, versus not-a-member-of) or just running a shell game.

Two people in the group, Marisol and Devon, got into a real back-and-forth about it while the rest of us mostly watched. (1) Marisol opened by calling it a textbook equivocation: the Advocate slides between two different claims -- that 'white horse' and 'horse' aren't identical terms (trivially true), and that a white horse doesn't belong to the category 'horse' (obviously false) -- and once you write it out with white horses as a subset of horses while the two terms stay non-identical, the puzzle dissolves. She added that even some of Gongsun Long's own contemporaries reportedly mocked these paradoxes as hollow tricks, so she wasn't inventing the objection. (2) Devon pushed back that the same slide is buried in every modifier-plus-noun pair in every language (a small elephant is still an elephant, but not small the way a small mouse is small), yet nobody feels tricked using 'is' both ways in ordinary speech -- so a philosopher isolating that ambiguity two thousand years before formal semantics existed deserves more credit than 'shell game.' He compared it to nominal versus structural typing: whether something 'is a' Horse because it's declared as one, or because it merely has the right shape, is exactly the ambiguity the dialogue is circling, and language designers still don't fully agree on which should govern. (3) Marisol answered that this proves her point: type systems formalize the distinction cleanly and without paradox, so the appearance of depth here comes from natural language's sloppiness, not a real problem. (4) Devon's comeback was that 'cleanly' is doing too much work: nominal versus structural typing is still a live, contested design choice (it's why Go's implicit interfaces, TypeScript's structural types, and Java's explicit 'implements' keyword all made different, sometimes controversial bets) -- so if anything the tension got relocated into type-system design, not dissolved.
