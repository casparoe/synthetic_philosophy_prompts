In my seminar on the philosophy of AI alignment, we've been reading Yudkowsky's proposal that a beneficial superintelligence should act on humanity's "coherent extrapolated volition" (CEV) — roughly, not our current preferences, but what we would want under suitable idealization — alongside the older debate over "full-information" and "ideal advisor" theories of well-being (Railton, Rosati, and their critics), which CEV structurally resembles. My professor raised an objection to those older theories that seems to apply just as forcefully to CEV: idealization has to go far enough to resolve our confusions and inconsistent preferences, but the further it goes, the less claim the resulting idealized agent has to being us at all, rather than some other, merely related, agent whose preferences needn't bind us. Could you write a dialogue between two equally sophisticated philosophers — one defending CEV-style idealization as a workable target for alignment, the other pressing this dilemma against it — who genuinely engage with each other's strongest moves rather than talking past one another, with neither side winning by default?
