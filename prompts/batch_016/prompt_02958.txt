I'm taking a seminar on ancient political thought this semester and we just spent a week and a half on Aristotle's various bits and pieces on money and exchange, and I keep feeling like there's something not quite adding up between two parts of his view. In the Nicomachean Ethics (the justice book, book V) he's got this whole account of proportionate reciprocity where money basically exists to solve the problem that a shoemaker's shoes and a housebuilder's house aren't naturally comparable — money stands in for chreia (need/demand, "that which holds everything together") so that a fair exchange ratio can be worked out. It's actually a pretty elegant little theory and he seems to genuinely respect money for doing this job.

Then in the Politics (book I) he's talking about household managment and distinguishes natural acquisition from chrematistike, and by the time he gets to usury he's basically fuming — lending money at interest is, he says, the most unnatural way there is of making a living, because money was only ever meant to be used up in exchange, not to "breed" more of itself. He even leans on the fact that the Greek word for interest, tokos, literally means offspring, like the interest is currency giving birth to more currency against nature.

What I can't quite work out is whether these two bits of Aristotle actually fit together or whether there's a real tension there. If money's whole deal (per the Ethics) is just being a conventional, invented tool that measures need so exchange can happen, why does it suddenly become "unnatural" for that same tool to be lent out for a fee — isn't lending just another service, no different in kind from the shoemaker charging for his labor? Is the tokos/breeding argument actually doing philosophical work, or is it more of a rhetorical move / borderline naturalistic fallacy, projecting a biological telos onto something he himself says is a mere convention? Could you dig into both passages and give me your actual take on whether Aristotle's ethics-side and politics-side accounts of money are consistent, and if not, where exactly the theory breaks down?
