A friend and I spent an evening arguing about whether penal substitutionary atonement is even a coherent doctrine, and they brought up David Lewis's short essay "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" (1997). Lewis notes that none of us would accept a friend serving out a burglar's prison sentence or a murderer's death sentence, yet few of us object when a friend pays off someone else's fine, even a serious one. He concludes that this asymmetry makes all of us, not just Christians, "of two minds" about penal substitution, so that the inconsistency gives Christians a tu quoque against their critics rather than a genuine vindication of the doctrine.

Compare Lewis's argument to the traditional doctrine of penal substitutionary atonement that prompted it. Does his fine-paying case really help the doctrine's defenders, given that what Christ is supposed to have suffered in our place looks much more like the prison-sentence and death-sentence cases that Lewis says we reject than like paying someone else's fine? Please justify your answer by engaging with the specific structure of his argument rather than with generic objections to substitutionary atonement.
