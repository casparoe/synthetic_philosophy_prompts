This term, in my graduate seminar on the philosophy of punishment, we spent a session on David Lewis's short 1997 paper "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" Lewis opens with the case of a devoted, innocent friend who volunteers to serve a burglar's prison term, or a murderer's death sentence, in the offender's place, and he takes it as obvious that no one would ever let such an offer excuse the actual offender. His diagnosis is that the metaphor of a "debt of punishment" is misleading: with a genuine debt, a creditor cares only that the loss is made good, not by whom, but a debt of punishment has no creditor to be repaid, so it is not discharged merely because somebody suffers — unless punishment exists mainly to compensate the victim, and Lewis points out that our actual practices are not built to do that (we go on punishing murderers even though nothing can compensate the dead). Yet he then notices that we tolerate a structurally identical case without complaint: when an offender owes a serious fine, one heavy enough to rival a prison term rather than a trivial parking-meter-style fee, we see nothing wrong with a friend quietly paying it on the offender's behalf, even though by Lewis's own lights this is just as much a case of penal substitution as the prison or death-sentence cases. He considers legislating the practice away — say, by forbidding gifts to the convicted — and rejects that route as impractical to the point of being, in his words, "a cure worse than the disease." But he never finds a principled reason to treat fines differently from other punishments, and the paper ends on a deliberately deflationary note: Lewis suggests only that Christians who accept penal substitution for the Atonement, and the rest of us who accept it only for fines, are equally "of two minds," and that this shared inconsistency is faint, mutual evidence that substitution might somehow make sense after all.

That inconclusive ending has always struck me as an admission of defeat rather than a real answer, and when I put the paper to the class, the room split: half the seminar wanted to defend Lewis's own resignation as the intellectually honest place to land, and half insisted that a principled line between fines and other punishments must be there for the finding, if only someone were willing to do the constructive work that Lewis declined to do. I would like you to argue for the second camp, as rigorously as you can. Specifically, construct and defend an account of what punishment is and what it is for that (a) explains why it is unobjectionable for a third party to pay a serious fine on an offender's behalf, (b) entails that third-party substitution is illegitimate for imprisonment, corporal punishment, or execution, and (c) says something about what this implies for the coherence of penal-substitution theories of the Atonement. I would rather you engage directly with the specific moves in Lewis's argument — the debt-of-punishment metaphor, the compensatory-function proposal, and the distinction between real fines and mere fee-like "fines" — than fall back on a generic survey of retributivist and consequentialist theories of punishment.
