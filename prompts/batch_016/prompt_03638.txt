I'm working on my IB Extended Essay this year on the ethics and philosophy of advanced AI, and in the course of my background reading I keep running into two ideas that I initially assumed were basically synonyms but now suspect are not. The original MIRI paper on the subject frames "corrigibility" as a whole cluster of properties an AI system should have: not resisting shutdown, not manipulating the people trying to correct it, not fighting attempts to change its goals, and passing all of this on to any sub-agents it might create. More recent papers, though, treat "the shutdown problem" as something narrower and more formally precise: can you design an agent that shuts down on command, doesn't try to influence whether the shutdown button gets pressed one way or the other, and otherwise still competently pursues its goals?

What I can't quite get my head around is why so many people working on this treat the narrower, shutdown-specific version as so stubbornly hard to solve, especially for an agent that is otherwise just behaving like a standard expected-utility maximizer. I understand the basic instrumental convergence argument that almost any goal gives an agent a reason to avoid being switched off, since a disabled agent can't accomplish much of anything. But I've also read about proposed fixes, like the "off-switch game" idea of making the agent uncertain about its own true objective so that it treats a human reaching for the switch as useful evidence, or approaches that try to make the agent mathematically indifferent to whether the button gets pressed -- and apparently both run into serious trouble, either relying on assumptions that fall apart once the agent is confident enough in its objective, or quietly reintroducing an incentive to influence the button after all. Could you help me understand, in fairly intuitive terms and without assuming a background in formal decision theory, why corrigibility in this shutdown-specific sense seems to be in real tension with the basic rationality assumptions built into most models of goal-directed agents, rather than being something you could patch by just adding one more rule?
