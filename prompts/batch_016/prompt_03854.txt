Over the last few months I have been teaching myself some legal philosophy in my spare time, mostly through the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy and whatever law-school course materials I can find posted online for free, and I keep coming back to the argument that H. L. A. Hart and Lon Fuller had in the late 1950s and mid-1960s over whether law and morality are necessarily connected. The part that interests me most is Fuller's idea of an "inner morality of law": his claim that before a set of rules can even count as a working legal system, it has to satisfy eight purely procedural conditions — there have to be general rules rather than one-off decrees, the rules have to be made public, they have to apply going forward rather than retroactively, they have to be reasonably clear and free of contradiction, they cannot demand the impossible, they have to stay stable enough for people to plan their lives around them, and officials actually have to apply them the way they are written rather than announcing one thing and doing another. Fuller thought a regime that genuinely respected all eight would be nudged toward decency almost as a side effect, which is part of why he was willing to say that the worst Nazi enactments barely counted as "law" at all. Hart's reply, as I understand it, was that this proves nothing, because in principle you could satisfy every one of those eight conditions in the service of thoroughly monstrous substantive rules, which would show that "legality" in Fuller's sense buys no real moral protection.

Every source I have read illustrates this with either Nazi Germany or apartheid-era South Africa, and by now I have those two examples memorized without their having actually helped me test the idea for myself. What I would like from you is a different example: invent a specific law, or a small self-contained legal system, with no connection to any real historical regime, and walk through it against all eight of Fuller's conditions one at a time, showing concretely how it satisfies each one, while the substance of what it requires people to do remains clearly cruel or unjust by ordinary standards. Once you have built the example, tell me directly whether you think it actually vindicates Hart's side of the dispute, or whether there is some way a defender of Fuller could still explain it away.
