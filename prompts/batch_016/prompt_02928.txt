So this week in my "Borges and Philosophy" seminar (comp lit, cross-listed with the philosophy department) we read "The Doctrine of Cycles" from A History of Eternity, and I'm still kind of stuck on it. Everyone talks about Borges being obsessed with circular time as a literary device in his fiction, but in this essay he's actually trying to seriously refute Nietzsche's eternal recurrence. His first move borrows Cantor's set theory: Nietzsche's argument (as Borges reconstructs it) says a finite amount of matter can only form finitely many configurations, so given infinite time everything must eventually repeat -- but Borges argues that since the universe contains infinitely many points, that whole "finite combinations" premise collapses. Then, almost as an afterthought, he throws in a second argument from the second law of thermodynamics (heat death, irreversibility) to say the universe just runs down instead of cycling back. What gets me is that by the end of the essay Borges basically admits he isn't even convinced by his own arguement, which seems like a weird note for a "refutation" to end on.

I brought this up with my roommate, who's a math major, and she immediately said the Cantor move looked like a category error to her -- that the fact that space has infinitely many points doesn't obviously tell you anything about whether the finite matter sitting in that space can only be rearranged in finitely many ways, so Borges might just be equivocating between two different senses of "infinite." My professor raised a totally separate worry in office hours: that Borges is attacking eternal recurrence purely as a cosmological claim about atoms literally repeating, while a lot of Nietzsche scholars read the doctrine (the demon thought-experiment in The Gay Science, the vision of eternal return in Zarathustra) as more of an existential test about whether you can affirm your life enough to want it back exactly as it was, forever -- not a literal prediction about physics.

Could you actually compare Borges's refutation with Nietzsche's doctrine as Nietzsche seems to intend it? In particular: does the Cantor argument hold up, or is my roommate right that it equivocates between two senses of infinity, and even setting that aside, is Borges refuting the version of eternal recurrence that actually mattered to Nietzsche, or just a strawman cosmological one? Justify your answer -- I've got a short response paper due on this and would rather actually understand the issue than just repeat what got said in seminar.
