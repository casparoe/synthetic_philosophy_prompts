So I'm a retired physicist (spent my whole career in laser optics, never had to touch foundational quantum stuff professionally) and about a year ago I started sitting in on a philosophy discussion group at the university library, mostly to finally read the things I skipped in school. We just spent a few sessions working through David Lewis on modal realism - the view that literally every way a world could possibly have been is realized in some other world, just as real and concrete as ours, with "actual" being basically an indexical, like "here" or "now," just meaning "the world we happen to be in."

Here's my hangup though: this sounds to me exactly like the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics, which I've been reading about for years in the popular books (Deutsch, Sean Carroll, Tegmark, that crowd) - every possible outcome of a measurement actually happens, just in a seperate branch of the universe. When I brought this up at group, one of the grad students got kind of cagey and just said "it's not really the same thing" without explaining why, and we ran out of time before I could push her on it. So am I wrong that Lewis basically anticipated, or has now essentially been vindicated by, what physicists are increasingly finding out about the structure of reality? It seems like if many-worlds keeps gaining ground as the mainstream reading of QM, that should more or less settle the modal realism debate too, shouldn't it? My old labmate tells me I'm overreaching but he's about as much a philosopher as my cat is, so I'd genuinely like to know where my reasoning breaks down, if it does.
