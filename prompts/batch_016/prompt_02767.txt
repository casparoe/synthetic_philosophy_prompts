I'm several chapters into a historical novel set during the Warring States period, and I've just reached a scene I've been looking forward to for months: a set-piece debate at the residence of the Lord of Pingyuan, in which my protagonist — an itinerant disputer modeled closely on the historical Gongsun Long (c. 320–250 BCE) — must defend, before a skeptical court, his notorious claim that a white horse is not a horse. His rival is loosely based on Kong Chuan, a descendant of Confucius whom other anecdotes place in actual disputation with Gongsun Long at that same court. Scholars still disagree about whether Gongsun Long meant any of this in earnest or was essentially a court entertainer who built his reputation on defending outrageous theses to amuse regional rulers and their retinues, and I'd rather my novel not flatten that ambiguity by having him say the same clever thing five times over. So I want the debate to escalate: I need my protagonist to deploy several genuinely distinct lines of argument for "white horse is not horse," moving from whichever is easiest for his rival to bat away toward whichever leaves the rival visibly stuck.

As I understand the surviving "White Horse Discourse," five distinct arguments are offered for the thesis. The Naming Argument holds that "horse" is the word used to name a shape and "white" the word used to name a color, and since naming a color is not the same act as naming a shape, the two words cannot be picking out the same thing. The Errand Argument observes that if you send a groom to fetch "a horse," a brown or black animal will do, but if you send him to fetch "a white horse," those same animals will not do, so the two errands cannot be seeking one and the same thing. The Combination Argument notes that horses come in colors, which is exactly why "white horse" is available as a phrase in the first place — but "white horse" just means horse-combined-with-white, and a thing-plus-an-added-feature is not simply identical to the bare thing it was added to. The Brown-Horse Analogy points out that anyone will grant that "having a horse" is not the same as "having a brown horse" — yet by that very admission, "brown horse" turns out not to be "horse" either, so a rival who accepts this about brown horses while resisting it about white horses is holding inconsistent commitments. The Exclusion Argument holds that "horse," as a term, excludes no color whatsoever, so a brown or black animal answers to it equally well, whereas "white horse" excludes every color but one, and a term that excludes nothing cannot be the same term as one that excludes a great deal.

What I'd like your help with is ranking these five arguments from logically strongest to logically weakest — strongest meaning hardest to dismiss as simply trading on a confusion between identity ("a white horse is not identical to a horse") and class membership ("a white horse is not a kind of horse"), weakest meaning most transparently guilty of exactly that confusion.
