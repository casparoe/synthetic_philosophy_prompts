I am writing a historical novel set in Oxford in June 1956, in the days before Convocation is due to vote on whether Harry Truman should receive an honorary degree. One scene I need is a Senior Common Room argument, over port after dinner, between Elizabeth Anscombe — who has announced that she will speak against the degree — and a fictional colleague, Gerald Fenwick, a tutor in moral philosophy who intends to vote for it and regards her campaign as an embarrassment to the university.

Please draft the scene, mostly as dialogue, in roughly 600–800 words. Anscombe should make her actual case: that the deliberate killing of the civilian populations of two cities as a means of ending the war was murder whatever it achieved; that "innocent" here is not a matter of personal guilt but means only "not currently engaged in harming"; and that the demand for unconditional surrender is what made such methods seem necessary in the first place. Fenwick is not a fool and must not be written as one — give him the strongest reply available, whether by disputing what was truly intended at Hiroshima and Nagasaki, by reviving the argument that a nation mobilised for total war cannot be neatly divided into combatants and innocents, or by arguing that no absolute prohibition survives contact with a war of that kind. Each should answer what the other has just said, and the scene should end not in resolution but in a genuine impasse, with both characters able to state, in a single sentence, exactly where they think the other's argument first goes wrong.
