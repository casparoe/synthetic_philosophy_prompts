This week we looped back to the Hume passage Lewis leans on right from chapter 1 — he literally introduces the rowboat case as "an example from Hume's Treatise" before he's even given his official definition of coordination equilibrium. I got stuck writing up the notes for presenting Thursday. I've actually read this bit of the Treatise before, for a totally different seminar on Hume's moral psychology, but now that I'm trying to explain whats going on in it for people thinking about it game-theoretically, I realize I never really pinned down what I think is happening.

Here's the passage (Treatise, Bk. 3, Pt. 2, Sec. 2, "Of the origin of justice and property," the paragraph right after he first denies that this kind of convention is a promise):

"This convention is not of the nature of a promise: For even promises themselves, as we shall see afterwards, arise from human conventions. It is only a general sense of common interest; which sense all the members of the society express to one another, and which induces them to regulate their conduct by certain rules. I observe, that it will be for my interest to leave another in the possession of his goods, provided he will act in the same manner with regard to me. He is sensible of a like interest in the regulation of his conduct. When this common sense of interest is mutually express'd, and is known to both, it produces a suitable resolution and behaviour. And this may properly enough be call'd a convention or agreement betwixt us, tho' without the interposition of a promise; since the actions of each of us have a reference to those of the other, and are perform'd upon the supposition, that something is to be perform'd on the other part. Two men, who pull the oars of a boat, do it by an agreement or convention, tho' they have never given promises to each other. Nor is the rule concerning the stability of possession the less deriv'd from human conventions, that it arises gradually, and acquires force by a slow progression, and by our repeated experience of the inconveniences of transgressing it. On the contrary, this experience assures us still more, that the sense of interest has become common to all our fellows, and gives us a confidence of the future regularity of their conduct: And 'tis only on the expectation of this, that our moderation and abstinence are founded. In like manner are languages gradually establish'd by human conventions without any promise. In like manner do gold and silver become the common measures of exchange, and are esteem'd sufficient payment for what is of a hundred times their value."

Two things I can't get straight. First: Hume wants "convention" to cover cases with zero communication and zero agreement in any normal sense — just two guys rowing who fall into sync and then have reason to stay there. Isn't that asking a lot of the word? Like, has he actually earned the normatively loaded bit (that you *ought* to keep rowing in time, respect possession, etc.) or is he just describing a self-interested equilibrium and then calling it a "convention" after the fact, so the normativity is smuggled in rather than explained? Second, and this is the part that actually matters for the presentation — Hume is explicit that this arises "gradually" through "repeated experience," which sounds like a learning/evolutionary story, a convention as something that settles into place over time with nobody designing it. But Lewis's official apparatus (coordination equilibrium sustained by common knowledge of preferences and rationality) sounds much more static and idealized — common knowledge is a pretty demanding epistemic state that doesn't obviously build up "gradually" at all. So did Lewis quietly swap in a different, more rationalist phenomenon while keeping Hume's label, or is there a good way to read Lewis as just characterizing the equilibrium state that a Humean gradual process eventually converges on? Would love a fairly detailed answer if possible, since I want to actually have a view before Thursday and not just wave my hands at both texts.
