I started a new job three months ago as a data scientist at a small health-tech company, where much of my work involves building causal inference models—mostly structural causal models and Judea Pearl's do-calculus—to estimate treatment effects from observational patient data. It is the first time I have had to take the philosophical foundations of "causation" seriously rather than treating it as a statistical convenience, and it has turned out to matter more than I expected for how confidently we can defend our results.

At a recent internal seminar, one of our scientific advisors, who trained in philosophy of science before moving into biostatistics, remarked that our whole modeling apparatus is essentially an application of James Woodward's "interventionist" (or manipulationist) theory of causation, and that this theory arose partly as a response to shortcomings in the older counterfactual theory of causation associated with David Lewis. When I mentioned this afterward to a friend from graduate school, she insisted the two theories are "basically the same thing wearing different clothes," which only confused me further. I have to walk a group of skeptical clinical collaborators through our modeling assumptions next month, and I would like to actually understand this dispute rather than just gesture at "Pearl's framework" as though it settled the matter.

Could you compare Lewis's counterfactual theory of causation with Woodward's interventionist theory in some depth? I would especially like you to address: (1) how each theory handles classic problem cases for counterfactual analyses of causation, such as late preemption (the standard "Suzy and Billy" rock-throwing case) and symmetric overdetermination, since this bears directly on how confidently one can attribute a clinical outcome to a treatment rather than to a confounder; and (2) whether interventionism is best understood as a genuine rival to counterfactual theories of causation, or merely as a more tractable way of specifying the relevant counterfactuals—via hypothetical interventions on a structural model—within a broadly counterfactual framework, in which case my friend's dismissive comment would be closer to the truth than my advisor's framing of the two theories as rivals. Please end with a direct, justified verdict on which of the two views you find more defensible as a general theory of causation.
