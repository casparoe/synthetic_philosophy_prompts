Ok, this is going to sound like a weirdly specific question, but it's been stuck in my head for weeks. I'm auditing a metaphysics seminar this term (my actual background is linguistics, so half the time I'm lost), and we spent a couple sessions on the Kripke-vs-Lewis fight over transworld identity — basically, when someone says "I could have been a philosphy major instead," is that literally the same me existing in some other possible world (roughly Kripke's picture), or is it really a similar-but-numerically-distinct "counterpart" standing in for me over there, so that strictly speaking it was never possible for *me*, only for someone enough like me (Lewis's picture)? This was all pretty dry until my mom mentioned, totally out of nowhere at dinner, that she almost took a research post in Australia the year before she met my dad — if the visa had gone through she'd have been on another continent when they actually met. Ever since, I can't stop chewing on what, if anything, happened to "me" in the branch where that visa comes through.

While digging around for a seminar paper I found out Leibniz was already circling this exact issue a good 250 years early. In the correspondence with Arnauld he says that if Adam hadn't sinned, the sinless version "would not have been our Adam, but another Adam" — a totally different individual, not our Adam minus one property. And in the Theodicy he has that scene where a goddess walks a priest through a palace containing endless "Sextuses," resembling the real Sextus Tarquinius but living out all the lives he didn't, none of them actually being him. Both of those sound like he's basically inventing Lewis's counterparts centuries ahead of schedule. But then he's also got this whole apparatus of individuals as "complete concepts" containing literally every property they'll ever have, which just as easily reads like an extreme form of essentialism that's fully compatible with strict transworld identity, not a denial of it.

So here's my actual question: given what Leibniz was actually committed to — complete individual concepts, the identity of indiscernibles, possible worlds as ideas in the divine understanding rather than concrete places the way Lewis wants — does he genuinely come down on the Lewis side, the Kripke side, or does the whole dichotomy just fail to carve his view at the joints? And if you're up for it, take a swing at the Australia scenario specifically: on a reconstructed-Leibniz view, what, if anything, is true of "me" in the branch where my parents never meet? I want an actual argument for this, not just a vibe, and I'd genuinly be interested if your answer is that his framework just wasn't built to handle the modern question at all.
