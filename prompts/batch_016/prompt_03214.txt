I'm taking a seminar this term on testimony and assertion, and this week's session centered on the disagreement between Timothy Williamson's knowledge norm of assertion (the view that one must assert that p only if one knows that p) and Jennifer Lackey's rival "reasonable to believe" norm, which she developed specifically to accommodate her well-known selfless assertion cases. The example that dominated discussion was her creationist teacher, Stella. Stella was raised within a faith committed to creationism, yet she recognizes that the evidence overwhelmingly favors evolutionary theory and regards it as her professional duty not to let her private convictions distort what she teaches; so, in her fourth-grade classroom, she tells her students that modern humans descend from more ape-like ancestors. Since Stella does not believe what she asserts, she cannot know it either, yet almost everyone in our seminar agreed that her assertion was perfectly proper — which is exactly the intuition Lackey wants to leverage against Williamson.

What I could not settle afterward, in conversation with a couple of classmates, is how the exchange should actually go once both sides have to answer their opponent's best case rather than simply restate their own view. Could you write a dialogue between K, a committed defender of the knowledge norm, and R, a defender of the reasonable-to-believe norm, structured so that K first has to confront Stella and R first has to confront Williamson's lottery-proposition worry — that overwhelming statistical grounds for believing your ticket lost still do not seem to license flatly asserting "your ticket lost," which is normally taken to show that any norm weaker than knowledge is too permissive? I would especially like to see whether K can get any traction out of treating Stella's assertion as a blameless violation of the knowledge norm rather than a genuine counterexample to it, and whether R has a principled way of resisting that move that does not just re-assert the intuition that Stella did nothing wrong. Neither character should land a decisive blow or simply concede the point — I would rather see precisely where the disagreement gets stuck. If it helps sharpen the exchange, either of them is welcome to bring in Moore's paradox as well, but I'd like Stella and the lottery case to carry most of the weight.
