In February 1925, Frank Ramsey read to the Cambridge Apostles a paper later published as "Epilogue," in which he pointedly distinguished his own project from Bertrand Russell's recent lecture "What I Believe": where Russell had set out what he believed, Ramsey wanted to set out what he felt. Russell's naturalism rejects God and personal immortality yet keeps a register of stoic resignation before humanity's transience and insignificance, its constructive core being the claim that "the good life is one inspired by love and guided by knowledge." Ramsey accepts the same godless, mortal cosmos but refuses the resignation itself, insisting "I don't feel the least humble before the vastness of the heavens" and grounding value instead in a picture of the world drawn to human scale rather than to physical or temporal scale.

Compare these two responses to the threat that an impersonal, indifferent universe seems to pose to a meaningful human life. Does Ramsey offer a genuine solution to the problem that preoccupies Russell, or does he merely refuse to feel its force, leaving the underlying difficulty untouched? Justify your answer by engaging with the specific commitments of each view, not just their shared naturalism.
