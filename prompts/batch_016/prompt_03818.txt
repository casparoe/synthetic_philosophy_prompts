I cover science and ideas for a living, and lately I've been down a rabbit hole on how AI-safety and rationalist types keep dragging 1970s decision theory into their arguments about how a "rational" agent -- human or artificial -- should act. Newcomb's problem comes up constantly and I feel like I've basically got a handle on it, but a philosopher I was interviewing offhandedly mentioned a meaner, less famous cousin called "Death in Damascus" (Gibbard and Harper's case, apparantly), and I haven't been able to let it go. The setup, as best I understand it: a man in Damascus has an appointment with Death at midnight, and can choose to spend the night in either Damascus or Aleppo. Death is a very reliable predictor of where the man will actually be, and has already picked a city to look in before the man decides anything. Same city, he dies; different city, he lives. The part that's supposed to make this so much nastier for causal decision theory than Newcomb ever was is that whichever city the man currently leans toward, that very leaning is evidence Death guessed the same city -- so he always wants to switch, and there's no stable "ratifiable" choice, just a coin flip that rescues the theory.

What I can't work out on my own is why evidential decision theory is supposed to come out ahead here. Could you walk through both theories' reasoning on this exact case, side by side, and tell me whether EDT actually dodges the instability CDT runs into, or just relabels it as a shrug-worthy tie between two equally doomed options -- and, on balance, which of the two theories ends up looking worse because of this particular case, worse than either looks from Newcomb's problem alone? I also came across a causal decision theorist arguing that the instability isn't a flaw at all -- that it's the correct verdict, and biting that bullet is totally fine. Curious whether you think that defense actually holds up.
