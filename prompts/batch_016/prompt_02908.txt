Hey, so I'm working on a longer feature about the different scientific theories of consciousness (partly pegged to that adversarial-collaboration thing between the global-workspace and integrated-information-theory camps that's been kicking around in the news the last couple years). Higher-order theories keep coming up as one of the other big contenders, so I've been trying to actually understand David Rosenthal's version properly instead of just repeating the one-line summary you get in every explainer ("a mental state is conscious if there's a thought representing you as being in it").

While reading around it I kept running into an objection that apparently causes proponents a lot of grief: the "targetless" or "empty" higher-order thought problem. As far as I can tell, since the higher-order thought and the state it's supposedly about are two seperate mental events, in principle nothing stops the higher-order thought from firing off on its own — representing you as being in pain, say, when there's no pain state there to represent at all. And from what I've read, Rosenthal basically bites the bullet and says that would still count as a genuine conscious experience of pain, just a "targetless" one, which seems like a pretty wild thing to have to accept just to keep your theory tidy.

I don't fully get why this counts as such a deep problem for the whole theory rather than a weird edge case someone could just shrug off, and I'm also fuzzy on why you couldn't patch it by simply requiring the higher-order thought to be reliably caused by the lower one. Could you walk me through why this objection is supposed to really bite, and how Rosenthal (or higher-order theorists who don't love his answer, like the self-representationalist crowd) try to handle it? If you can tie it back to why any of this matters for stuff like animal or AI consciousness, even better, since that's probably where it'll land in the piece.
