A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about a mutual friend who keeps going back to a relationship that is obviously bad for him. I said he has a reason to leave whether or not he currently feels like it; my friend, invoking Bernard Williams, replied that a reason can only be his if there is a "sound deliberative route" to it from what Williams calls his subjective motivational set -- his actual desires, projects, and cares -- so that insisting he "really" has a reason to leave is just a polite way of expressing what we wish he wanted, not a fact about him. We went back and forth for an hour and neither of us moved an inch.

Write a dialogue between a committed Williams-style internalist about reasons and an externalist critic in the spirit of Korsgaard, McDowell, or Parfit, who denies that every genuine reason must trace back to an agent's existing motivations. Have them argue about a case structurally similar to our friend's, with the externalist pressing on whether internalism leaves any room to call someone irrational rather than merely differently motivated, and the internalist pressing back on what an "external" reason could even amount to for someone who could never be argued or deliberated into caring about it -- perhaps invoking something like Williams's own case of a man with no reason to drink from a glass he takes to contain gin but which actually contains petrol. Make sure each side directly engages the other's strongest move rather than talking past it, and don't let either character concede just to give the dialogue a tidy ending.
