So this happened at a dinner party a couple weekends ago and I'm still thinking about it. Two friends of mine are both pretty deep into local election-reform stuff -- one's been canvasing for a ballot measure to bring approval voting to our city council races, the other is a die-hard ranked-choice voting (RCV) person -- and somehow it turned into a full-on argument about the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem (hope I'm spelling that right). I'm taking a mechanism design elective this semester so I recognized the name, but the lecture never got into the weeds like this, so I couldn't actually tell who was winning.

Here's roughly how the exchange went:

1. He opened with: "Gibbard-Satterthwaite proves that once you've got three or more candidates, a dictatorship is literally the only strategy-proof rule there is. So drop the thing where you act like approval voting fixes the strategic-voting problem RCV has -- it's just as manipulable, the theorem doesn't care what your ballot looks like."
2. She shot back: "That's too fast. The theorem's about resolute rules defined over the full domain of strict rankings. An approval ballot isn't a ranking, it's a yes/no on each candidate. There's an actual theorem -- Brams and Fishburn, from the 70s -- that approval voting is strategy-proof: honestly approving everyone you'd rather see win than not is a dominant strategy. RCV doesn't have anything like that."
3. He came back with: "Sure, but that 'dominant strategy' only gives you one clean answer if your real preferences are already binary -- love these people, don't care about the rest. Nobody's preferences actually work like that, people have a real order of who they like best. The second your true preference has more than two tiers, approving is a judgment call about where to draw the line, and deciding whether to approve your second choice depending on who looks close is exactly the kind of strategizing RCV voters have to do too. You've just relocated the manipulation to the threshold."
4. She said: "Okay, that's a fair hit on the naive version of what I said. But it's still a smaller, different problem than what Gibbard-Satterthwaite is actually about. GS says: given your true, fully-ranked preference, there are situations where lying about that ranking changes the winner to someone you honestly like better than the truthful winner would've been. What you're describing is a voter with a rich true preference choosing how to compress it into a coarse ballot -- there's no single 'true approval ballot' to lie about in the first place, so it isn't manipulation in the GS sense. That's a real strike against approval voting, sure, but it's not the failure mode RCV has, where sincerely ranking your actual favorite first can get you a worse outcome than burying them lower down."

Neither of them budged, and honestly by that point i'd had enough wine that I just nodded along.
