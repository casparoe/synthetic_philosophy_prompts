This term I am running a graduate seminar on Frege, and for this week's meeting I paired "Der Gedanke" (1918) with the surviving fragments of the diary he kept between 10 March and 9 April 1924, a little more than a year before his death. That diary sat unnoticed in the Nachlass until Michael Dummett came across it in 1954 while working through what had survived of Frege's papers in Germany; it was not actually published until 1994, in German, edited with commentary by Gottfried Gabriel and Wolfgang Kienzler, with Richard Mendelsohn's English translation following two years later. It is a genuinely unpleasant document: hostility to parliamentary democracy and universal suffrage, contempt for Social Democrats, Catholics, and the French, and, above all, a program for depriving Jews of political rights and, ideally, expelling them from Germany, delivered in the same dry, exacting prose Frege reserves for dispatching a bad definition.

What produced real disagreement in seminar was not the diary itself but what to make of it. Dummett, who had revered Frege as a model of dispassionate rationality, was careful in his later writing to keep the philosophy quarantined from the politics: shocking as the diary is, he insisted, it leaves the truth and the ingenuity of the logical work untouched. A few students wanted to resist that verdict, and pointed to something more specific than guilt by biographical association: Frege's lifelong campaign against psychologism, and in particular the doctrine of "Der Gedanke" that thoughts (Gedanken) are neither things in the external world nor private mental occurrences, but objective inhabitants of what Frege literally calls a "third realm" (drittes Reich) — graspable in exactly the same way by any properly functioning mind, and wholly indifferent to what any particular thinker, or community of thinkers, happens to find congenial. (So that no one spends the whole response on it: the verbal coincidence with that term's later political sense is almost certainly just that, a coincidence drawing on a stock phrase in German intellectual vocabulary, and Frege was using it years before it acquired its infamous later meaning. I don't want an argument that leans on the name.) The real question is whether that logical doctrine — together with Frege's insistence that admitting psychologism into logic would make its laws hostage to the contingent mental constitution of this or that thinker or people — has any genuine affinity with a politics that likewise wants to insulate legitimate authority from the shifting, plural, "merely psychological" will of a democratic electorate, or whether that is a false analogy dressed up in borrowed vocabulary.

I would like you to argue, against Dummett's own express verdict, for the stronger claim: that there is a real and specifiable line of philosophical continuity between Frege's anti-psychologistic conception of logic and thought and the authoritarian, exclusionary politics of the 1924 diary, rather than a mere personal coincidence of a brilliant logician who also happened to hold repugnant opinions. Please construct the strongest version of this argument you can, engaging with the actual content of Frege's anti-psychologism (for instance in the preface to the Grundlagen and in "Der Gedanke" itself) rather than resting on tone or biographical inference, and be explicit about exactly which Fregean doctrine is meant to be doing the work at each step of the analogy to his politics.
