So this started with me rereading an old SSC open thread (I know, I know) where the comments went on a tangent about the St. Petersburg paradox, and somebody linked to Nover and Hajek's "Pasadena game" from 2004, which I'd somehow never run into before. Setup: you flip a coin til it lands heads for the first time, and your payoff on the nth flip is (-1)^(n-1) * 2^n/n. Sum that in the natural order (n=1, 2, 3...) weighted by the probability 2^-n and you get 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ... = ln(2), except that sum is only conditionally convergent (it's literally just the alternating harmonic series), so per the Riemann rearrangement theorem you can reshuffle the terms to make the "expected value" come out to any number you want, or +infinity, or -infinity, or nothing at all -- without the actual game changing one bit. Apparently this spawned a solid two decades of decision theorists proposing fixes: relative/comparative expected utility theories, "weak expectations" that only ask for convergence in some averaged probabilistic sense, people insisting there's secretly a privileged summation order after all, folks who want to just replace expectation-maximization with stochastic dominance, and at least one attempt to bring in surreal numbers. I mostly know all this third-hand from blog posts and paper abstracts, and definitly don't have the patience to sit down and read fifteen papers against each other.
