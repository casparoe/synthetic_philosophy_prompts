So I've been down a rabbit hole this week for a decision theory class, on the economics of information, and there's a tension I can't quite shake. There's this old result called Good's theorem that says a rational Bayesian agent should always want more free information before making a decision -- the intuition being you can always just ignore the info if it turns out useless, so in expectation it can never make you worse off. Fine, that tracks for one person sitting alone in a room making a decision. But then my professor brought up this other idea (Hirshleifer, I think, from some 1971 paper) that once you put people in a group who could otherwise insure each other against risk, this completely flips: if everybody finds out too early how things are going to turn out for them individually, the whole insurance arrangement can unravel, because nobody who's about to "win" wants to keep subsidizing the people who are about to "lose" anymore. So the same information that's great for each individual on their own can quietly destroy something valuable that only existed because of everyones shared ignorance. I tried explaining this to my housemate over dinner last night and it did not land at all.

Can you write me a short story that gets at this without turning into a lecture? Doesnt have to be long, a few paragraphs is fine. I'm picturing something like a small community that's always pooled its risk somehow -- fishing village, farming co-op, whatever setting you think works best -- and then some new source of foreknowledge shows up (an oracle, an invention, a data broker, up to you) that lets everyone see their own fate ahead of time. I'd like the ending to land on the irony that the thing which was supposed to be an unambiguous gift to everyone involved is actually what quietly kills the arrangement that had been protecting them.
