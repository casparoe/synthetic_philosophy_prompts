My mother died six weeks ago after a long illness, and in the middle of a fairly conventional grief, I have been reading the Zhuangzi on a friend's recommendation. Three scenes keep returning to me: Zhuangzi squatting and drumming on a basin, singing, shortly after his own wife's death (from 'Perfect Happiness'); the dying Master Lai's speech likening the 'Great Clod' that burdens us with form and rests us in death to a smith casting metal that has no business insisting on becoming a famous sword (from 'The Great and Venerable Teacher'); and the skull on the road to Chu that, offered the chance to live again, refuses it, preferring the freedom of the dead to the entanglements of the living. Zhuangzi seems to treat all three as instances of one process he calls the 'transformation of things' -- but I cannot decide whether that framing amounts to a genuine, hard-won equanimity about death or an unusually elegant way of not feeling something one ought to feel. Could you write me a short story, a few pages at most, set in the present day, in which a grieving character actually encounters this material in its specific, strange form -- not flattened into generic talk of 'acceptance' -- and leave it genuinely ambiguous which of those two readings the story supports?
