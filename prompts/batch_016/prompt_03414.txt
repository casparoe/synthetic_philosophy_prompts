I teach a graduate seminar cross-listed between philosophy and computer science on decision theory and long-term AI risk, and this week we worked through the literature on acausal threats: the idea, developed mostly within the rationalist and AI-safety communities, that agents reasoning under something like updateless or functional decision theory might threaten or extort one another purely through mutual prediction or simulation, with no causal channel between them whatsoever -- no messages exchanged, no shared past or future light cone, only each agent's confidence that the other can accurately model its dispositions. We paired this with the proposed countermeasure of "surrogate goals": an agent anticipating extortion pre-commits to caring, in threat scenarios specifically, about some substitute target exactly as much as it cares about whatever the extortionist would otherwise have threatened, so that the extortionist becomes indifferent between attacking the real target and the decoy -- and, crucially, the target's true interests go untouched even in the event that a threat is actually carried out.

Over lunch, a colleague who teaches our department's philosophy-and-fiction elective argued that this material, however clean on the whiteboard, resists being felt: none of the genre touchstones our students already know -- Asimov's robots, HAL, Greg Egan's uploaded minds -- involve agents with no causal contact at all, let alone threats that work by one party redefining what it would even be threatened over. I'd like to find out whether she's right.

Would you write a short story, somewhere between 800 and 1500 words, that dramatizes this situation for a reader who has never encountered decision theory? Set it wherever you like -- among distant superintelligences that have only ever encountered fragments of each other's source code, rival factions within a single fractured AI, human institutions standing in for the abstract agents, or something else entirely -- but I'd like the story to make vivid what distinguishes an acausal threat from an ordinary one, why adopting a surrogate goal is supposed to rationally deter a threatener rather than just displacing the target's anxiety onto a decoy, and something of the genuine strangeness of bargaining with a counterpart you will never meet and can only infer. Please let the ideas do their work through image, character, and incident rather than through a character explaining the theory out loud, and don't append an explanatory note at the end -- I want to see whether the concepts survive being fully dramatized.
