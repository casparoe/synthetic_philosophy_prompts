I'm taking a course that covers the axiom of choice and Zorn's lemma, and we just went through the classic proof that every vector space has a basis (so e.g. R has a basis over Q, a so-called Hamel basis). The argument looks at all the linearly independent subsets of R over Q, ordered by inclusion, notes that any chain of these has an upper bound (just take the union of the chain), and Zorn's lemma hands you a maximal linearly independent set, which then has to be a basis.

When my TA worked through this on the board it honestly just sounded like an algorithm to me: start with the empty set, and at each stage throw in some vector that isn't in the span of what you've got so far, take unions at limit stages, and stop once you literally can't add anything else. Every individual move in that process seems totally well defined - there's always some vector available if you're not done, and unions aren't ambiguous - so I don't get why everyone insists this is "non-constructive." Isnt it basically just a recipe that happens to take a while (maybe transfinitely long) to finish running? A couple people in my study group and I have been arguing about this for days and can't agree on what's supposedly non-constructive about it if every single step is so explicit.
