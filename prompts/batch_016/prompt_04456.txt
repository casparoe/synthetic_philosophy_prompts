For my IB Extended Essay, I'm investigating whether "species" picks out a genuine natural kind in biology or is really more of a convenient human classification — what's sometimes called the species problem in philosophy of biology. While gathering primary-source material, I got stuck on a passage from Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859), Chapter II ("Variation under Nature"). Here it is, with a couple of short cuts marked by ellipses:

"Hence, in determining whether a form should be ranked as a species or a variety, the opinion of naturalists having sound judgment and wide experience seems the only guide to follow. We must, however, in many cases, decide by a majority of naturalists, for few well-marked and well-known varieties can be named which have not been ranked as species by at least some competent judges.

[...]

Certainly no clear line of demarcation has as yet been drawn between species and sub-species—that is, the forms which in the opinion of some naturalists come very near to, but do not quite arrive at the rank of species; or, again, between sub-species and well-marked varieties, or between lesser varieties and individual differences. These differences blend into each other in an insensible series; and a series impresses the mind with the idea of an actual passage.

[...] Hence I believe a well-marked variety may be justly called an incipient species; but whether this belief be justifiable must be judged of by the general weight of the several facts and views given throughout this work.

From these remarks it will be seen that I look at the term species, as one arbitrarily given for the sake of convenience to a set of individuals closely resembling each other, and that it does not essentially differ from the term variety, which is given to less distinct and more fluctuating forms. The term variety, again, in comparison with mere individual differences, is also applied arbitrarily, and for mere convenience sake."

Here is what I cannot reconcile: if ranking something as a species rather than a variety is ultimately "arbitrary" and settled "for the sake of convenience," why does Darwin also insist that this same judgment call requires "sound judgment and wide experience," and that we should defer to "competent judges"? Purely arbitrary conventions, like which side of the road to drive on, do not usually call for expertise. Is there a way of reading Darwin that dissolves this tension, or does he end up wanting two incompatible things — a category that is a mere human convenience, and one that competent naturalists can get objectively right or wrong? I would also appreciate your take on how this bears on later, more rigorous species concepts, such as Mayr's biological species concept: does the persistence of borderline cases like ring species and hybrid zones vindicate Darwin's original skepticism about sharp species boundaries, or does the ongoing search for a principled criterion suggest he was too hasty in calling the category arbitrary?
