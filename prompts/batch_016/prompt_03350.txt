Over the past year I've been slowly working through material on AI safety in my spare time. I work in IT support, not research, so I have no formal training in mathematics or logic, and a lot of what I read takes real effort to get through. One particular sticking point has been bothering me for weeks now, and a couple of people in an online reading group I'm part of haven't been able to talk me through it either. It's what gets called the "Löbian obstacle" to an AI trusting its own future self, or trusting a successor system it builds and then hands control to. From what I've pieced together, the root of it is Löb's theorem: a consistent formal system can only prove the statement "if I can prove X, then X is true" for some particular X when it can already prove X outright, on its own. Writers on this topic say that as a consequence, an agent reasoning this way can only ever fully certify the safety of a successor whose proof system is strictly weaker than its own, never one just as capable as itself, which seems to rule out, in principle, a self-improving AI ever being able to formally stand behind its own upgrades.

What I can't work out is why this counts as a deep obstacle rather than a technicality you could patch over. I trust my own future judgment, and other people's, all the time without anything like a formal proof that our future reasoning will be sound, so I don't see on the face of it why an artificial reasoner couldn't adopt a similarly less airtight form of self-trust and get on with things. Is the whole issue really just a by-product of demanding proof-level certainty specifically, such that it would dissolve under a looser standard of trust, or is there something about self-reference itself that would bring back the same wall no matter how "trust" ends up being cashed out? I'd also like to understand, on a gut level, why simply moving the guarantee up to a stronger outer system doesn't just settle the matter once and for all — why that outer system's trust in itself doesn't just become the new place where the identical problem resurfaces, rather than a genuine fix. Could you walk me through the reasoning behind Löb's theorem itself first, in plain terms, and then build from there to why it specifically bites for an agent trying to formally trust its future or successor self — ideally with a small, concrete example of the kind of self-referential proof step that actually breaks down?
