I'm putting together a linked collection of short fiction where each story has to be built around an actual philosophical crux rather than using one as decoration — I want the effect Ted Chiang gets, where you couldn't strip the idea out without the story collapsing. My draft for this entry kept coming out as a fairly limp riff on chaos theory (the flapping butterfly wing, etc.), and a friend who works in academic ethics told me, not unkindly, that I'd flattened something much stranger: the problem consequentialists call "cluelessness." As I understand it from her and from some reading since, the worry isn't merely that the future is hard to predict — it's that a consequentialist, who is supposed to choose whatever action does the most good, has no principled way to work out which option that is, because the effects of any ordinary act ramify outward forever and dwarf whatever good or harm she can actually see in front of her.

What's made this click for me is a distinction that Hilary Greaves draws, refining James Lenman's original argument: "simple" cluelessness, where the unforeseeable further effects of an act seem to cut for and against it in roughly equal measure, so that, on reflection, you can set them aside and act on the visible reasons alone; versus "complex" cluelessness, where you can actually name a further effect of an act — that it will shift a population's size, say, or change who is even born a few generations on, since shifting the timing of anyone's conception by even a fraction of a second changes which child results — and yet have no common scale on which to weigh that named effect against the good you meant to do. It's the second kind that interests me: not ignorance so much as two live, specific considerations that refuse to be made commensurable.

Could you write me a short story of around 1000-1500 words that gives this second kind of cluelessness a body rather than a lecture? I keep picturing a matchmaker of some kind as the protagonist — someone whose entire vocation is nudging particular people toward each other at particular moments, which sits uncomfortably close to the very mechanism philosophers point to — though I'd genuinely rather you found the right setting and voice than followed my hunch if something better occurs to you. What I most want is for the story to inhabit the vertigo of complex cluelessness rather than resolve it: no closing turn where she's vindicated for trusting her instincts, and no easy slide into "nothing matters" nihilism either, since neither of those is the actual shape of the problem. If you can find a way for the story's form, and not just a character's stated dilemma, to enact that sense of two reasons that don't cancel so much as fail to speak the same language, so much the better.
