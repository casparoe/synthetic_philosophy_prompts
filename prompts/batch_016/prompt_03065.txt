So the basic move in this whole research program, going back to Joyce's 1998 paper, is: lay down some seemingly-minimal conditions on what counts as a legitimate measure of "epistemic utility" (i.e. accuracy), then prove that any credence function violating some Bayesian norm is guaranteed to be dominated -- or just beaten in expectation -- by one that satisfies it, no matter how the world turns out. That's the classic argument for Probabilism. People have since tried to run the same style of move to vindicate a bunch of other norms too: Conditionalization (Greaves & Wallace), the Principal Principle (Pettigrew -- roughly, defer to the known objective chances), the Principle of Indifference (via minimax-style reasoning about the worst case), the Reflection Principle (roughly, trust your own future credences the way you'd trust an expert's), and even the Lockean thesis connecting outright belief to credence (Dorst, Easwaran). My vague sense from reading around is that these arguments are definitely not all equally convincing -- some feel like clean, load-bearing dominance results, and others feel like they're smuggling the conclusion in through the choice of decision rule or scoring-rule axioms.

Can you rank these six norms -- Probabilism, Conditionalization, the Principal Principle, the Principle of Indifference, the Reflection Principle, and the Lockean Thesis -- by how successful/rigorous you think the epistemic-utility arguement for each one actually is?
