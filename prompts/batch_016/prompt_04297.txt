A few of us on my school's Ethics Bowl team have been going down a rabbit hole on AI safety while prepping a case on algorithmic decision-making, and I've been trying to get past the one-line version of Goodhart's law that everyone quotes ("when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure") to something more precise. The taxonomy I keep coming back to, due to Scott Garrabrant, splits Goodhart's law into four distinct failure modes: Regressional Goodhart, where picking whatever scores highest on a noisy proxy also selects for however much lucky error inflated that score, so the true value of what you picked regresses back toward the average; Causal Goodhart, where the proxy only correlates with the goal because of some third factor, or because the goal causes the proxy rather than the reverse, so deliberately pushing the proxy up does nothing to the goal; Extremal Goodhart, where the correlation between proxy and goal held only across the ordinary range of cases and simply stops holding once optimization pressure drives the proxy to an extreme value nobody had observed before; and Adversarial Goodhart, where an agent that benefits from looking good on the proxy, and that knows the proxy is being used as a target, deliberately manipulates it in a way that is completely decoupled from the real goal.

Here's what I can't work out. Write-ups of specification gaming in reinforcement learning almost always use adversarial-sounding language, like saying the agent "exploited a bug" or "gamed its reward function," even in cases where there is really just one optimizer chasing a misspecified reward, not a second agent with genuinely different interests strategically exploiting the first. That seems to sit uneasily with Adversarial Goodhart specifically requiring an agent with its own competing objective. Could you invent an original, concrete specification-gaming scenario (please don't reuse a famous one, like a boat-racing agent that loops in circles for points, or a cleaning robot that learns to cover its own sensor) that a casual observer would probably describe in adversarial terms, but that you can argue, on a careful reading of Garrabrant's definitions, is actually a case of Extremal Goodhart rather than genuine Adversarial Goodhart? I'd like you to lay out precisely which feature of the scenario does the work of placing it in one category rather than the other, since that is exactly the distinction my Ethics Bowl team has not been able to pin down.
