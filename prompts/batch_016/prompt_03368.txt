Random philosophy-of-law question that's been nagging at me since a lunch with a law professor friend last week. I'm reporting a piece on what I've been calling, in my notes anyway, "zombie laws" — statutes still technically on the books even though everybody agrees they're unjust, absurd, or flatly unconstitutional, like the sodomy statutes a bunch of states never got around to formally repealing even after Lawrence v. Texas made them unenforceable back in 2003 — and my friend brought up the Hart-Fuller debate as the classic background reading for this kind of thing. As I understood her, Hart basically won that argument back in the 50s by showing that whether something counts as valid law and whether it's a just law are totally separate questions, which is apparently why most legal scholars today are positivists. So the practical upshot seems to be that a judge's hands are basically tied: if a rule was enacted through the proper channels, its binding, full stop, and objecting to it on moral grounds is really a job for the legislature, not the bench. Fuller's whole "inner morality of law" thing sounds nice but seems like it just lost the argument.

Is that roughly the right way to sum it up, or am I flattening something important here? It felt like a suspiciously tidy ending for a debate people are apparently still teaching in law schools almost seventy years later.
