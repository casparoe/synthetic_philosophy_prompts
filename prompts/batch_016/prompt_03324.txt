I'm a PhD student in History and Philosophy of Science working on a dissertation chapter about mid-century scientists' anticipations of technological risk, and I've spent the last few weeks closely rereading John von Neumann's 1955 Fortune essay "Can We Survive Technology?" (I found it by accident, chasing a footnote about early industrial-CO2 warming estimates, and ended up reading the whole thing — he wrote it, incidentally, while sitting as an Atomic Energy Commissioner, two years before his death in February 1957.) The argument turns out to be much more specific than his reputation for generic Cold War pessimism suggests. His diagnosis of the underlying crisis is structural: industrial progress used to be absorbed by an expanding geographical and political "Lebensraum" — more territory, more room to renegotiate political arrangements — and that safety valve has now closed because the earth is a fixed size, so the same acceleration that used to be dispersed outward now collides directly with existing institutions. And when he turns to remedies, he explicitly dismisses technology prohibition as a "pseudosolution," in a section called "Science the Indivisible": useful and dangerous applications of any given technique, he argues, are always too entangled to separate cleanly, so banning one in practice means banning the underlying science; enforcing such a ban worldwide would require an international authority whose very existence would already presuppose the political unification the ban is supposed to substitute for; and prohibition in general is basically incompatible with the temperament of an industrial civilization.

The section I keep coming back to, though, is the one on climate. He already cites contemporary estimates that industrial CO2 emissions had warmed the planet by roughly a degree Fahrenheit, discusses deliberately altering the reflectivity of ice and cloud cover to warm or cool particular regions on purpose, and stresses that any workable climate-control technique would rest on the same knowledge base as unimagined new forms of climatic warfare — his entanglement argument, applied to this one case — with effects crossing borders in ways that leave it genuinely unclear who is being helped and who harmed by any given intervention. He states plainly that global climate control could make the nuclear dangers of his own day look simple by comparison, and adds that "once such possibilities become actual, they will be exploited" — immediately followed, in the same breath, by the claim that developing new political forms and procedures will therefore be necessary. The essay then closes by denying that any permanent fix exists at all: no cure-all, just an open-ended sequence of small, ad hoc, opportunistic corrections, carried by nothing more dependable than ordinary human patience, flexibility, and intelligence.

What I can't work out is what this actually implies for a fight that obviously postdates him: solar geoengineering. In January 2022, a large group of scholars published an open call for a binding International Non-Use Agreement that would prohibit deployment of things like stratospheric aerosol injection and constrain even outdoor research on it; in 2024, Harvard shut down its long-planned SCoPEx balloon experiment after years of exactly that kind of pressure; and other researchers keep insisting that refusing to study the technology now just guarantees we'll be unprepared if some state, or a sufficiently rich private actor, deploys it unilaterally later. Von Neumann's essay seems to pull in two directions here: the anti-prohibition argument sketched above reads like a direct brief against something like a Non-Use Agreement, especially insofar as it targets research and not just deployment; but his insistence that new political machinery is nevertheless necessary suggests he wasn't simply content to let capability outrun governance. Reasoning from that actual tension, rather than from the generic image of him as a hawk who trusted deterrence and little else — which side of the current fight do you think he would have found more persuasive, and does his own essay leave him any principled way of treating climate engineering as different in kind from the technologies he had in mind when he waved prohibition aside as a pseudosolution?
