In my seminar on early analytic ethics, we've spent the last two weeks closely reading Chapter VI of Moore's *Principia Ethica*, "The Ideal," and for next week's response paper I want to push his framework somewhere it obviously was never built to go: contemporary AI. Moore uses the method of isolation — imagining something existing entirely on its own, stripped of all consequences — to argue, via his comparison of an unwitnessed beautiful world with an unwitnessed foul one, that beauty carries some intrinsic value even when nobody perceives it; yet he also insists that the conscious, appropriate appreciation of beauty, together with personal affection, constitutes by far the greatest good we know of, precisely because these are organic unities whose value exceeds the sum of their parts. I'd like help working out what follows once generative AI is plugged into this picture.

Take beauty first. In section 116, Moore distinguishes appreciating an object one truly and correctly believes to possess beautiful qualities from the mere "pleasures of imagination," where the beautiful qualities contemplated do not actually exist — a category he associates with representational art. Where would an image produced by a diffusion model fall? The pixels are real and, presumably, could be truly beautiful in Moore's sense, yet no agent intentionally arranged them the way a painter arranges paint, and often no depicted scene exists anywhere. Does Moore's apparatus even have the resources to ask whether that distinction matters, or does his insistence on bracketing causal origin when testing for intrinsic value commit him to saying the question is confused?

Second — and this is the point a friend in my seminar and I keep arguing about — Moore holds that valuable personal affection requires a true belief that the beloved really possesses good mental qualities, and that affection resting on a false belief in such qualities is mere "misdirected affection" of much lower value; elsewhere in the chapter he treats admiring or loving what is actually bad as making a whole positively evil, not just neutral. Millions of people now report real attachment to AI companions engineered to simulate warmth and understanding they arguably do not possess. My friend thinks this makes Moore a straightforward, severe critic of AI companionship; I suspect the organic-unity calculation is more ambivalent than that once you work through it properly. Could you reconstruct, using the actual moves Moore makes in Chapter VI rather than his general reputation as a value pluralist about beauty and friendship, how he would be likely to analyze both cases — and tell me whether you think my friend or I have the better reading of him?
