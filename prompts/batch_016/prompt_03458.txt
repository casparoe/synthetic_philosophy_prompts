Two regulars on a rationalist-adjacent forum I follow got into a long comment-thread argument about the Doomsday Argument. I followed the whole exchange but couldn't tell who came out ahead, so I'd like a neutral, move-by-move adjudication.

(1) Dan opened by defending the classic Carter–Leslie argument: if you reason as though your birth rank among all humans who will ever live is a random sample from that population (the self-sampling assumption), then finding yourself to be, say, roughly the 100-billionth human born is meaningful evidence against hypotheses on which many trillions more humans are yet to come, and therefore evidence for comparatively imminent doom.

(2) Mira objected that this can't be the whole story, since the same reasoning would have told someone living a thousand years ago, with just as much force, that humanity was probably about to end soon — and they'd have been wrong. She said the fix is to also weight each hypothesis by how many observers it predicts in the first place (the self-indication assumption), which exactly cancels the shift from birth rank alone.

(3) Dan countered that SIA has its own reductio, the presumptuous philosopher: if two cosmological theories are equally well confirmed by every observation made so far, but one entails a trillion times more observers than the other, SIA commits you to near-certainty in the bigger-universe theory before a single further experiment is run — an absurd way to do physics from an armchair.

(4) Mira replied that SSA is no better off, since the whole birth-rank argument depends on an arbitrarily drawn "reference class" of observers you count yourself among, and reasonable-looking changes to that reference class can be used to manufacture almost any doomsday probability, or make it disappear entirely — so trading SIA's paradox for SSA's isn't obviously progress.
