I handle allergen labeling at a small regional bakery and catering company, and over the past year I've developed a habit I'm not entirely proud of. Company procedure says that before using certain ingredients — for instance, a sauce base that our supplier occasionally reformulates to include tree nuts without much warning — in an order marked nut-free, we're supposed to call the supplier's hotline and confirm the current formulation. On a quiet shift that's a minor inconvenience, but during a rush it can mean a fifteen- or twenty-minute delay, so more often than I'd like to admit, I've just gone with whatever the label said the last time we checked, assuming it probably hadn't changed. So far it never has, and nothing I've sent out has ever caused a problem.

A few months ago, an employee at another branch of the same company did precisely what I sometimes do: during a rush, he skipped the confirmation call and assumed the formulation was unchanged. This time it wasn't, and a customer with a severe tree nut allergy ended up in the emergency room. He was fired, and people across the company now talk about him as careless, maybe even someone who shouldn't be trusted around food again. I still skip the call from time to time, and as far as anyone here knows, I've never made a mistake. When I brought this up with a coworker, expecting her to see why it bothered me, she just said, "well, nothing's happened, so you're fine" — which is exactly the part I can't get past.

I don't have any background in ethics, but I can't stop turning this over in my head. If what he did was careless enough to get him fired and talked about that way, then what I've been doing must be equally careless, since it's the same shortcut taken for the same reason — the only difference is that his supplier's formulation happened to change and mine hasn't, at least not yet. And yet, if I'm honest, I don't feel anywhere near as guilty about my own shortcut as I imagine I would if it had actually sent someone to the hospital. So I keep going back and forth: is he genuinely more at fault than I am, or are we equally to blame and I've simply had better luck so far? And is it actually reasonable for how things happen to turn out — something neither of us controlled at the moment we made the choice — to change how much blame we deserve, or is that just a convenient story people tell themselves after the fact?
