So this actually happened at my ACX meetup a few weeks back and i haven't stopped thinking about it since. We were doing that "guess how long this project will really take" icebreaker, using a friend's app rewrite as the example, and two regulars (I'll call them Renata and Marcus) ended up derailing the whole exercise arguing about reference classes instead of just giving a number.

(1) Renata's opening move was pure outside-view: forget the team's actual plan, past rewrites of comparable scope at her old company slipped by about 9 months on average, so just assume this one will too. (2) Marcus objected that "comparable scope" is doing all the work: define the reference class as "rewrites at big companies" and you get a 9-month slip, define it as "rewrites by teams that shipped their last three projects roughly on time" and it's more like 3 weeks -- picking whichever class spits out the scarier number isn't objectivity, its base-rate shopping, basically the reference class problem live at a meetup. (3) Renata shot back that this proves too much: you can always slice a reference class narrower and narrower until it's just N=1 and you've smuggled the inside view back in wearing a trenchcoat, and besides, insurers price policies off base rates constantly and do fine, so the method clearly isn't bankrupt. (4) Marcus granted the N=1 point but said "reasonably broad and relevant" is itself a judgment call that no amount of outside-viewing can justify from within the method, and that insurance is disanalogous because insurers get corrected across thousands of repeated, scorable bets, while a one-off software estimate never gets that kind of error-correcting feedback.

Nobody settled it (we never did get an actual estimate out of the icebreaker, RIP). Could you go through the four moves and say who's got the better of it at each one -- not just who "wins" overall -- and flag anything you think both of them are missing? Also, is there an actual principled answer to the reference class problem lurking in here, or does it just dissolve into "it depends on your priors"? Even after Marcus's move (4) refinement, his position can start to sound like you can never justify applying a statistic to a genuinely one-off case -- which feels like a nuts place to land, but maybe that's just the correct, if uncomfortable, conclusion?
