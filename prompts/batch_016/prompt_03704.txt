A friend and I spent the better part of a long evening arguing about the ethics of the atomic bombings after we watched Oppenheimer together a few nights ago, and the conversation drifted from Oppenheimer's own culpability to the more specific question of Truman's. I ended up bringing in Elizabeth Anscombe's pamphlet "Mr. Truman's Degree," which I had read for a seminar a while back, and defended her central charge: that Truman was, in her own terms, a murderer, because "to choose to kill the innocent as a means to their ends is always murder," and that Hiroshima and Nagasaki were not cases of foreseeable-but-unintended civilian deaths of the kind the doctrine of double effect can excuse, but cases in which the mass killing of civilians was itself the mechanism by which Japan's leadership was meant to be terrorized into surrender. Anscombe herself treats this as an easy case rather than a borderline one: she takes it as more or less obvious which side of the intending/foreseeing line the bombings fall on.

My friend was not persuaded, and her objection was sharper than the usual "an invasion would have cost more lives" reply. She argued that the intending/foreseeing distinction is doing far less work than Anscombe needs it to, because an agent's aims can always be redescribed after the fact in whatever terms make double effect apply. American planners, she pointed out, could just as truthfully say their aim was to destroy Japan's remaining capacity and will to continue fighting, with the deaths of well over a hundred thousand civilians as a certain, foreseen, but strictly unintended side effect of achieving that aim — a description with exactly the same logical shape as the sort of case Anscombe would treat as legitimate elsewhere. If that redescription is always available, she concluded, the intending/foreseeing line is not tracking anything real, and Anscombe's confidence is just moral condemnation dressed up as conceptual precision. I did not have a good reply at the time, though I also was not willing to grant that the distinction is simply empty.

I would like you to help me work out whether it is empty. Please construct the strongest argument you can for the position that the intending/foreseeing distinction, as applied to Truman's decision, is principled rather than infinitely manipulable. Concretely, I would like you to: (1) state a general, non-question-begging criterion for when a redescription of an agent's aims amounts to illegitimate special pleading rather than a genuinely different characterization of what was done; (2) show why that criterion supports reading the Hiroshima and Nagasaki bombings as cases of intending civilian deaths as a means, rather than merely foreseeing them as a side effect; and (3) apply the same criterion to at least one contrasting case that Anscombe would treat as legitimate double effect, so as to show that your criterion is not simply gerrymandered to reach the conclusion about Truman that Anscombe already wanted. If the distinction genuinely cannot bear this weight, I would rather you tell me that plainly than construct a strained defense of Anscombe for its own sake.
