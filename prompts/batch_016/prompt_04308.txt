I am a retired experimental physicist who has only taken up philosophy seriously since retiring, and I have been working through Frege largely on my own, since he kept coming up, as the father of modern quantification theory, in the philosophy of mathematics I was reading. Partway through, I came across something none of that earlier reading had prepared me for: a private diary Frege kept for several weeks in the spring of 1924, unpublished until nearly seventy years after his death, in which he records himself as a fierce antisemite and opponent of parliamentary democracy, an admirer of Bismarck who despised the Weimar Republic and wanted Jews stripped of political rights, if not expelled from Germany outright. Michael Dummett, who probably did more than anyone to establish Frege's reputation among English-speaking philosophers, admitted in print to having been badly shaken on first reading this material, since he had revered Frege as the very model of a rational mind. Even so, Dummett's considered verdict was to treat the diary as a distinct, separable fact about the man, leaving his assessment of the logic and the philosophy of language untouched.

This reminds me of an episode from my own field. Philipp Lenard and Johannes Stark, both Nobel laureates, built their antisemitism directly into their physics, denouncing Einstein's relativity as "Jewish physics" and championing a rival "Deutsche Physik" they considered properly Aryan in spirit and method -- a case where, by most historians' lights, the ideology really did corrupt the science rather than sit beside it unconnected. I gather that some recent Frege scholarship pushes back against Dummett's clean separation, arguing for something similarly intimate in Frege's own case: that his late political writings do not merely borrow logical-sounding language in passing but redeploy specific pieces of his technical apparatus, for instance the theory of sequences and the ancestral relation from the Begriffsschrift, repurposed to define lines of "Aryan descent," together with his own insistence that a properly formed concept must have sharp boundaries, which he worried made "Jewish" difficult to specify precisely enough for workable legislation. Other commentators consider this kind of textual echo superficial, holding that it implicates Frege's logic no more than a mathematician's use of arithmetic to justify a monstrous policy implicates arithmetic itself.

I would like to see this disagreement worked out properly rather than merely gestured at. Could you write me a dialogue between two contemporary Frege scholars, evenly matched in competence, one of whom holds that these technical borrowings show a substantive continuity between Frege's logic and his politics that should genuinely unsettle how we teach and evaluate his legacy, and the other of whom holds that the connection is trivial and that his properly philosophical achievements -- the sense/reference distinction, the analysis of generality and number, the anti-psychologistic conception of logic -- remain entirely untouched by it, much as Dummett concluded. I would like each side to engage directly and specifically with the other's strongest points rather than talking past one another, and I would rather the exchange end in genuine, unresolved disagreement than in one party simply conceding, since I am still trying to work out for myself which side has the better of it.
