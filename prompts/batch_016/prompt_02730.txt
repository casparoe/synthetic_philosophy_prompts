I spent my career in the natural sciences and only took up philosophy in earnest after retiring, which may be why one aspect of Goethe strikes me more forcefully than it seems to strike his literary biographers: he was as much a working scientist as a poet, and I do not think the two vocations were as separate as we now assume. His Farbenlehre was a sustained polemic against Newton's reduction of color to measurable, mathematical quantities, and his morphological studies of plants and animals rested on a patient, qualitative attentiveness to the phenomenon itself — what he called "zarte Empirie," or delicate empiricism — rather than on subsuming it under abstract general law. I find the same temperament at work in his political life: his decades as a Weimar minister overseeing mining, road-building, and public finance; his well-documented discomfort with the revolutionary politics of his age; and his refusal to share in the nationalist fervor of the Wars of Liberation, preferring to regard even Napoleon as a world-historical phenomenon rather than a national enemy.

Suppose Goethe were confronted today with a German or European state increasingly governed by algorithm: welfare eligibility, policing priorities, or migration decisions determined by predictive statistical models rather than the discretionary judgment of officials. Reasoning specifically from the connection between his scientific epistemology and his political temperament, what do you think Goethe's verdict on this style of governance would be, and why? I would like a definite answer, not merely a survey of considerations on both sides.
