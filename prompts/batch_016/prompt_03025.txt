So for my formal epistemology seminar we've spent the last couple weeks on Jeffrey conditionalization, the update rule for when an experience shifts your credences around without ever making you certain of anything (the standard example is glancing at a cloth by candlelight and getting more confident it's green, while still leaving open blue or violet). This week's problem set had us do two Jeffrey updates back to back on the same prior: an uncertain glimpse of the cloth's color, then an uncertain feel for whether it's silk. Owen updated color-then-texture and Sasha did texture-then-color, and when they compared notes afterward the final probabilities for "is it silk" were noticeably different, like a 15-point gap, not just rounding. They spent a while assuming somebody had messed up the arithmetic, but both calculations check out fine - the order genuinely changes the answer, unlike with ordinary "learn E for certain, then learn F for certain" conditionalization, where order never matters at all.

That turned into a whole thing between them in our group chat that I just got to watch unfold. Sasha's opening move was that this shows Jeffrey conditionalization is basically a broken updating rule: if two people can end up holding the exact same total evidence but land on different credences purely because of which order they happened to process it in, then belief isn't tracking evidence the way its supposed to, period. Owen said that's comparing unlike things - ordinary conditionalization is order-independent because intersecting two propositions you're certain of is symmetric, but a Jeffrey shift works by holding fixed your credences conditional on a partition (he kept calling this "rigidity"), and nothing guarantees rigidity for the second partition survives once the first update has already disturbed your unconditional probabilities, so the two orders were never really running on "the same evidence" in the relevant sense to begin with. Sasha's comeback was that this doesn't save the rule, it just relocates the problem one level down: since you can't check in advance whether rigidity will actually survive an update, the rule can't tell you which of the two resulting credences, if either, you should trust, which is a pretty bad look for something that's suppose to be telling you how to update. Owen replied that plenty of rational norms lean on background conditions you can't fully verify either, like your senses being reliable in the first place, so that's not a special defect of Jeffrey's rule specifically, and he half-remembered reading that if you require rigidity to hold for both partitions simultaneously, the two orders do provably agree, so non-commutativity is really a symptom of the two bits of evidence being entangled with each other rather than proof the rule itself is broken. Sasha's last word was that this "fix" is useless in practice: the cases we actually care about updating on are exactly the ones where the two things you're learning about bear on each other, so we're guaranteed to land in the entangled case whenever the order-dependence would actually matter to us.

Neither of them budged and honestly I can't tell who comes out ahead here. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right about what at each step, rather than just handing out an overall winner? And if there's something about Jeffrey conditionalization you think both of them are missing, something that would actually dissolve part of the disagreement, I'd really like to know - this has been living in my head rent free since Tuesday.
