I spent most of my working life as a field herpetologist, with several seasons devoted to the Ensatina salamanders that form a horseshoe of interbreeding populations around California's Central Valley — a textbook case I used more than once to illustrate gradual divergence to first-year students. Since retiring I have been auditing a university course on philosophy of language, where we recently spent two sessions on the sorites paradox. Reading Crispin Wright and then Timothy Williamson on the subject, it occurred to me that my old study organism might be a sharper test case than the heaps-of-sand and bald-men examples the readings kept returning to, so I sketched out the argument below and would value an honest reaction before I inflict it on anyone in my old department.

The thought is this. Most textbook sorites series turn on what Wright calls a tolerance relation: reflexive and symmetric between neighbouring cases (grain n and grain n+1 are both "still a heap," or not), but not actually transitive across the whole series, which is what generates the paradox once the inductive argument is run from one end to the other. Ensatina eschscholtzii supplies what looks to me like a natural, non-toy instance of exactly this structure. Every subspecies around the ring interbreeds freely with its immediate geographic neighbour, all the way from Northern California down both the coastal and inland arms — yet the two subspecies that finally meet at the closed end of the ring, in the south, are reproductively isolated and behave as good biological species. "Interbreeds with" is tolerant in Wright's sense, and it fails to be transitive, exactly as the sorites requires. What makes this more than a cute illustration, I think, is that the failure of transitivity here is not a fact about how coarsely a word carves up a smooth underlying reality (as with "heap"), and not a fact about the limits of an observer's discrimination (as with the color-patch versions of the paradox), but a fact about gene flow and reproductive compatibility that holds independently of anyone's classifying activity. If so, Ensatina looks like a rare naturally occurring case of ontic rather than merely semantic or epistemic vagueness.

I would welcome your reactions on a few points. First, does the disanalogy I am leaning on — a mind-independent causal relation, interbreeding, as against an at-least-arguably observer-relative one, phenomenal indiscriminability — actually earn me the stronger, ontic conclusion, or does Gareth Evans's well-known argument against vague objects, if I have the reading right, show that talk of a genuinely vague fact of the matter about conspecificity is incoherent from the outset, whatever the source of the vagueness is supposed to be? Second, I understand that Stebbins's clean 1949 picture has gotten messier since — there is apparently some hybridization even at the terminal contact zone, and a few herpetologists now dispute that Ensatina counts as a "true" ring species at all — and I am not sure whether that empirical untidiness quietly sinks the philosophical argument or whether the point can be salvaged on a suitably idealized version of the case. And third, quite apart from whether I have gotten any of the details right, do you think this is a genuinely useful angle on the sorites debate, or does it just dress up, with salamanders, a point that vagueness theorists have already made more carefully with their own examples?
