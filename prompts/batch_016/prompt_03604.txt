I don't have any formal training in philosophy, but I've spent a long time working through Bayesian epistemology on my own, and the Dutch Book argument was the first result that really grabbed me. The classic version shows that incoherent credences license a set of bets that look individually fair yet jointly guarantee a loss, but this always struck me as showing, at most, that betting on incoherent credences is imprudent, not that the credences themselves are epistemically defective. I've since read that this worry motivated so-called "depragmatized" versions of the argument, which try to relocate the defect: on this view, an incoherent credence sanctions a package of bets as fair even though the bets in that package cannot all be fair together. But this rescue seems to me to smuggle in the assumption that fairness carries over from each bet taken separately to the whole package taken together, which simply reintroduces the additivity axiom that the argument was supposed to justify in the first place. Please argue for the position that even depragmatized Dutch Book arguments fail to show that being probabilistically incoherent is an epistemic failing rather than a merely practical one, because they cannot close the gap between individual and joint fairness without presupposing the very coherence they are meant to establish.
