I'm a PhD student in history and philosophy of science working through Kepler's writings on astrology, and I'd like to move past the usual "believer or skeptic" framing. In Tertius Interveniens and De Fundamentis Astrologiae Certioribus, Kepler explicitly rejects the twelve-fold zodiac, the houses, and planetary rulerships as arbitrary conventions with no physical basis, retaining only the aspects — the angular distances between planets at a nativity — as the genuine mechanism, and he grounds their validity in the same geometric theory of constructible regular polygons that he used to explain musical consonance in Harmonices Mundi. Yet he also spent years casting conventional horoscopes for patrons and once quipped that astrology was a foolish daughter who nonetheless kept her respectable mother, astronomy, fed.

Given this specific package of commitments, how do you think Kepler would have judged the sun-sign newspaper horoscope column — a genre dating only to the 1930s that discards aspects entirely and reduces a chart to whichever zodiac sign the sun occupied at birth? Please reconstruct the reasoning he would most plausibly have offered, rather than a generic verdict of "superstition," and address whether his pragmatic career as a paid astrologer would have tempered his mathematical objections.
