I spent thirty-five years working as a condensed-matter physicist, much of it thinking about phase transitions and so-called "emergent" phenomena: phonons, quasiparticles, collective excitations that behave for all practical purposes like genuine entities, even though, in some more fundamental sense, there is "really" only a lattice of atoms and the forces between them. Since retiring I have been reading my way into metaphysics, and I was startled to discover that philosophers argue over an everyday version of the very same worry. Some hold that, strictly speaking, there is no such thing as a table or a chair, only simples (fundamental particles, say) "arranged chair-wise," and that talk of the composite object itself is just a convenient way of speaking, not literally true; others insist that ordinary objects are every bit as real as whatever they happen to be made of. What has really stopped me in my tracks is a move I keep encountering in the literature. Perhaps this whole dispute is not about the world at all, since "there exists" might simply carry a different meaning for each side (something one author calls "quantifier variance"), so that both descriptions could be equally true of one and the same reality. Against this, other authors reply that reality has one objectively privileged, "joint-carving" sense of existence, which at most one side is using correctly — so the disagreement is real after all.

Rather than have the debate summarized for me, I would like to see it fought out properly. Could you write a dialogue between a nihilist about ordinary composite objects and a commonsense realist who defends them, built around a single concrete case — say, whether the wooden chair in my study exists as something over and above the simples arranged chair-wise? I would like each side to respond directly to what the other has just said, rather than simply restating a fixed position — working through, in turn, the suggestion that the nihilist's claims can be translated into "arranged-wise" talk without any loss, the quantifier-variance defense of treating that translation as removing all disagreement, and the joint-carving-style rebuttal to it. If it fits naturally, have them also test their own dispute against some general mark of a merely verbal disagreement — for instance, a method I came across that involves temporarily banning the disputed term, introducing two subscripted replacements for its rival senses, and checking whether every resulting claim then becomes acceptable to both sides. I do not want either position handed an easy win here; I would be quite content to finish still genuinely unsure which of them has the better of the argument.
