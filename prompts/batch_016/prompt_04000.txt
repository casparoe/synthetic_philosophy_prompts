I'm a cognitive science PhD student working on folk causal judgments about omissions — e.g., when people are and aren't willing to say that someone's failure to act "caused" an outcome — and before I finalize my experimental materials I want to make sure I've got the underlying metaphysical dialectic straight. Could you write a dialogue, set over coffee after a workshop talk, between two philosophers of causation who disagree about whether omissions can be genuine causes?

One should defend a difference-making view, in the spirit of Lewis's work on absence causation or Schaffer's and Sartorio's accounts, on which an omission counts as a cause whenever the outcome counterfactually depends on it. The other should defend a physical-connection or process view, in the spirit of Dowe or Beebee, on which real causation requires a connecting causal process, so that omissions are at best "quasi-causes."

Ground the exchange in the standard case from this literature: someone promises to water a friend's plant, fails to, and the plant dies. Have the process theorist press the problem of profligacy — if the friend's omission is a cause, unrestricted counterfactual dependence seems to make every other person on Earth who also didn't water the plant a cause too — and have the difference-making theorist give a substantive answer rather than simply biting the bullet or gesturing at "context." Each should also say whether they think the metaphysics of causation is answerable to our ordinary practices of assigning moral and legal responsibility for omissions, or can simply come apart from them.
