A friend who's really into AI sent me a PDF of Alan Turing's original 1950 paper on the imitation game a while back, and I finally got around to reading the whole thing properly this week, mostly out of curiosity about where all the current AI chatter ultimately traces back to. Partway through the list of objections Turing raises against his own proposal, I ran into a section that genuinely surprised me. He titles it "the argument from extrasensory perception," and rather than doing what he does with basically every other objection on that list — arguing that it doesn't hold up — he seems to more or less accept the phenomenon as real and tries to design around it instead. He says the statistical case for telepathy struck him as "overwhelming" and calls the objection "quite a strong one" in its own right. He then works out a version of the imitation game where a human who is good at telepathy or clairvoyance correctly names the suit of a hidden playing card far more often than chance, while the machine can only guess randomly and falls behind, letting the interrogator spot the human. He even notices that if the machine's guesses come from an internal random-number generator, that generator itself might be swayed by the interrogator's own psychokinetic powers, so that, in his words, "anything may happen." His eventual fix is to run the whole test inside what he calls a "telepathy-proof room."

Setting aside whether ESP is actually real, which I'm aware it almost certainly isn't and isn't really what's confusing me, I can't quite work out the internal logic of what's going on in this passage. Why did Turing treat this one objection so differently from all the others in that section — as something to be structurally designed around rather than argued against — and what, precisely, would make a genuinely telepathic or clairvoyant participant a threat to the imitation game in a way that an ordinary lucky guesser wouldn't be? And does his own "telepathy-proof room" fix actually take care of the psychokinesis-and-random-numbers wrinkle he raises just before proposing it, or does it quietly leave that part of the problem unsolved? I'd appreciate it if you could walk me through what's really going on in his reasoning here.
