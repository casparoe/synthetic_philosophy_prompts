Random rabbit hole from this week: rereading Turing's original 1950 paper (yes, THE Turing test paper) and forgot how wild section 6 gets. He spends several paragraphs taking ESP dead seriously, flatly stating "the statistical evidence, at least for telepathy, is overwhelming," and even proposes sticking both parties in a 'telepathy-proof room' so a psychic interrogator couldn't cheat at the imitation game. Turns out the single most celebrated piece of British telepathy evidence from that era, S.G. Soal's card-guessing experiments, was only found to be rigged years after Turing himself had died, once a statistician combed back through the old score sheets. Apparently this wasn't random for Turing personally either - he'd been quietly fixated on whether minds survive death ever since a close friend of his died when they were both teenagers. So you've got maybe the most rigorous thinker of the century getting totally taken in on a question he had real emotional stakes in, using the exact same "look at the stats" reasoning hes normally so careful with. Can you lay out the most important implications of this, both for (a) how much I should still trust the rest of that paper, and (b) more generally, how much epistemic credit to extend to someone I consider rigorous when they confidently call the evidence for something surprising "overwhelming," especially outside their main wheelhouse?
