For a long time I've wondered why, if God is real and actually wants a relationship with people, he doesn't just make his existence obvious instead of leaving so many sincere people stuck in doubt. That question is what got me reading Pascal's Pensées on my own — I have no formal background in philosophy or theology. I got stuck on two consecutive fragments, numbered 585 and 586 in the edition I've been using online, where Pascal tries to explain why God's hiddenness is actually fitting. Here they are, in the W. F. Trotter translation:

"That God has willed to hide Himself.—If there were only one religion, God would indeed be manifest. The same would be the case if there were no martyrs but in our religion. God being thus hidden, every religion which does not affirm that God is hidden is not true; and every religion which does not give the reason of it is not instructive. Our religion does all this: Vere tu es Deus absconditus [Truly thou art a hidden God].

If there were no obscurity, man would not be sensible of his corruption; if there were no light, man would not hope for a remedy. Thus, it is not only fair, but advantageous to us, that God be partly hidden and partly revealed; since it is equally dangerous to man to know God without knowing his own wretchedness, and to know his own wretchedness without knowing God."

Could you actually walk through the logic here rather than just summarizing what Pascal believed about hiddenness in general? Two things trip me up. First, I can't reconstruct why he thinks the mere existence of a single religion, or of martyrs found only within Christianity, would make God "manifest" — the inference seems to skip several steps. Second, I don't understand what precise work "partly hidden and partly revealed" is doing: is hiddenness a deliberately calibrated middle point meant to balance the two dangers he names at the end of the second fragment, or is it more like a filter that separates people who are genuinely seeking God from those who aren't? Finally, once you've worked through the argument, does it actually hold up against the modern complaint that a loving God wouldn't leave so many honest, non-resistant doubters without enough to go on — or does Pascal's reasoning only work if he's quietly assuming that no doubt is ever fully honest?
