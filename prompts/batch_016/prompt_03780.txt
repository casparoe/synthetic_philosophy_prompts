I've never studied philosophy formally, but the sorites paradox has occupied me for a long time now: the fact that removing a single grain from a heap never seems to turn it into a non-heap, yet enough single removals obviously do. Reading around on my own, I've come across two rival theories that both try to hold onto something like classical logic rather than soften it: epistemicism, in the form Timothy Williamson defends, on which "heap" really does have a perfectly sharp cutoff, one we are simply and permanently unable to know, and supervaluationism, on which there is no sharp cutoff and no fact of the matter about any individual borderline case, even though a sentence like "either this is a heap or it is not" still comes out true. What troubles me is that both theories seem to face the very same difficulty one level up: the line between the clear cases and the borderline cases looks every bit as unsettled as the line between heap and non-heap, something usually called higher-order vagueness. Please compare how epistemicism and supervaluationism each try to cope with this higher-order problem, and say which of the two comes out ahead once it is taken into account. Justify your answer.
