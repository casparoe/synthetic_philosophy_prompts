I'm currently taking a seminar on decision theory, and in the last couple of sessions we moved from Newcomb's problem and superrationality to the more exotic idea of "acausal trade": roughly, that two agents who can never causally interact (say, because they lie outside each other's light cones, occupy different branches of a multiverse, or one exists only in the other's distant future) can still benefit one another, each doing what it predicts the other wants, purely on the strength of mutual predictability.

For a short response paper, I want to sort a set of conditions that come up in discussions of such trade according to how indispensable each one actually is. Please classify each of the eight conditions below into exactly one of three categories: "Necessary" (acausal trade cannot occur without it), "Facilitating" (it makes the trade easier or more robust, but isn't required in principle), or "Not required" (it sounds relevant but does no real work once the other conditions are met).

1. Each agent assigns a non-negligible subjective probability to the other's existence.
2. The agents can exchange messages beforehand to negotiate specific terms.
3. Each agent can predict or simulate the other's decision procedure with reasonable accuracy.
4. The two agents run identical or near-identical decision algorithms.
5. Each agent's decision theory treats its own choice as entangled with the other's choice (as in updateless or functional decision theory), rather than treating the other's behavior as causally fixed (as in causal decision theory).
6. There is some way for each agent to detect the other's defection after the fact and retaliate.
7. The agents are similar enough in power or resources that what one offers is worth roughly what it asks for in return.
8. The agents' preferences are such that mutual concessions yield a net gain for both sides, rather than being purely zero-sum.
