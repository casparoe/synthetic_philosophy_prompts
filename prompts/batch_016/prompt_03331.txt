So I spent almost 40 years as an experimental physicist before retiring, and the last year or two I've fallen down a philosophy rabbit hole -- mostly epistemology, since it's the part that feels closest to what I used to do. Lately I keep bumping into this thing called the "problem of logical omniscience": in standard possible-worlds models of belief, and even in plain old Bayesian probability (since every tautology has to get probabilty 1), an agent basically has to already know every logical and mathematical consequence of what they believe. Which is obviously nuts for real reasoners -- mathematicians spend whole careers not knowing wether some statement follows from the axioms. I get that this is supposed to be a genuinely deep problem, not just a harmless idealization like a frictionless plane, but every summary I find just namedrops fixes -- impossible-worlds semantics, awareness logic, Garrabrant's logical induction stuff -- without explaining what actually breaks. Could you walk me through, fairly intuitively, why logical omniscience is such a stubborn problem to fix, and where each of those proposed solutions ends up falling short?
