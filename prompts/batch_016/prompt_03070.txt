For the novel I am currently drafting, my protagonist, a number theorist named Renata, is the sole survivor of a collapse at a remote Antarctic research station. She spends nine years there with no human contact, filling notebooks with a proof she and a collaborator had begun together before she lost contact with him, extending it step by step using inference rules that only the two of them had ever employed. Once the notebooks are recovered, I want two philosophers of language to debate her case in an invented dialogue that will stand as a self-contained interlude in the book.

One philosopher should argue a position close to that of Kripke's Wittgenstein: since Renata has had no one to agree or disagree with her for nine years, there is no fact that could settle whether she faithfully continued the original rule or silently lapsed, at some unnoticed point, into a "quus"-like deviation from it (one that matches her earlier practice at every step so far but diverges from it beyond that point); at most, each step has simply gone on seeming right to her. The other philosopher should resist this conclusion, arguing that Renata's isolation does not by itself collapse the distinction between being right and merely seeming right, and should press the first philosopher on why communal agreement is supposed to escape the very finiteness that is claimed to undermine facts about a lone individual, given that a community's own history of agreement is likewise finite and open to exactly the same skeptical reinterpretation.

Please have the two actually engage each other's strongest objections rather than restate their opening positions, keep the exchange tied to the concrete details of Renata's notebooks rather than letting it drift into pure abstraction, and aim for roughly 700-900 words.
