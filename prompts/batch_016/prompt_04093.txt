I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm currently reporting a longform feature on the more philosophical corners of AI safety research. In the course of interviewing researchers for the piece, I kept being steered toward a cluster of problems that goes under the name "embedded agency," laid out at length in Abram Demski and Scott Garrabrant's 2018 sequence of that title, which starts from the observation that a real agent is not a detached observer of the world it reasons about, the way classical decision theory tends to imagine, but a physical piece of that same world: too small, and too entangled with its surroundings, ever to hold a complete and accurate model of itself from some vantage point outside itself. The consequence I can't stop turning over is what researchers sometimes call the Löbian obstacle to self-trust: owing to a limitation related to Löb's theorem in mathematical logic, a reasoning system generally cannot prove, using methods no stronger than its own, that a second system exactly as capable as itself, including some future or revised version of itself, will only ever "prove" things that happen to be true. That turns out to be a genuine formal problem, not merely a sentimental one, for any agent that might need to certify a successor before handing over control, since the checking can never be any more trustworthy than the checker performing it.

What matters most to me is that the story convey the precise shape of the dilemma — not generic unease about software updates, but the sharper fact that no amount of internal proof-checking can fully close the gap when the checker and the checked are, on paper, equally powerful peers.
