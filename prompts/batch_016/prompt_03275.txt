I recently joined a small AI-governance team that reviews decision-theoretic proposals for limiting the harms of extortion between advanced AI systems, and one idea I can't stop turning over is Tobias Baumann's "surrogate goals." The proposal is that an agent facing an acausal threat—the kind that works purely through mutual prediction or simulation rather than any direct causal contact, as in the reasoning behind Roko's Basilisk—can protect what it truly values by manufacturing a substitute goal, deliberately arbitrary and low-stakes, and then convincing any would-be extorter, and perhaps itself, that this decoy is what it really cares about. What unsettles me is that the whole scheme only works if the caring is genuine: the agent has to talk itself into treating something it invented as sacred. Write a short story that inhabits this problem from the inside—a character, human, AI, or otherwise, who constructs a surrogate goal in order to survive a threat, and who gradually loses track of where the performance ends and the belief begins.
