Here is a well-known passage: section 17 of G. W. Leibniz's *Monadology* (1714), in Robert Latta's translation.

"Moreover, it must be confessed that perception and that which depends upon it are inexplicable on mechanical grounds, that is to say, by means of figures and motions. And supposing there were a machine, so constructed as to think, feel, and have perception, it might be conceived as increased in size, while keeping the same proportions, so that one might go into it as into a mill. That being so, we should, on examining its interior, find only parts which work one upon another, and never anything by which to explain a perception. Thus it is in a simple substance, and not in a compound or in a machine, that perception must be sought for. Further, nothing but this (namely, perceptions and their changes) can be found in a simple substance. It is also in this alone that all the internal activities of simple substances can consist."

This "mill" thought experiment is often cited as an ancestor of the explanatory gap and, more specifically, of the contemporary conceivability argument for philosophical zombies. I'd like help pinning down how strong that lineage actually is. First: is the inference Leibniz draws here best reconstructed as a conceivability argument in anything like David Chalmers's sense, where conceiving a scenario without contradiction is taken to license an inference to its metaphysical possibility—or is Leibniz doing different argumentative work that only superficially resembles the zombie argument, and if so, what premises would need to be supplied to convert his reasoning into a genuine one? Second, Leibniz is a panpsychist who holds that every simple substance has some degree of perception and that bodies are merely aggregates grounded in such substances. Given this, does his own system actually leave logical room for a full physical duplicate that lacks perception entirely, or does his broader metaphysics rule out that very possibility even as this passage seems to gesture toward it?
