So a friend of mine got really into NFTs a while back, and we had this argument I never really resolved. His whole pitch was that owning the NFT of some digial artwork makes his copy "the real one," and everyone else who just right-clicks and saves the same image is stuck with a worthless fake. I pushed back that this is a strange thing to claim about a digital file -- there's no canvas, no single physical object involved, its just bits, and copying bits is more like cloning a git repo than forging a painting: you don't end up with a degraded knockoff, you end up with something functionally and informationally identical to "the original." Anyway, I went down a rabbit hole about this afterward and found out there's actual philosophy on exactly this kind of question (just, obviously, decades before NFTs were a thing) -- something about how a guy named Nelson Goodman argued that paintings are the kind of art where a perfect duplicate is just a forgery and doesn't count as the work, whereas music and literature are a totally different kind of art, where any correctly-made copy just IS a full instance of the real work -- he apparently called these "autographic" vs "allographic" art, if I'm remembering the terms right. That distinction feels like the actual crux of the argument I had with my friend, but I don't understand it well enough to use it properly myself.

Could you write me a dialogue -- a real back-and-forth, not two people giving separate speeches -- between two sharp people arguing this out: one defending something close to my friend's position (that NFTs successfully import the "painting" kind of original/forgery distinction into the digital world, so there's a genuine sense in which the NFT holder has "the real one" and everyone else has a copy), and the other arguing the "music/literature" side (that digital files are inherently the kind of thing where a copy just is the original, so NFTs don't create a real original at all, just a social and legal fiction pretending otherwise). Neither one should be a pushover or a strawman -- I want them each actually responding to the other's strongest point instead of just repeating their own talking points. Specifically, the NFT defender needs a real answer to the fact that the underlying file is perfectly, costlessly copyable with zero loss, and the skeptic needs a real answer to the fact that people clearly do care about provenance and "owning the real one" for reasons that don't seem to be purely aesthetic (status, supporting the artist, being part of the object's history, whatever).
