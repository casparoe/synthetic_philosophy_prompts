Since retiring from a career running an electrochemistry lab, I have been filling the gap left by grant applications and journal referee reports with a rather undisciplined tour through philosophy. It was via Franklin's scientific writings that I recently arrived at his Autobiography. I already knew the kite-and-key version of the man from school, but I had never sat with the section on his "bold and arduous project of arriving at moral perfection": the thirteen virtues, deliberately ordered so that mastering one would ease the next, tracked week by week in a little book with a column for each day and a black mark for every lapse, the whole thirteen-virtue course repeated four times a year. As an experimentalist, I found the design oddly familiar - one variable isolated at a time, data logged daily, the procedure iterated. I described this resemblance to a friend who works in virtue ethics, and she was unpersuaded: on her Aristotelian view, the virtues are not thirteen separable modules to be perfected in sequence, since practical wisdom (phronesis) is precisely what binds them into a single character, so that one cannot really possess temperance in the full sense while one's justice or courage lag behind. She also pointed to two moments where Franklin seems to undercut his own project on its own terms: his confession that he achieved mostly the appearance rather than the reality of humility, adding that even if he ever fully conquered pride he "should probably be proud of my humility," and the episode of the "speckled axe," in which he talks himself into settling for a permanently imperfect character rather than continuing to strive.

I would like to see this disagreement actually played out rather than adjudicated from a distance. Could you write a dialogue, with clear speaker labels, between Franklin defending his method and a philosopher arguing from within the Aristotelian tradition - long enough for several genuine exchanges on each side, rather than a single opening salvo and rebuttal? Franklin should be free to draw on his own stated reasons: the logic behind his ordering of the virtues, the case for treating self-cultivation as a matter of isolating one habit at a time, and his explicit view that virtue is worth pursuing because it serves worldly happiness rather than because it is divinely commanded. The Aristotelian should press the unity-of-virtue objection and the humility episode as hard as they can, but should have to reckon with Franklin's actual replies rather than simply score points against him. I am less interested in declaring a winner than in whether each position can withstand the other's strongest objection, so please do not feel obliged to resolve the disagreement neatly by the end.
