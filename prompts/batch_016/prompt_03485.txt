I'm several drafts into a novel whose protagonist, Elif, grew up trilingual: English from her mother, German from school and the streets of the city where she was raised, and Turkish from her father's side of the family, whose kitchen table was the one place where all three generations spoke at once. The structural conceit I've built the book around is that Elif's inner monologue isn't merely translated across these languages but restructured by them: clipped and heavy with subordinate clauses when she's thinking in German, hedged and careful about the line between what she witnessed herself and what she was only told when she's thinking in Turkish (I've been leaning on that language's grammatical marking of evidentiality for this), and looser and more associative in English. I even want the prose itself to shift its rhythms chapter by chapter to mirror whichever language is dominant in Elif's head at that point in the story.

To give this some intellectual ballast—and because an editor once fairly accused me of hanging an earlier subplot on a half-digested, pop-science version of the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis, a mistake I'd rather not repeat—I went looking for something more rigorous and settled on Jerry Fodor's language of thought hypothesis. My understanding is that it actually makes a stronger and more precise claim than Sapir-Whorf: that what Fodor calls "Mentalese" just is whichever natural language a person happens to be thinking in at a given moment, so that switching languages doesn't merely color Elif's thoughts but reshapes the very medium they're couched in—German Mentalese in the German-dominant chapters, Turkish Mentalese in the Turkish ones, and so forth. That seemed like exactly the theoretical backbone my chapter-by-chapter conceit needed, and I was planning to have a linguist character walk Elif through a version of it partway through the book, both as exposition and as a kind of thematic key to the novel's structure. Before I commit that scene to the page, though, I'd like a sanity check from someone who isn't me: does the language of thought hypothesis actually say anything like this? If I've gotten it wrong, I'd rather find out now, and I'd welcome hearing whether there's a real theory—Fodor's or otherwise—that would let me keep the substance of what I'm doing with Elif without misrepresenting the science to my readers.
