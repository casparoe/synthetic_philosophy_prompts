In a seminar on early modern rationalism I have been working through Leibniz's Monadology alongside parts of the Discourse on Metaphysics and the Theodicy, and, quite separately, I recently came across updateless decision theory (UDT) through an online reading group. UDT was developed within the online "rationalist" community associated with LessWrong (Wei Dai's original proposal dates to 2009) as an alternative to the standard causal and evidential decision theories, motivated by puzzles like Newcomb's problem. Its central claim is that an agent should not simply choose, at each moment, whatever action looks best given what it currently knows; instead, it should commit, before making any observations at all, to a complete policy specifying an action for every possible thing it might later observe, and it should select whichever policy has the highest expected value from the standpoint of its prior. The case that makes this vivid is "counterfactual mugging": a perfectly reliable predictor tosses a fair coin; on tails, it asks you for $100; on heads, it would instead have handed you $10,000, but only because it predicted that you would be willing to pay the $100 had the coin come up tails. Suppose the coin does come up tails. An agent that simply updates on this fact and asks what is best to do from here has no remaining reason to pay; an updateless agent, evaluating the whole policy from before the coin was tossed, pays anyway, and so does better, ex ante, than an agent that would refuse.

This struck me as oddly close to Leibniz's own picture. His monads are "windowless" and cannot act on one another at all, and yet their internal states remain in perfect step because God, surveying the entire array of possible worlds from outside time, chose in advance, by the principle of sufficient reason, the one complete history - including the full career of every monad within it - that was best overall. Coordination, on this picture, is achieved not by anything updating on or reacting to anything else, but by everything having been settled together, once and for all, from a vantage point prior to any of it playing out, with each substance's present state already containing, in seed form, everything that will ever follow from it.

I would like you to work out, in some detail, how Leibniz himself would most plausibly have responded to updateless decision theory, had it somehow been explained to him. Would he recognize pre-established harmony, together with the doctrine that each substance already contains its own future, as an updateless solution to a coordination problem avant la lettre, or would he resist the comparison, and on what precise grounds? I am especially interested in two things: first, whether committing to a policy "from the prior" sits well with his distinction between necessary and contingent truths, given that he needs contingent truths to require an infinite analysis in order to preserve any real freedom against the pressure of the principle of sufficient reason; and second, whether the kind of dependence UDT posits between different instances of one decision procedure that never causally interact is really the same relation as pre-established harmony, or whether it is a distinctively logical form of dependence - closer to a predicate's being contained in a complete concept - that his theological framework does not, in the end, capture.
