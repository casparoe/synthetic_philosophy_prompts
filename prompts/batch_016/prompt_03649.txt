For my AP European History class, we recently spent a week on the Marquis de Condorcet, and I have been a little preoccupied with him ever since. What stood out most was his 1785 jury theorem, which shows that if voters decide independently and each has just better than a fifty percent chance of picking the correct answer to a yes-or-no question, a majority vote among them becomes almost certainly correct once the group is large enough. We also covered the constitution he drafted for the Girondins in 1793, which tried to build that same voting theory directly into the machinery of French government, only for the whole project to be discarded when the Jacobins gained control; Condorcet was outlawed soon afterward and died in a prison cell before the Terror had even run its course.

Around the same time, for a debate club project on alternative models of government, I started reading about Robin Hanson's idea of "futarchy," which he sums up as "vote on values, bet on beliefs." Citizens would vote to fix a single welfare metric, something like an adjusted measure of national income that also accounts for health and the environment, while real-money prediction markets, rather than politicians or courts, would decide which specific policies are expected to raise that metric, with wealthier or more confident traders moving prices further than everyone else. It strikes me that Hanson's markets and Condorcet's juries are both meant to turn the combined judgment of many people into something more reliable than any single expert's opinion, yet they arrive there in almost opposite ways, since one treats every voter as an equal and independent data point while the other openly lets money and confidence buy extra influence.

Condorcet obviously died two centuries before anyone proposed betting on government policy, but given everything he believed about political equality, the mathematics of collective decisions, and the proper construction of constitutions, and given how his own confidence in rational institutional design actually played out, what do you think he would have said about futarchy? I would like you to work through, in some depth, where the logic of his jury theorem would actually support the idea and where it would cut against it, rather than just guessing at whether he personally would have approved.
