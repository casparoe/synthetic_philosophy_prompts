So this happened at my writers' group last week and it's kind of been stuck in my head since. Two people there, Dana and Theo, got into a whole thing over blurbs -- which, full disclosure, is a topic I've got skin in too, since I've had to write a couple myself and always feel a bit weird about it. Dana had agreed to blurb a friend-of-a-friend's debut novel, and mentioned, pretty casually, that she'd only actually read the first eighty-ish pages and skimmed the rest before turning in a line calling it 'a stunning, fully realized debut that announces a major new voice.' (1) Theo said that's flatly dishonest -- you can't hand someone a verdict on a book's aesthetic merit that you haven't earned by actually experiencing the whole thing, that's not like passing along a fact you heard secondhand, its presenting somebody else's (or nobody's) judgment as your own settled perception.

(2) Dana pushed back that blurbing works exactly like restaurant recommendations -- if a friend with great taste tells her a tasting menu is incredible, she'll happily tell other people 'you have to try this place, it's incredible' without ever having eaten there, and nobody calls that lying, it's just testimony doing its normal job. (3) Theo said the restaurant case is different because that kind of talk is implicitly flagged as hearsay ('I heard it's amazing'), whereas Dana's blurb was written in her own voice, first person, no hedge, dressed up as there own aesthetic judgment -- that framing is the actual problem, not testimony as such. (4) Dana answered that review culture runs on exactly this move -- critics recycle consensus into their own confident verdicts all the time ('now regarded as a modern classic,' 'an instant landmark') -- so if Theo's standard is right, most of the literary world is quietly lying to itself, and singling her out for it is a bit much.
