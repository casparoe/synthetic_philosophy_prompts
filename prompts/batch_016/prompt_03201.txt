I'm a second-year PhD student working on the foundations of decision theory, and this term a seminar on subjective probability led me far deeper into Frank Ramsey's work than the syllabus required. Alongside "Truth and Probability," I ended up reading, via Cheryl Misak's recent biography, the short piece Ramsey wrote in 1925 that was published posthumously as the "Epilogue" to *The Foundations of Mathematics and Other Logical Essays* — usually treated as the closest thing he left to a statement on the meaning of life. There he explains why he isn't troubled by the vastness, or the eventual heat-death, of the universe: his picture of the world, he says, is "drawn in perspective, and not like a model to scale," with human beings filling the foreground and the stars shrunk to the size of coins, because the stars themselves "cannot think or love," and it's thinking and loving he cares about, not sheer size. He was dead within five years, at twenty-six.

What has turned this from an antiquarian curiosity into something more personal is that, over the past year or so, I've watched AI systems begin to match or outperform very good mathematicians and logicians at exactly the kind of formal reasoning Ramsey himself was a prodigy at, and I'll admit it has left me genuinely unsettled about what, if anything, is supposed to be special about the work I've spent years training to do. Philosophers now tend to respond to this the way Ramsey responded to the stars, by insisting that raw capability isn't what matters and that genuine understanding is — and that current systems still lack it. But Ramsey strikes me as an odd ally to recruit for that argument, since his own account of belief in "Truth and Probability" is famously pragmatist and dispositional: to believe something to a given degree just is to be disposed to act, or bet, in certain ways, with no further inner ingredient required. That looks uncomfortably close to a criterion that a sufficiently capable system could, in principle, satisfy.

So here is what I would like help thinking through, as a piece of intellectual history rather than as a claim about present-day AI: given Ramsey's actual, documented commitments — his standard, from the Epilogue, that what matters is thinking and loving rather than size; his dispositional theory of belief; and his general temperament as an unmystical, rather cheerful pragmatist (he is usually credited with helping push Wittgenstein away from the more mystical portions of the *Tractatus*) — how do you think he would have responded to a machine that satisfied every behavioral test for "thinking" he might plausibly have proposed? Does his own framework commit him to widening the foreground of his picture of the world to include such machines, or is there something else in his work that would let him resist that extension while keeping the rest of the Epilogue's argument intact?
