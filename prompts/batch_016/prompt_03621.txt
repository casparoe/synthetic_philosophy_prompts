I'm working on a longer feature about analytic philosophers who occasionally wandered into territory you wouldn't expect from a metaphysician, and I keep coming back to David Lewis's short, dry essay "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" (Philosophical Papers, 1997). A philosopher friend and I have been arguing about it over drinks for weeks, and I'd like a more rigorous adjudication than either of us can manage.

For context, in case you don't know the piece: Lewis opens with a thought experiment in which a justly sentenced offender has a devoted, innocent friend who volunteers to serve the sentence in his place. We find this absurd for prison terms or a death sentence, he says — no one seriously proposes that a murderer's friend could hang in his stead — because the folk idea that punishment is a kind of debt the offender owes turns out to be a bad metaphor: with a literal debt, a creditor is indifferent to who pays so long as the loss is made good, but deserved punishment seems to require that the specific wrongdoer be the one who suffers. Yet Lewis then notes that we do tolerate a friend paying someone else's serious fine, even though a stiff fine is every bit as punitive as a prison term and serves none of the compensatory functions that might otherwise justify a substitute. He considers and rejects the easy explanation that we only tolerate this because policing gifts and cheques would be impractical, arguing that a principled objector to substitution would instead count that impracticality as a strike against fines as a mode of punishment — which we never do. His conclusion is a tu quoque aimed at secular critics of the Christian doctrine of penal substitutionary atonement: since everyone, not just Christians, turns out to be, in his words, "of two minds" about substitution — rejecting it for prison and death, tolerating it for fines — he treats this shared, stable inconsistency as weak evidence that substitution might somehow be defensible after all, even though nobody can yet say how.

My friend thinks the fines case is a red herring that lets defenders of penal substitutionary atonement off the hook too easily; I suspect she's right but can't say why. Could you settle it for us? Please argue that Lewis's tu quoque fails: make the strongest case you can that consistency does not actually require treating the fines case as being on a par with the prison and death cases, so that our toleration of paying someone else's fine provides no real cover for the coherence of penal substitution as a theory of the Atonement. I'm after an actual argument for some specific disanalogy or governing principle — not just a restatement of the intuition that death and fines simply feel different.
