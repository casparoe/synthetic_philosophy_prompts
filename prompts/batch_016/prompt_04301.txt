I'm a novelist currently assembling a collection of short fiction that tries to dramatize particular intellectual and ethical postures rather than merely explain them in an afterword. The subject I keep circling back to is George Eliot's religious position, which is more specific and stranger than the standard "great Victorian who lost her faith" summary suggests. Having translated Strauss's Life of Jesus (1846) and then Feuerbach's Essence of Christianity (1854) — Feuerbach's central move being to recast God as humanity's own best qualities projected outward and worshipped as something external to itself — Eliot came away treating this demolition of the supernatural not as license to relax her ethics but as a reason to tighten them. There's the well-attested account, from F. W. H. Myers, of her telling him in the Fellows' Garden at Trinity College, Cambridge, on a rainy evening in 1873, that of the three words "God, Immortality, Duty," the first was inconceivable, the second unbelievable, and the third nonetheless peremptory and absolute. It's the same posture behind the closing pages of Middlemarch, which credit the "growing good of the world" to people who lived unnoticed and "rest in unvisited tombs," and behind her own later description of herself, only half-jokingly, as a "meliorist" rather than an optimist: someone committed to gradual moral improvement while explicitly denying herself any cosmic guarantee that improvement is the direction things are actually headed.

What I would like from you is a short story, roughly 900 to 1,400 words, that puts an ordinary character in exactly that position rather than stating it discursively: someone for whom God and any afterlife have become, by their own honest lights, unavailable, but for whom some quite small obligation — a debt only they know about, a confidence they could break at no real cost, an inheritance they could quietly fail to pass on — has become, if anything, more binding for having no cosmic backstop and no witness. Please avoid a deathbed or courtroom setting, and please don't let the story resolve into either a return to belief or a shrug into "nothing really matters," since both endings would let the character off a hook that Eliot is very deliberately trying to keep them on. Beyond that, the period, setting, and character are entirely yours to invent; I'm less interested in the plot furniture than in whether the moral weather of the piece is recognizably hers.
