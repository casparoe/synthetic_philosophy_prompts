I'm a software engineer with no formal training in logic or philosophy, but a debate at work about whether large language models could ever "really" do mathematics the way a person does sent me down a rabbit hole into the Lucas-Penrose argument. I ended up reading J.R. Lucas's original 1961 paper and the relevant chapter of Penrose's The Emperor's New Mind, and I'd like to sanity-check the core reasoning with someone who actually knows this literature, because it strikes me as a genuinely rigorous piece of logic rather than the hand-wavy "computers will never really be conscious" claims I usually see in pop-science articles.

As I understand it, the argument runs like this. Take any Turing machine -- equivalently, any consistent formal system S powerful enough to encode arithmetic -- that is proposed as a complete model of some mathematician's reasoning, in the sense that S proves exactly the arithmetical statements that mathematician would ever come to accept as true. Gödel's first incompleteness theorem lets us mechanically construct, from a specification of S, a sentence G(S) that essentially says "I am not provable in S," and shows that, since S is consistent, S can never prove G(S), even though G(S) is true. Crucially, we, standing outside S, can carry out this very reasoning ourselves and thereby see that G(S) is true -- something S itself can never output. Since this recipe works for any candidate formal system anyone proposes as a complete model of a given mathematician's reasoning, we can always exhibit a truth that eludes whatever system is supposed to capture that reasoning. So no fixed algorithm can be equivalent to a human mathematician's reasoning, which seems to undercut not just the computational theory of mind but the very idea of an AI that genuinely understands mathematics rather than just manipulating symbols according to fixed rules.

Given how clean this seems as a piece of logic, I don't understand why it isn't treated as more or less settled among philosophers of mind, logicians, and AI researchers, most of whom, from what I can tell, still treat computationalism as a live, respectable position. Is the field mostly just declining to accept an inconvenient conclusion, or is there an actual gap in the argument as I've reconstructed it here?
