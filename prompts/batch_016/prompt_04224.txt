I'm a philosophy PhD student taking a graduate seminar on classical Islamic theology this term, and I'm currently drafting a response paper on the Seventeenth Discussion of al-Ghazālī's Tahāfut al-Falāsifa -- the discussion in which he denies that fire's contact with cotton necessitates the cotton's burning. Two of the secondary readings we were assigned describe al-Ghazālī as having anticipated Hume's problem of induction by some six centuries, on the grounds that both thinkers deny that reason can demonstrate any necessary connection between cause and effect, and both trace our expectation that the one will follow the other to mere custom rather than to insight into the nature of things.

There's something about this parallel that I can't quite work out, though. Al-Ghazālī explicitly raises, in order to answer, the philosophers' worry that denying necessary connections opens the door to total arbitrariness -- that a man might leave a book on his shelf and come home to find it transformed into a horse, since nothing in the nature of books or horses rules this out once necessity is abandoned. His reply is that God simply maintains a stable, freely chosen "habitual course," from which he departs only rarely, in evidentially marked cases such as the miracles granted to prophets. But if al-Ghazālī's position is truly of a piece with Hume's, this reply looks question-begging: our sole ground for expecting the habitual course to hold tomorrow is that it has held so far, which is exactly the sort of inference Hume's argument is supposed to have discredited. And yet al-Ghazālī plainly doesn't take himself to have landed in a general skepticism about nature's reliability -- elsewhere in his writings on law and everyday conduct, he reasons about medicine and the practical affairs of life in perfectly straightforward causal terms. So which is it: has al-Ghazālī simply failed to notice that his own premises, taken seriously, undercut the ordinary causal reasoning he elsewhere relies on without comment, or is there a principled reason his occasionalism doesn't actually saddle him with Hume-style inductive skepticism, despite the real similarities between the two arguments? I'd value a careful comparison that pins down exactly where the analogy to Hume holds and exactly where it breaks down.
