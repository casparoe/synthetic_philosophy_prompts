I am putting together a short paper for a volume on philosophical theology, built around David Lewis's 1997 note "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" Lewis is responding to the standard charge that substitutionary atonement is simply incoherent, since punishment, unlike an ordinary monetary debt, cannot be discharged by anyone but the person who deserves it. His reply is a tu quoque: secular practice is just as inconsistent as the theology. We are all confident that a devoted friend cannot serve an offender's prison sentence, or take his flogging for him, yet we are largely untroubled when a friend simply pays the offender's fine, even one so heavy that most people would rather serve the equivalent time. Lewis rules out two deflationary readings of this asymmetry: that fines are simply milder (some plainly are not), and that we merely tolerate what we cannot practically police, since, on that story, the leakiness of fines to substitution should itself count as a standing complaint against fines as a mode of punishment, and it manifestly does not. He concludes that Christian and secular practice rest on exactly the same unexplained tolerance for substitution, so neither camp is well placed to lecture the other.

I presented the next section of my paper at a departmental work-in-progress seminar, where a colleague argued that Lewis has simply won the point and there is nothing more to say. I am not convinced, and would like your reaction before committing the argument to print. My thought is that Lewis overlooks a distinction he himself draws in passing, right after his opening thought experiment: with an ordinary debt, all that matters is that the creditor not be left out of pocket, regardless of whose money satisfies it, whereas punishment is supposed to fall specifically on the person who deserves it. I want to argue that fines tolerate substitution not because they are mild but because money is fungible in exactly that sense: a transferred sum retains no trace of who transferred it, while time served, pain endured, or public disgrace cannot be handed over even in that thin, bookkeeping way. If that is right, an official, serious fine was never really functioning as "punishment" in the sense Lewis's own opening distinction requires, whatever the sentencing statute calls it, and his tu quoque dissolves rather than being earned.

What I would like from you is not a verdict on the theology but a stress test of the move itself: does the fungibility criterion do the explanatory work I want, or have I merely relabelled Lewis's puzzle rather than solved it? Community service worries me most as a possible counterexample, since its output seems just as impersonal as a payment into court funds, yet no jurisdiction I know of lets a friend serve out your hours. I would also like to know whether I am simply unpacking, rather than improving on, the passage from Philip Quinn that Lewis himself quotes, about older legal codes in which even killing carried a purely pecuniary debt of punishment; and, more basically, whether you think I have represented Lewis's own argument fairly in the first place.
