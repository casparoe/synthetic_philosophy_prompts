I'm a science journalist writing about the cognitive science of joint action — work, following Natalie Sebanz and Günther Knoblich, on how two people manage something as simple as carrying a table through a doorway in step or clapping in unison. Before covering the experiments, I want to understand the philosophical account of "shared intention" underneath them, and exactly where it breaks down into genuine disagreement.

Write a dialogue between a philosopher defending Michael Bratman's reductionist theory of shared intention — built from interlocking individual intentions, meshing sub-plans, mutual responsiveness, and common knowledge — and a second philosopher defending Margaret Gilbert's non-reductionist plural-subject theory, on which joint action requires a sui generis joint commitment that no single party can unilaterally rescind. Anchor the exchange in one test case: two people are jointly repainting a fence when, halfway through, one of them simply walks off for a better opportunity, leaving the job unfinished. The Gilbertian should argue that this wrongs the other participant in a way mere disappointment can't capture; the Bratmanian should try to either earn that same verdict from purely individualist materials or explain why we shouldn't want it. Both philosophers should be equally sharp, engage each other's actual arguments rather than talking past one another, and the dialogue needn't end in agreement.
