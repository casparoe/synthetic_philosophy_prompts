This term I'm teaching a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and we recently spent two sessions on Bas van Fraassen's 1984 paper "Belief and the Will" and his defense of what he calls the Reflection Principle. Stated informally, the principle says that if I am now certain that my credence in some proposition at a specified future time will equal some particular value r, then my credence in that proposition right now should already equal r as well. Van Fraassen defends this, in the spirit of the diachronic Dutch book argument for ordinary conditionalization, as a way of making precise the thought that I ought to treat my own anticipated future opinion as at least as trustworthy as my present one, barring some specific reason to think otherwise.

What stalled the seminar for the better part of an hour was the question of what could possibly count as such a "specific reason." Van Fraassen wants to exclude cases where I expect my future credence to be corrupted, say by memory loss, manipulation, or drugs, but critics along the lines of David Christensen have pressed cases that sit uncomfortably close to ordinary life rather than to science-fiction brainwashing scenarios. My students kept generating their own examples: a junior colleague who expects to grow more confident in her dissertation's central thesis mainly because she has staked a decade of her career on it; a man who expects his political convictions to drift rightward as his assets grow, independently of any argument he might hear in the meantime; a patient who knows that the mild sedative she is about to be given reliably makes her feel far more confident than she otherwise would that a risky procedure will go well.

I would like your help thinking through the implications of this debate rather than restating its formal machinery, which we have already covered. Could you describe what you take to be the most important implications of the Reflection Principle, and of objections like Christensen's, for how someone in one of the situations above ought to regard their present belief in light of the future belief they can already foresee? I am especially interested in whether there is any principled way of separating "legitimate" anticipated belief change from the kind the principle was never meant to license, or whether the search for that distinction just relocates all the interesting normative work outside the principle itself.
