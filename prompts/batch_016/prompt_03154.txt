I'm a philosophy PhD student working on jurisprudence. Two people in my reading group got into a running argument about Hart and Fuller on the postwar "grudge informer" case: a woman denounced her husband under a Nazi statute in 1944, and after the war a German court convicted her, ruling that the statute was too unjust to be law.

(1) Ana defended the court: the Nazi legal order ran on secret directives, retroactive decrees, and courts that ruled however the regime wished, so calling the outcome "not really law" isn't the dodge Hart alleged, but an honest verdict on a system that had stopped functioning as law. (2) Marcus objected that this proves too little: the statute itself was public, prospective, and applied consistently to thousands of others, so nothing about it alone fails Fuller's eight criteria - Ana's move only works by borrowing a verdict on the whole regime and pinning it on one enactment that passes those tests fine by itself. (3) Ana replied that this misreads Fuller: legality is a property of a system, not of statutes one at a time, so a tidy rule from a regime that otherwise governs by terror and exemption doesn't thereby inherit genuine validity. (4) Marcus countered that this just re-imports, under the label "legality," the very judgment about the regime that positivism wants kept separate from validity - and invoked Hart's later jab that you could satisfy all eight of Fuller's desiderata in service of something monstrous, so they read as conditions for ruling efficiently, not for something counting as law in any morally loaded sense.

Go through moves (1)-(4) and say who has the better argument at each step, then tell me what you think both of them are missing.
