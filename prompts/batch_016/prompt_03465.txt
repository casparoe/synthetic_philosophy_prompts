Robert Nozick died in 2002, over a decade before effective altruists began seriously proposing large-scale interventions in nature — including the use of gene drives to suppress predator populations — on the grounds that wild animals suffer immensely from predation, starvation, and disease. In the section of Anarchy, State, and Utopia titled "Constraints and Animals" (pp. 35–42), Nozick had tentatively floated, without fully endorsing it, a hybrid view of animal ethics he called "utilitarianism for animals, Kantianism for people": maximize total happiness for all living beings, but impose strict side-constraints on how human beings may be treated. Would the aggregative, maximizing character of the animal-welfare half of that formula commit Nozick to endorsing such interventions, or would his broader commitments — his preference for a decentralized "framework for utopia" over any single imposed vision of the good, and his general wariness of overriding existing patterns of interaction for the sake of some aggregate outcome — lead him to resist that conclusion? Defend your answer with reference to the specific tensions within his own views.
