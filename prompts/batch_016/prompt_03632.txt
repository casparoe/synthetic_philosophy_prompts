I spent about thirty years running a long-term field study on stream salamanders before I retired, and like most field biologists of my generation I mostly used significance tests as a black box: run the numbers, see if p sneaks under .05, move on. In retirement I've been working through some philosophy of statistics for fun (Deborah Mayo's book on severe testing, some Ian Hacking), and it's honestly making me squirm about stuff I did on autopilot for decades, like checking my data partway through a field season and quietly deciding whether to keep going. Anyway, I still do a monthly video call with two former postdocs of mine, Ingrid and Marcus, who are both faculty now, and last month they got into an argument on the call that I haven't been able to shake.

It started when Ingrid mentioned, kind of offhand, that in her new lab they check the data every ten or so subjects and stop as soon as p dips below .05. (1) Marcus pounced: that's "optional stopping," and it's well known that peeking and stopping the moment you hit significance inflates your true false-positive rate way past 5% -- in the limit, if your willing to peek forever, you can more or less guarantee a "significant" result even when the null is true, so her reported p-value doesn't mean what it says. (2) Ingrid said their old labmate, a committed Bayesian, once convinced her this isn't actually a sin if you think like a Bayesian: the likelihood/stopping-rule principle says your evidence, and hence your posterior, depends only on the likelihood of the data you actually got, not on whatever rule made you stop collecting it -- so peeking is a problem for frequentists, not for her. (3) Marcus said fine, that's true for an honestly-computed likelihood from a model that wasn't fiddled with, but that's exactly the catch: nobody's stopping rule is the only flexible thing in a real analysis. We never see whether covariates got added or dropped along the way, how many "looks" happened before the one that got written up, or whether the model itself shifted to fit what was found -- optional stopping is just one researcher degree of freedom among several, and a Bayesian who peeked and quietly adjusted things is no more auditable than a frequentist doing the same, so celebrating the likelihood principle solves a toy version of the problem while ignoring the part that actually drives the replication crisis. (4) Ingrid replied that this is a completely general point about disclosure and has nothing to do with her being Bayesian specifically -- a frequentist who claims to have pre-registered a sequential design with a proper alpha-spending correction can just as easily be lying about that, so Marcus's objection undercuts frequentist fixes exactly as much as it undercuts her. (5) Marcus's comeback was that there's still an asymmetry: the whole culture of pre-registered stopping boundaries and registered reports grew up around frequentist error control, precisely because frequentists needed some public, falsifiable commitment for their error rates to mean anything -- so that accountability machinery already exists for her to borrow, whereas nothing in Bayesian philosophy, on its own terms, ever demanded it.

I can't tell who's landing real punches versus who's just being stubborn, and nobody else on the call wanted to referee it. Could you go through the exchange move by move (I've marked the five moves above) and say who has the better of it at each one, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also be curious whether there's some important consideration neither of them raised.
