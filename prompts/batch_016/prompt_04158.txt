I spent about 35 years running an analytical chemistry lab, and now that I'm retired I've fallen down a philosophy rabbit hole, which is how I stumbled onto Henry Sidgwick. The guy wrote maybe the most painstakingly rigorous utilitarian treatise ever, and he also spent decades running the Society for Psychical Research, trying to find hard evidence that people survive death, and never got anything conclusive before he died in 1900. Then his collegue Myers died the next year, and for decades afterward mediums scattered across different countries started producing these fragmented bits of automatic writing, full of obscure classical allusions, that apparently only cohered into a real message once researchers compared notes across sittings -- like Myers and Sidgwick were trying to prove their own survival to the very people they'd spent their careers training to distrust that kind of evidence. Something about that setup gets me more than any ghost story ever did. Could you write me an actual short story (fiction, not a history lesson) built around that situation -- maybe from the POV of one of the investigators sifting through the scripts years later -- that really sits with the question of what would even count as proof, and whether Sidgwick's own standards of rigor could ever be satisfied by evidence like that?
