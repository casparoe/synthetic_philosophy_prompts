I'm a PhD student in political theory working on party democracy, and I keep getting stuck on a tension between two texts on my reading list. In "On the Abolition of All Political Parties" (1943), Simone Weil argues that any party is definable by three traits — it manufactures collective passion, it exerts organized pressure on its own members' private judgment, and its real end, whatever it professes, is its own unlimited growth — and concludes that every party is totalitarian in aspiration and should simply be abolished, replaced by elections run on individual platforms and by loose, non-membership circles of affinity that the law would forbid from ever hardening into standing organizations.

Robert Michels, studying the pre-WWI European parties most committed in principle to internal democracy, concluded in Political Parties (1911) that oligarchy is an iron law for any sufficiently large, durable organization pursuing a collective goal, rooted in the technical need for specialized leadership and the rank and file's dependence on it, regardless of founding ideology.

Write an essay that puts these two diagnoses in dialogue and reaches a definite verdict — don't hedge with "it depends" unless you can say precisely what it depends on and argue for that. Does Michels's account show that Weil has mistaken a structural feature of organized collective action for a pathology specific to "parties," such that her fluid circles would simply re-oligarchize under another name — or is there something in her proposal that could genuinely escape the iron law? Defend your verdict against the strongest form of the opposing view.
