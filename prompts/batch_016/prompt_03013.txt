A few weeks ago, my school's philosophy club worked through the Ship of Theseus, and I got so hooked that I ended up reading well past whatever our club sponsor originally assigned, into the more technical version of that debate: endurantism versus perdurantism. As far as I can tell, endurantists hold that a persisting object is "wholly present" at every moment it exists, while perdurantists (also called four-dimensionalists) think of a persisting thing more like a worm stretched out across time, made up of distinct temporal parts, so that what actually exists at any given instant is just a thin cross-section of the whole four-dimensional object. David Lewis apparently thought this second picture was needed to solve his "problem of temporary intrinsics": the puzzle of how a single, wholly-present object could have flatly incompatible properties (bent, then straight) at different times without contradiction.

What I really can't stop thinking about, though, is a use of this framework I stumbled on almost by accident: some philosophers have applied it to the metaphysics of bodily resurrection. The underlying worry is that if a person's existence has an actual gap in it — she dies, her body is destroyed, and someone qualitatively just like her is produced much later out of entirely different matter — it is not obvious that the person who results is strictly identical to the one who died, rather than an extremely convincing copy. One response I came across, which I believe is associated with the philosopher Hudson, is essentially a resurrection-flavored version of David Lewis's own solution to fission cases: treat the pre-death and post-resurrection portions as temporal parts of two different four-dimensional "worms" that share an initial stretch of stages and only branch apart at death, the way two paths can share a common beginning before forking, so that nothing genuinely drops out of existence at any point. It is much less clear that endurantism has an equally tidy answer available, since it seems to require that the whole person — not just a temporal part of her — show up again after a total gap.

Could you write me a short story (roughly 800–1200 words) that dramatizes this specific puzzle — persistence across a genuine gap in existence, as opposed to mere gradual change — through a character who actually lives through something like it? It doesn't need to be explicitly religious; a secular equivalent, such as cryonic revival or a mind rebuilt from an old backup long after the original body and brain are gone, would raise exactly the same issue. What I'd most like is for the endurantist and perdurantist verdicts about the character to genuinely diverge, and for the philosophical content to emerge through her situation and reasoning rather than through some other character pausing the plot to explain the two theories out loud.
