I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on metaethics, currently drafting the introduction to a dissertation chapter on fitting-attitude and buck-passing accounts of value. Before I commit to a stance, I'd like a second opinion. Recall the setup: on Scanlon's buck-passing account, for an object to be good just is for it to have other, "lower-level" properties that give us reasons to take up a favorable attitude toward it. This runs into the well-known wrong-kind-of-reasons (WKR) problem: a demon credibly threatens to make you suffer unless you admire it, or come to value some perfectly undistinguished object such as a saucer of mud; the threat certainly seems to give you a reason for the relevant attitude, yet nobody wants to conclude that the demon, or the mud, is thereby good. So buck-passers need some principled way of separating "right-kind" reasons, the very reasons that are supposed to constitute value, from "wrong-kind" reasons like the demon's threat — and it has proven surprisingly hard to draw this line without either quietly presupposing the distinction one needs or opening the door to further counterexamples of the same shape.

I've been going back and forth with a friend from my reading group about which of the going proposals is genuinely the best response to the problem, as opposed to a mere relabeling of it, and I'd like your independent judgment before my next supervision meeting. Please rank the following five strategies from most to least promising as solutions to the WKR problem, where "most promising" means doing the best job of principledly excluding demon-style reasons while (a) not simply stipulating the very distinction it needs to earn, (b) not falling to further counterexamples of the same general shape, and (c) preserving as much as possible of the original motivation for going buck-passing in the first place.

1. Rabinowicz and Rønnow-Rasmussen's object-given/state-given distinction, on which the demon's threat gives you only a "state-given" reason to want or bring about the attitude, never an "object-given" reason to have it, and only the latter bears on value.
2. Jonathan Way's transmission-based argument for a similar verdict — that wrong-kind considerations are reasons to want and bring about an attitude, but never reasons for the attitude itself — defended not through a metaphysical distinction between reason-types but through the distinctive way such considerations transmit to reasons for other things.
3. Ulrike Heuer's restriction strategy, which retreats to a buck-passing account of reasons for action alone, holding that the WKR problem is generated specifically by extending buck-passing from actions to attitudes.
4. The "fittingness-first" strategy associated with Danielsson and Olson's Brentano-inspired proposal (and, more radically, with Chappell's fittingness-primitivism), which stops analyzing value directly via reason-provision and instead treats a response's being fitting as more basic, so that a wrong-kind reason is one that makes an attitude advisable without making it fitting.
5. A deflationary dissolution of the problem, on which no principled, general right-kind/wrong-kind line can be drawn at all, so that the whole distinction should be abandoned in favor of just distinguishing good reasons from bad ones, with buck-passing correspondingly restricted or given up.
