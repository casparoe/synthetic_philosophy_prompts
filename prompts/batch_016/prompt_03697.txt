In a reading group on free will, a biographical curiosity turned into a real philosophical disagreement among us: Galen Strawson, who argues via his "Basic Argument" that no one can ever be truly or ultimately morally responsible for anything, is the son of P.F. Strawson, whose "Freedom and Resentment" is often treated as the classic naturalistic defense of our responsibility practices -- one that grounds them not in any libertarian, contra-causal freedom but in the reactive attitudes (resentment, gratitude, indignation, and the like) that simply structure how human beings relate to one another. We could not settle whether the son's argument is actually answerable by the father's strategy, or whether it changes the subject in a way that leaves the naturalistic defense untouched.

Please write a dialogue between a philosopher defending the Basic Argument and a philosopher defending the elder Strawson's reactive-attitudes naturalism. The Basic-Argument side should set out the regress problem for self-creation -- why nothing can be causa sui in the sense required for ultimate responsibility -- and should explain why this challenge is more general than the one the elder Strawson was originally answering, since it does not depend on the truth of determinism and is meant to apply even if some of our choices are undetermined. It should also press the reactive-attitudes side on the following point: if we already withdraw reactive attitudes and adopt what the elder Strawson calls the objective attitude toward people whose conduct flows from conditions they never chose -- compulsion, severe psychological abnormality, and so on -- what principled reason is there not to extend the same treatment to every agent, given that everyone's character is ultimately the product of factors they never chose? The reactive-attitudes side should not simply reply that determinism is no threat to the practice, since that is not quite the challenge on the table, and should instead say what is actually wrong with the regress argument or with this generalizing move -- or concede that something in it is right while explaining why our practice of holding people responsible nonetheless survives. Throughout, both sides should engage with each other's specific moves rather than restate their own position, and each should register at least one genuine concession before explaining why that concession does not settle the dispute.
