For a piece I'm reporting on amateur forecasting communities -- the spreadsheet-and-Brier-score culture that has grown up around sites like Metaculus and the old Good Judgment Project -- I sat in on an argument between two of my best sources that I haven't been able to stop turning over. Mara and Deepak have run a two-person forecasting pool together for almost six years, logging predictions on hundreds of questions ranging from ballot measures to drug approvals; when I looked at their spreadsheets, their calibration and Brier scores across that whole span are close enough that neither has any real claim to being the more reliable forecaster. Recently, working from the same public reporting, they independently priced the odds that a pending merger would clear antitrust review: Mara said 20 percent, Deepak said 70. The two walked each other through their full reasoning, and neither could find an actual error in the other's argument. The ruling won't come down for months, so there's no way to check who was actually right. For what it's worth, I'd rather set that aside anyway.

What followed, as I understood it, went like this. (1) Mara said that since their long-run records are essentially identical, they're epistemic peers on this question, and the only defensible move is for both of them to converge on something near the midpoint, because neither has any standing to think the other made the mistake. (2) Deepak said he'd since gone through his model three separate times and still couldn't locate the flaw, and that if "I checked carefully and still can't find an error" counts for nothing, nobody could ever be entitled to trust their own reasoning about anything. (3) Mara answered that this is exactly what it feels like from the inside to be the one who's wrong: a mistaken reasoner rechecking his own work will usually also come up empty. She added that if they were both being fully honest and fully rational, simply knowing each other's numbers should have already closed the gap between them, so the fact that it hasn't is itself evidence that something has gone wrong which no amount of rechecking will surface. (4) Deepak replied that tidy stories about rational people converging once they know each other's views quietly assume things -- shared starting assumptions, unlimited mutual knowledge -- that two actual people never fully have; that if a matched resume were enough to force a big swing toward whoever disagrees with you, you'd owe the same deference to any similarly credentialed stranger who happened to take the other side, which seems to prove too much; and, pointedly, that professional epistemologists have spent two decades arguing over this exact "equal weight" idea without settling it, so by Mara's own reasoning she ought to be hedging her confidence in the very principle she's using against him. (5) Mara said that might be fair, but it's an argument for landing somewhere more moderate than her original 20 percent, not a license for him to sit tight at 70.

I'm not a party to any of this and genuinely can't tell who came out ahead. Could you go through the five moves in order and say, for each one, who has the better of it and why -- not just who comes out ahead overall? I'm especially curious whether Deepak's closing move -- turning Mara's own reasoning back on her -- actually holds up or is more of a clever dodge, and, stepping back from the individual moves, what you think they're both missing about how to handle disagreement with someone you have no principled reason to consider less reliable than yourself.
