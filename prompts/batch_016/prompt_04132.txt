So I'm a phil PhD student working mostly on formal epistemology (Bayesian stuff), but I did my MA thesis on Hume and I can't help dragging him into everything these days, much to my advisor's mild annoyance. Our reading group did van Fraassen's Reflection Principle this week -- from "Belief and the Will" (1984), the diachronic Dutch-book argument for the claim that your current credence in A, conditional on the supposition that your future credence in A will be r, should just equal r. So basically: barring special defeaters, your anticipated future opinion is already rationally binding on you now, as if it were evidence already in hand.

Everyone was going through the standard objections -- the amnesia cases, the "what if I get drugged/brainwashed between now and then" cases, and van Fraassen's kind of ad-hoc-feeling patches for those -- but what I actually got stuck on afterward is a dumber historical question I can't shake: what would Hume make of any of this? His whole picture is that belief is just a lively idea that gets its force transfered to it via custom and association, plus theres the famous bundle-of-perceptions stuff where the self has no real persistence over time. So it's not obvious to me he'd even grant the setup -- Reflection seems to presuppose that "my credence next Tuesday" belongs to someone I have a special rational obligation to defer to, which assumes a pretty robust continuity between me-now and me-then that Hume explicitly denies. Except he's also got that whole distinction between "philosophical" and "unphilosophical" probability from Treatise 1.3.13 -- some belief-shifts track genuine causal/evidential reasoning, others are just custom running on stuff like recency or education or the "general rules" people pick up -- and that distinction honestly sounds like it could do a lot of the same work as van Fraassen's defeater clauses, just for very different reasons.

I keep wanting to throw a version of this into a dissertation footnote, so can you actually work through what Hume's verdict would be, using his real commitments (the custom/association theory of belief, the bundle theory of the self, the philosophical/unphilosophical split) instead of just the generic "well he's an empiricist, he wants evidence" move? Mainly I want to know whether there's a Hume-flavored version of Reflection he could sign onto, and if so, exactly where it would need to depart from van Fraassen's own version.
