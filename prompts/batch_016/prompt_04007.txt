In my school's philosophy club, we've spent the last few meetings on David Hume, and last week one member brought in an article on AI safety that mentioned Nick Bostrom's "orthogonality thesis" — the claim that a system's level of intelligence and the content of its ultimate goals are essentially independent, so that a very intelligent agent could in principle be built around almost any final goal, however arbitrary (the standard illustration being an AI that single-mindedly maximizes the number of paperclips in the universe). Our faculty advisor described this as "basically just Hume for computers," but when we tried to pin down which idea of Hume's she meant, we disagreed. Some of us thought of the is-ought gap, the point that you cannot derive a normative conclusion from purely descriptive premises, while others thought of Hume's separate claim, from the Treatise, that reason is and can only ever be the "slave of the passions," unable by itself to supply or revise our ultimate ends.

I would like your help thinking this through more carefully. First, which of these two Humean ideas, if either, is actually the more relevant point of comparison for the orthogonality thesis, and why? Second, using whichever one you settle on, please work out the comparison in some detail: in what specific ways does Bostrom's thesis just restate the Humean position in new vocabulary, and in what ways, if any, does it go further or commit itself to something that Hume's own argument does not actually establish? Finally, as something of a bonus question, I would be curious whether "instrumental convergence" — the idea that agents with wildly different final goals will nonetheless tend to converge on similar sub-goals, such as self-preservation or the acquisition of resources and power — already falls out of the Humean picture once you accept it, or whether it smuggles in a further assumption that a strict Humean is not automatically entitled to. Please justify each step of your reasoning rather than simply asserting conclusions.
