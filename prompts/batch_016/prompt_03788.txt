I am putting together a graduate seminar on procreative ethics, and this week's session centers on the opening argument of David Benatar's *Better Never to Have Been*. My students grasp the core axiological asymmetry easily enough in the abstract: the presence of pain is bad, the presence of pleasure is good, but the absence of pain is good even when nobody exists to enjoy that good, whereas the absence of pleasure is bad only when somebody exists to be deprived of it. What leaves them unconvinced is Benatar's strategy for defending this asymmetry against the charge that it is simply an ad hoc rationalization of an antecedent antinatalist conviction. He tries to earn the premise by arguing that the same underlying asymmetry is already implicit in four further judgments most of us accept: that we have a duty not to create suffering people but no duty to create happy ones; that a child's prospective suffering counts as a reason against conceiving them while a child's prospective happiness carries comparatively little weight as a reason for conceiving them; that we can retrospectively regret, for the sake of a person we did bring into existence, that we brought them into existence, yet never regret, for the sake of a person we declined to create, that we failed to create them; and that we feel compassion for distant strangers who exist and suffer, but no corresponding sadness on behalf of the merely possible happy people who never came to exist on some remote, uninhabited island.

One of my students pressed a dilemma in discussion that I would like a fuller answer to than I could give on the spot. Either these four supporting asymmetries already presuppose the very axiological asymmetry they are meant to corroborate independently, in which case Benatar's argument is viciously circular, or they can be motivated on genuinely independent grounds, in which case—so the objection runs—they turn out to support only weaker, comparative or agent-centered claims that fall well short of the strong, impersonal claim Benatar needs, namely that coming into existence is always a serious harm to the person who comes into existence. Could you write an essay that takes this dilemma seriously: reconstruct the strongest version of each horn, consider the best replies available to Benatar (drawing on his "Still Better Never to Have Been" reply to critics if it helps), and then actually settle the matter rather than leaving it open? I want a specific, defended verdict on whether the four supporting asymmetries can rescue the axiological asymmetry from the charge of question-begging, not a survey that closes on a diplomatic "there is much to be said on both sides."
