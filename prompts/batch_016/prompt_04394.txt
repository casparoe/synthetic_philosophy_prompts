As a novelist, I am currently drafting a book narrated by a structural engineer who has spent her career calculating exactly how safe her city's bridges are, yet who is overcome by genuine, visceral fear every time she walks across one. I want this fear to be psychologically precise rather than a stock "ironic phobia," and I understand philosophers of emotion treat precisely this kind of case — fear that persists despite the subject's own confident judgment that there is nothing to fear — as a named phenomenon, the "recalcitrant emotion," used to adjudicate between rival theories of what emotions fundamentally are. Could you walk me through, with real technical care, why recalcitrant emotions are considered a serious embarrassment for theories that identify emotions with evaluative judgments, how theories that instead model emotions on perception try to defuse the problem by likening the fear to a persistent visual illusion, and what is supposed to be wrong with that move in turn — along with whatever a bare feeling-based theory would have to say about the case? I would like the actual shape of the debate, not a one-paragraph summary of the three positions.
