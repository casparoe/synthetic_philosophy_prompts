I cover science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and I'm currently working on a piece about how machine-learning researchers invoke "Occam's razor" when discussing Solomonoff induction and Kolmogorov complexity. Wanting to go back to the primary source rather than the textbook slogan, I discovered that the famous tag "Entia non sunt multiplicanda praeter necessitatem" appears nowhere in Ockham's surviving writings; later commentators put it in his mouth. What he did write is narrower, and in one case considerably stranger. Here are three genuine formulations:

From the Summa Logicae, Part I, chapter 12: "Frustra fit per plura quod potest fieri per pauciora" — it is pointless to do with more what can be done with fewer.

From his commentary on Peter Lombard's Sentences, Book I, distinction 27, question 2: "Pluralitas non est ponenda sine necessitate" — plurality is not to be posited without necessity.

And, elsewhere in that same commentary, a fuller version: "Nulla pluralitas est ponenda nisi per rationem vel experientiam vel auctoritatem illius, qui non potest falli nec errare, potest convinci" — no plurality is to be posited unless it can be established by reason, by experience, or by the authority of someone who can neither deceive nor be deceived (Ockham's examples of the last are things like scriptural and ecclesiastical authority).

It's this third formulation I'd like help with. It builds an exception for "infallible authority" into what's usually presented as a purely metaphysical principle of ontological economy. Does that exception clause suggest that "necessity" is doing epistemic work for Ockham — tracking what we're entitled to believe, given our evidence and our authorities — rather than simply tracking what the world contains? And if so, when computer scientists say that Solomonoff induction or Kolmogorov complexity "formalizes" or "vindicates" Occam's razor, are they really capturing anything recognizable as Ockham's own principle, or quietly substituting a different one, on which simplicity means shortest program length relative to some fixed universal machine — itself pinned down only up to an additive constant that depends on which machine you choose, per the invariance theorem? I'd like your help first understanding the passage on its own terms, and then your honest assessment of whether the modern algorithmic story is really a continuation of Ockham's principle or simply borrows its name.
