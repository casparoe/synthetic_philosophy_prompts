so this is something i've been turning over in my head for years without ever taking a philosophy class: why should it matter WHY a person does the right thing, as long as they actually do it? like say a guy always pays his workers fairly — does it change anything if he does it because he genuinely respects them vs he's just scared of lawsuits and bad reviews? my gut says the first version is somehow "more moral" but i could never explain why, since even "respect" or "doing your duty" is still just some feeling pushing you around, not that different from being scared of a lawsuit.

i went looking around online for how philosophers handle this and apparently kant has a whole answer to pretty much this exact worry, not in the famous shopkeeper bit everyone quotes from the Groundwork, but in a denser chapter of his other book, the Critique of Practical Reason, in a part called "Of the Motives of Pure Practical Reason." i found an old translation of the relevant bit and i've read it like six times now and i still can't hold the whole argument in my head at once:

"Pure practical reason only checks selfishness, looking on it as natural and active in us even prior to the moral law, so far as to limit it to the condition of agreement with this law, and then it is called rational self-love. But self-conceit reason strikes down altogether, since all claims to self-esteem which precede agreement with the moral law are vain and unjustifiable, for the certainty of a state of mind that coincides with this law is the first condition of personal worth... But as this law is something positive in itself, namely, the form of an intellectual causality, that is, of freedom, it must be an object of respect; for, by opposing the subjective antagonism of the inclinations, it weakens self-conceit; and since it even breaks down, that is, humiliates, this conceit, it is an object of the highest respect and, consequently, is the foundation of a positive feeling which is not of empirical origin, but is known a priori. Therefore respect for the moral law is a feeling which is produced by an intellectual cause, and this feeling is the only one that we know quite a priori and the necessity of which we can perceive."

and then a bit further down in the same chapter he adds this line which really threw me:

"Thus the respect for the law is not a motive to morality, but is morality itself subjectively considered as a motive, inasmuch as pure practical reason, by rejecting all the rival pretensions of self-love, gives authority to the law, which now alone has influence."

can you break this down for me in plain english? specifically:

1. whats the actual difference hes drawing between plain "self-love" (which is apparently fine, even "rational") and "self-conceit" (which reason supposedly "strikes down" completely)? they sound almost the same to me.
2. that last quote sounds like a word trick — like he's just saying "respect IS morality" instead of "respect causes morality" and calling that an explanation. is there something substantive going on there or is he just relabeling the mystery?
3. does any of this actually solve my original worry, that "acting from duty" is still just acting from a feeling (respect), so how is that fundamentally different from acting from any other feeling? or is he just moving the goalposts by saying this particular feeling is special because reason produced it instead of the world producing it?

be honest even if the answer is that kant doesn't really pull it off here, i'm not trying to defend the guy, i just want to actually understand what he's arguing.
