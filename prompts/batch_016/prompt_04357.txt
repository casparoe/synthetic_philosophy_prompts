I'm a philosophy PhD candidate finishing a dissertation chapter on Frege's construction of "the ancestral" of a relation — the device he introduces in the Begriffsschrift and relies on in the Grundlagen der Arithmetik to give a purely logical definition of natural number, later carried over by Russell and Whitehead into the Principia. Until this year I only knew about Frege's infamous 1924 political diary as a footnote: Michael Dummett's admission, in the preface to his book Frege: Philosophy of Language, that he was "deeply shocked" to discover, while working through the Nachlass, that the man he had revered as coldly rational had spent his last years as a virulent antisemite and enemy of the Weimar republic. With a conference presentation of this very chapter coming up, though, I've been reading further, including Stephen D'Arcy's recent book Frege and Fascism, and what I found there was more specific, and more unsettling, than ordinary biographical embarrassment.

D'Arcy argues that Frege repurposed the same logical apparatus he had illustrated back in 1879 with family-tree examples of descent — essentially, the ancestral — to justify legal privileges for "citizens of Aryan descent," and that he separately drew on his own scruples about vague predicates and sharp conceptual boundaries to worry, as a logician, about how a legally workable definition of Jewishness could ever be drafted. In other words, the claim isn't that a great logician simply happened to hold repugnant views in some separate compartment of his life, but that specific pieces of his technical apparatus were put to work in the service of those views.

When I raised this with my advisor, she waved it off as irrelevant to assessing the logic on its own merits, and most of the Frege scholars I've since consulted agree with her. But other commentators on D'Arcy's work insist the connection is real and specific rather than just borrowed vocabulary dressing up ordinary prejudice, and disagree among themselves about whether it makes Frege's case closer to Heidegger's than to Hume's. I'd like an essay that works through this properly, rather than resting on the easy move of evaluating the ideas and setting the man aside. Please address two questions in turn: first, whether the specific, textually grounded claim that particular pieces of Frege's technical apparatus were themselves pressed into service for his politics is more plausible than the alternative — that this is superficial rhetorical borrowing with no real conceptual continuity; and second, granting whatever answer you reach on the first question, what follows for how someone in my position should now treat, teach, or write about that same apparatus. Don't split the difference for the sake of politeness — tell me what you actually conclude, and defend it.
