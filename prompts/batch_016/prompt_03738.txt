I'm a PhD student in epistemology currently drafting a paper on responses to Kripke's dogmatism paradox, and I'd like your help stress-testing one of my arguments.

The paradox: if I know that h, then I know that any future evidence against h would be misleading, and since I should disregard evidence I know to be misleading, I seem entitled to dismiss counter-evidence to h in advance. Harman's canonical response is that knowledge is defeasible: sufficiently strong counter-evidence encountered later would cost me both my present knowledge of h and my knowledge that such evidence would be misleading, so I'm never actually licensed to ignore evidence I haven't yet met. Sorensen and Hawthorne refine this by arguing that my present knowledge of that conditional is mere "junk knowledge" -- a conditional so unstable that, on learning its antecedent, I should simply drop it rather than infer its consequent.

Sharon and Spectre object that the junk-knowledge move only works in cases structurally like Sorensen's own, where the antecedent (someone reporting contrary evidence to me) cannot become true without my noticing. Where counter-evidence could exist unbeknownst to me, they argue, my "junk knowledge" of the conditional actually depends on a false background belief -- that no such evidence has yet turned up -- which makes it a Gettier case rather than genuine knowledge.

Please construct the strongest argument you can that the junk-knowledge strategy survives Sharon and Spectre's objection, so that Harman's defeasibility solution to the dogmatism paradox can be rescued even for counter-evidence I might never learn about.
