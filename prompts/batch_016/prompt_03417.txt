So a friend of mine who's way deeper into the rationalist/EA blog world than I am linked me this old post (I think it was cross-posted between LessWrong and the EA forum) about "Harsanyi's aggregation theorem." The gist, as I understood it: if every person in a group has well-behaved ("VNM-rational") preferences over gambles, the group's own preferences over those same gambles are also well-behaved in that sense, and the group ends up indifferent between two options whenever literally every individual is indifferent between them, then it turns out the group's overall utility function is forced to be a weighted sum of everybody's individual utility functions. People in the comments were basically treating this as a clean mathematical proof of utilitarianism -- way more rigorous than Bentham or Mill just asserting "greatest good for the greatest number," like Harsanyi did in an afternoon what centuries of moral philosophy couldn't.

That set off my bs detector a little, because in my day job as a software engineer I see people oversell "provably correct" results all the time -- the proof itself is airtight, but its answering a much narrower question then everyone assumes it is. So here's what I'm hoping you can help with: compare Harsanyi's theorem to utilitarianism as the actual, full-blooded ethical theory that people like Bentham, Mill, or Singer defend. Where does the theorem really earn the "utilitarian" label, and where's the gap? Like, does the theorem actually force equal weighting across people or just some weighting, does it say anything about whether "utility" means pleasure vs. preference-satisfaction vs. something else, and does summing everyone's utilities even make sense without smuggling in some extra assumption about comparing utility across different people? Basically, I want to know how much real philosophical work the theorem is doing on its own, versus how much is being read into it after the fact.
