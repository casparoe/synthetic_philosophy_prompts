Random causation thing that's been bugging me – I'm a few years into a metaphysics PhD and I TA the intro causation unit, and a student's question about omissions this term kind of stumped me and I havent fully recovered since. There's this whole literature (Beebee's "Causing and Nothingness," Lewis's "Void and Object," Dowe's stuff on "quasi-causation") that treats causation by omission as some deep standing puzzle for the counterfactual theory of causation, and I genuinely don't get why. Take the standard example: the gardener forgets to water the plant and it dies. Lewis's own analysis says c causes e iff e counterfactually depends on c, and "if the gardener had watered it, it wouldn't have died" is just an ordinary, obviously true counterfactual — structurally no different from "if Suzy hadn't thrown the rock, the bottle wouldn't have shattered." So the omission slots right into the same machinery that already handles regular event causation, no modification needed. Given that, why do people act like omissions are some special problem case requiring a whole extra apparatus (quasi-causation, normative theories of when an absence "counts" as a cause, etc.) rather than just a boring instance of the same counterfactual dependence that does all the other work?

Is there an actual substantive problem here that I'm missing, or is this basically just philosophers being fussy about a case the existing theory already handles fine?
