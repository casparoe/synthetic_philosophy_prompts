Hey, I'm outlining a hard sci-fi novel where two AI civilizations sit in branches of the multiverse that never causally touch each other, they only know about each other because each is powerful enough to simulate the other in detail. One thread of the plot has one side trying to blackmail the other, threatening to torture simulated copies of the others citizens unless they hand over resources. Falling down a research rabbit hole for this, I found this idea called "surrogate goals" (Tobias Baumann and some of the s-risk crowd seem to have started it) — basically, the target of a threat deliberately starts caring a lot about some totally arbitrary decoy thing (doesn't matter what, could be almost anything) so the threatener's leverage get redirected onto that instead of anything the target actually cares about. It's presented as a genuinely new tool for surviving these acausal blackmail situations.

Except the more I read about it the more it just sounds like the old "we don't negotiate with terrorists" logic, or Schelling's bit about winning a game of chicken by tearing your own steering wheel out and chucking it out the window so the other driver can see you literally can't swerve anymore. Both feel like the same basic trick: precommit somehow so that making the threat stops being worth it. So — is "surrogate goals" actually doing something conceptually different from that older, more classical style of deterrence/commitment, or is it basically the same move dressed up in decision-theory language for an AI-safety audience? And does it matter that my two civilizations can only ever interact acausally, via simulating each other, with no actual signal ever passing between them — does that change which of the two strategies would even be available to them? Would love a proper answer I can steal for a character instead of me hand-waving past the hard part.
