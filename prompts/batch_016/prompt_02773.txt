I've been rereading Cicero's *De Senectute* after a rather long argument with some friends from my local rationalist meetup about whether aging should be treated as a disease to be cured rather than a stage of life to be accepted with equanimity. The conversation kept circling back to Bostrom's "Fable of the Dragon-Tyrant," Aubrey de Grey's SENS project, and the general "longevity is an engineering problem, not a metaphysical fact of life" ethos you see across LessWrong and adjacent forums, and one friend pointed out that this stance is almost a mirror image of the position Cicero has Cato the Elder defend. Cato spends most of the dialogue working through the four standard complaints against old age — that it withdraws us from active affairs, weakens the body, deprives us of physical pleasures, and brings us nearer to death — and argues in each case that the underlying good is either still available in another form or was never as valuable as the young assume, before closing with the claim that death itself is nothing to fear, since it is either annihilation (in which case there is no one left to suffer it) or a passage to something better.

I'm curious how you think Cato's actual arguments, rather than a generic "ancient wisdom about accepting mortality" gloss, would hold up against a present-day interlocutor pushing radical life extension. Would he regard SENS-style biotechnology as a legitimate extension of the very active, reason-governed virtue he praises throughout the dialogue — simply letting more people spend more years farming, advising, and mentoring the young the way he does — or would he diagnose the whole longevity project as a symptom of exactly the fear of death he spends the dialogue trying to talk Scipio and Laelius out of? I'd also be interested in whether you think his argument holds together on its own terms, independent of how it fares against modern biotech.
