My grandmother died in early spring, and in the months since, a friend who studies comparative religion steered me toward the Zhuangzi rather than the Stoic consolations everyone else kept recommending. This led me into a small monthly reading group working slowly through Burton Watson's translation, and the text has ended up mattering to me more than I expected a 2,300-year-old collection of dream sequences and animal fables to. At our last session, spent on chapters 2, 6, and 18, two of the more outspoken members of the group, Daniel and Claire, got into a disagreement about what Zhuangzi actually thinks death is, and I haven't been able to settle it since, even after rereading the passages twice on my own.

Daniel's view was that Zhuangzi holds a fairly definite positive thesis: death is simply one more phase in a natural process of transformation, so mourning it is a kind of category error. His main evidence was the scene in which Zhuangzi, caught by Huizi drumming on a tub instead of weeping over his dead wife, explains that she passed from no spirit to spirit, from spirit to body, from body to life, and now from life to death, a sequence he likens to the ordinary succession of the seasons, and concludes that wailing would only show he didn't understand fate. Daniel also pointed to the deathbed scene in "The Great and Venerable Teacher," where the dying Master Lai reasons that whatever force eases him into old age and now lays him to rest in death is the same force that gave him a good life, so he has no more grounds to resent one than the other. Claire pushed back that this quietly upgrades a modest claim into a much stronger one: what Zhuangzi says in his own voice to Huizi only licenses the negative conclusion that grief is a mistake, not the positive claim that death is actually good, or better, or a relief. That stronger claim, she pointed out, is only ever voiced by someone other than Zhuangzi himself: a skull that visits him in a dream and boasts that death beats a king's throne, Master Lai talking himself into equanimity on his own deathbed, or, in the second chapter, another figure who tells the story of a captive woman who wept bitterly at first and later regretted her tears, in order to ask how we can know that the dead don't regret ever having clung to life. Treating the strong claim as simply "Zhuangzi's view," she argued, ignores who is actually saying it. Daniel's reply was that drumming and singing is a real, positive, celebratory act, not mere dry-eyed composure, and that a merely negative conclusion doesn't obviously license it; he also noted that an author who repeatedly, across three separate chapters, hands the more composed and rhetorically victorious lines to the "pro-death" side is doing something authorial, whatever character's mouth he borrows to do it. Claire's rejoinder was that the drumming could just as easily express relief at an emotion loosening its grip as an endorsement of whatever replaces it, that we shouldn't credit Zhuangzi with more than his stated reasons actually support, and that the pattern Daniel cites might show the opposite of what he thinks: that the text keeps stopping just short of letting anyone, including its own protagonist, assert the strong thesis in his own name, as though flagging a gap between what the story makes us feel and what anyone in it actually claims to know.

I'd find it useful to have your read on this, going through the disagreement move by move rather than just declaring an overall winner. Where does each of them have the better textual or philosophical case, and where is each overreaching? I'm also curious whether you think the "transformation" reading and the "skepticism" reading are really as mutually exclusive as Daniel and Claire assume, or whether there's a way of holding both that neither of them considered. Please end with a short paragraph giving your overall sense of whose reconstruction of Zhuangzi's view is more defensible, and what the exchange as a whole is missing.
