I'm putting together a seminar for the spring that pairs classical treatments of weakness of will with the more recent hyperbolic-discounting stuff (Ainslie and that whole picoeconomics literature), the idea being to get students to see what's actually philosophically at stake versus what's just redescription in fancier math. While prepping the Aristotle week I got stuck on a passage from Nicomachean Ethics VII.3 that I've taught probably a dozen times over the years and, if im honest, never fully worked out to my own satisfaction. Brought it up with a colleague over lunch and we just ended up going in circles, so now I'm curious what a totally outside pair of eyes makes of it.

Here's the bit (Ross translation), coming right after Aristotle has distinguished "having knowledge but not using it" from actually exercising it: "...the possession of knowledge in another sense than those just named is something that happens to men; for within the case of having knowledge but not using it we see a difference of state, admitting of the possibility of having knowledge in a sense and yet not having it, as in the instance of a man asleep, mad, or drunk... It is plain, then, that incontinent people must be said to be in a similar condition to men asleep, mad, or drunk. The fact that men use the language that flows from knowledge proves nothing; for even men under the influence of these passions utter scientific proofs and verses of Empedocles... so that we must suppose that the use of language by men in an incontinent state means no more than its utterance by actors on the stage. ... When, then, the universal opinion is present in us forbidding us to taste, and there is also the opinion that 'everything sweet is pleasant', and that 'this is sweet'... the one opinion bids us avoid the object, but appetite leads us towards it... Now, the last premiss both being an opinion about a perceptible object, and being what determines our actions this a man either has not when he is in the state of passion, or has it in the sense in which having knowledge did not mean knowing but only talking, as a drunken man may utter the verses of Empedocles. And because the last term is not universal nor equally an object of scientific knowledge with the universal term, the position that Socrates sought to establish actually seems to result."

What I cant pin down is what's actually supposed to be doing the explanatory work here. Is Aristotle saying the akratic person genuinely lacks the particular premise ("this is bad for me, right now") at the moment of acting -- in which case this looks like real, if temporary, ignorance, not so different in kind from being drunk -- or is he saying they have it, but only "notionally," mouthing it the way a drunk recites Empedocles without it meaning anything to him, in which case I don't see how that's an explanation of weakness of will rather than just a fancier redescription of it. Relatedly: is this passage actually vindicating something like the Socratic thesis that no one acts against knowledge, or is Aristotle quietly conceding that Socrates was wrong while pretending otherwise? My students are going to ask me this in a few weeks and I'd like an actual answer instead of the vague hand-wave I've gotten away with in past years. Bonus question if you're up for it: does this "has-it-but-not-really" structure map onto anything in the hyperbolic discounting picture, where there's no analogous moment of failing to "know" or "perceive" that the tempting option is bad, or is that comparison just a category mistake?
