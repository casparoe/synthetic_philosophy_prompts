On a long drive up to a cabin this past weekend, a friend and I got into a surprisingly heated argument that started from something mundane: a highway app was warning that the mountain pass might close overnight because of snow, and I said something like, "if the pass closes, the valley road is going to be a parking lot tomorrow." My friend, who has a background in formal epistemology, pushed back on how I was thinking about the confidence I should have in that claim. Her position was that how confident I should be in an indicative conditional "if A, C" just is my conditional credence in C given A -- that's not an approximation or a heuristic, she insisted, it's constitutive of what it is to believe a conditional at all. I found this extremely intuitive in the moment (it's certainly how I was reasoning about the pass and the traffic), and only later remembered why I should have pushed back harder: David Lewis apparently proved, back in the 1970s, that this equation -- often called Adams' Thesis or Stalnaker's Thesis -- cannot hold in full generality without forcing something close to absurdity.

The trouble is that I could only gesture vaguely at "something about a triviality result" in the argument, which is not a satisfying way to lose an argument. Since then I've looked at the proof enough to see that the key move involves conditionalizing on the consequent and on its negation separately, and then reassembling things via the law of total probability to derive a contradiction unless the antecedent and consequent turn out to be independent -- but I can follow the algebra without really feeling why this particular maneuver is so lethal, as opposed to, say, some artifact of an unrealistic idealization about probability functions. Could you walk me through the intuition behind the triviality result in a genuinely gut-level way, ideally anchored in a small concrete example with just a handful of possibilities, so that the "aha" of why the equation can't be sustained actually lands, rather than my just being able to repeat that Lewis proved it?

Separately, and more briefly, I'd be curious what you think is the strongest way for someone in my friend's position to hold onto the spirit of her original claim in light of this -- whether that means denying that conditionals express propositions at all, restricting the class of credence functions the equation is required to hold across, or something else entirely -- and how much of the original intuition survives on whichever route you find most promising.
