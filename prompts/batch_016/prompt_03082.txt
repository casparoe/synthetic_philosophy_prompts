So this happened on a backpacking trip with two people from my epistemology seminar and I can't stop turning it over. My friend Nora tested her water filter the morning we left — ran some water through it, tasted fine, said "yeah, I know this thing's still good." About an hour into the hike, Theo mentioned the route got changed last minute and now there's exactly one water source for the whole five days, way past any bailout point, so if the filter's actually bad we're in real trouble. Nora immediately went "wait, no, I don't actually know it's good, I should of tested it with the iodine strips too" — same filter, same morning test, nothing new about the filter itself, just new info about what's riding on it. That night, two people in the group would not let it go. (1) Theo said this is exactly what "pragmatic encroachment" predicts: whether Nora knows the filter works isn't fixed by her evidence alone, because knowing requires the evidence to be good enough to act on, and once the cost of being wrong shot up, the very same evidence stopped clearing the bar. (2) Dana said no, that's contextualism, not encroachment — nothing about Nora's actual epistemic position changed, what changed is that "know" itself picks out a stricter standard once an error-possibility becomes worth taking seriously, same word, different threshold, the same way "empty" means something different for a fridge than for a warehouse. (3) Theo shot back that Nora wasn't in any conversation when it hit her, she was just walking by herself, and the "wait, do I actually know that" feeling showed up before she'd said a word to anyone — so there's no attributor context doing any work, and it's simpler to just say her knowing itself is sensitive to the stakes, full stop, no detour through shifting word-meanings required. (4) Dana admitted the timing thing was a fair hit, but said Theo's fix has its own cost: if knowing can just switch off because of some unrelated fact about the trail, with the filter and Nora's evidence about it held fixed the entire time, then "knowledge" stops looking like a stable state you're in and starts looking like a bet you're willing to place — which is exactly why plenty of epistemologists don't want practical stakes anywhere near the truth conditions for knowing in the first place.

Both of them seemed onto something and also both a little slippery, and nobody backed down. Can you go through the four moves and say who's more right at each step, rather than just handing one of them the overall win? Also curious whether you think Theo and Dana are conflating or missing something about how contextualism, pragmatic encroachment, and the alternatives to both actually fit together.
