In epistemology, two of the standard responses to Keith DeRose's bank cases are epistemic contextualism and pragmatic encroachment (often developed as subject-sensitive or interest-relative invariantism). The basic setup, in the version that has become standard in the literature, features a woman named Hannah who has exactly the same evidence that her bank is open on Saturday in two scenarios: in the low-stakes version we are comfortable saying she "knows" this, while in the high-stakes version, where missing the bank would be disastrous, we become much more reluctant to attribute knowledge to her even though nothing about her evidence has changed. I have been sitting in on a graduate epistemology seminar where both views were presented as rival explanations of this pattern, and on a first pass they can seem like two labels for the same idea, since they agree on the verdict in the textbook version of the case.

What I would like is an explanation that goes past that surface agreement and gets at what actually makes these two views distinct theories with different commitments, rather than a glossary-style contrast of the two labels. Concretely, I would like you to: (1) explain, in terms of the mechanism each view actually posits, where the semantic "action" is happening and relative to whose situation, the attributor's or the subject's; (2) construct or describe a case where the two views come apart and issue different verdicts about whether a knowledge attribution is true, rather than reusing one of the standard cases where they happen to agree; and (3) lay out the strongest objection typically pressed against each side, including the charge that contextualism saddles ordinary speakers with an implausible kind of blindness to the alleged context-sensitivity of "knows," and whatever you take to be the most damaging analogous cost that pragmatic encroachment incurs by abandoning the traditional idea that knowledge is settled purely by truth-relevant factors.

I am not looking for a survey of who defends which position. I would like the kind of explanation that would let someone actually predict, for a novel case, what each theory says and why: the sort of gut-level grasp you would want before trying to adjudicate between the two views yourself.
