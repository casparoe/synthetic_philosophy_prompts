So I've been buried in Bentham's unpublished manuscripts on sexual morality this week, prepping for my history of ethics seminar, and its honestly wild stuff -- way more radical than the "greatest happiness" cliché most people know him for.

Theres the early essay from around 1785 that he titled, in the language of the day, "Offences Against One's Self: Paederasty" (a term that in 18th-century legal usage covered sex between men generally, not the narrower sense it has today) -- he was too nervous to publish it and it sat in a drawer until 1931. Decades later he came back to the topic in a cluster of essays ("Of Sexual Irregularities," "Sextus," "General Idea of Not Paul, But Jesus") that didn't even get a proper scholarly edition until 2014. What strikes me rereading this stuff is how much actual argumentative work Bentham does -- he goes after the "principle of asceticism" and mere "antipathy" by name as illegitimate, non-utility grounds for criminalizing "irregular" sex, insists a harmless act has no business being punished, and explicitly rebuts the standard objections of his day (that homosexuality weakens men, threatens population, undermines marriage). It all connects to his flatly quantitative hedonism too -- no ranking of "higher" and "lower" pleasures, to the point that he's happy to say a game like pushpin is worth exactly as much as poetry if it produces as much pleasure.

Here's the question I want to try out on you, partly as a possible discussion prompt for my seminar (my TA thinks its a gimmicky thing to ask an AI, but I think it actually tests how far Bentham's own principles generalize): if you briefed Bentham on the existence of AI companion apps and sex robots -- machines and chatbots built specifically to be objects of romantic/sexual gratification for a human user -- what would his own utilitarian machinery actually spit out as a verdict? I don't want "he's a hedonist, so pleasure is pleasure" as an answer -- reason it through with his actual tools. Does the fact that the robot, unlike the animals he elsewhere grants real moral standing to on the grounds that they can suffer, isn't itself capable of pleasure or pain make the whole question a non-issue for him -- just another private matter of taste, on par with preferring pushpin to poetry? Or does he have to take seriously some analogue of the "harms real relationships / undermines the population" objection he rebutted in the homosexuality case -- and if he does take it seriously, would he rebut it the very same way, or would he think this time the objection actually has some teeth?
