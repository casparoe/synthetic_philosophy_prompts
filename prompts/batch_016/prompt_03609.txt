Been on a bit of an Embedded Agency kick lately (rereading the old Garrabrant/Demski sequence, including the shorter illustrated version with the little cartoon robot standing inside the maze it's supposed to be reasoning about), and it's made me kind of annoyed at how most AI short fiction handles this stuff. It's always the Asimov thing, or these days the Ex Machina thing, where "the AI" is basically a black box that wakes up conscious or misaligned one day, rather than a story that's actually built out of the specific weirdness in that sequence. Like: an embedded agent can't fit a complete, perfectly detailed model of the universe in its head, because it IS part of that universe and is therefore smaller then it, so any self-model has to be a lossy stand-in that can come apart from the real thing at exactly the worst moment. Or the tiling/self-trust problem, where by Löb's theorem you can never fully certify from inside your own proof system that a future (or child) version of you, reasoning the same way, will only ever prove true things -- so trusting your future self, or building a successor, takes a leap that can't be formally closed. Or the decision-theory end of it, like the 5-and-10 problem or Death-in-Damascus type setups, where once something in your environment can predict your own decision procedure, "just pick whichever option has higher expected value" stops being as well-defined as it sounds.

So here's the actual ask: write me a short story, any genre or setting you like (doesn't need to be far-future sci-fi, and the "agent" doesn't even need to be a literal computer -- could be a person, a bureaucracy, an oracle, whatever), that's trying to do for embedded agency roughly what Permutation City did for personal identity -- i.e. the plot mechanics themselves have to turn on the character(s) being stuck inside the system they're reasoning about, with no clean outside view, rather than that just being flavor text. Whatever bind that puts them in should be what actually produces the ending, not something the story raises and then quietly resolves with a normal plot device. Pick whichever sub-problem you like best (the self-modeling regress, the successor/self-trust one, the predicted-agent decision theory mess, or some mix) -- I'd honestly rather see one of these done well than all three gestured at. Something like 1000-1800 words feels right but don't sweat the exact count.
