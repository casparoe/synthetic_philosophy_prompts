So in my AP Gov class we're on the unit about the Constitution, and my teacher was explaining why Article V makes it so hard to amend (2/3 of Congress AND 3/4 of the states, which basically never happens for anything actually controversial). She called this a "Ulysses contract" the country made with itself — like the country pre-committing itself the same way Ulysses did when he had his crew tie him to the mast and stuff wax in their ears, so future-him couldn't get seduced by the Sirens even though future-him would obviously want to swim toward them. We'd literally just finished that part of the Odyssey in English, so half the room got the reference immediately, and it turned into this whole side argument between two kids in my discussion group, Owen and Farah, that went on way longer than it should of.

I'll number their points so its easier to respond to. (1) Owen said the comparison is kind of a con: Ulysses tied up Ulysses, same guy, but the people who wrote and ratified the Constitution are not us — they're a totally different set of people who've been dead 200+ years — and he brought up that Jefferson apparently wrote Madison a letter arguing one generation has no more right to bind the next one than one country has to bind some completely different country, and that by his math a constitution should basically expire every 19 years. (2) Farah said that's a cute quote but it proves too much, since literally every law that outlasts the people who voted for it would be illegitimate by that logic, and we don't actually think that — we treat "the United States" as one continuous thing even though every single person in it eventually gets swapped out, kind of like how a ship is still "the same ship" even after every plank's been replaced (she used that exact example, which felt like cheating right after we'd just read a whole book about a boat). (3) Owen said ok, but there's still a difference: teenage-him didn't lock in a rule that current-him needs 75% of a jury of total strangers to ever change, whereas he can update his own commitments constantly as he learns new stuff, and the amendment bar is set so high that almost nothing controversial actually gets fixed through it — so calling it "self-binding" is generous when really it's just "extremely hard to undo." (4) Farah's comeback was that this is the entire point and not a bug: if 51% of the country in a bad mood could undo it whenever they felt like it, you'd lose exactly the protection the whole setup was supposed to give you, same as how Ulysses being able to just yell "actually, untie me" the second he heard the singing would've defeated the whole plan. (5) Owen's last word was that fine, maybe that's a good reason for change to be hard, but that's a different argument from "this is the country rationally binding itself the way a person binds themselves," and Farah kept sliding between those two arguments whenever it was convenient for her.
