I'm taking a seminar on formal epistemology this semester, and we recently spent two sessions on the Sleeping Beauty problem: Beauty is put to sleep on Sunday, a fair coin is tossed, and depending on the result she is woken once (Monday, if heads) or twice (Monday and Tuesday, if tails, with an amnesia drug erasing her memory of the Monday waking); unable on any waking to tell what day it is, she must state her credence that the coin landed heads. We covered the standard thirder argument for 1/3 against the halfer's 1/2, and I feel I have a decent grip on that debate.

While preparing a presentation, though, I came across Piccione and Rubinstein's "absent-minded driver" puzzle, and learned that a version of the Sleeping Beauty case actually appears as an example within the very 1997 paper that introduced the driver puzzle -- a few years before Stalnaker coined the name "Sleeping Beauty" and Elga's paper made it famous. The driver's version: a driver passes through two subjectively indistinguishable highway intersections; exiting at the first is worth 0, exiting at the second is worth 4, and driving straight through both is worth 1. Since he cannot tell the intersections apart, any strategy he adopts must assign the same continuation probability p to both. The plan maximizing expected payoff, evaluated before he sets off, is p = 2/3. But Piccione and Rubinstein observed that things look different once he's actually standing at an intersection: reasoning that he's more likely at the first intersection than the second, since the second is only reached by having continued at the first, a fresh calculation from that vantage point seems to favor a different p. They took this mismatch to be a genuine paradox of planning versus action -- a diagnosis Aumann, Hart, and Perry later disputed with their notion of "action-optimal" behavior.

I'd like help working out how seriously to take the parallel between the two cases. First, in what precise sense do they involve the same kind of self-locating uncertainty -- is the driver's belief that he's "more likely at the first intersection" just a version of thirder-style reasoning about relative frequencies of indistinguishable situations? Second, is there a principled disanalogy blocking a full identification of the cases -- specifically, that the driver's own choice of p determines whether the second intersection is ever reached at all, while Beauty's credence has no bearing on whether the Tuesday awakening occurs? Please say directly whether the two puzzles ultimately stand or fall together, so that settling one (say, by accepting thirdism) commits you to an analogous verdict on the other, or whether the disanalogy licenses treating them differently -- and justify your answer.
