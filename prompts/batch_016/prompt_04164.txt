For the past year I've worked as a research analyst at a small foundation that allocates grants across global-health interventions, which means a good part of my week goes into building spreadsheets that rank charities by projected cost-effectiveness — lives saved, or disability-adjusted life-years averted, per dollar. A few weeks ago a visiting researcher led an internal seminar on an objection to consequentialism I'd somehow never properly absorbed despite years of reading in this space: the "cluelessness" problem, as James Lenman first laid it out and Hilary Greaves later refined. I went back and reread both papers afterward, and it has genuinely unsettled how I think about my own job.

What struck me most was Greaves's distinction between "simple" and "complex" cluelessness. Simple cluelessness — the possibility that saving an arbitrary life today might, through some untraceable causal chain, hasten or delay a genocide a thousand years hence — can more or less be shrugged off: we have no more reason to think the unknown effect cuts one way than the other, so by a kind of restricted principle of indifference it nets out to nothing. Complex cluelessness is worse. That's the situation where we have positive reason to think an intervention shifts the far future systematically in some direction — the standard case is that averting child deaths from malaria plausibly increases long-run population size — while also having reason to think that shift could be good or bad along some other axis entirely, with no principled way to weigh the two considerations against one another. It isn't that we lack information the way we lack information about a coin flip; it's that the theory seems to need us to settle something that isn't even well-posed for us to settle, given that our short-term, "visible" reasons for acting are only underwritten, on the consequentialist story, by a long-term "invisible" ledger we have no access to and no real way to estimate. I've caught myself running through our own grant comparisons and wondering whether the confidence we report in our cost-effectiveness numbers is closer to theater than to anything epistemically earned.

I'd like you to write a short story that sits inside this specific problem, rather than the more generic "unintended consequences" or butterfly-effect framing it sometimes gets flattened into. I don't want a story about chaos or fate in the abstract; I'd like one where a character has to act under something closer to Lenman and Greaves's actual picture, where ordinary risk — the kind expected-value reasoning handles perfectly well — is clearly distinguishable, in the texture of the story itself, from this deeper, structural not-knowing that resists being averaged away. You might, for instance, use some device — a narrator with a wider causal vantage point than the protagonist, or a plot that follows one small, well-intended decision through consequences the character never lives to see — to let the reader feel the gap between what the character can honestly justify at the time and what actually follows from it. I'd rather it not resolve into either "so nothing really matters" or "so it all works out in the end"; I would like it to leave the reader with something closer to the vertigo I've been sitting with for the past couple of weeks.
