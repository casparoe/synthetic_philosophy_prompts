I don't have any formal background in economics or political science, but I've spent a good deal of time reading about voting theory on my own, and Arrow's impossibility theorem keeps coming up as though it proves that no electoral system can ever be satisfactory. The more closely I look at how the theorem is actually derived, though, the more its force seems to depend on the "unrestricted domain" assumption: the requirement that a voting rule behave well no matter what preferences the electorate happens to have, however incoherent. Real electorates rarely look like that. People's rankings of candidates or policies usually line up along a single dominant axis, so that each voter has one favorite point and likes the alternatives less the further they sit from it in either direction. As I understand it, once preferences are restricted in this "single-peaked" way, ordinary majority rule stops cycling and even becomes resistant to strategic manipulation. I'd like you to argue for the position that Arrow's theorem, however important formally, is considerably less damning for the design of real-world democratic institutions than it is usually made out to be. Please make the strongest case you can for this, and don't dodge the obvious objection that many real political disputes, especially ones bundling together several distinct issues at once, may not be single-peaked at all.
