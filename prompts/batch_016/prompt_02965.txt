I'm a retired experimental physicist (spent 30-odd years doing low-temperature condensed matter work) who's fallen into philosophy of mind headfirst in retirement, mostly triggered by my daughter gifting me Philip Goff's "Galileo's Error" as a half-joke birthday present. Panpsychism appeals to the scientist in me since it at least tries to fold experience into the basic inventory of physics instead of just declaring it a brain-only phenomenon and moving on. But I keep running into the same wall everyone calls the combination problem: if electrons, or whatever the truly fundamental stuff turns out to be, carry some tiny flicker of experience, how do enough of them wired up right ever add up to the one unified, throbbing headache I have right now, instead of just billions of seperate flickers doing their own thing? Goff's own fix, some special "phenomenal bonding" relation that lets micro-subjects fuse into one macro-subject, never quite sat right with me, and the fashionable alternative, cosmopsychism, just seems to relocate the exact same puzzle in the other direction, into a "decombination problem" about how one cosmic mind splits into me and, say, my dog.

This got me thinking back to the one philosophy course I took as an undergrad, forty-some years ago, where I have a hazy memory of Leibniz's monads and his "mill" thought experiment: you shrink yourself down and walk around inside a machine built to think, like walking through an actual mill, and all you ever find is gears pushing gears, never a perception anywhere. So here's what I'm curious about: if you could actually brief Leibniz on the modern combination problem, phenomenal bonding and cosmopsychist decombination included, what do you think he'd say? Would he see the whole contemporary debate as just backing into his own 300 year old conclusion, that composite things can never be genuine unified subjects no matter how cleverly you bond or split them, so that the real alternative was always his picture of a single simple substance confusedly representing the whole universe from its own point of view? Or is there something in the modern proposals, maybe the idea that bonding or cosmic grounding could count as some new, non-mechanical kind of relation, that just doesn't fit his framework and would force him to actually revise it? I'd like an answer that gets into his specific machinery, windowless monads, pre-established harmony, perception vs. apperception, rather then just the "Leibniz believed in monads" summary I could get anywhere.
