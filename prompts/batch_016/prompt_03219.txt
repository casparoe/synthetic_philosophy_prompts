For the past couple of years I've been teaching myself formal logic in the evenings using whatever open courseware and lecture notes I can find online, since I never had the chance to study philosophy at university. A friend recently sent me an article describing how, among Kurt Gödel's papers, there is a formal proof of God's existence written out in modal logic, and how, decades after his death, a couple of logicians fed the whole argument into automated theorem-proving software to check it step by step. The article made a point of noting that the software confirmed the argument was logically valid, and that the story even made headlines in the German press at the time. My friend and I have been going back and forth about what to make of this ever since.

What I can't get past is that the earlier ontological arguments I had read about, from Anselm through Descartes, always struck me as clever wordplay that fell apart once you looked closely at the assumptions being smuggled in along the way. But once an argument has been translated into strict symbolic logic and checked by a machine that has no stake in the outcome and cannot be swayed by rhetoric, that seems like a fundamentally different and much sturdier kind of result, closer to a proven theorem in mathematics than to an ordinary philosophical argument. So I am left wondering: given that we apparently now have a computer-verified proof of God's existence on record, why do philosophers of religion and logicians keep publishing papers debating whether God exists, as though the question were still open? It feels almost as though the field is simply refusing to update on a settled result, and I would like to understand what I am missing.
