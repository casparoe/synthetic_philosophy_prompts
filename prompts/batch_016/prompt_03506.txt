I'm a retired physicist -- thirty-five-odd years doing condensed matter work before I hung up the lab coat -- and for the last year or so I've been happily lost in early 20th century philosophy of science, mostly the Vienna Circle crowd. Somewhere along the way I learned that Moritz Schlick, before all the verificationism stuff he's famous for, wrote a strange little essay in 1927 called "Vom Sinn des Lebens" ("On the Meaning of Life") that's apparently not very positivist in tone at all -- more like an odd, half-romantic argument that the meaning of life comes down to a certain capacity for play, which he ties to "youth." I got a kick out of finding out Schlick himself started out as a physicist -- doctorate under Max Planck, no less -- before crossing over into philosophy. Since my grad-school German (learned mostly to suffer through old Heisenberg and Sommerfeld papers) is still just about functional, I decided to track down the original pamphlet rather then hunt around for a translation, dictionary at my elbow.

There's a bit where he pivots from artists and poets to scientists specifically, which obviously grabbed me, and I want to make sure I'm actually reading it right and not just seeing what I want to see. The photocopy I'm working from is smudged in two spots, marked below with [...]:

"Solche Tätigkeiten gibt es wirklich. Wir müssen sie folgerichtig Spiel nennen, denn das ist der Name für freies, zweckloses [...] Künstler. Zum Beispiel der Forscher. Auch das Erkennen ist ein reines Spiel des Geistes, das Ringen um wissenschaftliche Wahrheit ist ihm Selbstzweck, ihn freut es, seine Kräfte zu messen an den Rätseln, die die Wirklichkeit ihm aufgibt, ganz unbekümmert um den Nutzen, der irgendwie daraus fließen mag [...]"

My own rough stab at it, for what it's worth: such activities really do exist, and we have to call them "play" accordingly -- that's the name for free, purposeless [...] artist(s) -- the researcher, for instance. Knowing/inquiry too is a pure play of the mind; the struggle for scientific truth is an end in itself for him; he takes pleasure in matching his powers against whatever riddles reality sets him, entirely unconcerned with any usefullness that might somehow come of it.

Could you help me with two things? First, tell me where that translation is too loose or outright wrong -- I'm particularly unsure I'm doing justice to "Selbstzweck" and to "ganz unbekümmert um den Nutzen." Second, and this is what's actually been nagging me: right after "der irgendwie daraus fließen mag," my copy shows the sentence carrying on into what looks like a concessive aside, "(der aber, wie bekannt, ..." -- "(which, however, as is well known, ..." -- before it turns illegible. Given how that clause opens, it sure looks like he's about to concede that real, practical usefulness does reliably flow out of this supposedly "purposeless" scientific play, which, as someone who spent a career doing exactly that kind of research, I'd say is basically correct. But if he grants that, doesn't it undercut the whole point of calling the activity purposeless and self-justifying in the first place? He seems to want it both ways -- science as pure Selbstzweck, and science as quietly, reliably useful. Am I being unfair to him, or is that a genuine soft spot in the argument, and if it's a known one, how do people who work on Schlick usually try to patch it up?
