I'm a retired physicist (condensed-matter experiment, mostly) and somewhat late in life I've fallen hard for philosophy of mathematics. A few old colleagues and I run an informal reading group over video call every couple of weeks, and last session was devoted to the Axiom of Choice -- specifically, whether anyone actually has good reason to believe it's true, as opposed to just finding it convenient. Everyone had there own pet argument for or against, and rather than just going with whoever talked loudest (usually me, if I'm honest), I wrote the six considerations down afterward so I could sit with them properly. Paraphrased, they were:

(a) The Banach-Tarski result -- that choice lets you cut a solid ball into finitely many pieces and reassemble them, using only rotations and translations, into two balls each identical to the original -- strikes some of us as close to an empirical refutation: any axiom with a consequence that physically absurd has to be paying for something.

(b) Someone pointed out that "every vector space has a basis" isn't merely a consequence of choice, it's actually equivalent to it (I gather this is a result of a mathematician named Blass, from the 80's) -- taken by one member as proof that giving up choice means giving up ordinary linear algebra, not just some exotic corner of set theory.

(c) The one constructivist in the group brought up Diaconescu's theorem: that choice, all on its own, forces the law of excluded middle even in a logical setting that doesn't otherwise assume it. His point was that choice isn't an innocent "existence" principle -- it smuggles in the whole of classical logic.

(d) Someone else countered with Cohen's independence result plus a later model (Solovay's, I believe), in which -- granting one mild extra assumption, an inaccessible cardinal -- dependent choice holds, everything mathematicians use day-to-day still works, and yet literally every set of real numbers is Lebesgue measurable. So the paradoxes aren't the price of doing serious math, just the price of full choice specifically.

(e) But then somebody objected that this is too quick, because it's apparently been shown that the Hahn-Banach theorem, which is provably weaker than full choice and which nobody in the room wanted to give up, is already enough by itself to reconstruct both the Banach-Tarski paradox and a non-measurable set. So retreating to a weaker, more "reasonable" choice principle doesn't actually buy you a paradox-free universe.

(f) And finally there was the old Russell's socks-and-shoes point: for infinitely many pairs of shoes you can define a selector with no special axiom at all ("always the left shoe"), but for infinitely many pairs of plain socks, no such rule can, even in principle, be written down. Half the group took this as evidence that choice is a real, substantive assumption we're sneaking in under the table; the other half said it just explains why an axiom is needed in the first place, since we clearly do want to be able to pick a sock from each pair.

I'd like a reasonably neutral outside opinion on this, sorted by how much actual philosophical weight each point deserves in the debate -- regardless of which "side" it happens to sit on -- rather than by how convincing it sounded live on a Saturday morning.
