I've spent a long time reading blogs in the LessWrong/Overcoming Bias cluster, and one idea from that world that has stuck with me is "conservation of expected evidence": roughly, you shouldn't be able to know in advance which direction a future piece of evidence will push your beliefs, because if you were already certain some observation would confirm what you currently believe, you should in some sense be updating on that fact before you've even made the observation. I've been turning this over since jury duty last month, because something that happened there feels related, though I haven't been able to work out whether it's really the same issue or something else entirely.

A friend of mine, Sam, was on the same jury. About two-thirds of the way through the trial, once the prosecution had laid out its case, he told me privately that he was completely convinced of the defendant's guilt. When the defense subsequently called an alibi witness, Sam admitted afterward that he'd barely registered her testimony, reasoning, as he put it, that "I already knew he did it, so whatever she said had to be mistaken or dishonest somehow, even if I couldn't say exactly how." At the time this struck me as an obviously bad way to treat what is supposed to be a juror's central job. But then it occurred to me that I do something with the same shape all the time without a second thought: if someone handed me a long, carefully constructed argument for a flat earth, I also wouldn't feel any obligation to work through it premise by premise, since I already know the earth is round and can be fairly confident in advance that the argument contains an error somewhere, whatever that error turns out to be.

So I'm stuck on what, if anything, actually separates these two cases. Either Sam and I are both reasoning badly, or we're both reasoning perfectly well, or there's a real principle distinguishing them that I haven't managed to put my finger on. Is "I already know X, so I'm entitled to discount whatever comes in against it" ever a legitimate way to reason, and if it is, what exactly would separate its legitimate uses from Sam's?
