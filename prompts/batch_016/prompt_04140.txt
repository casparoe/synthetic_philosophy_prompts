Write a dialogue between two philosophers of biology. One defends Larry Wright's etiological account of function, on which a trait's function is the effect for which it was favored by natural selection -- the effect that explains why the trait is there at all. The other defends Robert Cummins's causal-role account, on which a trait's function is simply whatever it currently contributes to some capacity of the containing system, with no essential reference to history. Have them argue through the Swampman case: a being that coalesces by sheer chance into a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of an existing organism, and so possesses no evolutionary history whatsoever. Does Swampman's heart have the function of pumping blood? Each should press the other on the real cost of their view -- the etiologist on why a missing selective history should matter to whether an organ has a function right now, the causal-role theorist on how their view is supposed to make sense of malfunction, where a trait conspicuously fails to do what we ordinarily say it's supposed to do. Neither should simply concede the point.
