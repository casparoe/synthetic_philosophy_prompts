So this semester I'm taking a 19th century aesthetics seminar and we just spent like two weeks buried in Schopenhauer's philosophy of music (the big section in Book 3 of The World as Will and Representation, plus that supplementary essay on music in vol. 2). I'm also kind of a film score nerd - I play trumpet in my school's film scoring ensemble - so I can't stop mentally translating everything he says into movie-music terms, even tho obviously cinema didn't exist until decades after he died.

Here's what's bugging me: two things he argues seem to point in totally opposite directions once you apply them to film scoring. On one hand he's adamant that "real" music (what later got called absolute music) doesn't imitate the phenomenal world at all, it bypasses representation completely and is a direct copy of the Will itself - which is why he's definately dismissive of imitative music that tries to directly depict specific things (a storm, whatever) instead of expressing feeling itself. That seems like it should make him hate something like Mickey Mousing, where the score tracks onscreen action almost frame by frame. But then there's this other passage (still Book 3 I'm pretty sure) where he describes something basically identical to what we'd now call the "soundtrack effect" - that when music suited to a scene or action is played alongside it, it seems to reveal the scene's secret meaning and works like the most accurate possible commentary on it. Which sounds exactly like what a good film score does for a living. And to make it even messier, the leitmotif technique that's everywhere in modern scoring (Vader's theme, the Fellowship theme, etc) comes pretty directly out of Wagner, who worshipped Schopenhauer - except apparently Schopenhauer personally couldn't stand Wagner's actual music and told him he should've stuck to poetry, since he himself preferred simple Rossini-style tunes.

So genuinely, reconstructing this from his actual commitments rather than vibes: what do you think Schopenhauer would conclude about film scoring as a whole? Would he count it as music finally getting to do, on purpose and at scale, the thing he already admired (directly expressing/commenting on the will behind a scene), or would he see it as a degraded, bastardized use of music - subordinated to drama and imitation the same way he was suspicious of opera being? Extra credit if you can say whether leitmotif-based scoring and mickey-mousing should actually count as the same problem for him or as two separate issues. This is for my seminar paper so I'd genuinely rather have a real argument worked through than a surface-level guess.
