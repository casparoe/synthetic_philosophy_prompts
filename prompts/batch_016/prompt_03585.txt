I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, but over the last year or two I've gotten pretty into amateur forecasting (I even wrote a small script that tracks my own calibration against Manifold and Metaculus questions). A few months ago, a friend and I spent an entire evening arguing about a geopolitical forecast, laid out our full reasoning and evidence to each other, and still ended up several percentage points apart. Someone in our group chat brought up Aumann's agreement theorem and claimed it basically proves that if we were both honest and rational, we couldn't still disagree once we each knew the other's number — so the remaining gap meant one of us had to be irrational, or was quietly holding back evidence. That sent me reading everything I could find on the actual theorem, and the more I read, the more it seemed like almost all the work was being done by an assumption that got stated very casually along the way: the "common prior assumption," the idea that before either of us had seen any evidence at all, we would have assigned exactly the same probabilities to everything. Nobody in the group chat treated that as something needing defense; it was just baked into the setup.

That's the piece I'd like you to take a real position on. Please argue for the claim that the common prior assumption is not actually a requirement of rationality — that two people can be fully rational Bayesians, share very similar life and educational histories, update correctly on every piece of evidence they receive, and still legitimately hold different priors, so that a stable disagreement between them, even once their posteriors are common knowledge, doesn't have to mean either one is irrational or dishonest. I don't want the essay to just gesture at "priors are subjective" and stop there — the obvious comeback, which I think is basically Harsanyi's move, is that any apparent difference in priors is itself just an unacknowledged difference in information or life experience, so that "we simply started out with different priors" is a cop-out rather than an explanation. I'd like you to take that rejoinder seriously and explain why it does or doesn't succeed. It would also help if you addressed whether it even makes sense to speak of a "prior," in the relevant sense, for a real person who has never existed for a single second without some information already shaping their beliefs, as opposed to an idealized agent stipulated for the sake of a proof. Please end with a clear position rather than a "both sides have a point" hedge.
