Fitting-attitude analyses of value—including buck-passing accounts such as Scanlon's—hold that for something to be good just is for there to be reasons to adopt a pro-attitude toward it, such as desire or admiration. These analyses face the wrong kind of reasons problem: sometimes there is a compelling reason to adopt such an attitude toward something that has nothing to do with whether the object actually deserves it, as when an evil demon threatens to torture you unless you admire him, or unless you come to desire some utterly worthless object, such as a saucer of mud. The difficulty for defenders of these analyses is to explain what distinguishes such "wrong kind" reasons from the "right kind" of reasons that make an attitude fitting, without covertly presupposing the very notion of value the analysis was meant to explain.
