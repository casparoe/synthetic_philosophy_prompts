A friend and I spent much of dinner last night arguing about whether Thomas Aquinas would have approved of the July 20, 1944 plot against Hitler, and the question has been nagging at me ever since. She is finishing a thesis with a chapter on medieval political theology, and she kept invoking the Summa's treatment of sedition (II-II, q. 42), where Aquinas argues that because a tyrannical regime is ordered to the ruler's private good rather than the common good, there is nothing seditious about disturbing it — so, in her reading, Stauffenberg and his circle were squarely vindicated. I countered with what I recalled of De Regimine Principum, where Aquinas sounds far more anxious about the whole enterprise: he explicitly warns that allowing private individuals to decide for themselves that a ruler deserves death is more dangerous than the tyranny itself, since it invites ambitious men to brand any inconvenient ruler a "tyrant" and dissolves authority into competing acts of violence. Neither of us could settle how these two strands of his thought are meant to fit together, let alone how they would bear on a case as tangled as Hitler's Germany.

What I would like from you is a genuine attempt to work through this, rather than a survey of "Aquinas opposed tyranny." Two issues in particular remain unresolved for me. First, the scholastic tradition distinguishes a tyrant who is simply a usurper with no legitimate title at all from a ruler who holds legitimate title but governs unconstitutionally, and Hitler is an awkward hybrid: legally appointed Chancellor by Hindenburg, and then hollowing out the Weimar constitution from within via the Reichstag Fire Decree and the Enabling Act. Which category would Aquinas actually place him in, and does the answer change the verdict on the conspirators? Second, the conspirators were not lone assassins; they were general staff officers acting through the chain of command and an existing contingency plan (Valkyrie), intending to use the Reserve Army to reimpose lawful government, at a moment when the German state had no surviving internal check on Hitler at all, and nothing resembling the pope or emperor to whom a medieval subject might have appealed over a wayward king. Does that make them closer to the "public authority" Aquinas insists must act in place of private persons, or would he still have regarded them as private men usurping a judgment that was never theirs to make? I would like you to reason through the primary texts and the tensions between them rather than settling for a tidy verdict.
