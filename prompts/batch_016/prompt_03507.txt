I'm drafting a piece on the limits of expected-value reasoning for a general audience, and before I file it I'd like a rigorous sanity check on its technical core, since this passage is carrying real argumentative weight rather than just supplying color. Here is the section in question:

Most people who have taken a philosophy or decision-theory course have met the St. Petersburg paradox: a casino offers a game in which a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads, paying $2^n$ dollars if the first head appears on toss number n. The expected payout is infinite (1/2·2 + 1/4·4 + 1/8·8 + ... = 1 + 1 + 1 + ...), yet almost no one would pay more than a modest fee to play. The standard response is that money has diminishing marginal utility, so a suitably concave utility function tames the sum into something finite.

There is a quieter, stranger relative of this game that resists that fix altogether. In their 2004 paper "Vexing Expectations," published in Mind, Alan Hájek and Harris Nover proposed the "Pasadena game": once again a fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads, but if the first head appears on toss n, the payoff is (-1)^(n-1) · 2^n/n dollars — so 2, then -2, then 8/3, then -4, then 32/5, alternating in sign and growing in size. No re-description in terms of utility rather than money rescues you here, because the trouble isn't that the expectation is too large. It's that the expectation does not exist.

The reason is instructive. The probability of the first head landing on toss n is (1/2)^n, so each outcome's contribution to the expectation is (1/2)^n · (-1)^(n-1) · 2^n/n = (-1)^(n-1)/n. Summed in the natural order — first toss, then second, then third — this gives the alternating harmonic series, 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which converges to ln 2. But the convergence is conditional, not absolute: the series of absolute values, 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + ..., diverges. By the Riemann rearrangement theorem, the terms of a conditionally convergent series can be reordered to sum to any real number at all, or to diverge to positive or negative infinity, or to oscillate forever without settling down. Because the mathematical definition of expectation is only satisfied by absolutely convergent sums — precisely so that its value cannot depend on the arbitrary order in which a countable set of outcomes happens to be listed — the Pasadena game has no expectation whatsoever: not an infinite one, but none. Philosophers have since floated technical rescues (weak expectations, dominance-based pluralism, relative-expectation theories), but none commands consensus.

This looks like a more radical failure than St. Petersburg's. An infinite expectation at least tells you something. An undefined one tells you nothing: expected-value theory does not merely strain here, it goes silent exactly where we wanted it to speak. And once the trick is visible — calibrating a lottery's payoffs to trace out a conditionally convergent series — it is not obvious why something structurally similar could not, in principle, be built into any decision problem that mixes very-low-probability outcomes of both enormous benefit and enormous harm, which is precisely the territory that expected-value arguments about existential risk and the far future like to occupy. If that is right, some real-world appeals to "the expected value of this intervention is enormous, so we must pursue it" may rest on foundations no firmer than a coin-flipping game invented to make a mathematical point.
