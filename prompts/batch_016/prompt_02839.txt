I am a retired physicist — condensed-matter, mostly low-temperature transport — who, since leaving the laboratory a few years ago, has taken to auditing philosophy courses at the university near where I live, largely for the pleasure of following an argument through to its conclusion. This term's seminar on philosophy of economics devoted a session to Samuelson's revealed-preference approach to consumer theory and the question of what, if anything, "utility" denotes once preference is defined purely in terms of observed choice. Afterward, over coffee, two of the regulars in the seminar fell into a dispute that the rest of us simply watched unfold: Ines, who came to the seminar from an economics background, and Callum, who came from philosophy. I have reconstructed their exchange as faithfully as I can, in the order it happened.

(1) Callum opened by calling the whole apparatus a shell game: define "preferred" as "chosen," define "utility" as a numerical stand-in for that preference ordering, and then, when an economist says a consumer "gained utility" from a trade, all that has actually been said is that she made the trade. No independent fact about her is being tracked, so the theory can never be falsified and explains nothing beyond what was already observed.

(2) Ines answered that this runs together two different things: the stipulative definition of preference, which is indeed just bookkeeping, and the consistency requirements — the Weak and Strong Axioms of Revealed Preference — that actual choices must satisfy across different budgets in order to be representable by any preference ordering whatsoever. Those axioms are violated constantly in real behavior, through framing effects and preference reversals, so confirming them in a given body of choice data is a genuine empirical finding, not a tautology.

(3) Callum granted that the axioms are falsifiable claims about the consistency of choice, but argued that this is a much thinner result than what "utility" is asked to do in welfare economics. Passing the consistency tests shows only that behavior is coherent; it does not show that whatever is being consistently maximized is good for the chooser. A person could be consistently self-destructive, consistently deferential, or consistently a creature of habit, and sail through every axiom — so calling the maximized quantity "utility," in any sense that could support a welfare judgment, is a further and unearned step.

(4) Ines conceded the point as applied to welfare economics, agreeing that economists often slide from the positive claim (choices are consistent) to the normative one (satisfying them is good for the person) without argument. But she pushed back on Callum's opening charge of vacuity: restricted to the purely descriptive project, the fact that real people's choices across countless unrelated situations exhibit this kind of coherence at all is a substantive and unobvious regularity, not something guaranteed by the definitions alone.

(5) Callum's rejoinder invoked Sen's distinction between sympathy and commitment: even the purely descriptive project runs aground on cases where someone chooses against their own ranking of what is best for them, for ethical or social reasons. The only way revealed-preference theory can absorb such choices, he said, is by redefining "preference," after the fact, to match whatever was chosen — which reintroduces exactly the vacuity worry from move (1), and does so precisely for the choices that a theory of wanting most needs to get right.

Neither would concede the larger point, and I lack the technical grounding in choice theory to referee the dispute myself. Would you go through the five moves individually and say who has the better of it at each step, rather than simply declaring an overall winner? I would also be grateful if you would flag anything you think both Ines and Callum are overlooking.
