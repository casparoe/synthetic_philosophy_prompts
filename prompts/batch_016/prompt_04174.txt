I convene a graduate reading group on Nietzsche that meets every other week, and during our session on Ecce Homo two of my students, Marcus and Elena, fell into a disagreement the rest of us never managed to settle. We had just gone through the "Why I Am So Clever" section, where Nietzsche holds forth on his rules of diet, climate, and recreation — his contempt for German cuisine and beer, his claim that a single glass of wine is enough to ruin his day, his insistence on exactly one strong cup of tea and nothing more — all presented as expressions of what he calls an instinct of self-preservation. (1) Marcus argued that this lifelong, near-obsessive management of his own appetites sits badly with Nietzsche's attack on the ascetic ideal in the Genealogy: here is a thinker placing his body under constant surveillance and restriction, which looks a great deal like the self-denying discipline he elsewhere diagnoses as ressentiment turned inward, a will at war with itself. (2) Elena replied that this elides a distinction Nietzsche draws quite explicitly elsewhere, in the discussion of morality as anti-nature in Twilight of the Idols: between castrating or extirpating an instinct, which is what the ascetic priest does out of hatred of life, and merely spiritualizing or disciplining an instinct in the service of a richer, more integrated life, which is what an artist or a philosopher does. On her reading, Nietzsche's dietary vigilance is the second thing — style imposed by strength, not denial imposed by weakness.

(3) Marcus's rejoinder was that this distinction is being asked to do more work than it can bear: read the letters, and the sheer anxious detail of these passages, and what you find is not confident mastery but a man desperately negotiating with migraines and a ruined stomach, which sounds far closer to fear of one's own body than to affirmation of it. (4) Elena answered that this gets Nietzsche's own chronology backwards, since he explicitly presents his cycles of sickness and convalescence as the very source of his insight and as exactly the kind of fate amor fati is supposed to affirm — so vigilance about nutrition is the disciplined management of a fate already loved, not evidence that it hasn't been. Neither would give ground, and I told them I'd put the exchange to someone with no stake in the seminar's internal politics.

Could you work through moves (1) through (4) in order and say, for each, whether it succeeds, fails, or lands somewhere in between, rather than simply declaring an overall winner? I'm especially interested in whether Nietzsche's own texts really supply a principled line between "ascetic self-denial" and "disciplined self-enhancement" robust enough to carry the weight Elena puts on it, or whether Marcus is right that the line collapses once the biographical detail is taken seriously. Please close with a one-line verdict in the form Verdict: Marcus / Verdict: Elena / Verdict: split.
