For most of my working life I was an experimental physicist, and it is only since retiring that I have had the time to read philosophy with any real care, largely through a small group of former colleagues who now meet monthly at the local library to work through one philosopher a year. This year it has been Thomas Hobbes, and one thing that has genuinely surprised me is how much weight geometry bears in his system — far more than Leviathan alone would suggest. Having worked through Douglas Jesseph's account of the long and rather undignified quarrel between Hobbes and John Wallis, I have come to understand that Hobbes regarded geometry, together with his own civil philosophy, as the only genuine science available to human beings, on the grounds that its objects are things we construct ourselves from definitions of our own choosing. This is apparently also why he rejected Euclid's definitions of the point and the line as unfit to found geometry at all, since no partless points or breadthless lines exist among actual bodies, proposing instead that a line simply is whatever path a moving point traces out; and it is why he spent something like the last quarter-century of his life insisting, against Wallis and eventually most of the mathematical establishment, that he had squared the circle, trisected the angle, and duplicated the cube, by constructions the professionals regarded as simply mistaken.

What our group could not settle is what Hobbes would have made of two later upheavals in geometry itself. The first is the nineteenth-century proof, due to Gauss, Bolyai, and Lobachevsky, that denying Euclid's parallel postulate yields a geometry every bit as internally consistent as Euclid's own. The second is Riemann's generalization of that result into the curved geometry that Einstein later used to describe the actual shape of physical space. Both developments seem to threaten something Hobbes needed to be true: that there is, in effect, one geometry, which we construct and thereby know with a maker's certainty, rather than an open family of equally constructible but mutually incompatible geometries whose fit with the physical world could only be judged empirically after the fact — exactly the second-class, merely hypothetical sort of knowledge that Hobbes thought natural science was condemned to, by comparison with geometry's demonstrative certainty.

Write an essay reconstructing, as faithfully as you can to Hobbes's actual arguments in De Corpore and in his exchanges with Wallis, how he would most plausibly have responded to the discovery of non-Euclidean geometry. Could his materialism and his "maker's knowledge" account of certainty have been adapted to accommodate it — for instance, by relativizing geometrical certainty to whichever consistent system we happen to construct — or would the discovery have struck at the very root of his reasons for ranking geometry above the natural sciences? I would rather you reach and defend one specific verdict than simply set out the considerations on both sides.
