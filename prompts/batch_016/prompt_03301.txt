I've been working on a novel that includes an invented late-life dialogue between Borges and a young physicist, and I want Borges's side of the conversation to sound like something the real man could have said, rather than the usual pop-culture flourishes about labyrinths and mirrors. The character I have in mind is thinking along the lines of his essay "A New Refutation of Time" (collected in Other Inquisitions), where he borrows Berkeley's denial of matter and Hume's claim that the self is nothing more than a bundle of successive perceptions, and pushes their logic one step further: if there is no enduring substance and no enduring self, he argues, then even a single perception recurring, whether within one life or across two different lives, would be enough to undo any real distinction between "before" and "after." Tellingly, the essay doesn't end in triumph. Borges admits that the argument cannot actually rescue him from his own situation, closing with the confession that "the world, unfortunately, is real; I, unfortunately, am Borges." I'd also like the character to have Funes somewhere in the back of his mind: the man whose memory is so perfect and so unremittingly particular that he is honestly troubled by the thought that a dog seen in profile a minute ago and the same dog seen face-on just now should be called by one and the same name.

The physicist in my scene has just finished explaining, in lay terms, a real development that postdates Borges's death in 1986 and that he could not have known about: Julian Barbour's 1999 book "The End of Time," which argues that time is not fundamental at all, that physical reality is better modeled as a vast, static landscape of every possible instantaneous configuration of the universe (Barbour calls the whole landscape "Platonia"), with no flow or privileged direction connecting one configuration to the next, and where our sense of a coherent past is generated only because some configurations happen to contain internally consistent "records" of a history, which Barbour calls "time capsules," built into them.

What I actually want from you isn't the dialogue itself but the thinking that should sit behind it. Given what "A New Refutation of Time" actually argues, and the sensibility Borges shows in "Funes," would he have treated Barbour's Platonia as a vindication of his own case against time, delighted that a physicist had arrived independently at the same conclusion, or would he have insisted on a real difference between an epistemic argument that we cannot ground succession in a persisting self, and a physical theory that simply dispenses with succession as a feature of the world? I'd like you to commit to one answer rather than surveying both, and to keep the reasoning tied closely to what these two specific texts say, rather than falling back on the kind of general remarks about "Borges and time" that don't actually require having read either of them.
