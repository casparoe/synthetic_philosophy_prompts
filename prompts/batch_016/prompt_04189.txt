Hey, so this actually happened at a dinner a few weeks back and I haven't been able to shake it. My university just rolled out a new "research productivity" bonus that's basically a formula built on citation counts, and by the second semester people were self-citing nonstop, chopping single papers into three "minimum publishable units," and a couple of people set up informal citation-swaps with folks in other departments. Two old grad-school friends of mine, Nadia (an economist) and Ben (who does ML safety work), got into a whole thing about it over dinner, and it kept escalating. Roughly how it went:

1. Nadia: "This is Goodhart's law, textbook. The moment citations became the target they stopped being a good measure of quality."

2. Ben: "Eh, not really -- that's not Goodhart's law, that's just cheating. Goodhart's is about a statistical regularity quietly collapsing under optimization pressure, no bad faith required. What you're describing is people knowingly exploiting a rule. There's a taxonomy for this now -- regressional, extremal, causal, adversarial -- and what you want is 'adversarial Goodhart' at best, which people in my field would just call specification gaming."

3. Nadia: "Sure, but the original case wasn't so innocent either. Goodhart was writing about the Bank of England's monetary targets, and those targets broke down because banks and borrowers strategically restructured there behavior once a given money measure got singled out as the target. That's agents gaming a rule, same as my colleagues. So the 'pure,' non-strategic version you want to protect isn't even the original case."

4. Ben: "Maybe, but that just shows the original statement was ambiguous, and the taxonomy exists to clean that up. Mix up the noisy-proxy problem with the cheating problem and you'll reach for the wrong fix -- better statistics vs. audits and consequences."

5. Nadia: "You're assuming it's got to be one or the other. Citation counts were always a noisy proxy, gaming or no gaming, so some of this is regressional, some adversarial, and there's no fact of the matter about which Goodhart effect 'really' explains the department's mess."
