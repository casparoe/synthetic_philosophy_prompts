I'm part of my high school's philosophy club (we mostly read primary texts over lunch and argue about them until the bell rings), and for the last several meetings we've been slowly working through Leibniz's Monadology. Around the same time, my psychology class started a short unit on theories of consciousness, during which my teacher mentioned Giulio Tononi's Integrated Information Theory almost in passing. The overlap between the two hit me immediately, and since I'm hoping to study cognitive science or philosophy in college, I haven't been able to stop turning it over since.

The argument from the Monadology that stumped most of the club was the mill argument in section 17, where Leibniz asks us to imagine a machine built to think and perceive, enlarged to the size of a mill so that we could walk around inside it. He insists that no matter how closely we inspected the gears and levers pushing on one another, we would never find anything resembling perception, so perception must belong to a simple, non-composite substance, a monad, rather than to any machine or compound. Integrated Information Theory (IIT), meanwhile, measures consciousness with a quantity called phi, which depends on how much a system's parts causally constrain one another as a single irreducible whole rather than on the function the system happens to compute; its proponents even argue that a digital computer running a flawless behavioral simulation of a human brain would remain conscious to only a negligible degree, since reproducing a brain's function is not the same as reproducing its physical causal structure. That sounds to me like Leibniz's mill, rebuilt out of modern computer science.

What I would like your help with is reconstructing, as carefully as you can, how Leibniz himself would likely have reacted to IIT if he could somehow be briefed on it today. Would he treat phi as the rigorous, mathematical vindication of the mill argument that none of his contemporaries could give him, finally locating something that mere shapes and motions cannot produce, or would he insist that phi is itself just another pattern computed over the arrangement of physical parts, something you could in principle still find while touring the mill, and therefore no closer to the simple substance he thought perception required? Please also draw on some of his less commonly discussed commitments, such as the pre-established harmony among "windowless" monads, his view that even ordinary matter is built from lower monads capable only of dim, unconscious petites perceptions, and his insistence that only genuine unities, not aggregates like a heap or a machine, count as real substances, to work out whether he would be sympathetic or hostile toward IIT's willingness to assign a small but nonzero phi to very simple physical systems, and toward its rule that only one maximally unified structure within any system can be conscious at a time. I would rather you work through the real disanalogies as well as the parallels than simply conclude that Leibniz would obviously have embraced or dismissed IIT, since I'd like to bring a genuinely well-reasoned answer back to the club next week.
