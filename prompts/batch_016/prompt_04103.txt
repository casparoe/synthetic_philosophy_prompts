Retired condensed-matter physicist here — spent about 35 years running a lab and never had much time for philosophy until I retired, and now I'm working through Aristotle's Politics and the Nicomachean Ethics with a little reading group at my local library. Slow going, but I'm enjoying it a lot more then I expected to.

The bit that's been rattling around in my head all week is his account of money. In the Ethics, around 1133a, he says nomisma is only a conventional stand-in for "need" (chreia) and not a natural thing at all — it exists "by law" (nomos), not by nature. Back in the Politics, around 1257a, he tells this story about money arising just because staple goods weren't portable enough for trade, with a stamp eventually added so people wouldn't have to weigh everything out each time. Then in 1258b he goes after usury (tokos — literally something like "offspring" or "birth") specifically, calling it the most unnatural kind of wealth-getting there is, since money's whole job is supposed to be as the neutral middleman in exchange, and interest perverts that by having money directly "breed" more of itself.

Given all that, what do you reckon he'd make of something like bitcoin? What really gets me, as an old physicist, is mining — you're generating brand new units of currency by burning a genuinely enormous amount of real electricity on arbitrary computation, with no exchange between two people happening anywhere near the point the money actually comes into existence. That seems like it could end up even further from "nature," in his sense, than ordinary usury, since usury at least starts out from money doing its normal job before getting misused. Would he even grant that bitcoin counts as nomisma to begin with, given that its "stamp" of legitimacy comes from decentralized computational consensus rather than the kind of civic convention he seems to have in mind? And where would something like DeFi "staking" or "yield farming" — earning a steady interest-like return just for holding coins in the right protocol — land relative to ordinary usury on his scale of naturalness? I'd love an actual reconstruction of the reasoning here, not just a "he'd have hated it, obviously" shrug.
