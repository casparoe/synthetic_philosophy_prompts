I've spent a lot of time over the years reading Robin Hanson's writing on signaling, both on Overcoming Bias and in The Elephant in the Brain, and I've never quite pinned down how much work the biological pedigree of the idea is actually doing. The standard story starts with Zahavi's handicap principle -- the claim that costly, wasteful traits like a peacock's tail are honest signals of quality precisely because only high-quality individuals can afford them -- which Grafen later put on a rigorous game-theoretic footing. But Hanson, and rationalist writing on signaling more generally, tends to move fluidly between that biological story and the general theory of costly-signaling equilibria that shows up independently in economics, especially Spence's job-market model, treating peacock tails, conspicuous consumption, and educational credentials as more or less interchangeable illustrations of "the same" mechanism. I've come to suspect that's a bit too quick, but I can't quite pin down where the analogy strains.

I'd like you to compare the handicap principle with the more general notion of a costly-signaling (separating) equilibrium, and use the comparison to adjudicate that suspicion. Two things I'd especially like you to dig into: first, whether Zahavi's original argument and Grafen's vindication of it really turn on the same formal requirement that drives Spence-style separating equilibria -- what economists call the single-crossing, or Spence-Mirrlees, condition, roughly that the marginal cost of producing a given level of the signal falls as underlying quality rises -- or whether Zahavi's further insistence that a signal be wasteful, not merely differentially costly, is doing independent conceptual work with no real counterpart in the economic case. Second, I'd like you to work through how countersignaling complicates matters in each domain, since it has the highest types signaling less, or not at all, and seems to lean on background assumptions -- like the availability of independent, noisy evidence about type -- that play no role in the basic handicap story.

Once you've worked through those two points, I'd like a direct answer: in what ways, if any, is the handicap principle a special or restricted case of the general signaling story rather than just a biological relabeling of it, and in what ways does it introduce something the economic framework doesn't actually need?
