For an independent project in my high school's philosophy elective, I've been trying to move past the standard "knowledge is justified true belief, then Gettier broke that" narrative and into the more specific literature on why knowledge is actually better than mere true belief — the problem Plato raises in the Meno. Reliabilists answer this by defining knowledge as true belief produced by a reliable process, with reliability supplying the extra value; but Linda Zagzebski's "swamping problem" argues this can't work, using the analogy of a good cup of coffee that isn't made any better by having come from a reliable espresso machine rather than an unreliable one, since once a good product exists, the value of the process that produced it seems to be completely swamped by the value of the product itself. Some virtue epistemologists (e.g., Sosa, Greco) try to block this by treating knowledge not as a reliably-caused product but as a creditable cognitive achievement, on the idea that a success which occurs because of skill carries its own final value, the way we value a skilled archer's bullseye differently from a lucky one even though the arrow lands in the same place. Could you write an essay that explains why the swamping problem is a genuine problem for simple reliabilism, tests whether the "achievement" move actually escapes the argument or just relocates it, and then closes by defending an actual verdict on whether the value problem has been solved, rather than leaving the question open?
