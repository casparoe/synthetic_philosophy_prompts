I'm a physics PhD student (numerical relativity, mostly binary black hole merger simulations) auditing a philosophy of science seminar this term to knock out a breadth requirement, and this week's reading was Earman and Norton's hole argument paper. The construction itself was easy enough to follow: take a solution of the field equations, pick a matter-free "hole" region H, apply a diffeomorphism that's the identity everywhere outside H but smoothly different inside it, and drag the metric along by it. You get a second solution that agrees with the first everywhere outside H but disagrees inside. Per the professor, and I think per the paper too, the moral is supposed to be that GR is indeterministic: fixing the fields on a Cauchy surface, or everywhere outside the hole, doesn't fix what happens inside it.

Here's my problem: that seems to flatly contradict something my whole dissertation leans on, namely that the Einstein equations have a well-posed initial value formulation. When we evolve puncture data forward through a merger we get exactly one waveform out, not several equally good but different candidates that some extra ingredient has to arbitrate between. So either decades of numerical relativity have quietly been relying on some hidden piece of structure to break a real tie inside the hole that nobody ever mentions in the NR literature, or "indeterminism" means something different to Earman and Norton then it does when I say my code converges to a unique solution. Assuming it's the latter, can you actually pin down what's doing the work there, rather than just waving at "gauge freedom" — concretely, why doesn't whatever indeterminism the hole argument is pointing at ever show up as an actual ambiguity in a solvable Cauchy problem? I tried asking my advisor and he just said "that's a philosophy problem" and went back to his coffee, so thanks for nothing there.
