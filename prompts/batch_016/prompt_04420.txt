I keep running into this idea under the "embedded agency" umbrella about "tiling agents" and something called the Lobian obstacle, and I feel like I keep circling it without ever actually landing on it. From what I can piece together, the setup is: you've got an AI that wants to build, or upgrade into, a smarter successor, and the "safe" way to do that is to only let the successor take an action once it has actually proven that the action is safe. Sounds reasonable enough. But apparently there's a mathematical reason the original AI can never fully trust that "the successor proved it's safe" actually means the action IS safe, even when the successor runs on the exact same logic and rules as the original does. Every writeup just says "this is because of Lob's theorem, a cousin of Godel's incompleteness theorem," like that's supposed to click, and then jumps straight into box-and-diamond notation that I bounce right off of.

What I don't get on a gut level is why this specifically breaks down when an agent is trying to trust a copy of its own reasoning, as opposed to, say, two independent systems double-checking each other, or the agent just sandbox-testing the successor a thousand times and seeing what happens. People trust other people, and even trust there future selves, without any formal proof of anything, so why would proof-based self-trust be uniquely cursed here instead of just difficult? Is this an actual dead end for building AI that can safely improve itself, or is it more of a "mathematicians being fussy about an edge case" type of problem?
