I was an experimental physicist for 40 years, and now that I'm retired I've been reading my way through philosophy of biology for fun. Got stuck lately on the handicap principle -- Zahavi's claim that showy, costly traits like peacock tails are honest signals of quality because they're wasteful, so only a truly high-quality male can afford one. Every popular account tells the same story: Zahavi got mocked for years until Alan Grafen came along and vindicated him with a rigorous ESS model in 1990.

Being an ex-physicist I couldn't resist working through Grafen's actual argument instead of just trusting the summary, and what it really requires is just that low-quality signallers face steeper marginal costs than high-quality ones -- nothing in it demands that the signal be wasteful in Zahavis stronger sense, costly over and above whatever's needed to keep it differentially costly. That looks like a noticably weaker claim than what Zahavi originally argued for, closer to Spence's old job-market signalling model in economics than the "handicap" story as usually told.

So: did Grafen's maths actually vindicate the handicap principle, or did everyone just decide it had been vindicated because impressive-looking maths turned up? Write me an essay that weighs both readings properly and then actually commits to one -- don't just conclude "it's complicated."
