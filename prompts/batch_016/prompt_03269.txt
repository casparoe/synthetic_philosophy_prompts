For the last several months I've been chasing a rabbit hole that started, of all places, from an argument with a friend about whether her elderly cat is really "gentle," given that it has never once been provoked enough to show whether it would scratch anyone. Trying to work out what a claim like that could even mean sent me into reading about how philosophers analyze dispositions in general, and I landed in the middle of a technical debate I hadn't known existed: the argument over "finks," "masks," and "mimics" as counterexamples to the natural idea that having a disposition just amounts to a conditional being true of you (a glass is fragile, on this view, just in case it would break if struck). I have no formal training in philosophy, but I've now spent a lot of evenings working through as many of the primary papers and summaries as I could get my hands on, enough to have a decent feel for the classic cases: the wire hooked up to a device that flips it from dead to live the instant someone touches it, the fragile glass whose disposition to break is "masked" by protective packing, and the styrofoam dish that isn't fragile at all but still gets torn apart because someone standing nearby who can't stand the sound it makes reacts the moment it's struck.

What I haven't been able to settle for myself is which of the proposed fixes on offer actually deserves to be taken seriously, rather than just relabelling the problem. As best I can reconstruct the debate, the live options are roughly: (1) deny there's a genuine counterexample at all, by insisting the packed glass or finked wire simply has a different, more narrowly specified disposition than the one in question (it's disposed to break when struck, but only in the absence of interference) — a move that strikes me as a little too convenient; (2) David Lewis's "reformed" conditional analysis, from a paper I think dates to the late 1990s, which requires that the object retain, for long enough, an intrinsic property that together with the stimulus would actually go on to cause the manifestation; (3) simply bolting a "ceteris paribus" or "under normal conditions" clause onto the plain conditional, even though I gather this risks collapsing into something trivially true; (4) Manley and Wasserman's move away from a strict would-if test toward saying that something is disposed to do X in circumstances C just in case it does X in a sufficiently high proportion of C-cases; and (5) giving up on reducing dispositions to anything else at all, and simply treating dispositionality, or causal power, as a basic and unanalyzable feature of at least some properties, along the lines of the "powers" ontologies defended by people like Molnar and Bird.
