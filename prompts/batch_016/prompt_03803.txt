Condorcet's name is attached to two landmark results in social choice theory: the Jury Theorem, which shows that if each member of a group is independently more likely than not to judge correctly, majority voting becomes almost certainly correct as the group grows large; and the voting paradox that bears his name, in which pairwise majority voting over three or more options can produce a cyclical, seemingly irrational collective ranking even though every voter's own preferences are perfectly transitive. Two centuries later, theorists of law and social choice identified a third, formally distinct pathology of majority voting, now known as the discursive dilemma (or doctrinal paradox). Suppose a three-judge court must rule on liability for breach of contract, where liability holds if and only if there was a valid contract and the defendant breached it. Judge 1 affirms both the contract and the breach; Judge 2 affirms the breach but denies the contract; Judge 3 affirms the contract but denies the breach. Each judge's verdict is internally consistent, a majority affirms the contract, and a majority affirms the breach — yet a majority also denies liability, since only Judge 1 affirms it. Voting premise-by-premise and then applying the rule yields one collective verdict; voting on the verdict directly yields the opposite one; and no cyclical majority appears anywhere in the example.

Condorcet was not just a theorist of this kind of aggregation but a practical designer of it, having chaired the committee that drew up an elaborate constitution and electoral system for revolutionary France. Given his actual mathematical assumptions and his Enlightenment confidence in reasoned collective judgment, how do you think he would have reacted to being shown the discursive dilemma? Would he have recognized it as a variant of a problem he already understood, or would it have exposed something his framework had no resources to address — and can you see any way he might have tried to resolve it without abandoning the spirit of the Jury Theorem?
