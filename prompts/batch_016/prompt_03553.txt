For the past few months I've spent a fair amount of my free time on a rabbit hole in the philosophy of language, which started when my debate coach off-handedly mentioned that conditional claims like "if we pass this resolution, unemployment will fall" are more philosophically loaded than they look. That comment sent me searching, and I eventually came across an idea due to the philosopher Ernest Adams: that the right "probability" to assign a sentence like "if A, then C" is simply the conditional probability P(C|A), rather than the probability of some standalone proposition. This felt intuitive to me. My confidence in "if this card is a face card, it's a king" should track P(king given face card), which is 1/3, not the probability of the associated "material conditional" version of that sentence, which comes out far higher because it's automatically satisfied by every non-face card as well.

Here is where I got stuck. Robert Stalnaker wanted conditionals to be genuine propositions, true at certain possible worlds, and he conjectured that this same equation — probability of the conditional equals the conditional probability — should hold quite generally, even when conditionals are nested inside other conditionals. David Lewis then proved, in 1976, that this generalized version is fatal: given a fairly innocuous-looking assumption about how the conditional probabilities of nested conditionals ought to factor, the probability of "if A, then C" is forced to collapse to the plain, unconditional probability of C, as long as A doesn't already settle C's truth one way or the other. I gather there are now several variants of this "triviality result," due to Lewis and later writers, all with basically the same moral. I can follow the individual algebraic steps of the original proof — something about splitting on C and not-C and invoking that factoring assumption twice — but I don't have a real feel for why the conclusion has to be so drastic rather than some milder restriction.

Could you help me build actual intuition for this, rather than just re-deriving the steps? I'm especially curious what's being smuggled in when we demand that the same probability equation hold not just for someone's current credences, but for every probability function they might come to have after learning something new. And, stepping back, do you think Lewis's result should be read as showing that everyday "if...then" sentences don't behave like ordinary declarative statements at all — that they aren't really in the business of expressing propositions in the first place?
