The Ramsey rule splits the social discount rate into "pure time preference" (ρ -- discounting future wellbeing just because it's later) and a growth term (ηg -- discounting because future people will probably be richer). Ramsey himself thought positive ρ was "ethically indefensible," and there's a similar Sidgwick/Rawls-style argument that mere distance in time isn't a rational ground for caring less about someone's welfare. But ρ clearly does real work in practice -- its a big part of why the Stern Review (ρ≈0.1%, justified basically only by extinction risk) got such different climate policy conclusions than Nordhaus's models (ρ more like 2-3%). Write me an essay that actually takes a stand: can a positive pure rate of time preference ever be ethically defensible, or should it be zero (extinction risk aside)? Weigh the best arguments on both sides -- temporal neutrality, but also stuff like agent-relative partiality and the fact that nobody's actual savings behavior looks like ρ=0 -- and land on a real verdict, not a "reasonable people disagree" cop-out.
