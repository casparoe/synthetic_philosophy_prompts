In my epistemology seminar this week, we discussed Jennifer Lackey's "selfless assertion" cases as a challenge to Williamson's knowledge norm of assertion. I understand why her example of Stella — a devoutly Christian fourth-grade teacher who, despite not believing it herself, asserts to her class that modern humans evolved from Homo erectus because she regards it as her professional duty to teach whatever the evidence best supports — creates obvious trouble for any norm that requires belief. Since knowledge is standardly taken to entail belief, it seems like this same case should refute the knowledge norm just as directly, via the same line of argument. Yet much of the literature treats resisting Lackey's argument as a distinct, ongoing project specifically for defenders of the knowledge norm. Could you explain what maneuvering room the knowledge norm has here that the belief norm lacks — for instance, by reconceiving what "knowledge" requires, or by denying that Stella's utterance is a genuine assertion at all — and say which of these responses, if any, you find most plausible?
