I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'd like to commission a short story to run alongside an upcoming feature on functionalism in the philosophy of mind. Functionalism holds that what makes a state mental is the causal role it plays, not the material that plays it -- a view whose great selling point, multiple realizability, is that a mind needn't be made of neurons at all. Critics have long treated that same feature as the theory's weak point.

In 1978 the philosopher Ned Block gave the worry its most vivid form in a thought experiment now sometimes called the Chinese Nation, or China Brain. Reorganize the population of China so that each citizen plays the role of a single neuron, relaying signals to a fixed set of others by two-way radio in exactly the pattern of an actual brain's firing, with the whole arrangement wired to a body that supplies it with sensory inputs and behavioral outputs. If the functional organization is right, functionalism seems committed to saying the resulting system has beliefs, desires, even felt experience -- a verdict Block intended as a reductio, though plenty of functionalists since have argued that our intuitions about who can be conscious are exactly what shouldn't be trusted here.

Write a short story, roughly 800 to 1,200 words, set inside this scenario or a close variant of it, from the perspective of someone embedded within the system -- one relay among hundreds of millions. Avoid turning it into a dramatized lecture: let the question of whether the whole adds up to a someone surface through what your character notices, suspects, or can never confirm, and let the story earn its ending rather than simply announce a verdict on the debate.
