I've spent a good chunk of the past few weeks working through Scott Garrabrant's "Geometric Rationality" sequence on LessWrong, partly prompted by an argument with a friend who works in decision theory and was pretty dismissive of it as sequence-writing dressed up in equations. I want to write a short post that either steelmans the central thesis properly or concedes she's basically right, and before putting anything public-facing out I'd like a skeptical read from someone not already primed to like the framework. What follows is working notes rather than finished prose — I care more about whether the argument holds together than about how it reads.

The core thesis, as I understand it: Kelly betting says to size a bet to maximize the expected logarithm of your wealth rather than its expected value, and the payoff is that over a long enough run of repeated bets you end up, almost surely, richer than someone following almost any other fixed staking strategy, even though on any single round the Kelly bet isn't what a plain expected-value maximizer would choose. Garrabrant's move is to argue that Nash bargaining is the same trick wearing a different coat: the Nash Bargaining Solution picks whichever outcome maximizes the product of each party's utility gain over their disagreement point, rather than the sum of those gains, and his gloss on why that's the right thing to maximize is that a Nash bargain is what falls out if you treat the different parties as different "selves" you might have turned out to be, and maximize your expected utility geometrically — rather than arithmetically — across that uncertainty. If that's basically right, maximizing the geometric expectation (informally, the exponential of the expected log of some quantity) deserves to stand as a rationality norm in its own right, on a par with ordinary expected-value maximization, rather than as two unrelated tricks that happen to both involve logarithms.

Two things stop me from just believing this, pulling in opposite directions:

1. The cost side. Von Neumann and Morgenstern get you ordinary expected-utility maximization from four fairly innocuous-looking axioms, and it's a clean, checkable fact that an expected-utility maximizer can never strictly prefer a coin flip between two options to both of those options individually — a weighted average can't exceed the larger of the two things being averaged. Garrabrant is upfront that geometrically rational agents violate exactly this: a Nash bargain between two parties with opposed preferences really can beat either pure option for both of them. That's not a footnote, it's a rejection of the independence axiom, and I haven't seen anyone spell out why we should pay that price outside the two showcase cases.

2. The thing that almost talks me back into it. Geometric expectation looks close to immune to Pascal's-mugging-style reasoning: a tiny-probability, giant-payoff scenario can dominate an arithmetic expectation, but it can only move a geometric one by something like the logarithm of the payoff. That feels like it's diagnosing what actually goes wrong with unbounded expected-value reasoning, rather than patching it by fiat.

The part I'm least sure of, and the main thing I want pushback on, is an extension I can't stop poking at. It's tempting to take "Nash bargaining is geometric maximization over uncertainty about which self you are" and run the same move across people instead of across possible selves — aggregating welfare across a population, or across competing moral theories under moral uncertainty, via something like a Nash social welfare function (a product of utility gains) instead of a utilitarian sum, on the grounds that it behaves like a built-in priority for the worst-off: any option leaving one party's gain near zero drags the whole product toward zero, however well everyone else does. My first instinct was that this fails for a boring reason: a von Neumann–Morgenstern utility function is only defined up to an arbitrary positive affine transformation, so "the product of everyone's utility" isn't even well-defined without a privileged zero. But then I noticed that the two-person Nash Bargaining Solution is provably invariant to each party's own rescaling anyway — multiply one party's whole utility function by a positive constant and the arg-maximizing outcome doesn't move, because the disagreement point gets rescaled right along with it. So maybe the real obstacle isn't a missing zero at all, but a missing disagreement point: an actual bargaining game hands you a principled reference outcome for free, and I don't know what's supposed to play that role once you're doing population-level welfare aggregation instead of literal bargaining. I gather there's already academic work applying bargaining theory to moral uncertainty, but I haven't been able to tell whether it resolves this problem or just relocates it.

I'd like comments on three things in particular: whether I've got the Kelly betting, Von Neumann–Morgenstern, and Nash bargaining claims technically right; whether "it's all the same trick" is genuinely illuminating or whether I'm eliding real disanalogies between the repeated-betting case and the bargaining case; and, most of all, whether my diagnosis of what does or doesn't go wrong in the population-ethics extension is on the right track. I'd rather be told exactly where this is confused than reassured that it's basically fine.
