I'm writing a piece on how philosophers formalize the idea of rational self-trust, and two principles keep turning up as though they were designed to solve the same problem from opposite ends. Bas van Fraassen's Reflection Principle holds that a rational agent's current credence in a proposition, conditional on her expecting to assign that same proposition credence r at some later time, must already equal r. David Lewis's Principal Principle holds instead that her credence should track the known objective chance of the proposition, subject to an "admissible evidence" clause. Some philosophers (Wolfgang Spohn among them) have argued that the Principal Principle is really just a special case of Reflection, with chance playing the role of an idealized future opinion one is obliged to defer to. Before I present that as a tidy unification in the piece, I'd like your assessment: does the reduction actually hold up, or does it obscure a real difference between deferring to one's own anticipated opinion and deferring to an objective feature of the world? And of the two principles, which strikes you as the more defensible constraint on rational belief, given that Reflection is notorious for counterexamples involving anticipated memory loss or foreseeable lapses into irrationality? Please justify your answer.
