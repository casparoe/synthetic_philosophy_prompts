I'm the co-captain of my high school's game theory and economics club, and a couple of meetings ago our faculty advisor introduced us to the centipede game: two players alternate turns, each choosing either to "take" a growing pot of money (ending the game immediately with an unequal split in their own favor) or to "pass" it along, after which the pot grows and it becomes the other player's turn. Backward induction reasons from the last possible move backward: the final player should obviously take, so the second-to-last player should anticipate that and take instead of passing, and so on back to the very first move — meaning the whole game supposedly "unravels" into the first player taking almost nothing, even though both players could have done far better by passing a few times. What really caught my attention was when our advisor mentioned that Robert Aumann has a well-known 1995 proof that "common knowledge of rationality" among the players formally forces exactly this outcome, and that other game theorists, including Ken Binmore, published direct replies arguing that the proof doesn't establish what it looks like it establishes, because it seems to require making sense of a player's rationality at a point in the game that rationality itself guarantees will never be reached.

I've since tried reading a bit more about this on my own (partly because I noticed that when the centipede game is actually run in experiments, almost nobody takes on the very first move, which seems like its own separate puzzle), but I'm still not sure I can state precisely what the disagreement is even about. Is the worry that "rationality" isn't a coherent property to assign to a player at a node that, by hypothesis, will never be reached? Is it that common knowledge of rationality can't survive an unexpected deviation without becoming inconsistent, so the whole notion is either vacuous off the equilibrium path or requires some account of how players revise their beliefs mid-game? Or is Aumann's proof simply correct on its own terms, with the objections resting on smuggling in a more everyday, looser sense of "rational" than the one he actually defines?

Could you write me an essay that actually resolves this for me, rather than just cataloguing the positions? Specifically, I would like it to (1) reconstruct, as precisely as possible in plain language, why common knowledge of rationality is supposed to force the backward-induction outcome, (2) lay out the strongest version of the objection(s) to this, and (3) tell me which side you think is actually right, and why, instead of concluding that "reasonable people disagree." If your honest answer is more nuanced than a flat "Aumann is correct" or "Aumann is mistaken" — for instance, a verdict that depends on exactly how "rationality" or "common knowledge" is defined — that is fine, as long as you commit to it and defend it, since I would like an actual position to bring back to my club rather than a survey of the literature.
