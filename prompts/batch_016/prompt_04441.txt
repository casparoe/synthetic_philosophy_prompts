I'm a PhD student in a cognitive science program that's half old-school philosophy of mind, half computational modeling, and our interpretability reading group has been pairing classic philosophy-of-mind texts with recent mech interp papers. This week's pairing — Ramsey's receptor-vs-structural-representation distinction from *Representation Reconsidered*, alongside Anthropic's Golden Gate Claude write-up and Neel Nanda's Othello-GPT post — kicked off an argument between two people in my cohort that I haven't been able to drop. Quick refresher in case its useful: Ramsey's "job description challenge" says positing a representation only earns its keep if it does explanatory work beyond describing something as a mere causal mediator — a bimetallic strip in a thermostat reliably covaries with temperature and causes the furnace to kick on, but Ramsey says that's just a receptor, not a representation; something only counts as a genuine ("structural") representation if it's used "off-line," the way a cognitive map is structurally isomorphic to terrain and can guide you toward a place that isn't currently in front of you. He originally wrote the book to argue that connectionist networks mostly only have receptors, so pairing him with 2024 interpretability work felt like the obvious next move for the group.

Ben (the more ML-inclined one) argued the Golden Gate Bridge feature clears Ramsey's bar: clamping it doesn't just make the model say "bridge" more, it makes Claude self-identify as the bridge across totally different prompts, languages, even images, which shows the feature is being used in a generalizing way, not merely triggered like a detector. Mira (more of a philosophy person) said generalization is exactly what a really good receptor looks like from the outside, and that Ramsey's whole argument against the "but it's used downstream" move is that it proves too much — by that logic the thermostat's strip represents room temperature too, since the furnace "uses" its output. Ben switched examples to Othello-GPT: the network reads off an internal board-state direction to compute legal moves on positions it never literally saw in training, which sounds like exactly the structural, off-line use Ramsey demands. Mira granted that's her hardest case, but pointed out that the "structure" only showed up once Nanda switched the board encoding from absolute black/white to a "mine vs. theirs" (relative to the current player) scheme — a linear probe on the original encoding basically failed — which to her proves the isomorphism is something researchers go hunting for once they already know the target structure (an 8x8 board) in advance; for something fuzzy like "deception," we have no independent grip on a target structure to check against at all. Ben's comeback was that finding the right basis for a real pattern isn't the same as inventing one — you can fail to notice points lie on a line until you rotate your axes — and that if Mira's bar for "independently characterizing the target structure" were applied to people, we'd have to say humans don't structurally represent deception or bridges either, which seems like a bad result for her view, not his.

Neither of them budged and it definately derailed the rest of the meeting.
