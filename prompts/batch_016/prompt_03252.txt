I've been working on a piece about early drug interventions after trauma, for a feature on the fuzzy edges of medical ethics -- specifically the still-pretty-experimental practice of giving ER patients propanolol shortly after something like a car crash or an assault, on the theory that it blunts the adrenaline surge that helps memories "burn in," and so lowers the odds the thing calcifies into full-blown PTSD later. I mentioned it at a dinner last weekend thinking it was just an interesting bit of trivia, and it accidentally set off a whole argument between two friends of mine, Marcus (an ER resident) and Nadia (finishing a philosophy PhD), that went on long enough I ended up taking notes on my phone under the table.

Roughly how it went, in order: (1) Marcus said this is obviously just preventative therapy, no different in kind from a rabies shot after a bite -- you intervene early to stop a real disorder from developing later, that's medicine, not enhancement. (2) Nadia pushed back that, unlike rabies, most people who live through something awful never develop PTSD at all -- she threw out a stat that its something like 1 in 10 car-crash survivors versus more like 6 in 10 rape survivors, so it swings a lot by trauma type -- meaning you'd be dosing a lot of people who were always going to be fine, on the off chance a few wouldn't be. (3) Marcus answered that medicine does that "treat many to spare few" thing constantly -- tetanus shots, prophylactic antibiotics after certain bites -- and nobody calls those enhancement just because most recipients didn't strictly need them. (4) Nadia said the disanalogy is that how intensely you remember a terrible event isn't a neutral biological fact like a wound, it's bound up with who you are and how you make sense of your own life, and that the President's Council on Bioethics raised almost exactly this objection about memory-blunting drugs in a 2003 report. (5) Marcus replied that this proves too much, since grief counselling and EMDR also change how intensely people feel about bad memories, and nobody thinks those threaten anyone's identity, so "authenticity" isn't doing any real work as an objection. (6) Nadia's comeback was that talk therapy works through your own reflective engagement with the memory, so you're still the author of the outcome either way, whereas a pill acts before the memory has even fully consolidated and bypasses you altogether -- though she admitted that alone can't be the whole story, or sedation during a colonoscopy would count as an identity threat too.

Neither of them budged, and I honestly went back and forth all night myself. Could you go through the six moves in order and say who's got the better of it at each one, not just an overall winner? And if you think there's some obvious consideration both of them are skating past that would actually move the debate forward, I'd want to hear that too.
