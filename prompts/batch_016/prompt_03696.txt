As someone who writes about science and ideas for a general audience, I like to think I have decent instincts about probability and evidence — which is probably why a wrinkle in my own reporting process has been nagging at me for weeks. I'm working on a feature about a corporate safety scandal, and the central factual question — whether the company's executives knew about a known product defect before it shipped — is one that not a single source will answer with a flat yes or no. Everyone hedges. A former compliance officer told me early on that she was "fairly confident, maybe 70 percent" that a warning memo had reached the general counsel's desk before launch. Weeks later, and quite independently, an engineer who had worked on the product told me he "couldn't swear to it, but would guess 60-40" that the defect had come up in a meeting the CEO personally attended. Neither source would commit to anything firmer than that.

When I sat down to work out, in my own notes, how convinced I should now be that the executives knew, I noticed something that has bothered me ever since. If I mentally rerun the process as though I'd heard the engineer's account first and the compliance officer's second — the reverse of how it actually happened — I seem to land on a noticeably different overall view than the one I actually hold. That strikes me as troubling, because my instinct is that once you have the same two pieces of testimony in hand, what you end up believing shouldn't depend on which source happened to call first, only on what you now know in total. I even tested that instinct against a simpler version of the same situation: if both sources had instead stated their claims as flat, certain facts, I'm confident the order in which I heard them would have made no difference at all to my final view. It's only once every piece of evidence is hedged and provisional, rather than certain, that the sequence seems to start mattering.

I mentioned this to an editor, half-joking that I must just be bad at doing the math in my head, but the longer I've sat with it, the less sure I am that it's a mere error on my part. So I'd like to put the question to you directly: when none of your evidence ever amounts to outright certainty, is it actually rational for the order in which you acquire it to change your final view, even though you end up holding the exact same total evidence either way? And if that really can be rational, what, if anything, is wrong with my "certain evidence" test case above, where the order so obviously didn't matter?
