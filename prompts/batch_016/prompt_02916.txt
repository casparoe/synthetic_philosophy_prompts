i've been going down a rabbit hole on Keynes lately, not the economics stuff he's famous for, but this essay where he predicts machines and compound interest will basically solve the "economic problem" within a hundred years, and the real challenge left will be figuring out how to actually live well once nobody has to grind for money anymore. he flat out calls loving money for its own sake a kind of mental illness, the sort of thing you'd hand over with a shudder to a psychiatrist. turns out this whole outlook came out of him and his Bloomsbury friends being obsessed with G.E. Moore's philosophy, where the only things worth chasing for their own sake are love and beauty and that kind of inner-life stuff, and Keynes apparently loathed what he called the "Benthamite calculus," calling it a worm that had been eating away at civilization from the inside for 200 years. so heres what i keep wondering: what would he make of effective altruism today, the earn-to-give crowd running the numbers on lives saved per dollar donated? would he see that as Bentham's calculus reborn with better math, or as a genuinely decent answer to the purposeless-leisure problem he was so worried about? i want your actual best guess at his reasoning, not just a one liner.
