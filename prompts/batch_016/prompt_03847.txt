I'm researching a piece on the semantics of conditionals, and I keep seeing Lewis's 1976 triviality result summarized as a flat refutation of the idea that "if A, then B" can carry a probability equal to P(B|A). What these summaries gloss over is that Lewis's proof needs an easy-to-miss extra premise — that the conditional connective means the same thing regardless of which credence function is evaluating it — and that Lewis himself later conceded, more cautiously, that a context-sensitive reading of "if" would slip past his own argument. Hájek's later cardinality argument, by contrast, reaches a similarly damaging conclusion — that in any sufficiently rich finite probability space, there are more achievable conditional-probability values than there are propositions available to serve as their referents — without apparently needing that premise at all. Could you explain, in intuitive rather than fully formal terms, where exactly the no-context-sensitivity assumption is doing its work in Lewis's proof, and how the cardinality argument manages to sidestep it? I'd like to actually understand the mechanics well enough to describe them accurately, not just repeat the headline conclusion.
