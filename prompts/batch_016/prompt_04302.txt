I spent most of my working life as an experimental physicist studying spin glasses and other disordered systems, which means I cut my teeth on "broken ergodicity" decades before I ever opened a philosophy book. Now that I'm retired, a small reading group of fellow retired scientists — assembled, somewhat against my will, by a former colleague — has gotten me interested in decision theory, and we've spent several recent sessions on a dispute I still can't settle to my own satisfaction. On one side is the lineage running from Kelly's 1956 information-theoretic paper on optimal bet sizing, through Ed Thorp's card-counting and hedge-fund work, to today's "ergodicity economics" school, which argues that an agent facing repeated multiplicative bets should maximize the time-average growth rate of wealth rather than its expectation, precisely because wealth under such dynamics is a genuinely non-ergodic quantity. I gather this same "geometric" logic has recently been generalized well past gambling, into things like Nash bargaining and the pooling of forecasters' probabilities, under the banner of "geometric rationality."

On the other side stands the older, and still dominant, expected-utility tradition, whose most entertaining defender is Paul Samuelson, who once wrote an entire paper attacking mean-log-of-wealth enthusiasts using nothing but words of one syllable, and whose intellectual descendants today point out that professional bettors and traders tend to stake only a fraction of the full Kelly amount — which looks very much like an admission that log utility was never mandatory in the first place, just one risk preference among many that happens to be mathematically convenient.

Could you write me a dialogue between two equally sharp characters holding these two positions? Set it at a betting table where both have been truthfully told that a coin is weighted 60/40 in favor of heads, handed a modest bankroll, and given many rounds in which to wager before the game ends — the sort of setup used in real experiments where quantitatively trained subjects were given a biased coin and actual money, and a startling fraction still went bust. I'd rather not see a rehearsal of the elementary case for the Kelly fraction that any textbook supplies; I want the geometric-rationality character pressed on whether non-ergodicity is doing genuine normative work or merely relabeling risk aversion after the fact, and I want the expected-utility character pressed, with equal force, on whether treating fractional-Kelly practice as evidence of legitimate risk preference isn't just a refusal to notice that full Kelly was tracking something the utility-function story leaves out. Please don't let either of them win too easily.
