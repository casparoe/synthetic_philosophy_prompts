I'm taking a philosophy of mathematics seminar this term, and this week's unit covers the foundational crisis in mathematics around the turn of the twentieth century — the paradoxes that turned up in Cantor's and Frege's work, and Hilbert's program for putting arithmetic on a secure footing. For our response paper we were assigned a close reading of part of Hilbert's 1900 Paris address, the section where he lays out his second problem, and after going over it with my study group a couple of times, I still feel like I'm missing the actual philosophical move he's making in the last paragraph. I was hoping you could help me work through it before I have to turn in my write-up.

Here is the passage, from Mary Frances Winston Newson's 1902 translation in the Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society:

> But above all I wish to designate the following as the most important among the numerous questions which can be asked with regard to the axioms: To prove that they are not contradictory, that is, that a definite number of logical steps based upon them can never lead to contradictory results.
>
> In geometry, the proof of the compatibility of the axioms can be effected by constructing a suitable field of numbers, such that analogous relations between the numbers of this field correspond to the geometrical axioms. Any contradiction in the deductions from the geometrical axioms must thereupon be recognizable in the arithmetic of this field of numbers. In this way the desired proof for the compatibility of the geometrical axioms is made to depend upon the theorem of the compatibility of the arithmetical axioms.
>
> On the other hand a direct method is needed for the proof of the compatibility of the arithmetical axioms. The axioms of arithmetic are essentially nothing else than the known rules of calculation, with the addition of the axiom of continuity. I recently collected them and in so doing replaced the axiom of continuity by two simpler axioms, namely, the well-known axiom of Archimedes, and a new axiom essentially as follows: that numbers form a system of things which is capable of no further extension, as long as all the other axioms hold (axiom of completeness). I am convinced that it must be possible to find a direct proof for the compatibility of the arithmetical axioms, by means of a careful study and suitable modification of the known methods of reasoning in the theory of irrational numbers.
>
> To show the significance of the problem from another point of view, I add the following observation: If contradictory attributes be assigned to a concept, I say, that mathematically the concept does not exist. So, for example, a real number whose square is -1 does not exist mathematically. But if it can be proved that the attributes assigned to the concept can never lead to a contradiction by the application of a finite number of logical processes, I say that the mathematical existence of the concept (for example, of a number or a function which satisfies certain conditions) is thereby proved. In the case before us, where we are concerned with the axioms of real numbers in arithmetic, the proof of the compatibility of the axioms is at the same time the proof of the mathematical existence of the complete system of real numbers or of the continuum.

Two things about this are giving me trouble. First, that last move — quietly identifying "is not contradictory" with "mathematically exists" — seems to me like a substantive philosophical thesis rather than something Hilbert is entitled to just assert in passing, and I can't tell whether he thinks existence just *is* consistency (so that talk of mathematical objects "existing" reduces to a claim about a formal system having a model or being contradiction-free), or whether he means something narrower. Could you spell out what's actually driving that identification, and what would be lost or gained by rejecting it? Second, I don't follow why he treats the arithmetic case as needing a fundamentally different, "direct" kind of proof from the geometric case he just described — geometry gets its compatibility proof by being modeled inside arithmetic, so why doesn't arithmetic just get the analogous treatment by being modeled inside something else? And since we've also covered Gödel later in the syllabus: could you say precisely which part of the hope expressed in that last paragraph the second incompleteness theorem actually closes off, versus what — if anything — a result like Gentzen's later consistency proof is normally taken to salvage? I'd rather understand the exact shape of the result than get handed the usual one-line "Gödel refuted Hilbert."
