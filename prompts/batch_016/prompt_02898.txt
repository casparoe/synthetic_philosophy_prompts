I teach a graduate seminar on philosophy of space and time, and this term, after we worked through the Leibniz–Clarke correspondence and the relevant chapters of Sklar's *Space, Time, and Spacetime*, several students came away with the impression that the substantivalism/relationism dispute was more or less settled somewhere in the eighteenth century. I'd like a piece of supplementary dialogue I can hand out that shows the debate has actually moved along with the physics, rather than offering one more rehearsal of the Newton–Leibniz exchange.

Please write a dialogue between a relationist and a substantivalist who are examining the spinning bucket together. The relationist should grant that Leibniz's own shift arguments — which use the identity of indiscernibles to deny that a universe translated or reflected in space would differ from our own — don't really touch the bucket case, since those arguments concern absolute position rather than the dynamical effects of rotation; she should instead reconstruct something closer to Mach's proposal that the water's concavity tracks rotation relative to the bulk of cosmic matter, leaning on frame-dragging (the Lense–Thirring effect) as at least suggestive empirical support for thinking that distant matter bears on local inertial structure. The substantivalist should not be defending Newton's container-like absolute space, but something closer to "sophisticated substantivalism," on which it is the spacetime metric field itself, rather than the fixed stars, that fixes facts about rotation; he should press the relationist with cases that seem to resist a Machian analysis, such as a lone rotating body in an otherwise matter-free universe, or Gödel's rotating cosmological models, where the very idea of "rotation relative to the bulk of matter" arguably breaks down. Both characters should be equally sophisticated and should respond to the specific point just made rather than simply restating a prepared position — for instance, the substantivalist may eventually have to face the hole argument's threat to substantivalism's compatibility with determinism, while the relationist has to explain why the metric field isn't itself just a disguised relational structure — and neither should be allowed to win by default. Something like two pages of natural back-and-forth would be ideal, written so that a reader who already knows this material would find the exchange substantive rather than a rehash of the textbook story.
