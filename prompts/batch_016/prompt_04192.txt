A friend and I spent a long dinner arguing about why promises are binding in the first place, and neither of us walked away convinced. The dispute turned on a case I posed to her: suppose that, alone at my grandmother's bedside in her final hours, I promise her I will scatter her ashes off a stretch of coastline she loved. She dies shortly afterward. No one else ever learns the promise was made, so keeping or breaking it cannot affect anyone's trust in me, or in promising as a practice; and since she no longer exists, she cannot be disappointed, form residual expectations, or be harmed by any reliance she placed on me. My friend insisted it would still obviously be wrong for me to quietly skip the trip, and took this to show that Scanlon's contractualist account of promising -- on which the wrongness of breaking a promise consists in manipulating expectations you intentionally created and abusing the trust this invites -- cannot be the whole story, since there is no one left whose expectations or trust are at stake.

I pushed back that the obvious alternative fares no better. Hume's view that promise-keeping is a convention we are bound by once society has adopted it also grounds the obligation in a system of mutual expectations: his argument is that the convention is worth everyone's adherence because it stabilizes cooperation, and a single unwitnessed, unrepeatable promise does not feed into that system in any way that keeping it would preserve or breaking it would damage. So we each ended up convinced the other's preferred theory failed the case, without either of us having anything better to put in its place.

I would like a more careful comparison than we could manage ourselves. Could you lay out what Hume's conventionalist account and Scanlon's contractualist account (his "Principle of Fidelity," or Principle F) are each actually committed to, beyond the simplified versions we were trading over dinner, and then apply both to the grandmother case? Specifically: does either genuinely explain the residual obligation on its own terms, or does explaining it require quietly importing some further resource -- a natural, non-conventional duty, say, or facts about what she would have wanted -- that the theory is not entitled to? If one account clearly comes out ahead, say which and why; and if you think the case does not actually discriminate between them as cleanly as we assumed, I would like to hear that too.
