Random rabbit hole from this weekend: I knew Sidgwick as the utilitarian guy with the "dualism of practical reason" problem at the end of the Methods of Ethics (the bit where he admits he can't rationally adjudicate between egoism and universal hedonism without some further postulate about the universe being set up to reward virtue), but I had no idea he spent like thirty years of his life doing psychical research -- co-founding the Society for Psychical Research in 1882, sitting through seances, and being part of the circle that personally caught several mediums cheating (including his own wife Eleanor, who was one of the investigators). Apparently this wasn't just a rich Victorian academic's odd hobby, it was tied pretty directly to the ethics: the idea seems to have been that if you could get solid empirical evidence that consciousness survives death, or that there's some kind of moral governance over the universe, that would supply the missing premise needed to rescue practical reason from incoherence. And then, from what I can tell, by the end of his life he'd basically concluded the whole research program hadn't given him what he needed and had been close to a waste of time, even though he never fully let go of it.

This is bugging me because it seems like a genuinely wierd combination of extreme intellectual rigor (the guy was famous for being almost pathologically careful and self-doubting, and it was his own circle that exposed the fraud in some of these cases) and something that looks, from the outside, a lot like wishful thinking about the one question he couldn't bear to leave unresolved. So I want an actual essay out of you on this, not a summary of his biography. Lay out the strongest case that turning to psychical research was a rational, even admirable, extension of his philosophical method, then lay out the strongest case that it was really a rationalization -- a sign that something had gone wrong either in the ethical theory itself or in how Sidgwick handled the fallout from it -- and then actually tell me what you think. Did the dualism of practical reason ever have a real shot at being resolved this way, and does the fact that his research came up empty tell us anything about whether that dualism is a permanent feature of ethics, or just a problem with how he personally tried to escape it? Don't hedge into a wishy-washy "good points on both sides" ending -- commit to a verdict.
