Ok so this is a bit of a rabbit hole but bear with me. I'm a backend engineer and got interested in probabilistic programming a few months back, which somehow led me down a Bayesian-reasoning blog wormhole and eventually to this old David Lewis paper from the 70s about "indicative conditionals" (basically "if A then B" statements) and something called a triviality result. As I understand it (and I could be butchering this), the intuitive idea that the probability of "if A then B" should just equal the conditional probability P(B|A) -- like, the probability that we'll ship today given the build passes should just equal P(ship | build passes) -- turns out to be impossible to maintain in general, without your whole probability assignment collapsing into somthing degenerate. Lewis apparently proved this rigorously and it kicked off decades of arguing about what to do with it.

From what I can tell there's two very different camps that came out of this: people who still want "if A then B" to be an ordinary statement that's true or false (like Stalnaker, with his possible-worlds semantics), who have to find some clever way of stopping Lewis's proof from applying to their theory, and people who basically bit the bullet and said fine, indicative conditionals just aren't the kind of thing that has a truth value at all -- believing a conditional is more like adopting a supposition and reasoning under it, not judging some proposition to be probably true (I think this is the Adams/Edgington line, but correct me if I'm wrong).

Could you write me a dialogue between one philosopher from each camp? I want them arguing face to face about a specific case -- feel free to use my build/ship example or invent your own -- where they each have to explain why their view gets it right and the other one's doesn't. Don't strawman either side: the truth-conditions person needs a genuinely good move for escaping the triviality result, not just ignoring it, and the no-truth-value person has to actually defend why giving up on truth conditions for something as basic as "if...then" isn't crazy. I want them responding to each other's specific points back and forth, like an actual argument, not two monologues stapled together. Doesn't need to be super long, just long enough to actually get into the weeds of it.
