I'm writing a scene for a novel in which two philosophy professors -- old friends who've spent thirty years needling each other -- finally have it out at a faculty dinner over the surprise examination paradox: a teacher promises a test next week whose day the students cannot predict in advance, and a student argues, by backward elimination, that no such test could possibly occur. I want the disagreement staged along a real fault line in the literature, not a straw fight.

One professor takes a broadly Quinean line: the student's elimination argument is a legitimate reductio of the claim that the student knows the announcement is true, not of the announcement itself, so nothing rules out a genuine surprise on any day, including the last. The other holds something closer to Sorensen's "blindspot" solution, and thinks that move is too cheap -- it doesn't explain why the very same announcement becomes unknowable to the student alone on the last possible day while staying perfectly knowable to a colleague of the teacher's, or why a perfectly rational student who forgets nothing and gains no new evidence can still lose knowledge of something true.

Give me the actual back-and-forth between them, dialogue tags and all, not a summary of the two positions. Each should press the other on the real soft spot in their view: the Quinean ought to answer for whether the solution smuggles in an implausible blanket skepticism about all future contingents, and the blindspot theorist ought to answer for whether positing a whole new category of unknowable truths buys any explanatory power that the plainer distinction doesn't already supply. They've had this argument before and know each other's moves -- let it actually land blows on both sides, and don't let either one come out the clear winner.
