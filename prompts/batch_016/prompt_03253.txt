I'm a philosophy PhD student working on the foundations of decision theory, and this term I've been sitting in on my university's graduate microeconomics sequence to shore up my formal background. We recently covered revealed preference theory in its classic Samuelsonian form: if a consumer chooses bundle A when bundle B was also affordable, A counts as "revealed preferred" to B, and, provided the choices satisfy the usual consistency conditions (WARP, which suffices when there are only two goods, and SARP once there are more), this revealed-preference ordering can always be represented by a utility function. A few of us were hashing this out after class, partly prompted by a philosophy of economics seminar where Sen's work on rational choice had come up, and we talked ourselves into what seemed like an inescapable conclusion: since utility, on this construction, is nothing over and above whatever ordering rationalizes a person's actual choices, there is no further, independent fact about a person's well-being that a choice could possibly fail to track. Consistent choice just is utility maximization, by definition.

If that's right, though, I'm not sure what to make of the vast literature on addiction, akrasia, and self-control — the smoker who lights up while sincerely wishing they wouldn't, say. Since the only way choice can fail to maximize utility, on the revealed-preference picture, is through outright inconsistency (a WARP or SARP violation), it seems to follow that as long as the smoker's choices are consistent over time, economic theory has no principled basis for calling the smoking "irrational" or "against their own interest" — that would just be smuggling in an external standard of welfare that revealed preference theory was designed to do without. Given how foundational this notion of utility is to welfare economics, how do economists working in that tradition still speak of self-defeating, exploitative, or manipulated choices at all?
