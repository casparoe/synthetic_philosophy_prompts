In my formal epistemology seminar, we've been working through the "Hypoxia" case that recurs throughout the higher-order evidence literature: a pilot works out from her instruments that she has enough fuel to reach her destination — call this conclusion G. She then notices she is flying at an altitude where hypoxia is common, and recalls that pilots who perform that same calculation while hypoxic reach the wrong answer about half the time, all while feeling perfectly lucid. The readings motivate the puzzle by appeal to the non-akrasia constraint: roughly, that it is irrational to believe P while also believing that your evidence does not support P. Since the pilot's higher-order evidence makes the belief "my evidence probably doesn't support G" available to her, this seems to show that non-akrasia alone requires her, and anyone in a relevantly similar situation, to lower her confidence in G to match. I want to build my term paper around this as a general result — that the non-akrasia constraint by itself grounds a policy of deferring to higher-order evidence about one's own reliability — so I want to work out the details of the implied rule: should the credence shift always track the cited reliability statistic exactly, or is there a more general formula for this in the literature?
