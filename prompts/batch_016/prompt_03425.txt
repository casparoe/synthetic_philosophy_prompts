I'm several drafts into a novel whose secondary plot follows a young logician, Clara, who is finishing a dissertation on Gottlob Frege—revered in my story, as he was by real admirers such as Michael Dummett, as perhaps the most rigorously rational mind of his era—only to stumble upon the surviving fragments of Frege's 1924 political diary. In the novel this discovery is meant to precipitate something like a small crisis for Clara: the diary, as it happens, reveals Frege in the last year of his life as a bitter opponent of parliamentary democracy and universal suffrage, and as an antisemite who thought Jews should be stripped of political rights and, ideally, expelled from Germany altogether—views apparently unsettling enough that the editors responsible for his posthumous papers kept the material out of the published Nachlass for decades, and that left even Dummett, on finally reading it, admitting to having been badly shaken in his admiration for a man he had considered almost purely rational.

Before I put reasoning in Clara's mouth, I'd like to work through the question properly myself, rather than lean on the easy "brilliant thinker, terrible man" cliché. So here is what I'd like your help with: compare, in some philosophical depth, the sensibility on display in the diary with the substantive commitments of Frege's mature philosophy—his anti-psychologism, his insistence in essays like "The Thought" on a mind-independent "third realm" of objective, timeless thoughts, his logicist ambition to ground arithmetic in pure logic, and his broader hostility toward relativism about truth and validity. Is there a real, traceable continuity between the craving for necessary, objective truths that animates his logic and the craving for political and racial certainties on display in the diary, such that the diary genuinely bears on how we ought to read the philosophy? Or is that connection actually rather illusory—more a novelist's tidy irony than a sound inference—with the case instead illustrating a broader and more unsettling puzzle about how little technical rigor in one domain guarantees about a thinker's character in another? I would like your own considered position, argued rather than merely asserted, since this is exactly the sort of interpretive judgment Clara would have had to work through herself.
