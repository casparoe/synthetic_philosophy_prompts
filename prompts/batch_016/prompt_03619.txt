I spent almost forty years as a physicist before I retired, and these days I've been slowly working through a pile of decision theory and philosophy books I always meant to get around to. A few weeks back I hit I.J. Good's old theorem on the value of information, and it's been rattling around in my head since. The gist, as I understand it: if your a Bayesian trying to maximize expected utility, and someone offers you a free look at some relevant evidence before you have to act, you should always take it -- at worst it changes nothing, since you can just go do whatever you would've done anyway, and at best it makes you switch to something better, so your expected payoff from looking can never be lower then your expected payoff from not looking. It's airtight as math, but I've been wondering how far it actually reaches once real human psychology is involved, and a dinner party a couple weeks ago handed me a live test case.

Some friends of ours, a married couple, had done one of those mail-in ancestry-and-health DNA kits together for fun. Turns out the company doesn't just show you the Alzheimer's-risk result (the APOE4 variant) -- you have to click through an extra warning screen to see it. He clicked through right away. She thought about it for a minute and decided not to, and still hasn't. Over dessert this turned into a real argument, roughly in this order. (1) He said her decision doesn't hold up: the result is already sitting there computed, looking costs her nothing, and if it's bad news she can just carry on acting exactly as she would have anyway -- so there's no way she ends up worse off by looking. (2) She said "acting like you don't know" isn't a move you actually get to make once you know something -- you can't unknow it -- and since there's no proven way to lower your Alzheimer's risk either way, looking would just be pure dread with nothing on the other side of the ledger. (3) He came back with: she can't know in advance that nothing hangs on it -- if the result's bad, that's precisely when you'd want to lock in long-term-care insurance while you're still healthy on paper, or stop postponing the trips they keep saying they'll take "someday," so there's real decision-relevant value she'd be throwing away by refusing to look. (4) She said that even granting that, she watched her own uncle get tested years back, and the result didn't change a single thing he actually did -- he got the same insurance and took the same trips regardless -- while it hollowed out his mood for a decade before symptoms could even show up, and that if you count that hollowing-out as a real cost, then "the value of information is never negative" quietly stops being true.

Neither of them gave an inch, and I've been going back and forth on it myself ever since. Could you go through the four moves one at a time (labeling each 1 through 4) and say who's got the better of it at each one, rather than just handing down an overall winner? I'm especially curious how far Good's theorem actually reaches here -- whether it settles things the way he thinks it does, whether she's onto a genuine limit on it, or whether they're both misapplying it in ways I haven't spotted -- and I'd like a short closing paragraph on anything you think both of them are missing.
