In my English class, we just finished reading The Adventures of Huckleberry Finn, and we spent a whole period discussing the moment when Huck decides not to turn Jim in to the men hunting for runaway slaves. What makes the scene so striking is that Huck has fully absorbed the pro-slavery morality of his society: he sincerely believes that helping Jim escape is a sin that will send him to hell, even drafts a letter exposing where Jim is hiding, and then, moved by his memories of Jim's kindness toward him, tears the letter up anyway, deciding "all right, then, I'll go to hell." Around the same time, in the ethics unit of my philosophy elective, we read the part of Kant's Groundwork where he argues that an action only has genuine moral worth if it is done from the motive of duty rather than from inclination, using the example of a naturally sympathetic philanthropist: if his charity flows purely from the pleasure he takes in helping people, Kant says it has no real moral worth, whereas if his natural sympathy were extinguished by his own grief and he still helped others from duty alone, then, and only then, would his action have genuine moral worth.

Comparing the two discussions afterward, I realized Huck's decision looks almost like the mirror image of Kant's ideal: he acts purely from sympathy for Jim, with no thought of duty at all, and he does it while consciously believing, by his own lights, that he is doing something wrong. Out of curiosity, I looked into whether any philosophers had written about this, and found that Nomy Arpaly actually uses cases just like Huck's against Kant's view. She argues that Huck's choice clearly has moral worth even though it involves no motive of duty whatsoever, since he is emotionally responsive to the very facts that make helping Jim the right thing to do, even as his explicit, conscious judgment tells him the opposite. From what I can tell, she calls this kind of case "inverse akrasia," since it flips the usual weakness-of-will story: instead of knowing the right thing and failing to do it, the agent believes the wrong thing and does the right thing anyway.

Could you compare Kant's account of moral worth with the kind of view Arpaly is defending here? I would like to understand exactly what each view identifies as the real source of moral worth in an action, why they end up disagreeing so sharply about a case like Huck's, and which of the two you find to be the more defensible general theory of moral worth, rather than simply the one that gives the more satisfying verdict about Huck specifically.
