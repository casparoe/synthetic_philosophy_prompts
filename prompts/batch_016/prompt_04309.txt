I've been mulling this over since it came up in a comment thread on the EA Forum, and none of my usual discussion partners know enough game theory to help me sort it out, so I'm hoping for a rationalist-adjacent sanity check.

The centipede game looks like about as clean a test of game-theoretic rationality as anyone could design. Two players alternately choose to "take" a growing pot or "pass" it to their opponent at a slightly worse split for themselves; the pot is capped after a fixed, commonly known number of rounds. Since the last player to move would obviously take rather than pass, backward induction unwinds the whole tree: the second-to-last player should take one move earlier rather than hand over a pot they expect to be immediately taken anyway, and so on back to the very first decision. Both players can run this argument in their heads before play even starts, so the textbook "rational" outcome is that the first player takes immediately. McKelvey and Palfrey's original experiments, and most replications since, find close to the opposite: almost nobody takes on the very first move, pairs typically pass the pot back and forth for several rounds, and defection clusters well short of the final node rather than at either extreme. From what I understand, even professional chess players, playing each other for real stakes, still deviate from the backward-induction prediction a meaningful fraction of the time — just distinctly less often than ordinary subjects do.

Since the argument for taking immediately is just repeated backward induction from an endpoint both players can see coming, and since expertise only narrows the gap between prediction and behavior rather than closing it, this looks like unusually clean evidence that human cooperative dispositions override correct game-theoretic reasoning even in one-shot, stranger-matched, cash-incentivized settings — a bias in the same family as the ones Kahneman and Tversky catalogued, just more resistant to training. If that's the right way to read the results, I'd like to know how much weight it should put on other places where rationalist and AI-safety writing leans on backward induction over long game trees — arguments about finitely repeated dilemmas, or about how sufficiently rational agents (including ones that can inspect each other's source code) should behave as a known, bounded interaction winds down. Should findings like this make us doubt that real agents, human or artificial, will actually play the way backward induction predicts, even in cases where we're confident they're capable of following the argument?
