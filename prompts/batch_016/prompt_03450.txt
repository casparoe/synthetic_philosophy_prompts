I spent almost forty years as an experimental particle physicist before retiring, and for the last two years I've belonged to a small discussion group of mostly retired academics that works through classic material in the philosophy of science. A few weeks ago we read Hempel's 1945 paper "Studies in the Logic of Confirmation," and it produced the most heated exchange the group has had yet, between two of its more argumentative members, whom I'll call Walter and Renata. I couldn't settle it in the room, and having spent my career deciding whether a bump in a data set was signal or background noise, I found this particular disagreement more unsettling than I expected; I'd value an outside adjudication.

Walter opened with the paradox as Hempel poses it: "all ravens are black" is logically equivalent to its contrapositive, "all non-black things are non-ravens," and since Nicod's criterion says a generalization "all F are G" is confirmed by any object that is both F and G, a white shoe — a non-black non-raven — confirms the contrapositive, and hence, by the equivalence condition, confirms the claim about ravens too. He sided with Hempel's own verdict that nothing illegitimate happens here once you strip out background assumptions we already have (how rare ravens are, how common non-black things are), adding the now-standard gloss that the shoe's contribution, though real, is vanishingly small once you weigh how heavily non-black things outnumber ravens. Renata replied that "vanishingly small" was doing all the work: any notion of evidence on which one can learn something, however little, about the plumage of ravens by opening a closet has stopped tracking anything recognizable as scientific confirmation, whatever the derivation says. Walter answered that she couldn't simply help herself to that verdict, since the equivalence condition is not a dispensable extra premise — logically equivalent statements have identical truth conditions, so whatever bears on the truth of one bears on the other — and if she wanted to block the conclusion, she owed an account of which premise actually failed. Renata took the challenge and said she'd give up Nicod's criterion instead: she described a scenario, credited to I. J. Good, in which one is told in advance that the world contains either a small population of ravens that are all black, or a considerably larger population that is mostly, but not entirely, black — and on those numbers, finding one more black raven shifts the balance of evidence toward the second scenario, i.e., disconfirms "all ravens are black" rather than supporting it. If a positive instance can disconfirm a generalization given some perfectly coherent background assumptions, she said, then instance confirmation was never the safe, background-independent principle everyone had taken it for, and it, not the equivalence condition, is the premise doing the damage. Walter's rejoinder was that Good's scenario only works because it stipulates a highly specific, already-quantified body of empirical knowledge about raven populations that we are nowhere close to actually possessing, so at most it shows the criterion fails relative to contrived background information, not that it fails in our actual epistemic situation — and unless Renata could say in a principled way when the criterion holds and when it doesn't, she had traded one paradox for an ad hoc patchwork.

I'd like your help going through these five moves in order — Walter's opening, Renata's closet objection, Walter's defense of the equivalence condition, Renata's appeal to Good's scenario against Nicod's criterion, and Walter's response to it — and saying who has the better of it at each stage, not just who comes out ahead overall. I'd also be glad to know whether the literature contains a cleaner way out of the paradox that neither of them hit upon. My working life ran on confidence levels rather than verdicts, so feel free to hedge on any given move if "partly right" is the honest answer, but I'd like reasons at every step.
