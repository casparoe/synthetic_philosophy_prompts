I'm a software engineer working on ML stuff, and part of my job lately involves reading mechanistic interpretability papers — the ones where researchers crack open a trained model and try to find "features" or "circuits" inside that supposedly correspond to specific concepts. The example that hooked me was that Anthropic "Golden Gate Claude" thing, where they found a direction in the model's activations that reliably fires for the Golden Gate Bridge and cranked it up until the model brought up the bridge in almost every answer. My coworkers will just casually say the model "represents" the bridge, or "has a concept of" sycophancy or deception or whatever, like that's obviously the same kind of thing as a person having an idea in their head. Nobody really defends that move, they just assert it and move on.

Then last week I was listening to a philosophy podcast on my commute, and the host was explaining this 19th century philosopher, Franz Brentano, who apparently made "intentionality" — basically a mental state's being ABOUT or directed at something, not "intention" like wanting to do a thing — into THE defining feature that seperates mental phenomena from physical ones. His whole point, as far as I understood it, was that no purely physical or mechanical process has this "aboutness" built in, only minds do. Obviously the guy died in 1917 and never heard of a neural net, but it sounds like his thesis is aimed almost exactly at the move my coworkers keep making without ever arguing for it.

So here's my actual question: if you explained modern interpretability findings to Brentano — a direction in activation space that reliably fires for a concept and causally steers the model's behavior around it, especially given that alot of these features apparently live in "superposition" rather than one-neuron-one-concept — do you think he'd grant that this counts as real intentionality, or would he say we're just pattern-matching meaning onto weights the way people see faces in clouds? And if he'd reject it, is that because his view actually gives him a principled way to draw that line, or does "mark of the mental" kind of just smuggle in the answer (physical stuff can't represent, this is physical, therefore it doesn't represent) — in which case doesn't that same move threaten to make actual biological brains suspect too, since they're physical and mechanical as well? I'd like an answer that actually engages with his arguments instead of a quick "well he was basically a dualist so no."
