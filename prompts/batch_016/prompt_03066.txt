I am assembling a cycle of short stories, each built around one precisely stated thesis in philosophy of mind, and I would like the next entry to take on higher-order theories of consciousness. Please write a short story, roughly 1,000–2,000 words, dramatizing the strangest consequence of David Rosenthal's actualist higher-order-thought theory. On that view, a mental state is conscious just when it causes, non-inferentially, an activated thought — itself normally unconscious — that one is in that very state, and Rosenthal accepts that such a thought can occur with nothing beneath it at all. A 'targetless' or 'empty' higher-order thought, as it is called, still yields, on his account, a genuine phenomenally conscious experience — of a color, a pain, a taste — answering to no first-order state whatsoever. This is specific to the actualist picture: dispositionalist theories (Carruthers) and self-representational theories (Kriegel and others) rule it out by construction, so the story should hinge on that particular architecture rather than on higher-order awareness in some looser sense. Rosenthal and Lycan reach for real clinical cases to make the possibility vivid — a dental patient with dead nerves who still panics at the drill, a patient on morphine who says the pain feels unchanged but no longer minds it — so please invent something stranger than either, and let the philosophy do its work through the plot rather than through a character explaining it.
