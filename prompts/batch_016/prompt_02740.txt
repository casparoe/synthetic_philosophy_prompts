I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I've fallen down a bit of a rabbit hole for a piece about how totally unconnected philosophical traditions kept independently running into the same puzzles about language, categories, and reference. The plan was to build it around the Greek Sophists, but a philosopher friend insisted I also cover the Chinese "School of Names," specifically Gongsun Long's white horse argument (白馬非馬, "a white horse is not a horse"), because apparently it's way wierder and more interesting than the one-liner that gets trotted out as a party trick. So instead of just repeating the soundbite, I actually tracked down a translation of the dialogue, and now I'm a bit stuck on the actual argument.

The core of it — this is me stitching together and paraphrasing a couple of translations I found, so don't quote me on the exact wording, but I think I've kept the logic intact — goes something like this:

Objector: Is it acceptable to claim that a white horse is not a horse?
Advocate: It's acceptable. Consider: "horse" names a shape, and "white" names a color. Naming a color is different from naming a shape, and that's the basis for saying a white horse is not a horse.
Objector: Hold on — if a white horse exists, you can hardly deny that a horse exists. Having a white horse amounts to having a horse. So why wouldn't the white one count as a horse too?
Advocate: Think about what happens when you ask someone to fetch a horse versus fetch a white horse. In the first case a yellow or a black horse satisfies the request; in the second case it doesn't. If "white horse" meant nothing more than "horse," the two requests would be interchangeable, so a yellow or black horse should satisfy both equally, or neither. Since it clearly answers to one and not the other, "white horse" and "horse" can't be picking out the same thing. Hence, a white horse is not a horse.

I get that the first move (shape-word vs. color-word) is basically a pun that treats "white horse" as though it were two separate labels stapled together rather than one compound name for a kind of animal. What I don't get is the second move with the yellow and black horses — it feels like its supposed to be doing something different and more substantial than just repeating the same trick, but I can't tell if that's actually true, or if Gongsun Long is just sliding between "is not identical to" and "is not a member of" again in fancier dress. Is the second argument genuinely independent support, or basically a restatement of the first? And is there a charitable reading where he's onto something real about how we individuate categories or classes, rather than him just being a sophist having a laugh at everyone's expense? I'd love an actual breakdown, because right now my only frame of reference is "ha, wacky ancient wordplay," and that doesn't feel like it's doing the guy justice.
