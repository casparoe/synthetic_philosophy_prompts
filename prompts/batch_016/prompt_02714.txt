I've been reporting a longform piece on how premodern thinkers rationalized religious practices that had already started to look primitive or embarrassing to sophisticated audiences of their own day, and Maimonides' treatment of animal sacrifice keeps pulling me back in as the richest case study I've found. In the Guide of the Perplexed (III:32), he argues that God never actually wanted or needed sacrifices for their own sake: since the Israelites emerging from Egypt had only ever known worship as animal slaughter offered up to a god, they could not have been asked to leap straight to a purely contemplative form of devotion, so the law redirected this existing, idolatry-soaked practice toward the one true God as a kind of pedagogical way station on the road to prayer. What keeps nagging at me is how badly this sits with his own law code: in the Mishneh Torah's Laws of Kings, Maimonides states flatly that the messianic king will rebuild the Temple and reinstate the sacrificial order exactly as it once stood. Nahmanides, glossing Leviticus 1:9, already rejected the concessive theory in favor of a view of sacrifice as inherently potent, and centuries later Jacob Emden was so unsettled by the mismatch that he floated the idea that the relevant material in the Guide must be a later forgery. The disagreement has never really been resolved, and it isn't merely academic: religious-Zionist institutions such as Jerusalem's Temple Institute are, at this moment, breeding red heifers and training kohanim on the premise that sacrificial worship is a live obligation awaiting resumption, not a stage of religious development that humanity has simply outgrown.

For a piece aimed at general readers rather than specialists, I'd like your help thinking through what actually rides on this philosophically, rather than just narrating it as a historical curiosity. What do you take to be the most important implications of Maimonides' concessive, developmental theory of sacrifice, and of its apparent tension with his own law code, for two things in particular: first, his broader theory of the reasons for the commandments, including which other biblical statutes ought to get the same treatment; and second, the long-running scholarly argument over whether Maimonides was a deliberately esoteric writer who used real contradictions to communicate one thing to the philosophically trained and another to everyone else? I'm less interested in an even-handed literature review than in your own reasoned judgment about which of these implications are the most consequential and best supported, and which look overstated once you push on them.
