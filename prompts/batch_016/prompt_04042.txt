In Knowledge and Its Limits, Timothy Williamson attacks the KK principle — if you know that P, you're in a position to know that you know it — by way of a more general argument against "luminosity," the idea that being in a mental state puts you in a position to know that you're in it. His test case is a subject who feels freezing cold at dawn and warms up so gradually, in increments too small ever to notice, that she is comfortably warm by noon. Since our powers of discrimination are limited, he argues, knowing that you feel cold at a given moment requires a margin for error: your belief must remain true at every moment too close to that one to be told apart from it. Chained across the whole morning, this requirement yields the absurd result that if you knew you felt cold at dawn, you'd have to count as still feeling cold at noon. Rather than accept that, Williamson concludes that not even feeling cold is luminous — and, since "knowing that P" is itself the kind of condition his argument targets, that KK fails quite generally.

Write a dialogue between two epistemologists: one who accepts Williamson's argument without qualification, and one who wants to preserve some restricted luminosity principle for phenomenal states without simply denying that his case is coherent. Have them work through the actual moving parts of the dispute — whether the margin-for-error principle is well motivated, how finely "cases" can be individuated, and whether the safety-style reliability requirement driving the whole argument is even the right condition on self-knowledge — rather than trading bare intuitions. Neither should get the last, unanswered word.
