I'm a novelist currently drafting a book set largely in a fictional maritime processing center for asylum seekers, and one of my point-of-view characters — a former philosophy postgraduate who now spends her days writing admissibility recommendations — keeps returning, half against her will, to an old undergraduate copy of Kant's "Perpetual Peace." I want her invocations of it to be more than decorative erudition, so I went back to the Third Definitive Article of the 1795 essay myself, and I've gotten stuck on a passage that seems to be doing something more careful, and more ambiguous, than the usual one-line paraphrase ("Kant thought we're all citizens of the world") suggests. Here it is, in the W. Hastie translation of 1891, which is the edition I've put on her shelf:

'The Rights of men as Citizens of the world in a cosmo-political system, shall be restricted to conditions of universal Hospitality.' In this as in the previous Articles, the question is not about a relation of Philanthropy, but one of Right. 'Hospitality' here indicates the Right of a stranger in consequence of his arrival on the soil of another country, not to be treated by its citizens as an enemy. As a stranger he may be turned away, if this can be done without involving his death; but so long as he conducts himself peacefully in the place where he may happen to be, he is not to be dealt with in a hostile way. The stranger may not lay claim to be entertained by right as a Guest,—for this would require a special friendly compact to make him for a certain time the member of a household,—he may only claim a Right of Resort or of visitation. All men are entitled to present themselves thus to society in virtue of their Right to the common possession of the surface of the earth, to no part of which anyone had originally more right than another; and upon which, from its being a globe, they cannot scatter themselves to infinite distances, but must at last bear to live side by side with each other.

What I can't quite pin down — and what I'd love help thinking through — is exactly how much Kant is granting the stranger here, and on what basis. He insists that the stranger has no claim to be treated "as a Guest," only the thinner "Right of Resort"; yet he also insists that the state may turn the stranger away only if this can be done "without involving his death," and only so long as it does not treat him "in a hostile way." That could be read quite minimally — states remain free to refuse settlement, and are merely forbidden from killing or brutalizing people at the border — or it could be read as importing something stronger: a genuine entitlement to attempt "social intercourse" that a blanket, permanent refusal would itself violate. I'd also like help with the premise doing the real work in the background: is the "common possession of the surface of the earth," invoked because the earth "is a globe" on which people "cannot scatter themselves to infinite distances," meant to ground an actual right, or is Kant dressing up a practical fact about unavoidable proximity in the vocabulary of Right? And finally, on your reading, does this passage end up lending more support to theorists who defend a state's right to exclude, or to open-borders theorists who claim Kant as a forerunner — or does it simply not map cleanly onto that later debate at all? I would rather give my character a genuinely defensible reading of Kant than a caricature in either direction.
