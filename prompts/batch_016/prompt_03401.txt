I cover physics and philosophy of science, and for a piece on how philosophers argue about the "reality" of spacetime I've spent the last couple of weeks working through Einstein's hole argument, in the form John Earman and John Norton revived it in the 1980s. I think I finally have the mechanics straight: take a solution of the field equations, apply a diffeomorphism that leaves the metric and matter fields alone outside some region H but redistributes them differently inside it, and you get a second, mathematically distinct solution that agrees with the first on every invariant and every observable. If spacetime points are individuals with identities of their own, the two solutions have to count as physically different possibilities — which buys the substantivalist a strange, cut-rate indeterminism no one asked for.

What I still can't render clearly for readers is the fight one level up: whether "sophisticated substantivalism" is a genuine rescue of the view or a retreat dressed up as a victory. I mean the move, associated with people like Hoefer and Pooley, of denying that spacetime points carry their identities across models, so that there's no fact of the matter about which point is "the same" one after the shift; or Maudlin's alternative of making a point's metrical properties essential to it. Belot and Earman, when they coined the term, more or less accused this family of views of quietly conceding everything the relationalist wanted, Leibniz Equivalence included, while keeping the old label.

Could you draft a dialogue between two philosophers of physics thrashing this out — one a sophisticated substantivalist (essentialist or anti-haecceitist, whichever you find easier to argue well), the other a relationalist convinced the whole maneuver is a merely verbal victory? I'd like the relationalist to press that charge hard and force the substantivalist to say what actually survives of their original commitment once haecceitism is gone, but I don't want a rout — give the substantivalist genuine resources too, for instance on why giving up transworld identity for points costs less than it sounds, or on what the relationalist is quietly still committed to that starts to look like a container spacetime by another name. If it fits naturally, have one of them raise whether this is all just Leibniz's shift argument against Newton and Clarke in modern dress, or whether running it on a theory where the geometry itself is dynamical changes the stakes. Neither side should be easier to write than the other, and I'd rather they reach a real impasse than a tidy resolution — the literature hasn't resolved this either, so a dialogue that pretends otherwise would ring false.
