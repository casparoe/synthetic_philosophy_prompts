In my IB Philosophy class, we just finished a unit on logical positivism and the Vienna Circle. The core idea, as I understood it, is the verification principle: a statement only counts as cognitively meaningful if it is either true by definition, as in logic or mathematics, or can at least in principle be checked against experience. Everything else — claims about the soul, the Absolute, objective values, and most of traditional metaphysics — doesn't come out false on this view so much as strictly meaningless, a string of words dressed up to look like a real claim. My teacher was emphatic that this was the whole point of the movement: showing that large parts of philosophy were pseudo-problems rather than genuine disagreements.

That's why I was caught off guard when, for a class reading, I came across an essay literally titled "On the Meaning of Life," written in 1927 by Moritz Schlick — who I later found out was the very philosopher who founded and led the Vienna Circle. A friend I was reading it with pointed out that "the meaning of life" seems like exactly the kind of grand, unverifiable question the positivists were supposed to throw out as nonsense, yet here was their own leader writing an entire serious essay trying to answer it rather than dissolve it. I raised this in class, and my teacher agreed it was a good catch but we moved on before really addressing it, so it's been bugging me since. If Schlick genuinely believed that unverifiable questions are meaningless rather than just difficult, how do historians of philosophy account for him spending real intellectual effort answering one of the most unverifiable questions imaginable instead of simply declaring it confused, the way his own principle would seem to require?
