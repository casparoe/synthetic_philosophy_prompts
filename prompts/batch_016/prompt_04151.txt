I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, currently stuck on a chapter of my dissertation on consumer-based teleosemantics, and I keep coming back to a thought experiment Paul Pietroski used against Ruth Millikan's biosemantics in the early nineties. The setup: the kimu are an otherwise color-blind species until a mutation arises that produces a brain state, call it B, caused specifically by red things, which makes its bearer want to climb to the top of the nearest hill every morning. As it happens, hill-climbing also keeps the kimu out of the valley, where a predator called the snorf hunts at dawn; it's this incidental safety benefit, and nothing to do with redness as such, that actually explains why the B-producing mechanism was selected. Millikan's own framework seems to force the conclusion that B does not represent redness at all: since what mattered for the fitness of B's consumers was the state's correlation with low snorf density rather than with red, B's content comes out as something like "fewer snorf this way." Pietroski takes this to be a reductio of biosemantics, and offers a simple test: plant a red flag in the middle of a field full of snorf, and the kimu will happily wander toward it, which seems to show that they're tracking redness itself, whatever the evolutionary backstory says mattered for their ancestors.

I brought this up in my advisor's reading group last week as a knockdown case against consumer-based teleosemantics and got fairly thoroughly outargued, which has left me wanting to see the strongest version of the position I was attacking before I decide whether to use it in my own chapter. So: please argue that Millikan's verdict here is correct — that B really does represent something in the vicinity of "fewer snorf this way" and not redness — and that this is not an embarrassing bullet for consumer-based teleosemantics to bite, but the predictable and correct consequence of taking selection history seriously as what fixes representational content. I'd particularly like you to address why Pietroski's red-flag scenario doesn't actually establish what he thinks it establishes.
