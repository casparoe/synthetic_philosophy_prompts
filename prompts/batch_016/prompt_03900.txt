A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about Pascal's mugging: whether an expected-value reasoner really is rationally compelled to hand over her wallet to a stranger who threatens some unimaginable disutility on the strength of a vanishingly small probability of being able to deliver it, and whether the standard escapes (bounding utility, discounting tiny probabilities to zero, and so on) are principled or merely ad hoc. Eventually we ended up back at the source of it all, Pascal's own wager, and got stuck on a single paragraph. Here it is, in Trotter's 1910 translation of the Pensées, §233:

"Let us weigh the gain and the loss in wagering that God is. Let us estimate these two chances. If you gain, you gain all; if you lose, you lose nothing. Wager, then, without hesitation that He is. 'That is very fine. Yes, I must wager; but I may perhaps wager too much.' Let us see. Since there is an equal risk of gain and of loss, if you had only to gain two lives, instead of one, you might still wager. But if there were three lives to gain, you would have to play (since you are under the necessity of playing), and you would be imprudent, when you are forced to play, not to chance your life to gain three at a game where there is an equal risk of loss and gain. But there is an eternity of life and happiness. And this being so, if there were an infinity of chances, of which one only would be for you, you would still be right in wagering one to win two, and you would act stupidly, being obliged to play, by refusing to stake one life against three at a game in which out of an infinity of chances there is one for you, if there were an infinity of an infinitely happy life to gain. But there is here an infinity of an infinitely happy life to gain, a chance of gain against a finite number of chances of loss, and what you stake is finite. It is all divided; where-ever the infinite is and there is not an infinity of chances of loss against that of gain, there is no time to hesitate, you must give all. And thus, when one is forced to play, he must renounce reason to preserve his life, rather than risk it for infinite gain, as likely to happen as the loss of nothingness."

My friend reads this as one continuous argument. I think Pascal quietly changes horses partway through: he starts from a case of equal odds, works his way up through increasingly generous finite payoffs (two lives, then three), and only then, almost as an aside, substitutes "an infinity of chances, of which one only would be for you"—a vanishingly small probability—while treating it as underwriting exactly the same conclusion as the equal-odds case did. Could you help me work through, in some detail, whether that final move really is a distinct argument smuggled in under cover of the first one, or just a restatement of it? And, more to the point for the mugging case: does this passage show that Pascal's own reasoning already contains the seed of the mugger's move—shrink the probability arbitrarily, keep the payoff infinite, and the conclusion is supposed to stand regardless—or is there something in how he sets up the wager (the insistence that you are "under the necessity of playing," say) that would block a mugger's parallel argument even on Pascal's own terms?
