I recently joined the board of my condo HOA (small building, five of us total) and last month we had to rule on whether a neighbor violated our short-term-rental bylaw. It turned into this whole logic argument between two other board members that's honestly been rattling around in my head since.

Here's the setup: the bylaw says a unit is only in violation if BOTH (a) it was rented out short-term (stays under 30 days) at least three times in the past year, AND (b) at least one of those stays had no documented reason on file (medical treatment, work travel, etc. count as documented). Both conditions have to hold, that's just how the rule is written. Before voting on the verdict itself, we went around on the two sub-questions separately: on (a), three of the five of us said yes, it happened 3+ times; on (b), three of the five said yes too, though not the exact same three people. But then when our chair asked everyone to just say straight out "violation, yes or no," only two of the five said yes — the other three said no, even though, like I said, three-out-of-five agreed with (a) and three-out-of-five agreed with (b) taken seperately.

That's when Hank and Renee, two other board members, really got into it. I've tried to lay out their exchange as fairly as I can:

(1) Hank said we should obviously just tally the direct yes/no votes on the verdict — 3 to 2 for "no violation," done, because that's what a vote literally is.
(2) Renee said that's indefensible: a majority of us think (a) is true and a majority think (b) is true, and the bylaw itself says a-and-b equals a violation, so the board's own majority positions, question by question, actually add up to a violation — calling it "no violation" contradicts what the board collectively believes about the facts.
(3) Hank replied that people don't reason in two neat little boxes — someone could vote "no" overall because of some consideration that doesn't map cleanly onto (a) or (b) alone (like thinking the owner's listing history is borderline even if it technically clears both thresholds), and forcing everyone into an issue-by-issue framework just throws that nuance out.
(4) Renee answered that this is exactly how bias sneaks in: if people can vote "no" on the bottom line without being tied down to (a) or (b), nobody ever has to show their work, and "I weighed some nuance" becomes an all-purpose excuse for any vote at all. Tying the verdict to the sub-questions at least forces people to commit to something checkable.
(5) Hank's comeback was that Renee's method produces a "board decision" that literally none of the five of us hold: two say "yes" outright, three say "no," so under her approach the official ruling is "violation" even though zero individual members, asked point-blank, believe that's true.
(6) Renee said that's just a fact about how groups work — a group can end up committed to a conclusion no single member actually holds, and that alone doesn't make her approach wrong.

Neither of them budged and the meeting kind of just ended there, awkwardly. Could you go through this move by move — for each of the 6 numbered points, say whether it's basically right, basically wrong, or somewhere in between (maybe score each from -2 to +2 in a quick JSON block up top, then explain below)? I'd also genuinely like to know if you think there's a better way our board should've handled a case like this that neither Hank nor Renee actually raised.
