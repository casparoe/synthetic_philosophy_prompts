Our school has an informal philosophy club that's really just a handful of us who stay after AP Comparative Government to keep arguing about whatever we didn't get to finish in class. A few weeks ago our teacher gave us a packet on John Stuart Mill's later writing on socialism: the chapter "On the Probable Futurity of the Labouring Classes" that Mill added to later editions of Principles of Political Economy, part of the unfinished Chapters on Socialism that only got published after he died, and the passage from his Autobiography where he describes his and Harriet Taylor's outlook as going well beyond ordinary democracy, close enough that it would "class us decidedly under the general designation of Socialists." Two people in the club, Owen and Sasha, ended up arguing about what to make of all this for basically the whole meeting, and since I couldn't tell who was winning, I wrote down the exchange afterward as best I could remember it.

(1) Owen said the Autobiography line settles the question: Mill directly calls himself a socialist, and the futurity chapter describes laborers eventually working in associations that collectively own their own capital and elect their own managers instead of working for a wage -- which just is what socialism means -- so this wasn't a passing enthusiasm but his real, considered position. (2) Sasha answered that the Autobiography line is carrying more weight than the rest of the record supports: in Chapters on Socialism, Mill spends most of his energy insisting that private property hasn't earned its exit yet and that any alternative has to prove itself through small, voluntary experiments first, and he worries at length that shared ownership would weaken people's incentive to work hard or to keep their own families small. That sounds like a cautious liberal giving a rival system a fair hearing, she said, not someone who has joined it. (3) Owen replied that Sasha was flattening a distinction Mill draws on purpose: he only turns harsh on revolutionary, all-at-once schemes for abolishing property, while he stays consistently warm toward the gradual, Fourierist-style cooperatives that let workers pool capital and share earnings. On his reading, the whole point of being so tough on revolutionary communism in that text is to clear the ground for the kind of socialism Mill actually favors, so treating the essay as a rejection of socialism mistakes a filter for a verdict. (4) Sasha's comeback was that even Mill's favored cooperatives are still supposed to earn their spread by out-competing ordinary capitalist firms on the open market, so it's the market's judgment that decides whether wage labor disappears. But on any view that deserves the name socialist, she said, collective ownership shouldn't have to win a competition against capital to count as legitimate -- removing exactly that kind of discipline is supposed to be the point. So what Mill ends up defending, in her view, is at most a worker-friendlier capitalism, and calling it socialism just because he used the word himself stretches the label past the point of being useful.

I'd rather not just be told who won overall. Could you go through the four moves in order and say who has the stronger case at each one, including anywhere you think both of them are missing something that Mill's actual texts would complicate? I'm also curious whether you think this is mainly a disagreement about what Mill believed and wrote, or whether Owen and Sasha are quietly using different definitions of "socialism" and arguing past each other underneath what looks like a historical dispute.
