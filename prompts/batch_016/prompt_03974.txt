I'm working on a novel with a scene where a crotchety retired physics professor tries to explain to his grandaughter why "cause and effect" isn't as simple as it sounds, and instead of inventing his little speech out of thin air I went and dug up the actual Hume passage everyone traces this stuff back to (it's from the Enquiry, Section VII, Part II, the bit on "necessary connexion"):

"Suitably to this experience, therefore, we may define a cause to be an object, followed by another, and where all the objects similar to the first are followed by objects similar to the second. Or in other words where, if the first object had not been, the second never had existed. The appearance of a cause always conveys the mind, by a customary transition, to the idea of the effect. Of this also we have experience. We may, therefore, suitably to this experience, form another definition of cause, and call it, an object followed by another, and whose appearance always conveys the thought to that other."

Heres what I can't figure out: Hume treats the "always followed by" bit and the "if the first hadn't happened, the second never would've" bit as the same claim, just restated ("or in other words"). But those feel like genuinely different claims to me - one's about a pattern that actually, always holds, the other's about what would've happened in a situation that never occurred. Is that a real gap he's quietly papering over, or is there a reading where the two collapse into each other? And if it is a real gap - is that throwaway counterfactual clause really the ancestor of later Lewis-style counterfactual theories of causation, like people say it is, or would someone from the more modern "interventionist" camp (cause = whatever changes under some hypothetical intervention, Woodward/Pearl-ish) say his whole definition is broken for a totally different reason than my professor is about to give? Want his little monologue to actually be sharp, not just quotable.
