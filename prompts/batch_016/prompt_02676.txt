I'm taking a seminar this term on the philosophy of artificial intelligence, and we just spent two weeks on the frame problem, which I find genuinely confusing because the label seems to cover at least two different puzzles that our readings kept sliding between without quite admitting it. There's the narrow version McCarthy and Hayes posed back in 1969 — essentially a bookkeeping problem about how to state, in first-order logic, which facts stay the same when an action is performed, without writing out an explosive number of "non-effect" axioms for every action-fact pair — and then there's the much broader version Dennett popularized with his fable about the battery-fetching robots that either get blown up or freeze in place while dutifully deducing irrelevant consequences of their own actions. Fodor picked up Dennett's version and used it to argue, via his claim that central belief-fixation is "isotropic" and "Quinean" in a way no encapsulated module could handle, that classical cognitive science may be structurally unable to explain ordinary relevance judgments at all. When I raised this in office hours, my TA guessed that the "solutions" people cite, like McCarthy's circumscription or Reiter's successor-state axioms, might just be answering the narrow bookkeeping question while quietly sidestepping Dennett and Fodor's harder point — but she admitted she wasn't sure, and this tension is more or less exactly what our final paper is supposed to untangle.

I'd like your help thinking it through. Could you write me an essay that stakes out a clear, defended position on whether the frame problem, in its broad epistemological sense (holistic relevance judgments, not just the logical bookkeeping issue), has actually been solved, has been dissolved as a kind of pseudo-problem, or remains a live and unresolved threat to classical/computational theories of mind? Please engage seriously with at least the logicist responses (circumscription, successor-state axioms), Fodor's pessimistic modularity argument, and the embodied/enactive line associated with Dreyfus that claims the problem only dissolves once representationalism itself is abandoned — and feel free to argue that some of these camps are talking past one another rather than genuinely disagreeing. I'd also be curious whether you think the empirical track record of contemporary deep learning systems, which handle open-ended commonsense reasoning without anything resembling explicit frame axioms, changes the verdict in either direction, or whether that's beside the point. Most of all, please don't hedge at the end: I'd rather you commit to a specific, argued verdict (even a qualified one, along the lines of "dissolved for embodied agents but still open for any system built on discrete symbolic representations") than leave the question hanging.
