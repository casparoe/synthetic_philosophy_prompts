I'm a philosophy PhD student working mainly on moral psychology, and this term I've been TAing a graduate seminar on free will and moral responsibility. We just spent a week on Galen Strawson's "Basic Argument" (from "Freedom and Belief" and the later "The Impossibility of Moral Responsibility"), and while prepping for section I realized I don't actually have a crisp grip on a point our instructor kept insisting on: that the Basic Argument is supposed to be entirely neutral on whether determinism or indeterminism is true, which is what supposedly makes it a fundamentally different kind of argument from standard incompatibilism rather than just a dramatic restatement of hard determinism. Most of what I read as an undergraduate elided this distinction, so I want to be careful not to pass the same conflation on to my students.

Could you help me think through the mechanics of this properly? I follow the core regress well enough — you act as you do because of the way you are, so being responsible for the act would require being responsible for the way you are, which would in turn require some prior, self-determining act of self-creation, and so on without end. What I'd like help with is why Strawson thinks granting genuine indeterminism somewhere in the causal chain, whether in the formation of character or at the moment of choice itself, does nothing to stop that regress; I gather he devotes a whole short paper, "The Unhelpfulness of Indeterminism," to essentially this point, but I haven't managed to get hold of a copy. Is the idea simply that indeterministic "swerves" are exactly as unchosen and unearned as deterministic causes, so that swapping one for the other can't manufacture the missing self-authorship? It would help to see that claim set explicitly against something like van Inwagen's Consequence Argument, which by contrast really does trade on the truth of determinism together with the fixity of the past and the laws of nature. And, if you're willing to venture a view: do compatibilist strategies that try to ground responsibility in something like Fischer and Ravizza's reasons-responsive "guidance control," rather than in full self-origination, actually meet the Basic Argument on its own terms, or do they just relocate the same regress one level up, into the question of how the responsiveness-conferring mechanism itself came to be?
