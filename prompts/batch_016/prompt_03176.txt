I'm prepping my seminar on conditionals for this week and I'm in one of those moods where the more I reread the literature the less sure I am how to present it without just hand-waving at the students. You probably know the Adams pair: "If Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, then someone else did" (which everyone wants to call true) versus "If Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have" (obviously false — there was no backup assassin waiting in the wings). Same antecedant and consequent more or less, just a shift in mood, and yet totally different verdicts. It's the sort of case that makes you wonder whether indicative and counterfactual conditionals are really the same kind of animal semantically, or whether we've just been sloppy lumping them together as "conditionals" this whole time.

What I'd love is a dialogue that actually stages this disagreement properly, between two people who both know the terrain cold. One should defend something like the Adams/Edgington line — indicative conditionals don't have truth conditions at all, just assertability conditions tracking conditional probability, and that's exactly why the Oswald case falls out for free. The other should defend a unified Stalnaker/Lewis-style possible-worlds semantics for both flavors of conditional, where the mood just tells you whether you're ranging over epistemically live worlds or objectively similar ones. Don't let the possible-worlds character win just by waving Lewis's triviality results around, and don't let the Adams character win just by pointing at Oswald/Kennedy and declaring victory — I want real engagement with the embedding/compositionality problem on both sides, with each of them landing at least one punch the other can't fully shrug off. Maybe stage it as two colleagues still going at it by the whiteboard after everyone else has cleared out of the seminar room.
