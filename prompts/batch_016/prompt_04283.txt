I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on a seminar paper about early modern theories of demonstrative science, and I've been reading through the less-discussed polemical writing Hobbes aimed at John Wallis during their decades-long fight over Hobbes's mistaken claim to have squared the circle. I understand the broad shape of Hobbes's position — that "science" properly speaking requires knowledge of causes, and that this is supposed to explain his very high opinion of geometry — but a specific passage from the Epistle Dedicatory to his Six Lessons to the Professors of Mathematics (1656) is giving me trouble, both on its own terms and in how it's meant to connect to the rest of his system. The passage (Molesworth, English Works, vol. VII, pp. 183–184) runs:

"Of arts, some are demonstrable, others indemonstrable; and demonstrable are those the construction of the subject whereof is in the power of the artist himself, who, in his demonstration, does no more but deduce the consequences of his own operation. The reason whereof is this, that the science of every subject is derived from a precognition of the causes, generation, and construction of the same; and consequently where the causes are known, there is place for demonstration, but not where the causes are to seek for. Geometry therefore is demonstrable, for the lines and figures from which we reason are drawn and described by ourselves; and civil philosophy is demonstrable, because we make the commonwealth ourselves. But because of natural bodies we know not the construction, but seek it from the effects, there lies no demonstration of what the causes be we seek for, but only of what they may be."

He continues a little further down the same page:

"And where there is place for demonstration, if the first principles, that is to say, the definitions, contain not the generation of the subject, there can be nothing demonstrated as it ought to be. And this in the three first definitions of Euclid sufficiently appeareth. For seeing he maketh not, nor could make any use of them in his demonstrations, they ought not to be numbered among the principles of geometry."

Three things here are tripping me up, and I'd like a close reading keyed to the actual text rather than a general summary of Hobbes's philosophy of science, which I can already get from the secondary literature. First, is the inference from "we construct the object" to "we can demonstrate truths about it" meant to be close to trivial — causal knowledge just is knowledge of generation, so a maker has it automatically — or is there a more substantive epistemological thesis lurking here, something in the neighborhood of what Vico would later call the verum-factum principle, about a certainty available only to makers? Second, what exactly does Hobbes think is wrong with Euclid's opening definitions, and how, on his view, does their being useless "in his demonstrations" show that they fail to state a generation — he seems to be treating these as a single complaint rather than two, and I can't reconstruct why. Third, and this is what I'd most like your help with: how should we square this whole picture — geometry as the one domain where error is supposedly foreclosed, because we made the objects ourselves — with the fact that Hobbes wrote this while actively defending a series of geometrical demonstrations, including his quadrature of the circle, that were simply false? Using only the resources of this passage, is there a principled place for Hobbes to locate the source of his own error, or does his account of geometrical certainty leave no room for that kind of failure at all?
