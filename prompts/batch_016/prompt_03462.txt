Over dinner last night, a friend of mine who is finishing a PhD in epistemology and I ended up in a two-hour argument about Timothy Williamson's anti-luminosity argument and what, if anything, it does to the KK principle. She kept pressing the standard "feeling cold at dawn" case: you are freezing at t0, warming imperceptibly millisecond by millisecond, roasting by noon, and you introspect diligently the entire time, yet the margin-for-error/safety reasoning appears to show that you cannot know you feel cold even at t0, when it is about as obvious as anything could be. I tried out the two replies I could remember: first, that "feeling cold" might simply be a self-presenting state (along the lines of Weatherson or Conee), so that the relevant safety premise fails for it even if it holds elsewhere; second, the Neta-and-Rohrbaugh-style complaint that Williamson's safety principle is just the wrong analysis of knowledge, since there are ordinary cases (such as knowing you are drinking unpoisoned water while standing next to someone who very nearly, but did not, poison it) where you know something despite a very similar nearby case in which you would have been wrong. She was not moved by either: on her view, both replies save at most a handful of toy cases and leave the general argument, and hence the death of the KK principle for any introspective or perceptual condition, essentially intact. We went back and forth on whether that concedes too much or too little and never got anywhere, though we did agree that the stakes are real: this argument is supposed to be a serious blow against strong internalist, Cartesian pictures of the mind on which our own current mental states are always transparently available to us.

Since neither of us could settle it, I would like you to do so in essay form. Reconstruct Williamson's argument carefully enough that a reader could see exactly where the controversial premises are doing their work — the safety/margin-for-error principle, the claim that the relevant condition can only be gained or lost gradually, and the inference to the failure of luminosity at some point in the resulting series — and then lay out the strongest objections you can, including but not limited to the two above. Do not simply present this as an irresolvable standoff between competing intuitions: tell me, and defend, your actual verdict on whether the argument succeeds in showing that no substantive mental condition is luminous, and correspondingly on whether the KK principle should be rejected outright, restricted to some special class of states (a priori or "cogito"-like ones, say), or preserved on some other conception of knowledge or safety. A qualified verdict is fine, so long as it is a verdict rather than a shrug.
