I've spent the past several months reading through Plato's dialogues on my own, in the evenings, with no formal philosophy background at all — no courses, no reading group, just me and the text (I'm working from a public-domain translation I found online). A friend recommended starting with the Meno, mainly for the famous scene where Socrates draws a geometrical proof out of an uneducated slave boy just by asking him questions, which I actually followed fairly easily. But right near the end of the dialogue, Socrates and Meno have an exchange about the difference between "true opinion" and "knowledge" that I've now reread several times without feeling like I've really got hold of the argument. I looked around a little afterward and gather that this exact exchange is the historical source of what's apparently still called "the Meno problem" in philosophy today — the question of why knowledge should count for more than a belief that merely happens to be true — but I'd rather work through the actual argument myself than settle for that label secondhand.

Here is the exchange, starting a little before the part that confuses me so the context is clear:

SOCRATES: If a man knew the way to Larisa, or anywhere else, and went to the place and led others thither, would he not be a right and good guide?
MENO: Certainly.
SOCRATES: And a person who had a right opinion about the way, but had never been and did not know, might be a good guide also, might he not?
MENO: Certainly.
SOCRATES: And while he has true opinion about that which the other knows, he will be just as good a guide if he thinks the truth, as he who knows the truth?
MENO: Exactly.
SOCRATES: Then true opinion is as good a guide to correct action as knowledge; and that was the point which we omitted in our speculation about the nature of virtue, when we said that knowledge only is the guide of right action; whereas there is also right opinion.
MENO: True.
SOCRATES: Then right opinion is not less useful than knowledge?
MENO: The difference, Socrates, is only that he who has knowledge will always be right; but he who has right opinion will sometimes be right, and sometimes not.
SOCRATES: What do you mean? Can he be wrong who has right opinion, so long as he has right opinion?
MENO: I admit the cogency of your argument, and therefore, Socrates, I wonder that knowledge should be preferred to right opinion — or why they should ever differ.
SOCRATES: And shall I explain this wonder to you?
MENO: Do tell me.
SOCRATES: You would not wonder if you had ever observed the images of Daedalus; but perhaps you have not got them in your country?
MENO: What have they to do with the question?
SOCRATES: Because they require to be fastened in order to keep them, and if they are not fastened they will play truant and run away.
MENO: Well, what of that?
SOCRATES: I mean to say that they are not very valuable possessions if they are at liberty, for they will walk off like runaway slaves; but when fastened, they are of great value, for they are really beautiful works of art. Now this is an illustration of the nature of true opinions: while they abide with us they are beautiful and fruitful, but they run away out of the human soul, and do not remain long, and therefore they are not of much value until they are fastened by the tie of the cause; and this fastening of them, friend Meno, is recollection, as you and I have agreed to call it. But when they are bound, in the first place, they have the nature of knowledge; and, in the second place, they are abiding. And this is why knowledge is more honourable and excellent than true opinion, because fastened by a chain.

Two things about this are tripping me up, and what I'm really after is help working through them, not a general summary of "the Meno problem." First, Socrates spends the whole Larisa example getting Meno to agree that someone with a merely true opinion is exactly as good a guide as someone who actually knows the way — and then, what feels like a sentence later, concludes that knowledge is nonetheless "more honourable and excellent," on the grounds that true opinion runs away like the statues while knowledge stays fastened. It looks to me like he has quietly swapped the question "which belief gets you to Larisa" for the question "which belief will still be there tomorrow," and I can't decide whether that's a legitimate way of resolving Meno's puzzlement or whether it just proves a different, easier claim — that knowledge is more durable — while leaving the original claim, that knowledge is more valuable, sitting there unexplained. Second, Socrates identifies the "fastening" with recollection, "as you and I have agreed to call it," clearly leaning on the earlier slave-boy scene, but I don't see how being able to state the reason why something is true is the same operation as recollecting a truth the soul supposedly already possessed — he seems to just assert that identification rather than argue for it. Is there a step connecting the two that I'm not seeing, or is this a genuine gap in the argument that others have pointed out? I'd appreciate a close reading of the logic here rather than a general overview of Plato's epistemology.
