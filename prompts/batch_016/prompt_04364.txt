Compare G. E. M. Anscombe's argument in "Mr. Truman's Degree" against honoring Truman for the atomic bombings with Michael Walzer's treatment of the same bombings under his "supreme emergency" doctrine in *Just and Unjust Wars*. Anscombe's case rests on the doctrine of double effect and a strict, intention-based prohibition on killing the innocent, so that no calculation of lives saved can excuse the bombings, whereas Walzer's framework explicitly permits overriding the rules of war when a community's very existence is at stake -- an allowance he uses to justify Britain's early wartime bombing of German cities -- yet he still concludes that Hiroshima and Nagasaki do not meet this threshold. Now suppose Japan's continued resistance had posed a genuine existential threat to the Allied war effort, comparable to the threat Nazi Germany posed to Britain. Would either philosopher's verdict on the bombings change, and what does your answer reveal about the structural difference between Anscombe's absolutist deontology and Walzer's threshold-based account?
