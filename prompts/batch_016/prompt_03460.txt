I'm a backend engineer, and a few months back I went down a rabbit hole on axiomatic bargaining theory for a side project on fair resource allocation between competing services (the kind of thing "proportional fairness" in network scheduling is built on) — the Nash bargaining solution, the Kalai–Smorodinsky (KS) solution, and that whole family of results that start from a feasible set of payoff pairs and a disagreement point and try to pick the "fair" one. I'd mostly forgotten the details until two friends of mine, Marcus and Elif, who co-built a small CLI tool a few years ago that unexpectedly took off after a Hacker News post, got into a surprisingly technical argument about how to split the payout from a company that now wants to acquire it. They'd apparently read the same explainer, because they started citing the actual theorems at each other, and I ended up as the confused bystander on the call.

Here's roughly how the exchange went. (1) Marcus opened by proposing they just use the Nash bargaining solution — the one that maximizes the product of each person's gain over their walk-away position — because it depends only on the deals actually on the table plus their fallback options, not on any hypothetical structure neither of them would pick; he said this "independence of irrelevant alternatives" property meant the acquirer couldn't shift what's fair just by dangling extra options on the term sheet. (2) Elif pointed out that the acquirer had, in fact, just added a new earn-out track that made Marcus's menu of options strictly better — for every payout level of hers, he could now do at least as well as before, and sometimes noticeably better — while her own options were untouched; yet recomputing the Nash solution gave Marcus a smaller share than the original offer had. She said any fairness concept that can penalize you for your own options improving is broken, and that this is exactly what the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution is built to prevent, since it's pegged to each person's best-case ("ideal") payoff and guarantees that a rising ideal point never leaves you worse off. (3) Marcus's comeback was that KS has the mirror-image problem: because it's built around that ideal point, it's hostage to options nobody would ever actually choose. He pointed out that if the acquirer tacked on some lopsided clause — say, a huge upfront sum for Elif paired with Marcus getting just his bare walk-away value and no upside — that clause alone would move the KS recommendation for both of them, despite changing nothing about what either of them would ever agree to; Nash's solution, being a straightforward maximization over the whole set, can't be perturbed that way by an option that was never going to win. (4) Elif granted the mechanism but said "nobody would ever choose it" is doing more work than Marcus wants it to: they can't fully anticipate every clause the acquirer's lawyers might float next, so a solution that behaves well only if you've correctly pre-identified all the "irrelevant" options isn't obviously more trustworthy than one that behaves well only as long as nobody's opportunities happen to expand. (5) The call ended with Marcus muttering that if the deal were just a single lump sum to split, none of this would even come up — the two solutions only pull apart because of how each of them converts fundamentally different deal structures (upfront cash versus royalty streams) into personal utility in the first place, and that prior question was one neither of them had actually settled.

I'd like your help sorting through this properly, since I don't have the formal background to tell genuine insight from half-remembered coursework here. Could you go through points (1) through (5) individually, say who has the better of it at each one (rather than just declaring an overall winner), and flag anything you think both Marcus and Elif are missing over the course of the exchange? I'd also be mildly curious whether there's a more defensible way for two people in exactly this situation to actually land on a number, but the point-by-point verdict is what I'm mainly after.
