I've spent years reading rationalist blogs and forums, and one lesson that has stuck with me is that a dispute which looks like a disagreement about facts or values is sometimes actually a collision with a hidden mathematical constraint that no amount of good faith or cleverness can overcome. I suspect I'm watching a real-life instance of this play out at work, and I'd like help figuring out whether that diagnosis is correct, and if so, what follows from it.

My company recently deployed a machine-learning model that scores loan applicants by predicted default risk; the score determines who gets fast-tracked for approval and who gets flagged for manual underwriting review. Our compliance lead audited the model and found it to be well calibrated: among applicants it scores as, say, a 15 percent default risk, roughly 15 percent actually go on to default, and this holds up separately within each of the two applicant groups we track internally (call them Group A and Group B). She considers this the gold standard of fairness and wants to ship the model unchanged. Our ethics review committee ran a different analysis and found that among applicants who ultimately did not default, those in Group B were flagged for manual review at a noticeably higher rate than those in Group A. They consider this straightforward discrimination and want the decision thresholds adjusted until the flagging rates match across groups. When I asked why we couldn't simply satisfy both conditions at once, our lead engineer explained, a little wearily, that doing so is provably impossible in general, short of an unrealistically perfect model, whenever the two groups have different underlying default rates — which ours do, and, as far as anyone can tell, always will. Each side now seems quietly convinced that the other is either statistically confused or arguing in bad faith.

I try to steelman both positions before forming a view, but I can't locate an actual crux between them, which makes me suspect I'm missing something structural rather than merely rhetorical. Is one of these two notions of fairness simply the correct one, with the other a kind of statistical mirage? Or is this really a disagreement about values dressed up in the language of statistics — and if so, is there any principled way to say which value should win when they conflict? I'd rather have a rigorous, opinionated answer than a diplomatic "both sides have a point," including, if you think it's warranted, an argument that one side is simply wrong.
