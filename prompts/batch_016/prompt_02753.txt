For a seminar this term pairing F. H. Bradley's *Appearance and Reality* with Russell's early remarks on the unity of the proposition in *The Principles of Mathematics*, I'm trying to reconstruct Bradley's regress argument and keep getting tangled in the following passage, from the end of §3 of Chapter III, "Relation and Quality" (1893), along with part of its footnote:

"But how the relation can stand to the qualities is, on the other side, unintelligible. If it is nothing to the qualities, then they are not related at all; and, if so, as we saw, they have ceased to be qualities, and their relation is a nonentity. But if it is to be something to them, then clearly we now shall require a new connecting relation. For the relation hardly can be the mere adjective of one or both of its terms; or, at least, as such it seems indefensible. And, being something itself, if it does not itself bear a relation to the terms, in what intelligible way will it succeed in being anything to them? But here again we are hurried off into the eddy of a hopeless process, since we are forced to go on finding new relations without end. The links are united by a link, and this bond of union is a link which also has two ends; and these require each a fresh link to connect them with the old."

In the footnote attached to this passage, Bradley adds: "The relation is not the adjective of one term, for, if so, it does not relate. Nor for the same reason is it the adjective of each term taken apart, for then again there is no relation between them. Nor is the relation their common property, for then what keeps them apart?"

Could you help me reconstruct the argument precisely enough to explain it in my own words on an exam? I'd especially like help with two things: first, walking through exactly which ways of conceiving how a relation connects to its terms the footnote is ruling out, and why appealing to a further connecting relation lands us in a genuinely vicious regress rather than just one more unproblematic relational fact; second, explaining why this passage, although officially about qualities and relations in general, is standardly taken to bear on what unifies a proposition like "Socrates is wise" into a single truth-evaluable claim rather than a mere list consisting of Socrates and wisdom, and whether Bradley's argument actually succeeds in showing that no relation could do that unifying job.
