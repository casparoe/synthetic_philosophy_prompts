I'm a PhD student in political theory (working mostly on early modern stuff) and I'm TAing a seminar this semester where we just spent two weeks on Spinoza. For my own paper I've been slowly working through the Tractatus Politicus, which is great fun except for the ending, which is honestly kind of a disaster and I can't tell if that's Spinoza's fault or mine. Quick context in case it's useful: the whole treatise builds its case for democracy out of the idea that political right just IS power/capacity (potentia) and nothing more - no extra moral layer on top - which is exactly why he thinks democracy is the regime closest to the equal right everyone already has in the state of nature. So I was expecting the chapter on democracy to be the payoff of that whole argument. Instead, chapter 11 (the last thing he wrote before he died, the ms. literally breaks off mid-topic right after this) ends like so - quoting the Elwes translation:

"But, perhaps, someone will ask, whether women are under men's authority by nature or institution? For if it has been by mere institution, then we had no reason compelling us to exclude women from government. But if we consult experience itself, we shall find that the origin of it is in their weakness. For there has never been a case of men and women reigning together, but wherever on the earth men are found, there we see that men rule, and women are ruled, and that on this plan, both sexes live in harmony. But on the other hand, the Amazons... did not suffer men to stop in their country... But if by nature women were equal to men, and were equally distinguished by force of character and ability, in which human power and therefore human right chiefly consist; surely among nations so many and different some would be found, where both sexes rule alike... And since this is nowhere the case, one may assert with perfect propriety, that women have not by nature equal right with men... But if we further reflect upon human passions, how men, in fact, generally love women merely from the passion of lust... we shall easily see that men and women cannot rule alike without great hurt to peace. But of this enough."

What I can't figure out is what kind of argument this even is by Spinoza's own lights. He raises exactly the question I want him to ask (nature vs. institution!), and he has a whole apparatus elsewhere - the theory of the affects, the stuff on superstition and imagination in the TTP - that seems custom built to explain how a merely customary arrangement could get mistaken for a natural one. Instead he just appeals to the historical record as if brute precedent settles a modal question about capacitiy, then tacks on this bit about lust and jealousy that doesn't obviously connect to a "right = power" framework at all. Can you help me reconstruct the argument as charitably as you can and then say where exactly (if anywhere) it breaks down given his other commitments? Also curious whether you think it's just a contingent biographical accident that the manuscript stops here, or whether there's something almost symptomatic about the theory unraveling at exactly this point - or if that's me reading way too much into an unfinished draft.
