So I've been falling down a rabbit hole for a piece I'm working on about the ethics of soldiering, partly sparked by all the online arguing over whether ordinary soldiers fighting in unjust wars are personally doing something wrong, and partly because I finally sat down and read big chunks of Walzer's Just and Unjust Wars. Theres this doctrine he lays out, the "moral equality of combatants," which says soldiers on both sides of a war have equal rights to kill each other and equal claim to the protections of the laws of war, totally independent of whether their side's cause is just -- so the guy fighting for the aggressor and the guy defending his home are on the same moral footing as long as they both fight "clean." A philosopher friend then pointed me to Jeff McMahan's work (Killing in War), which has spent something like the better part of two decades arguing this is basically a comforting myth: unless your cause is just, you're not actually licensed to kill the other guy no matter how by-the-book you fight, because you're posing an unjustified threat rather than defending against one.

I can't tell who's actually right and its been bugging me all week. Could you write me a dialogue between a committed defender of the traditional "equality" view and a committed McMahan-style revisionist, working through this using one specific case: a conscripted rifleman on the unjust side of a war, who never touches a civilian, follows every rule of engagement to the letter, and only ever kills enemy soldiers in ordinary firefights. I want the revisionist to actually defend, straight-faced, that this guy is doing something morally equivalent to murder every single time he pulls the trigger -- and I want the traditionalist to actually defend the equal-rights view against the obvious comeback that it's just a convenient legal convention with no real moral weight behind it. Neither one should fold or dodge the other's strongest point here; make them genuinely engage with each other's arguments. Bonus points if it gets into why McMahan apparently still doesn't want that rifleman prosecuted despite thinking what he's doing is wrong, or how the traditionalist explains why a soldier ever counts as having "agreed" to become legitimately killable in the first place.
