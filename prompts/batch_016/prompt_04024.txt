I cover science and ideas for a living, and I keep running up against a strange collision between cosmology and ethics. Cosmologists increasingly treat a spatially infinite universe, or an eternally inflating multiverse, as a live possibility, and philosophers have pointed out that this quietly unsettles utilitarian aggregation: if the world already contains infinite quantities of good and bad, summing well-being across people or across time no longer converges to a determinate total, and can even be rearranged to sum to different totals depending on the order in which contributions are added. Nick Bostrom calls the result "infinitarian paralysis" — since no finite action could change an already-infinite total, every choice becomes morally equivalent.

The proposed fixes amount to a small cottage industry: importing the "overtaking" and "catching-up" criteria that economists devised for infinite-horizon growth models; discounting well-being by time so the sum converges; restricting moral aggregation to whatever region of the universe we can causally affect; abandoning a cardinal sum altogether for Pareto-style comparisons between infinite worlds (the approach Amanda Askell has done the most to formalize and stress-test); and, in more recent work, using hyperreal- or surreal-valued utilities to keep infinite quantities of value comparable in fine-grained ways.
