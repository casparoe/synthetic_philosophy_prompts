This term I'm taking a seminar on formal epistemology, and this week's session was devoted to Bas van Fraassen's "Belief and the Will" and the Reflection Principle he defends there by means of a diachronic Dutch Book argument. As I understand it, the principle holds that my present credence in some proposition A, conditional on the supposition that my credence in A at a later time will equal some value r, must itself equal r — so I am effectively being asked to treat my own anticipated future opinion the way Lewis's Principal Principle tells me to treat a known objective chance: as a kind of "expert" function to which my current credence should simply defer. What tripped up several of us in the discussion afterward was how this is supposed to survive the standard objections once you allow that a future credence might arise from something other than calm conditionalization on new evidence — cases of anticipated memory loss, sleep deprivation, emotional upheaval, manipulation, or simply foreseeable bias. The Ulysses-and-the-Sirens case our instructor raised seemed to show that sometimes the rational thing to do is precisely to bind your future self against the very opinion it will go on to hold, which looks like an outright violation of Reflection rather than a mere restriction of its scope.

Given how much qualification the principle apparently needs in order to survive cases like these, I would like to understand what is actually left of it once those qualifications are in place. Could you set out what you take to be the three or four most important implications of a properly qualified Reflection Principle for how much epistemic authority I should grant my own future judgments in ordinary life — for instance, when deciding whether to defer to a calmer, better-rested version of myself rather than my present view, as opposed to when I should instead pre-commit now against whatever that future self will end up thinking? I am especially interested in whether the principle, once restricted to cases of anticipated rational updating, still does any genuine work in telling these two kinds of cases apart, or whether it collapses into the far more mundane advice simply to trust whichever credence is better evidenced.
