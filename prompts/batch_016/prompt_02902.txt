My company quietly rolled out one of those AI coding/analysis tools a couple months ago, and two people on my team got "restructured" out last week basically becuase the tool now does most of what they used to do. It's sent me into a low-grade spiral about technology and existential risk generally, and I went down a rabbit hole reading old essays by people who were thinking seriously about this stuff before it was trendy. That's how I ended up at John von Neumann's 1955 Fortune piece "Can We Survive Technology?" — I'd seen it name-dropped in a couple of AI-safety blog posts, so instead of just reading paraphrases I tracked down an actual copy of the text (reprinted from his Collected Works).

Most of the essay I can follow fine — the nuclear stuff, the weather-control stuff, the famous "for progress there is no cure" line everyone quotes. But there's an earlier passage I've now read about five times and still don't feel like I fully get. Here it is, two paragraphs from fairly early in the piece (skipping a stretch in between where he mostly just restates that "we are running out of room" and that the crisis is "inherent" rather than accidental — not trying to cherry-pick, just keeping this a reasonable length):

"In the first half of this century the accelerating industrial revolution encountered an absolute limitation—not on technological progress as such but on an essential safety factor. This safety factor, which had permitted the industrial revolution to roll on from the mid-eighteenth to the early twentieth century, was essentially a matter of geographical and political Lebensraum: an ever broader geographical scope for technological activities, combined with an ever broader political integration of the world. Within this expanding framework it was possible to accommodate the major tensions created by technological progress. [...] In all its stages the industrial revolution consisted of making available more and cheaper energy, more and easier controls of human actions and reactions, and more and faster communications. Each development increased the effectiveness of the other two. All three factors increased the speed of performing large-scale operations—industrial, mercantile, political, and migratory. But throughout the development, increased speed did not so much shorten time requirements of processes as extend the areas of the earth affected by them. The reason is clear. Since most time scales are fixed by human reaction times, habits, and other physiological and psychological factors, the effect of the increased speed of technological processes was to enlarge the size of units — political, organizational, economic, and cultural — affected by technological operations. That is, instead of performing the same operations as before in less time, now larger-scale operations were performed in the same time. This important evolution has a natural limit, that of the earth's actual size. The limit is now being reached, or at least closely approached."

Three things I could use help with. First: what does he actually mean by "safety factor" here — is that a standard term from somewhere, or is he basically coining it to mean something like "release valve for tension"? Second, the logic in the second half genuinely twists my brain a bit — the claim that increased speed didn't shorten time requirements but instead "extended the areas affected," supposedly because time scales are pinned down by human reaction times. I follow the individual sentences but can't quite reconstruct the chain of reasoning that gets him from "processes got faster" to "therefore the binding constraint is literally the size of the planet." Could you walk through that argument step by step in plain language? And third, kind of a side note, but it's bugging me: he uses "Lebensraum" totally matter-of-factly for this expansion mechanism, which seems like an odd word choice for a Hungarian-born Jewish scientist who left Europe largely because of the politics that word is associated with, writing barely a decade after the war ended — is that just neutral geopolitical jargon at the time, or is something more deliberate going on? And relatedly, if his whole "running out of room" framing depends on dangers being geographically bound the way nukes or factories are, does that framing even transfer to something like modern AI, which doesn't obviously need physical room to scale — or am I just stretching a 70-year-old essay past what it can bear?
