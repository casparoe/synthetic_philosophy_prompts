I've been reading LessWrong and Astral Codex Ten for well over a decade, and a handful of us who first met in the comment sections now run a small biweekly meetup devoted to working through classic decision-theory puzzles. At our most recent weekend retreat, one of the evening sessions had attendees pair off to actually play a few rounds of the centipede game for token stakes, ahead of a scheduled talk on backward induction: the toy game, played over a short fixed number of rounds, in which two players alternate between "passing" a shared pot to their opponent (which grows it) and "taking" it (which ends the game immediately and awards the taker the larger share of whatever is currently on the table). Marcus, one of our regulars, was paired against another attendee who passed for several rounds before he finally took; he ended up with more than he would have received by taking on the very first move, though considerably less than full cooperation through the final round would have paid both of them.

The exercise turned into a running argument between Marcus and another regular, Lena, that has continued ever since and that neither of them will let go of, so I'm hoping you can adjudicate it properly. (1) Marcus's opening position was that backward induction settles the matter unambiguously: since whoever faces the last move always does strictly better by taking than passing, that logic unwinds all the way back to the first move, so taking immediately is the only choice compatible with both players being rational and knowing it, and his opponent's repeated passing was simply a mistake he happened to benefit from. (2) Lena replied that this can't be the whole story, because the instant you actually observe a pass, you already know that the common knowledge of rationality driving Marcus's argument has failed, so invoking that same argument to justify taking on the very next move is question-begging: you would be reasoning from a premise the situation has already falsified. (3) Marcus answered that this is exactly what subgame perfection is designed to handle: requiring optimal play at every node, whether or not it is ever reached, is precisely how the theory pins down rational behavior even after a "surprising" deviation, so Lena's objection rests on a misunderstanding of what the solution concept demands. (4) Lena said that just relocates the problem: demanding optimality at every node still presupposes an expectation that one's opponent will keep behaving rationally afterward, and that expectation is exactly what a pass should undermine. She added that this isn't just her own hobbyhorse -- it's literally why philosophers call this "the backward induction paradox," a label Pettit and Sugden gave it in the 1980s, and why Aumann's later attempt to prove formally that common knowledge of rationality still delivers backward induction was met with direct published rebuttals from both Stalnaker and Binmore, who argued his result depended on his own specific and contestable way of modeling counterfactual belief revision. (5) Marcus conceded that the epistemics of off-path belief are murkier than any standard textbook treatment lets on, but maintained there is still one clean, local fact none of that touches: whoever actually faces the final-round choice, knowing it is the final round, is strictly better off taking than passing, so at minimum that much of his original claim stands regardless of how the earlier rounds should be reasoned about. (6) Lena's closing move was that this "clean local fact" does not get Marcus what he wants from it: she pointed to the McKelvey and Palfrey experimental work on centipede games, which explains exactly this kind of persistent early passing by modeling the situation as an incomplete-information game in which a small, commonly known probability that some players are genuine altruists makes it strictly rational for even a purely self-interested player to mimic that type for a while before cashing out -- meaning Marcus's opponent may well have been playing a fully rational strategy in the game they were actually in, and that it was Marcus's framing of them as simply "mistaken," not his math about the final round, that was the real error.

Could you go through Marcus's and Lena's six points in order and say, for each, whether it is basically correct, partly right, or mistaken, and why? Then stand back and address the underlying question directly: does backward induction really establish that taking immediately is the uniquely rational choice in a fixed-length, complete-information centipede game, and what does each of them still seem to be missing about the other's position? Please close with a one-line verdict in the form "Verdict: Marcus" or "Verdict: Lena," for whichever of the two of them you think has the stronger overall grip on the situation.
