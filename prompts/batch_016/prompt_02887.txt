In discussions of wild-animal welfare that circulate in rationalist and effective-altruist circles, it's often pointed out that most wild animals are r-strategists: insects, fish, and other species that produce enormous numbers of offspring, the vast majority of which die within days of birth from starvation, predation, or exposure. Some writers in this tradition draw out what looks like the logical upshot: if these short lives contain more suffering than positive wellbeing, then shrinking wild-animal populations -- including via habitat loss -- should reduce total suffering in expectation, and conservation shouldn't be treated as straightforwardly good. Others in the same tradition resist this, citing ecological uncertainty about the actual effects of intervention, a moral distinction between failing to bring a life into existence and destroying the habitat of animals who already exist, and the risks of acting unilaterally on contested and empirically shaky premises. Write an essay assessing whether a serious commitment to reducing wild-animal suffering does in fact commit you to favoring habitat destruction over preservation, at least in high-fecundity ecosystems. Weigh the strongest arguments on each side and defend a definite conclusion; a qualified verdict (e.g., "only under further assumptions X and Y") is fine, provided you argue for it.
