Below is a passage from Gongsun Long's "White Horse Dialogue" (白馬論), a short Warring States-era Chinese text associated with the School of Names and organized around the thesis "a white horse is not a horse." Earlier in the dialogue, the case for that thesis rests on the claim that "horse" names a shape while "white" names a color, so that "white horse," naming both, cannot simply be "horse." Later on, after several further rounds with an objector, the text offers this further argument for the same conclusion (translated fairly literally):

"'White,' by itself, is not fixed (定, ding) to any particular white thing; one may set the particular aside and it still makes sense. But 'white horse' means: white as fixed to a particular white thing. What is fixed to a particular white thing is no longer white. Now 'horse' involves no excluding (去, qu) among colors, so a brown or black horse can equally well serve as an answer to it. 'White horse' does exclude among colors: brown and black horses are excluded on account of their color, so only a white horse can serve as an answer to it. What does not exclude is not the same as what does exclude. Hence I say: a white horse is not a horse."

Please reconstruct the argument on its own terms. What is the distinction between "fixed" and "unfixed" white doing here, and why is fixed white supposed to no longer count as white? How is that point meant to connect to the closing claim about what "horse" and "white horse" each exclude? And does this argument really stand on its own, or is it just the earlier shape-versus-color point restated in different vocabulary?
