Quick one while I'm rebuilding the tyrannicide unit for my history of political philosophy course. For years I've opened it with the claim that Aquinas already gets us about 90% of the way to the "radical" early modern position: since a ruler who governs purely for his own benefit rather than the common good thereby forfeits any claim to legitimate authority, he's not really a king anymore but just a private enemy of the community, and so any ordinary citizen is free to kill him without waiting around for courts, councils, or anyone else to act first. I usually sell it to students as "Aquinas basically pre-loaded the Monarchomachs and Juan de Mariana's De Rege three hundred years early, he just wrapped it in more cautious Aristotelian packaging," and it's always a fun way to make the medieval stuff feel less stuffy on a Monday morning.

My TA sat in on section this week and said afterward that she thought I was flattening something important in the De Regimine Principum material, though she couldn't quite put her finger on what. I've been teaching it this way since grad school though, so I'm definately inclined to trust my own read over her hunch, but I'd rather sanity-check it with you before I lock in the slides. Is "any private citizen may licitly kill a tyrant" actually a fair one-line summary of Aquinas's considered position? And if it is, can you point me to two or three more passages beyond the usual "brave men" bit that I could put up to really drive home the parallel with Mariana for students?
