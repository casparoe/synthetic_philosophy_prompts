I mostly write novels, but I've been invited to contribute to a small anthology of short fiction about crises of certainty, and I keep circling back to a premise from the philosophy of mathematics that I don't think has been given full justice in fiction. Gödel's second incompleteness theorem tells us that no consistent formal system strong enough to encode arithmetic can ever prove its own consistency from the inside; consistency has to be taken essentially on faith, or argued for from some outside vantage point that isn't obviously more secure than the system itself. This isn't merely an abstract worry: Frege watched Russell dismantle the foundational system of his life's work with a single letter in 1902, and as recently as 2011 the logician Edward Nelson circulated what he believed was a genuine proof that Peano Arithmetic itself was inconsistent, only for Terence Tao and Daniel Tausk to independently spot a fatal gap within days, at which point Nelson graciously withdrew the claim. What has stayed with me is the counterfactual: what if, next time, nobody finds the gap?

Could you write me a short story of around 1,500 words built on that premise? I know Greg Egan has already staked out adjacent territory in "Luminous," where mathematicians discover that arithmetic breaks down past a certain scale and uncover an entire rival number system on the other side, complete with a corporation eager to weaponize the find; I'd like something quieter and more contained than that. Picture instead a proof of inconsistency in the ordinary, everyday axioms of arithmetic (or of ZFC set theory, if you'd rather work with that) that circulates among a skeptical community of logicians, gets checked and re-checked, and simply refuses to break, with the story centered on one or two characters who have to live with what it means. I'm less interested in external spectacle than in what happens to a working mathematician's sense of what a proof even is, once the principle of explosion means the system can now be made to "prove" both its own consistency and its own inconsistency in the same afternoon. You're welcome to invent your protagonist rather than fictionalizing Nelson directly, but I'd like the story to stay honest to the real logical stakes of the situation rather than treating the axioms as a loose metaphor for doubt in general.
