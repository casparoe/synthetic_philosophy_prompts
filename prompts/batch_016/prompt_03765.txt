I'm preparing a graduate seminar on formal epistemology and want to state one subtle point about the value of information with real precision rather than hand-waving over it. Good's theorem tells us that an ideal Bayesian agent facing a fixed decision problem should always be willing to consult a cost-free, relevant signal before acting, since doing so can never lower expected utility. Yet Hirshleifer's classic result in economics shows that the mere public arrival of information can leave every agent in an economy worse off ex ante, by destroying insurance and risk-sharing arrangements that depended on the relevant uncertainty persisting a while longer. Both are claims about how ideally rational Bayesian agents should value cost-free information, so on the surface they look flatly inconsistent. Please explain exactly why they are not: identify what Good's theorem holds fixed that Hirshleifer's scenario allows to vary, and show how that difference in scope, rather than any difference in the rationality of the agents involved, generates the appearance of paradox. I would rather not simply be told that Hirshleifer's setting involves multiple interacting agents; I want the mechanism that connects that fact to the breakdown of Good's guarantee.
