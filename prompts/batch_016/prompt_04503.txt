I write novels, and this past spring I agreed to serve as one of three judges for a small independent press's debut-fiction prize. During deliberations, two of my fellow judges, Nora and Felix, fell into a disagreement that has stayed with me since.

1. Weighing the shortlist, Nora argued for placing one novel first partly because its author has spent over a decade being turned down by every house she approached, and, in Nora's words, this might be "her last real shot" — rewarding the book would be a small act of justice.

2. Felix objected that this conflates two different questions: whether helping this author would be good, and whether her novel is the best one under consideration. The value of an outcome does not make a judgment about the book's quality true.

3. Nora replied that Felix's "the book itself, nothing else" standard is not actually the standard they had agreed to apply: the prize's own charter states that it exists "to sustain under-recognized voices," so unlike some invented threat that has nothing to do with literature, need is written directly into the criteria before them.

4. Felix answered that this only relocates the problem: if "would help a struggling career" and "is the better-written novel" can each independently secure first place, the prize can be won on grounds that have nothing to do with the prose, while every reader downstream will still take the result as a verdict on the writing — which he called a kind of institutional bad faith, charter or no charter.

5. Nora countered that Felix had, minutes earlier, placed his own former student's novel second without once mentioning that he had taught her, so the real asymmetry is that his favoritism gets to remain invisible while hers is fully on the record — which, if anything, makes her reasoning the more honest of the two.

Neither of them asked what I thought, so I am asking you instead. Please evaluate the exchange move by move, scoring each numbered move from -2 (clearly the wrong move) to 2 (clearly the right move), given as a JSON object of the form {"1": <score>, "2": <score>, "3": <score>, "4": <score>, "5": <score>}. After the JSON, briefly justify each score, then close with a short paragraph on what you think Nora and Felix are both missing — and, if this kind of dispute has a name in the philosophical literature, what it is.
