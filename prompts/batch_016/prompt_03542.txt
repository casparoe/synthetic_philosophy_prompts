I'm taking a course on early modern political philosophy, and this week's seminar covered Pascal's Pensées, in particular the fragments on justice, force, and what he calls the "orders." Afterward, two people in my discussion group got into an argument about it that was never really resolved.

1. Sana said Pascal is basically a debunker: his remark that, unable to make what is just strong, "we have made what is strong just," shows he thinks political "justice" just is whatever force has settled on and dressed up afterward.
2. Owen objected that this can't be the whole story, since Pascal also calls force without justice "tyrannical" — a real verdict, which only makes sense if force can fail some standard.
3. Sana replied that Owen was over-reading this: Pascal's point that three degrees of latitude can reverse an entire jurisprudence, and a meridian decides the truth, treats justice's content as shifting with power and custom, not as a fact force merely obscures — so "tyrannical" just marks force that hasn't yet won the argument.
4. Owen countered with Pascal's separate claim that tyranny means wanting, through one kind of power, what can only rightly be had through another — his example being the strong and the beautiful foolishly fighting over a mastery that belongs to different "orders" entirely. On Owen's reading, this gives "tyrannical" a precise sense: force turns tyrannical exactly when it reaches for jurisdiction belonging to the order where charity and true justice operate. So justice is real for Pascal, just categorically walled off from politics.
5. Sana answered that this only relocates the problem: if genuine justice lives in an order politics can't reach, that leaves anyone actually trying to govern with nothing beyond force and custom — a theological escape hatch, not a political resource.
6. Owen said it's not nothing: taking that gap seriously should make us permanently suspicious of any political actor who is certain their order is the true one, which is exactly what Pascal thinks breeds fanaticism — so the doctrine still counsels real humility, even if it isn't a theory of justice.
