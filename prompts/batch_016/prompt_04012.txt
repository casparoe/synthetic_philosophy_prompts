I spent my career as an experimental physicist, and only in retirement have I begun reading philosophy in earnest. I knew Henry Sidgwick chiefly as a utilitarian moral philosopher, so I was surprised to discover how much of his later life went into founding and running the Society for Psychical Research. In his inaugural presidential address to the Society (Proceedings of the SPR, vol. 1, 1882), having urged members to keep amassing testimony and experimental instances, he writes:

"We must keep 'pegging away,' as Lincoln said; we must accumulate fact upon fact, and add experiment upon experiment, and, I should say, not wrangle too much with incredulous outsiders about the conclusiveness of any one, but trust to the mass of evidence for conviction. The highest degree of demonstrative force that we can obtain out of any single record of investigation is, of course, limited by the trustworthiness of the investigator. We have done all that we can when the critic has nothing left to allege except that the investigator is in the trick. But when he has nothing else left to allege he will allege that."

I can't quite work out what standard of proof Sidgwick thinks he is holding himself to here. The opening sentences suggest that sheer accumulated weight of evidence will eventually overcome scientific incredulity, yet the closing two sentences seem to admit that a sufficiently determined critic always has an evidence-proof fallback available, no matter how much testimony accumulates. As a physicist, this looks to me like conflating two different kinds of error: piling up independent trials reduces random error, but repeatedly invoking the same fallback explanation — that this investigator, too, was "in the trick" — looks more like a shared, non-averaging-out bias that no amount of additional trials can wash out. Is that a fair diagnosis of where the argument runs into trouble, or am I importing a distinction that doesn't really transfer to the logic of testimony? And either way, does the passage suggest that Sidgwick himself sensed his evidential programme could never, even in principle, satisfy its own stated criterion?
