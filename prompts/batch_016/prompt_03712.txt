I'm a PhD student in philosophy, currently drafting a dissertation chapter on historical arguments against treating ideas as a species of property, and I've run into a snag with a text I thought I already understood. Anyone working on this topic eventually runs into the same few sentences from Thomas Jefferson's 1813 letter to Isaac McPherson — the candle-and-taper passage — which gets quoted constantly in the philosophical-foundations-of-IP literature and in casebooks, almost always trimmed down to the single sentence about the taper. I recently learned that someone has devoted an entire law review article (Jeremy Sheff's 2020 piece, aptly titled "Jefferson's Taper") to unpacking just that one metaphor, which finally prompted me to go back and read the whole surrounding paragraph instead of the sound bite. Now I'm stuck on how its moving parts actually fit together, and I'd appreciate help thinking it through. Here is the passage, in Jefferson's own occasionally archaic prose:

"It has been pretended by some, (and in England especially,) that inventors have a natural and exclusive right to their inventions, and not merely for their own lives, but inheritable to their heirs. But while it is a moot question whether the origin of any kind of property is derived from nature at all, it would be singular to admit a natural and even an hereditary right to inventors. It is agreed by those who have seriously considered the subject, that no individual has, of natural right, a separate property in an acre of land, for instance. By an universal law, indeed, whatever, whether fixed or movable, belongs to all men equally and in common, is the property for the moment of him who occupies it, but when he relinquishes the occupation, the property goes with it. Stable ownership is the gift of social law, and is given late in the progress of society. It would be curious then, if an idea, the fugitive fermentation of an individual brain, could, of natural right, be claimed in exclusive and stable property. If nature has made any one thing less susceptible than all others of exclusive property, it is the action of the thinking power called an idea, which an individual may exclusively possess as long as he keeps it to himself; but the moment it is divulged, it forces itself into the possession of every one, and the receiver cannot dispossess himself of it. Its peculiar character, too, is that no one possesses the less, because every other possesses the whole of it. He who receives an idea from me, receives instruction himself without lessening mine; as he who lights his taper at mine, receives light without darkening me. That ideas should freely spread from one to another over the globe, for the moral and mutual instruction of man, and improvement of his condition, seems to have been peculiarly and benevolently designed by nature, when she made them, like fire, expansible over all space, without lessening their density in any point, and like the air in which we breathe, move, and have our physical being, incapable of confinement or exclusive appropriation. Inventions then cannot, in nature, be a subject of property. Society may give an exclusive right to the profits arising from them, as an encouragement to men to pursue ideas which may produce utility, but this may or may not be done, according to the will and convenience of the society, without claim or complaint from anybody."

What I can't pin down is the relationship between the two arguments Jefferson is actually running here, and what that relationship implies for how the passage ends. The first argument is completely general: no property at all is natural, not even land; every stable ownership claim, over anything whatsoever, is a late "gift of social law." The second argument is specific to ideas: even setting aside what's natural, ideas have a further, peculiar feature — non-rivalrous, "incapable of confinement," expansible like fire or air — that land conspicuously lacks. My question has two parts. First, if the fully general argument already establishes that nothing is naturally property, what independent work is the idea-specific argument doing? Is Jefferson pointing to a genuinely separate obstacle to propertizing ideas, over and above their simply not being natural property in the way land is not, or is the fire-and-air imagery mostly rhetorical reinforcement for a conclusion he has already secured on other grounds? Second, does his closing move — that society "may" grant exclusive profits in inventions "according to the will and convenience of the society," exactly as, on his own account, it did with land — implicitly concede that ideas are, for the purposes that actually matter (what a legal system may do), no different in kind from land after all? That would seem to sit oddly with the elaborate case he has just made for why ideas are unlike ordinary objects. I'd be grateful for help working out whether Jefferson's position here is genuinely coherent, and if so, how best to reconstruct it so that both arguments are doing real, non-redundant work.
