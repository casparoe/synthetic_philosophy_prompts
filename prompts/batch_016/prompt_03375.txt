I'm currently taking a seminar on formal epistemology, and this past week we worked through Bas van Fraassen's well-known "Judy Benjamin problem" as a test case for the limits of Bayesian updating. The setup, briefly: a soldier, Judy Benjamin, is dropped into a region divided into Red and Blue territory, each of which is further split into a Second Company area and a Headquarters Company area, so that she initially assigns equal credence (1/4) to being in each of the four resulting quadrants. Just before radio contact is lost, her own headquarters sends her a single conditional piece of information: they cannot tell her which territory she is in, but if she is in Red territory, she is three times more likely to be in the Headquarters area than the Second Company area. Nothing at all is said about Blue territory.

Our professor then walked us through the standard, unsettling result: if Judy updates by minimizing relative entropy subject to that constraint (the usual way of extending Jeffrey conditionalization to evidence that is not stated as new probabilities over a full partition), her posterior credence that she is in Blue territory rises above one half, purely on the strength of a message that was ostensibly only about Red territory. Van Fraassen himself joked that the result makes Judy look as though she has talked herself into believing she is safely away from enemy headquarters. I can follow the derivation, but several of us from the seminar have been arguing for days about what it actually shows: a genuine defect in relative-entropy updating, or a sign that the conditional message secretly does carry information bearing on Red versus Blue that our intuitions are simply missing.

To get some real intuitive purchase on this, rather than just re-deriving the numbers once more, I would like you to write me a short story that stays inside Judy's head as that message comes in and the radio cuts out. It should respect the exact bones of the puzzle — the four quadrants, the precise content of the conditional, the total silence about Blue territory — but the narrative itself, whether through her private reasoning, her exchanges with the rest of the platoon, or something else you devise, should do the work of making it feel obvious, and then strange again, why her confidence about Blue territory moves at all. I would rather you let the ending sit with that strangeness than resolve it too neatly.
