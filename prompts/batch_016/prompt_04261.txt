A friend and I spent much of a long drive back from a ten-day meditation retreat arguing about whether the Buddhist doctrine of anatta can really support everything the tradition wants to build on top of it, and neither of us managed to convince the other. The argument was prompted by an offhand comment from one of the retreat teachers about karma trailing us the way a shadow trails the body, inseparably, which struck me as hard to square with everything else we had been taught that week. My contention was roughly this: Buddhism explicitly rejects both eternalism (the view that there is a fixed, permanent soul) and annihilationism (the view that death is simply the end, full stop), and presents itself as charting a "middle way" between the two. But once you take anatta seriously, once you really accept that there is no unchanging self underlying the stream of aggregates, only a rapid succession of physical and mental events with no further fact of identity connecting them, in something like the way Parfit describes persons, I do not see what is left to distinguish that "middle way" from annihilationism in any sense that matters practically. If the person who performs an action and the person who supposedly reaps its karmic fruit, whether later in the same life or in some future rebirth, are not in any deep sense the same entity, then the doctrine of karma seems to be either quietly smuggling back in exactly the kind of enduring self anatta was meant to rule out, or else it is just a convenient fiction dressed up as metaphysics, with nothing left to explain why the person who acted should be the one who is, in any meaningful sense, rewarded or punished later.

My friend kept insisting that a reductionist view of the self is not the same thing as denying that persons or moral responsibility exist at all, but when I pressed her to say what, exactly, was doing the metaphysical work of preserving that distinction, she could not get much further than repeating that Buddhist philosophers "don't treat it as a contradiction." I would genuinely like a sharper answer than that. Is there a principled difference between the no-self doctrine and a kind of practical nihilism about persons once the metaphysics is followed through consistently, or was I actually the one begging the important questions in that conversation?
