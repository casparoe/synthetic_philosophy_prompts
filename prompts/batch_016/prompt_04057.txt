Quick one from a rabbit hole I fell into while interviewing an economist for a piece I'm working on about prediction markets and superforecasters. We got onto the question of why smart, well-meaning people manage to keep disagreeing for years — macroeconomists arguing forever about whether some deficit will be inflationary, that sort of thing — and she brought up Aumann's "agreeing to disagree" theorem. Her summary, roughly: two rational Bayesians who trust each other's honesty literally cannot end up with different probabilities for the same event once they've told each other what those probabilities are, because the disagreement itself is new information both sides have to update on, and that updating keeps going until the numbers match. She also dropped the phrase "common prior assumption" like it was a minor footnote, and I didn't want to interupt and look dumb, so I just nodded and scribbled it down without asking what it meant.

If I've got the theorem right, that seems like a genuinely enormous result — basically a mathematical proof that two people who are both truly rational and honest with each other can't sustainably disagree, which would mean that whenever it does happen in real life — economists, epidemiologists, pundits going at it for years on end — at least one side has to be dishonest about their reasoning, sitting on information they haven't shared, or fooling themselves. That fits a hunch I've had for a while, that a lot of public disagreement is theater rather than a genuine clash of honestly-held beliefs. So: am I reading the theorem right? And is the whole "let's get our cruxes on the table" culture you see among forecasters and rationalists basically an attempt to force messy real arguments to run like the idealized ones in Aumann's proof, so that disagreement is supposed to dissolve the way the math says it must?
