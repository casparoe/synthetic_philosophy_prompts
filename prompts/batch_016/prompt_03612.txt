I've followed the rationalist blogosphere's engagement with program equilibrium since cousin_it's 2010 LessWrong post sketching what we'd now call FairBot, years before it grew into MIRI's paper on robust cooperation. The arc, in brief: Tennenholtz showed in 2004 that letting players submit source-code-reading programs instead of strategies recovers a folk theorem's worth of equilibria — mutual cooperation included — for the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, and FairBot/PrudentBot later used Löb's theorem to secure mutual cooperation with themselves and each other while staying unexploitable by straightforward defectors. Suppose that within a year or two I'm routinely delegating negotiations to a personal AI agent, and vendors start advertising that their agents' weights or reasoning traces are mutually inspectable by whatever counterpart agent they're bargaining against, on the theory that transparency begets cooperation. Please describe the most important implications of the program-equilibrium literature for how much stock I should put in that theory — specifically, whether mutual transparency reliably selects for cooperative equilibria, or merely enlarges the menu of equilibria, cooperative and exploitative alike, without telling us which one two arbitrary agents will land on.
