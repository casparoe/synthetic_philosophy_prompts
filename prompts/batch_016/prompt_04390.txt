For a group project on behavioral economics in my high school economics elective, my team presented the Ellsberg paradox: Urn A contains 50 red and 50 black balls; Urn B contains 100 balls, red and black in a ratio nobody knows. As part of the presentation we polled the class, and — like most people who see this setup — nearly everyone preferred betting on red from Urn A over red from Urn B, and also preferred betting on black from Urn A over black from Urn B. Afterward, two people on my team, Naomi and Theo, got into a real argument about whether that pattern is actually irrational, and I'd rather have your take on their exchange than mine.

1. Naomi said the pattern is incoherent: nothing distinguishes red from black inside Urn B, so if you had to name one probability for "red in B" it would have to be 0.5, the same as Urn A. But no single probability for Urn B can make sense of both strictly preferring Urn A on the red bet and strictly preferring Urn A on the black bet at once — which, she said, is exactly what it means for preferences to be incoherent rather than merely cautious.

2. Theo answered that he never needs one number for Urn B in the first place. He ranks a bet by its worst possible expected value across every ratio he can't rule out, rather than collapsing his ignorance into a single figure — which he says isn't a failure of reasoning, just a refusal to pretend he knows as much about Urn B as he does about Urn A.

3. Naomi then proposed a hedge: before the draw, flip a fair coin, unrelated to the urn, to decide whether Theo bets on red or on black in Urn B. She worked out that this coin-flip bet wins exactly half the time no matter what Urn B's true ratio is, making it objectively identical to the Urn A bet. If Theo wouldn't pay the same for both, she said, his caution was never really about the unknown ratio; and if he would, his rule looks suspiciously well-tailored to dissolve on exactly the case being used to test it.

4. Theo said the second option is the true one, and denied it's a patch: applied honestly to the coin-flip bet, his worst-case rule also comes out to exactly 50%, since the coin cancels out whatever skew Urn B has — the rule succeeding on a new case, not being rescued after the fact. He added that he'd read that when people are actually offered this exact coin-flip bet, most don't price it like a sure thing, which he took as a mark against his rule's accuracy as a theory of real behavior, but not against its standing as a correct answer to Naomi's original challenge.

Neither of them budged. Could you go through moves 1 through 4 and say who has the better of it at each step, rather than just naming an overall winner? I'm especially interested in whether Naomi's dilemma in move 3 really forces a bind, and whether Theo's closing distinction between a correct standard and an accurate description of real behavior genuinely lets him escape it. If you think they're both missing something, say what.
