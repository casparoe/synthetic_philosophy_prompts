A friend and I spent much of last night arguing about whether the theater can be a genuine school of virtue or is bound to corrupt whatever community stages it, and without quite meaning to, we ended up rehearsing the old quarrel between Rousseau and Diderot. I argued Rousseau's side from the Letter to d'Alembert on the Theatre: that no play, however upright its content, can close the gap between actor and spectator the way a real civic festival does, so that even the best-intentioned theater leaves citizens as passive onlookers rather than co-authors of their own shared life. My friend, defending Diderot, insisted that a seriously reformed drama can move an audience to genuine sympathy and thereby train real virtue in them, and that the actor's cool, disciplined control over his craft — the very self-mastery Diderot would later theorize in his Paradox of the Actor — is exactly what makes this possible, rather than the practiced insincerity Rousseau worried about. Neither of us gave any ground. Could you write a dialogue between Rousseau and Diderot that works through this specific disagreement: whether a reformed theater of Diderot's kind can actually overcome Rousseau's objection, or whether that objection targets the very form of theatrical spectatorship itself and so survives any reform of content? I'd like both figures to be equally sharp, genuinely responding to each other's arguments rather than simply restating their own positions.
