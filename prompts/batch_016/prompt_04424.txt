I teach a graduate seminar in the philosophy of mathematics, and this term we worked carefully through Gödel's construction of L and then Cohen's forcing technique, arriving at the now-standard result that the continuum hypothesis is independent of ZFC, provided ZFC itself is consistent. What proved much harder to settle, for my students and for me, is what that independence actually shows about the status of CH itself -- whether it is simply a further mathematical fact we haven't yet uncovered the right axioms to prove, or something more unsettling about the nature of the continuum, or of set-theoretic truth generally.

In office hours afterward, three of my strongest students staked out quite different positions, each of which has genuine defenders in the literature. One echoed Feferman's contention that CH simply is not a "definite" mathematical problem, on the grounds that the concept of an arbitrary set of natural numbers is too underdetermined to fix any fact of the matter about its cardinality. Another sided with the Gödel-Woodin tradition of searching for new axioms -- justified either intrinsically or by their fruitful consequences -- that might settle CH's truth value after all, whether through large cardinals, forcing axioms, or something in the spirit of Woodin's more recent "Ultimate L" program. The third found Hamkins's set-theoretic multiverse picture most persuasive: on that view, CH is perfectly determinate within any particular set-theoretic universe, but there is no single background universe for a "real" answer to live in, so the demand for one univocal fact of the matter is itself confused.

I would like your help thinking this through properly, rather than simply cataloguing the options the way I did in class. Please write an essay that addresses the question directly: does the continuum hypothesis have a determinate truth value, notwithstanding its independence from ZFC? Engage seriously with the strongest version of each of the three positions above -- feel free to redraw the map if you think it is missing something important -- explain what each gets right and where each is most vulnerable, and then commit to an actual conclusion rather than ending on a survey of the options. If your considered view amounts to some hybrid of the above, or to a position not represented at all among my students, that's fine, but state it explicitly and defend it.
