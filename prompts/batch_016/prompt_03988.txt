Three weeks ago I had an odd, almost comic brush with what I later realized was a real-world instance of Parfit's hitchhiker. My car died on an unlit stretch of rural road well after midnight, my phone had no charge, and the driver who eventually stopped agreed to take me into town only on my promise that I would withdraw cash from an ATM and pay him once we arrived, since he had no way of enforcing that promise afterward. I did pay him, without much hesitation, but I've caught myself replaying the moment since: standing at the machine with the money already in hand, I could not, strictly speaking, identify any further self-interested reason to hand it over. That nagging thought sent me back to Parfit's discussion, in Reasons and Persons, of what he calls "rational irrationality" — cases in which it seems rational to make oneself disposed to act against one's own future interest.

What I'd like from you is not a rehearsal of the thought experiment itself, which I know well by now, but a genuine comparison of two rival attempts to vindicate the driver-payer as actually rational, rather than merely irrational-but-fortunate. The first is David Gauthier's constrained maximization, from Morals by Agreement (1986), which locates the rationality of paying in the disposition rather than in the isolated act, and which depends on agents being "translucent" to one another — not perfectly transparent, but detectable by others with better-than-chance reliability. The second is the treatment on offer within functional decision theory (FDT), as developed by Yudkowsky and Soares, on which paying counts as rational because the driver's prediction is taken to be subjunctively dependent on the very decision procedure the agent runs at the ATM. Please compare these two accounts directly: where do they actually agree about what makes paying rational, and where do their underlying pictures of practical rationality genuinely pull apart?

I'm especially curious how each fares once the predictor is not an idealized oracle but an ordinary person sizing up a stranger's face and manner, as in my own case. Does Gauthier's appeal to imperfect translucency handle this sort of case more naturally than FDT's more idealized appeal to a shared decision procedure, or can the FDT story be restated to cover fallible human predictors without losing what makes it distinctive? And, on balance, which of the two theories do you think gives the more satisfying general account of practical rationality — one that could plausibly be extended beyond toy cases to something like my own?
