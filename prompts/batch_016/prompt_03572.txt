I'm taking a seminar on animal communication this semester (it counts toward my philosophy of biology minor), and we just spent two sessions on Zahavi's handicap principle. I came away suspecting that the "textbook" story — the one you get from popular treatments of peacock trains and gazelle stotting — is considerably more contested among specialists than it's usually made to sound, and I'd like to work through the underlying dispute properly rather than just repeat the simplified version in my response paper.

The standard picture, traceable to Zahavi's original proposal (1975) and supposedly given rigorous backing by Alan Grafen's 1990 game-theoretic (ESS) model, holds that a signal can only be a reliable guide to an organism's underlying quality if it is costly in a way that low-quality individuals cannot profitably fake — and, on the strict reading, that honest signalers must actually pay this cost at the stable equilibrium itself, not merely risk it counterfactually. But our professor pointed us to a line of criticism, associated especially with Getty and Számadó (among others), arguing that Grafen's own mathematics only requires a differential- or marginal-cost condition: cheating must be relatively more expensive for low-quality signalers than for high-quality ones, which is fully compatible with the signal being cheap, or even a net benefit, for honest signalers at equilibrium. On this view, the "waste" that Zahavi treated as doing the real explanatory work was never actually required, and some recent reassessments go further, arguing that Grafen's model was misread from the start and never vindicated the classical handicap principle at all. Cases like a stag's roar — whose acoustic properties are tied to its body size for anatomical reasons, and so are hard to fake without needing to be costly to produce — are often raised as signals that seem reliable without being handicaps in Zahavi's sense (sometimes called "index signals" in this literature).

Could you help me settle this for myself by writing an essay that directly addresses the question: does honest signaling actually require that a cost be paid at the evolutionary equilibrium, as the classical handicap principle claims, or does the differential-cost story fully explain signal reliability on its own, leaving "cost at equilibrium" as an incidental feature of some signaling systems rather than a necessary one? Please present the strongest version of each side, engage with the harder cases (index signals, and signals that are net-beneficial even for honest signalers), and close with a definite verdict rather than leaving the matter unresolved.
