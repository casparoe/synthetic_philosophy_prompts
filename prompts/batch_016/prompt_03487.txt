so I've been down a Clifford/James rabbit hole lately (the evidentialism-vs-"sometimes you get to just believe" fight that every third LW-adjacent post about religion, startups, or relationships name-drops) and I actually went back and read past the shipowner parable into section III of Clifford's 1877 essay, "The Limits of Inference," which basically nobody quotes. There's a bit in there that's got me stuck:

"We may, then, add to our experience on the assumption of a uniformity in nature; we may fill in our picture of what is and has been, as experience gives it us, in such a way as to make the whole consistent with this uniformity. And practically demonstrative inference—that which gives us a right to believe in the result of it—is a clear showing that in no other way than by the truth of this result can the uniformity of nature be saved.

No evidence, therefore, can justify us in believing the truth of a statement which is contrary to, or outside of, the uniformity of nature. If our experience is such that it cannot be filled up consistently with uniformity, all we have a right to conclude is that there is something wrong somewhere; but the possibility of inference is taken away; we must rest in our experience, and not go beyond it at all. If an event really happened which was not a part of the uniformity of nature, it would have two properties: no evidence could give the right to believe it to any except those whose actual experience it was; and no inference worthy of belief could be founded upon it at all.

Are we then bound to believe that nature is absolutely and universally uniform? Certainly not; we have no right to believe anything of this kind. The rule only tells us that in forming beliefs which go beyond our experience, we may make the assumption that nature is practically uniform so far as we are concerned. Within the range of human action and verification, we may form, by help of this assumption, actual beliefs; beyond it, only those hypotheses which serve for the more accurate asking of questions."

(for context, right before this he'd built up the point that literally every belief that outruns immediate experience — even "fire burned me yesterday so it'll burn me again" — already leans on assuming nature is uniform.)

What I can't work out: the "no evidence, therefore" move seems to guarantee in advance that any apparent counterexample to uniformity gets redescribed as "something wrong somewhere" rather than a real violation. Doesnt that make the uniformity assumption unfalsifiable by construction, which seems like an odd thing for the guy famous for "proportion your belief to the evidence" to be quietly resting his whole system on? And then the bit about an event that "really happened" outside the uniformity is even weirder to me — the person who actually experienced it apparently still gets to believe it, but can't draw a single further inference from it, even privately, ever? That's a strange exception coming from someone who spends the rest of the essay being deeply suspicious of people trusting their own sincere convictions (that's basically the whole moral of the shipowner story).

Can you help me actually reconstruct the argument here — is this just Hume's miracles argument in a different outfit, is the unfalsifiability worry a real problem or am I missing his point, and does any of this even come up in James's reply in "The Will to Believe," or is that essay fighting on totally different ground (live/forced/momentous options) and just never engages this part of Clifford at all?
