So my cousin got in a dumb bidding war on ebay last month over a vintage synth, kept bumping his max bid every time someone outbid him, and ended up paying way more than the thing goes for on Reverb. I told the story at a faculty dinner (it reminded me of the dollar auction game I use in my decision theory seminar) and two colleagues, Mara and Devon, ended up arguing about why he kept bidding.

Mara said it's textbook sunk cost: each new bid was really just him refusing to let go of the hours already spent watching the auction and the mental picture of himself winning it.

Devon said that's too quick -- it's structurally like the dollar auction. Once you're outbid, you're choosing between a guaranteed loss (of the item, of the "win") and one more bid that might still land it, so no single bid is crazy, it's the whole sequence that produces the bad outcome, not any one move in it.

Mara pushed back: in an actual dollar auction the second place bidder has to pay and gets nothing, that's the whole engine of the trap. On ebay, if you lose you pay nothing, so there's no structural force at all, just loss aversion over a "loss" that was never real, i.e. sunk cost again.

Devon said fine, no cash changes hands, but there's still a cost to visibly losing something you'd told people you were going to win, so there IS a price for coming second, just a reputational one, and that's enough to bring back the dollar auction structure.

Neither would budge and honestly I cant tell who's got the better of it. Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right about what at each step, not just who wins overall -- and flag anything you think both of them are missing?
