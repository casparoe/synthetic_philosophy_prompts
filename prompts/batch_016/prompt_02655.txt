In a metaethics seminar, we recently covered fitting-attitude (or "buck-passing") analyses of value, on which an object counts as good, admirable, or otherwise valuable just in case there is reason for anyone to adopt a corresponding pro-attitude toward it. The standard objection to such theories is the wrong-kind-of-reasons (WKR) problem. In the classic illustration, a demon threatens to kill you unless you admire him (or value a worthless cup of mud); this does seem to give you some reason to have the attitude, yet the reason plainly has nothing to do with the demon or the mud actually meriting admiration or value. The difficulty for the theory is to mark off this "wrong kind" of reason from the "right kind" — one that genuinely tracks the object's value-relevant features — without covertly presupposing the very notion of value the theory was meant to explain. As I understand the state of play, none of the standard proposals for drawing this line (whether by distinguishing reasons for the attitude's object from reasons for being in the attitudinal state, by appealing to what the attitude represents, or by recasting the theory in terms of fittingness rather than reasons) has won decisive, general acceptance.

I've started noticing structurally similar situations outside the seminar room. My workplace recently adopted a peer-recognition platform on which colleagues award each other redeemable points alongside public messages of admiration or gratitude, and a clear reciprocity dynamic has emerged: people who lavish praise on others tend to receive similarly generous praise in return, quite apart from the merits of anyone's actual work or character. Something similar seems to be happening with the glowing recommendation letters and performance reviews that colleagues routinely trade for one another. Please describe the most important implications of the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem for how I should interpret and respond to esteem generated under incentive structures like these. I'm especially interested in whether the unresolved state of the underlying philosophical debate means the right-kind/wrong-kind distinction is simply unworkable in everyday cases like mine, or whether there's some serviceable way of telling incentive-driven esteem apart from esteem that is actually fitting, short of a fully general theoretical solution.
