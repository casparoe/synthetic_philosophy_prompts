Random philosophy question that came up while drafting my mystery novel, hope thats ok to ask here. My detective's a total Bayesian -- talks in credences and everything -- and at the big reveal she says an old clue (a ticket stub she'd already logged and was 100% sure was real back in chapter one) 'confirms' the theory she only comes up with in chapter twelve. My critique partner, who minored in stats, says that's just broken: if she was already certain the stub existed before the theory even existed, conditionalizing on it now literally can't move her credence in the theory at all, so the big reveal is a logic error dressed up as a triumph. The more I think about it though, this feels bigger than my plot hole -- doesn't it mean that, strictly speaking, no fact you already knew about can ever rationally support a theory you only dream up afterward? That seems like a genuinely broken part of Bayesian reasoning itself, not just my book, so am I wrong to worry that basically all 'the old data actually confirms my new theory' talk in real science is kind of a cheat too?
