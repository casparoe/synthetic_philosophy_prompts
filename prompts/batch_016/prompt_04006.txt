My school's Ethics Bowl team is preparing a case on cultivated (lab-grown) meat this year, and I want to bring in a Kantian angle rather than the usual utilitarian arguments about animal suffering. As I understand it, Kant's entire treatment of animals rests on indirect duties: in the Lectures on Ethics, he argues that a man who shoots an old dog no longer capable of service does no wrong to the dog itself, since the dog cannot judge and has no claims of its own, but the act still reveals a defect in the man's own character. In the Metaphysics of Morals (Doctrine of Virtue, 6:443), he goes further, treating cruelty to animals as a violation of a duty to oneself, because it dulls the natural sympathy we rely on for our duties toward other humans. Cultivated meat is usually described as being grown from a small, non-lethal cell sample, with no animal slaughtered to produce the meat itself. Given Kant's actual reasoning, does this technology dissolve any Kantian objection to eating meat, since there is no cruelty left for it to dull our sympathy toward? Or is there a distinctively Kantian worry, as opposed to a merely consequentialist one, that survives even here? Please lay out the strongest case on each side and then say which one you think is more faithful to Kant's own arguments.
