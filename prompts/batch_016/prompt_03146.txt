I've followed the debates over updateless decision theory (UDT) and its more fully formalized descendant, functional decision theory (FDT), across LessWrong, the Alignment Forum, and the wider MIRI-adjacent literature for a long time, and there's one case I don't think gets resolved as cleanly as its defenders suggest: Will MacAskill's "Bomb" argument against FDT.

The setup: you face two open boxes, Left and Right, and must take exactly one. Left contains a live bomb that will kill you if you take it. Right is empty, but opening it costs $100. Long before you arrived, a predictor with an extremely low error rate (on the order of one mistake per trillion trillion cases) simulated your decision-making and placed the bomb in Left if and only if she predicted you would choose Right. You're told this is a one-off, isolated encounter -- you will never again be in a situation where your choices are predicted by anyone. Standing before the boxes, you can see with your own eyes that Left does contain a bomb, and a note from the predictor confirms she predicted you'd choose Right. FDT's proponents hold that the disposition a rational agent should have committed to in advance is one that takes Left anyway, accepting death in this unlucky branch, because that disposition does better in expectation across the predictor's simulations than one that pays up. Critics object that this can't be the right verdict: given everything you now know, paying $100 for Right is a guaranteed improvement over Left, and no defensible theory of rational choice should tell you to forgo a certain, cheap escape for the sake of a policy whose payoff has already failed to materialize.

Write an essay adjudicating this dispute. Present the strongest form of both the "guaranteed payoff" objection and the updateless rejoinder (including the claim that the proper object of rational evaluation is the agent's disposition or algorithm, not its momentary act), and don't let either side win by default. Close with a clear, defended verdict: does Bomb expose a genuine flaw in updateless decision theories, or does it just reveal a cost that a rational agent should be willing to pay? If your answer is conditional rather than flat, state precisely the principle that determines when updatelessness should win out and when it shouldn't.
