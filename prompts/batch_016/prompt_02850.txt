Last weekend, a friend of mine who is midway through a PhD in decision theory was agonizing over whether to accept a modest but guaranteed settlement in a dispute or gamble on a larger, uncertain payout, and the conversation inevitably drifted into the Allais paradox. We spent the better part of an evening on the textbook version: most people, offered a certain $1 million against a 10% chance of $5 million, an 89% chance of $1 million, and a 1% chance of nothing, take the certain million; but offered an 11% chance of $1 million (and 89% chance of nothing) against a 10% chance of $5 million (and 90% chance of nothing), most people take the shot at $5 million instead. The two pairs of options differ only in whether a shared 89% chance pays out $1 million or nothing, so no assignment of utilities to money can rationalize both choices at once — the pattern flatly violates the independence axiom of expected utility theory. My friend argued that this is not a bias to be trained away but a rationally permissible response to risk, and defended Lara Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility theory, on which an agent's risk function weights outcomes according to their rank within a gamble rather than only according to their probability, so that risk-aversion becomes a global feature of a whole prospect rather than something smuggled in through a concave utility function.

I pushed back with an objection I half-remembered from somewhere (I later tracked it down to a paper by Johanna Thoma and Jonathan Weisberg): that all of this looks far less impressive once the million-dollar bet is treated as embedded in a "grand world" of other background risks rather than as an isolated, self-contained gamble. Model the Allais choice against a realistic backdrop of ordinary life uncertainty, and risk-weighted expected utility theory apparently stops recommending the very preferences it was supposed to rescue — on some ways of doing the modeling, it even flips into recommending risk-seeking instead. My friend wasn't ready to concede the point but couldn't fully answer it on the spot either, and we ran out of night before resolving anything.

Could you write a dialogue between two characters who pick up the argument where we left it, as if the four gambles were still on the table between them: an orthodox expected-utility theorist who regards the Allais preferences as simply a mistake, and a defender of Buchak's theory who wants to vindicate them as rational. Have the expected-utility theorist press the background-risk objection in its strongest form, and have the REU defender offer the most serious reply available — whether that means resisting the objection, biting a bullet, or narrowing the scope of what the theory was ever meant to show — with each side responding to what the other has actually said rather than simply restating their opening position. I'd like both characters to be equally sharp and equally capable of landing a real blow; this shouldn't read as a demonstration that one of them was right all along.
