I'm a retired geneticist -- ran a lab for about thirty years before finally hanging it up last spring -- and I never had much patience for philosophy while I was working, too busy actually doing science, or so I used to joke. Now that I've got time on my hands I sit in on a philosophy of science reading group at the university near me, and given how many candidate-gene association studies I watched quietly fail to replicate back in the day, the replication crisis is a subject close to my heart. So I was pretty invested when last week's meeting turned into a real argument between two of the regulars, and I honestly still can't decide who came out ahead.

The two going at it were Sana, who's finishing a stats postdoc and is firmly on Team Bayes, and Owen, a philosophy grad student who's clearly read way too much Deborah Mayo for his own good. Sana opened by saying the replication crisis is basically a frequentist disease: p-hacking, optional stopping, running a dozen comparisons and reporting the one that "worked" -- and that switching to Bayes factors fixes the worst of it, because thanks to the likelihood principle the evidence only depends on the data you actually got, not on when you decided to stop collecting it or what you originally intended to test. Owen shot back that this immunity only holds in an idealized world with a fixed model and an honestly-chosen prior, and that in real practice the same wiggle room just migrates -- to which prior, which covariates, which subgroup -- citing Gelman's own point (and Gelman is about as Bayesian as it gets) that the "garden of forking paths" problem doesn't disappear just because your not running a literal fishing expedition. Sana granted that but said a prior is at least a stated, public commitment sitting right there in the paper for anyone to scrutinize, whereas a p-value collapses a dozen unreported researcher decisions into one falsely objective-looking number. Owen agreed a stated prior beats a totally silent decision, but said "checkable in principle" doesn't buy you much when almost no reviewer actually knows how to critique somebody's prior, and that the one intervention which has actually lowered false-positive rates in psychology, preregistration, has nothing to do with Bayes vs. frequentist at all -- it'd help a diehard frequentist exactly as much, which suggests the real disease is incentives to publish novel positive results, not which formula the data gets run through. Sana's closing line was that she'll grant preregistration helps either way, but a field that natively thinks in updated credences rather than reject/fail-to-reject verdicts would need that outside patch a lot less, since the inferential logic itself isn't dangling a magic threshold to cross.

Could you go through the back-and-forth move by move and say who's got the better of it at each stage, rather than just handing out an overall winner? And if it seems like they're both dancing around some further point that neither one actually names, I'd like to hear that too -- it felt like there was something fairly obvious that two people this deep in the weeds were somehow talking past each other on.
