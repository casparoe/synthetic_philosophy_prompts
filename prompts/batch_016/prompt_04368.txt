I have no formal training in logic, but for months I've been trying, on my own, to properly understand something I ran into while reading about self-modifying AI: the so-called Löbian obstacle to an agent trusting a future or "offspring" version of itself that reasons in exactly the same way. I follow the basic shape of Löb's theorem — that if a system can prove "if I can prove P, then P" for some claim P, it can already prove P outright, which is why it can never certify a general soundness schema for itself. What I can't get straight is why this rules out a simple step-by-step fix, where each version only has to vouch for a slightly weaker successor rather than an exact copy of itself; my understanding is that this naive approach forces the mathematical strength of each generation to strictly decrease, so the chain of trust can't be sustained indefinitely. Could you explain concretely why that decline is unavoidable under the naive approach, and then walk me through how the "parametric polymorphism" idea people eventually reached for is supposed to let successive agents stay equally strong instead of weakening each time? Finally, I'd like your honest assessment of whether that idea genuinely dissolves the obstacle to self-trust, or merely relocates the same underlying problem.
