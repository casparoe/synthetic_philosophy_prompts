This term I'm taking a small philosophy seminar on collective rationality and group agency, and a couple of weeks ago we covered judgment aggregation, starting from the doctrinal paradox that Kornhauser and Sager originally raised for appellate courts and that Christian List and Philip Pettit later formalized as the "discursive dilemma." The example that has stuck with me is the standard three-judge breach-of-contract case. Liability (R) legally requires both that a valid contract existed (P) and that the defendant failed to perform it (Q), so the governing rule is R if and only if (P and Q). Judge A believes both P and Q are true, and so votes that the defendant is liable; Judge B believes P is true but Q is false, and so votes not liable; Judge C believes Q is true but P is false, and so also votes not liable. If the court votes issue-by-issue and then mechanically applies the rule to whatever the majority concludes about P and Q (the "premise-based" procedure), a majority affirms P (Judges A and B) and a separate majority affirms Q (Judges A and C), so the court is committed to R: the defendant is liable, even though two of the three judges individually believe he is not. If instead the court simply puts the bottom-line question to a direct vote (the "conclusion-based" procedure), a majority -- Judges B and C -- finds the defendant not liable. Same three judges, same three sets of individual beliefs, opposite official rulings, entirely as a function of which procedure was used.

Our professor presented this as a genuinely open problem in the field -- she pointed out that Kornhauser and Sager themselves declined to say that one procedure is always correct -- but our response-paper assignment requires us to actually defend a position, and I would like help pressure-testing mine before I commit it to writing. I want to argue that a collegial body facing this dilemma should use the conclusion-based procedure rather than the premise-based one, even though the latter is usually presented as the more "principled" option, on the grounds that it forces the court's ruling to be logically entailed by whatever premises the majority itself endorses. Could you write the strongest version of that argument you can? I especially need it to directly engage with, rather than brush past, the obvious objection: that conclusion-based voting can hand down a verdict that fails to follow from any consistent application of the legal rule to any set of facts a majority of the court actually accepts, which makes it look less like adjudication under law and more like a straw poll on which party should prevail.
