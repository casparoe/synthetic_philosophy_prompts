I am a novelist stuck on a problem that neither my editor nor my writing group can settle. My protagonist begins the book as a sympathetic, decent man, but over some three hundred pages he makes a long sequence of small compromises — a convenient lie, a minor betrayal of confidence, a colleague's misconduct quietly overlooked, credit taken that he did not quite earn — none of which seems damning on its own. By the final chapters, every reader agrees he has become the villain of the book, yet no two readers point to the same scene as the moment it happened. My editor wants one unambiguous scene in which he decisively crosses the line, so that readers know exactly when to turn on him; half my writing group insists that manufacturing such a scene would falsify how people actually change, and that the reader's inability to name the moment is precisely the truth the book should preserve. Is there a real fact about which scene changed him that the rest of us have simply failed to notice, or is the search for such a scene misguided from the start? And does the answer suggest anything about how I should finish the book?
