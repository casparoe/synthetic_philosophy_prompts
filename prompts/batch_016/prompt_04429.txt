For my AP Research project on the "WEIRD samples" problem in the social sciences, I came across a study in experimental philosophy that I have not been able to fully make sense of. In Kripke's thought experiment, "Gödel" turns out to have taken credit for the incompleteness theorem after coming into possession of a manuscript written by an obscure mathematician named Schmidt; the standard reaction is that our use of the name "Gödel" still refers to Gödel, not Schmidt, which is supposed to refute the view that a name refers to whoever fits the description speakers associate with it. When Machery, Mallon, Nichols, and Stich actually presented this scenario to participants in the United States and Hong Kong, the two groups' verdicts diverged along lines tracking the two rival theories of reference (setting aside that the replication of this particular finding has since been disputed). What I do not understand is the further step: why should that kind of disagreement count as evidence against using invented cases like this to adjudicate philosophical theories at all, rather than simply showing that one of the two groups is mistaken? Could you reconstruct the actual argument from the empirical finding to the methodological conclusion, make explicit what it must assume about concepts and intuitions in order to succeed, and lay out the strongest ways philosophers have tried to resist it?
