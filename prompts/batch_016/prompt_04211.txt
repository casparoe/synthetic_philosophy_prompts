I'm taking a seminar on interpretations of probability, and this week's readings paired the standard von Mises/Popper material with some of Peirce's own writings on chance, which the secondary literature didn't really prepare me for. I keep getting stuck on the endnote Peirce added in 1910 to his 1878 essay "The Doctrine of Chances," reprinted as CP 2.664 in the Collected Papers:

"[T]o say that a die has a 'would-be' is to say that it has a property, quite analogous to any habit that a man might have. Only the 'would-be' of the die is presumably as such simpler and more definite than the man's habit as the die's homogenous composition and cubical shape is simpler than the nature of the man's nervous system and soul; and just as it would be necessary, in order to define a man's habit, to describe how it would lead him to behave and upon what sort of occasion — albeit this statement would by no means imply that the habit consists in that action — so to define the die's 'would-be', it is necessary to say how it would lead the die to behave on an occasion that would bring out the full consequence of the 'would-be'; and this statement will not of itself imply that the 'would-be' of the die consists in such behavior."

Peirce's earlier position, in the 1878 essay itself, was a fairly orthodox limiting-frequency view in Venn's mold. Commentators often cite this later "would-be" language as anticipating Popper's propensity interpretation decades before the fact, but I can't tell whether the passage actually earns that reading. On one hand, Peirce insists the die's would-be is not identical with "that action," as if he wants some standing modal fact that explains the frequencies rather than just summarizing them — which sounds like a genuine propensity, distinct from any actual or hypothetical run of outcomes. On the other hand, he explicates the entire content of the would-be by saying "how it would lead the die to behave" on the relevant occasions, which sounds like nothing more than a counterfactual-frequency gloss — that is, still frequentism, just modally sharpened so it no longer needs an actually-observed infinite series to fix its meaning. Is there a real difference between those two readings, or does the frequentism/propensity distinction simply collapse once frequentism is allowed to help itself to counterfactuals like this? I'd appreciate help working out exactly what Peirce is committed to in this specific passage.
