I'm part of a small team prototyping an AI agent that is only permitted to rewrite its own planning code after it has produced a formal proof, within a fixed logical system, that the rewrite preserves a stated safety property. In a recent design review, a colleague objected that this whole approach runs into what she called the "Löbian obstacle": the claim that such a system can never fully certify, from within its own logic, that its own proofs -- or a successor's proofs in that same logic -- are actually true, rather than merely derivable. I gather this traces back to Löb's theorem and is closely tied to Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, but I don't yet understand the underlying mechanics well enough to know whether this is a deep obstruction or just a design problem we haven't been clever enough to solve. Could you walk me through, with real precision, why a system can't grant itself (or an equally capable successor) this kind of blanket trust? I'd also like your honest assessment of the standard proposed fix -- having each successive version of the agent reason in a strictly weaker logic than the one before it -- and whether that genuinely dissolves the problem or just relocates it.
