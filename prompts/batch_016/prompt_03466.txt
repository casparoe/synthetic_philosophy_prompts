I am a philosophy professor putting together remarks as a discussant for a colleague's paper at a small workshop on social ontology next month. Rather than respond to her paper directly, I decided to use the slot to work out a nagging thought about David Lewis's account of convention that resurfaced while I was re-reading his book Convention for a graduate seminar this term. What follows is close to what I would actually say, and I would like your comments before I commit to it in front of an audience that includes people who have thought about this material far more carefully than I have.

Lewis defines a convention as a regularity in behavior, within a population facing a recurring coordination problem, that persists because everyone conforms to it, expects everyone else to conform, and prefers to conform on condition that the others do. The whole arrangement is sustained by common knowledge and by the availability of at least one equally workable alternative regularity that could have done the same job. On this picture, the "ought" attaching to a convention is purely instrumental: you ought to drive on the right given that everyone else does, in exactly the sense that you ought to carry an umbrella given that you want to stay dry. Margaret Gilbert has argued this cannot be the whole story, for two reasons. First, Lewis's definition requires near-total conformity, yet we still call something a convention when almost nobody follows it any more (her example is thank-you notes after a dinner party, a nominal convention in some social circles even though hardly anyone still writes them). Second, and more importantly, when someone breaks a convention we do not merely predict that things will go worse for them, as we would for someone who forgot an umbrella; we think they can be rebuked, that they owe the rest of us something. Her fix is that conventions rest on a joint commitment among the parties, and that it is this commitment, rather than the underlying coordination payoffs, that grounds the standing to rebuke.

My worry is that the diagnosis is right but the cure is not: "joint commitment" seems to just rename the explanandum. Why does a joint commitment bind, when a mere coincidence of individual commitments does not? And why do conventions nobody ever explicitly signed on to (I never agreed to drive on the right; I was born into it) inherit the same obligation-generating force that Gilbert reserves for cases of explicit joint agreement? It occurred to me that Brian Skyrms's evolutionary treatments of convention might help here, though not in the way Skyrms intends: he shows conventions of meaning, and even rudimentary fairness norms, emerging through reinforcement or replicator dynamics among agents with no common knowledge and, in his toy models, no beliefs at all. This is usually read as making Lewis's picture even more deflationary, but suppose the disposition to resent free-riders is itself among the traits selected for in populations that sustain such equilibria. Then the normative residue Gilbert is after would be neither a brute fact about joint commitment nor a case of instrumental rationality, but an evolved reactive attitude layered on top of the equilibrium, for reasons that have nothing to do with anyone's reasoning, individual or joint. On this reading, Cristina Bicchieri's distinction between mere conventions, stabilized by everyone's expectations about what others will do, and full social norms, which additionally require the belief that others think one ought to conform and a willingness to sanction, would not mark out a genuinely different kind of case, as she intends it to, but would just be tracking where this evolved layer happens to have attached itself.

I would like your comments on four things in particular: whether I have represented Lewis and Gilbert accurately and fairly to both; whether "joint commitment just relabels the explanandum" is a fair objection or whether there is an obvious reply on Gilbert's behalf that I am missing; whether the appeal to Skyrms plus an evolved reactive attitude is doing real philosophical work or is a non-sequitur dressed up as an explanation; and whether reading Bicchieri's normative expectations as tracking that same evolved layer is a plausible extension of her view or does violence to it. I am less interested in a verdict on the overall thesis than in where the argument's weakest joints are.
