I'm working on a longform piece about rationalist theologians who tried to explain away rituals that had become philosophically embarrassing to their own more sophisticated followers, and Maimonides' treatment of animal sacrifice keeps threatening to take over the whole article. Before I go further, I'd like your comments on a paragraph I've drafted covering the core of his position and Nahmanides' famous rebuttal to it. I'm less concerned with prose style than with substance, so please don't hold back if you think I've flattened, oversimplified, or gotten something wrong.

In the Guide of the Perplexed (III:32, extended in III:46), Maimonides argues that God commanded animal sacrifice not because it holds any value in itself, but as a concession to a people who had just left a land where sacrificing to idols was the only worship idiom they knew; better, on his account, to redirect an already-ingrained practice toward the true God than to demand an abrupt leap straight to prayer and contemplation that human psychology could not sustain. Nahmanides, writing in the following century, thought this not merely wrong but close to sacrilegious: in his commentary on Leviticus 1:9 he objects that Abel and Noah offered sacrifices that God received favorably long before the Egyptian and Chaldean idolatry Maimonides has in mind even existed, so weaning people off those particular cults cannot be the whole reason sacrifice exists. What interests me more, though, is that Maimonides himself never seems to have fully believed his own account: in the Mishneh Torah he still rules that the messianic king will rebuild the Temple and reinstate the very sacrifices the Guide treats as a stage the Israelites should eventually outgrow. That looks to me like a real inconsistency in Maimonides' own thought, not merely a difference of genre or audience between a philosophical work and a legal code.

Specifically, I'd like your comments on three things: first, whether I've stated Maimonides' position accurately rather than flattening it into a caricature; second, whether the Abel-and-Noah objection is really the strongest plank of Nahmanides' case, or whether I'm leaving out a more important part of his position, including whatever positive theory of sacrifice he proposes in its place; and third, whether the inconsistency I claim to find between the Guide and the Mishneh Torah is one that scholars generally treat as genuine, or whether there's a standard way of dissolving it that I should engage with before running the "real inconsistency" line in print. Feel free to tell me I'm wrong about any of it.
