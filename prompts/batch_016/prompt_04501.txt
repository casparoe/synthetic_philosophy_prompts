In my history and philosophy of mathematics seminar, we've been closely reading Hilbert's 1900 Paris address, where he lists Cantor's continuum problem first among his twenty-three problems and insists that every precisely posed mathematical question must admit of an exact settlement — either an actual answer or a rigorous proof that no answer is possible — famously declaring that "in mathematics there is no ignorabimus." Hilbert himself tried and failed to prove the continuum hypothesis in the 1920s, and before his death in 1943 he lived to see Gödel's incompleteness theorems already complicate that optimism. He did not, however, live to see Paul Cohen's 1963 forcing proof, which, together with Gödel's earlier work, established that the continuum hypothesis is independent of the ZFC axioms: neither provable nor refutable from them. That outcome does not obviously fit either of the two forms of settlement Hilbert said he would accept. Had Hilbert lived to learn of Cohen's result, do you think he would have counted independence as a genuine solution in his own 1900 sense, or would he have regarded it as exactly the ignorabimus he refused to accept? Please work through that specific tension in his stated views rather than simply summarizing his general philosophy of mathematics.
