Ok this is a weird thing to bring to an AI but bear with me, it's become surprisingly relevant to my life lately. I've been having migraines for the past few months, and my neurologist has me keeping a log of a specific warning sensation I get about 20 minutes before an attack -- not the visual aura, some other thing, that I genuinely cannot describe to another person (I've tried, with her, with my partner, with everyone). So I just started marking a capital "S" in my phone calendar every day I notice it, no other detail, just "yep, S again."

I told my roommate about this (she's doing a philosophy MA) and she about lost it -- apparently I've basically recreated, almost beat for beat, a thought experiment from Wittgenstein's Philosophical Investigations, section 258, where a guy tries to keep a diary of a private sensation by writing "S" in a calendar every time it happens. Her summary of Wittgenstein's point was that I have no actual criterion for whether today's S-feeling is really the same one as last week's, so "whatever is going to seem right to me is right," which apparently is just another way of saying the sign has no real meaning at all -- no genuine private language going on, just me making a mark on a screen. Mildly upsetting thing to hear about your own medical record-keeping, ngl.

Being newly obsessed I did some digging and it turns out this is definately not a settled question -- apparently A.J. Ayer went after Wittgenstein's people over pretty much this exact move, arguing something like: my memory of the S-feeling doesn't seem any worse off than my memory of what my dad's voice sounds like or what a specific chord sounds like, and we don't normally think THOSE memory-based judgments are meaningless just because I could in principle misremember -- so if the argument works, doesn't it prove way too much and wreck ordinary memory-based judgment generally, not just private sensation talk? I think someone named Rhees wrote a big rebuttal to Ayer at the time but I couldn't make heads or tails of it. Could you actually write me a proper essay on this: lay out the strongest version of Wittgenstein's "no criterion of correctness" argument from the diary case, then the strongest version of the Ayer-style "proves too much" objection, and then actually pick a side.
