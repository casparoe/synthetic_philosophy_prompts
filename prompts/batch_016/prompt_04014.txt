I've been a longtime reader of the Overcoming Bias/LessWrong family of blogs, where free will and moral responsibility come up constantly, usually filtered through determinism, Frankfurt-style cases, and the standing disagreement between compatibilists and hard incompatibilists. That eventually sent me to the primary literature, and I keep getting stuck on Galen Strawson's "Basic Argument" against ultimate moral responsibility. The argument is usually compressed into something like: nothing can be the cause of itself (causa sui); to be truly or ultimately responsible for what you do, you would have to be causa sui, at least with respect to the mental character from which your actions flow; therefore ultimate moral responsibility is impossible. What I find genuinely hard to pin down is Strawson's insistence that this conclusion holds whether or not determinism is true - that the Basic Argument is meant to be something more than hard determinism under a new name.

Since I gather that Strawson borrows the "causa sui" language, and even the line about it being the "best self-contradiction that has yet been conceived," directly from Nietzsche, I tracked down the original context myself rather than relying on secondary summaries. The passage is Section 21 of Beyond Good and Evil; the only version I had easy access to was the old 1907 Helen Zimmern translation, which reads:

"The CAUSA SUI is the best self-contradiction that has yet been conceived, it is a sort of logical violation and unnaturalness; but the extravagant pride of man has managed to entangle itself profoundly and frightfully with this very folly. The desire for 'freedom of will' in the superlative, metaphysical sense, such as still holds sway, unfortunately, in the minds of the half-educated, the desire to bear the entire and ultimate responsibility for one's actions oneself, and to absolve God, the world, ancestors, chance, and society therefrom, involves nothing less than to be precisely this CAUSA SUI, and, with more than Munchausen daring, to pull oneself up into existence by the hair, out of the slough of nothingness."

Two things about this would really help me. First, Nietzsche asserts that causa sui is a "logical violation" but never spells out which logical principle is actually at stake - is there a genuine argument buried here, something like a vicious regress, or a cause having to be distinct from and prior to its effect, or is this mostly a rhetorical and psychological diagnosis of why finite, caused creatures crave the notion, dressed up in the vocabulary of logic? Second, and this is the part I can't work out on my own: a few sentences later in the very same aphorism, Nietzsche goes on to reject "non-free will" as equally mythological, on the grounds that cause and effect are themselves human fictions rather than features of "being-in-itself" - which sounds like a much broader assault on the whole framework of causal explanation, not a point that leaves ordinary determinism standing unscathed. If that is the right reading, how is Strawson entitled to lift just the causa sui premise out of this aphorism and use it to argue for a conclusion that is supposed to be neutral between determinism and indeterminism? Is the self-causation regress that the Basic Argument actually needs independent of one's view about determinism, or is Strawson quietly relying on a picture of causation that this very passage seems to call into question?
