ok so this has been rattling around in my head for a while and I don't think I've seen anyone put these two things side by side. In epistemology there's a family of problems called bootstrapping (Vogel) and easy knowledge (Cohen). Vogel's version: a driver named Roxanne notices that every time her gas gauge reads "Full" the tank does in fact turn out to be full, but shes never checked the tank by any means independent of the gauge itself -- and yet by a simple deduce-then-induct move she seems to land on "my gauge is reliable." Cohen's version has a guy at a furniture store reasoning from "the table looks red" all the way to "it's not secretly a white table lit red," which intuitively feels like cheating even though every step looks fine in isolation. Both are treated in the literature as a genuine problem that reliabilism (or anything with what Cohen calls a "basic knowledge structure") has to actually answer, not just wave away.

Then theres Eliezer's old post "Where Recursive Justification Hits Bottom," where he faces basically the same shape of worry -- using induction to vouch for induction, using your brain to evaluate your own brain -- and lands on: that's fine, that's just what reflection looks like, and the only real sin is the dumb kind of circularity, like a note that claims everything on it is true and also asserts some made-up fact about the world.

So which is it: is Eliezer's move actually an answer to the Vogel/Cohen problem -- in which case does it commit him to saying Roxanne's reasoning is legit after all, bullet bitten and everything -- or is he quietly defending a milder, more diffuse kind of circularity than the one that made bootstrapping feel so bad in the first place, in which case the post doesn't actually tell us what's wrong with Roxanne? Want a genuinely careful walk-through of this, not a "well it's all circularity so it's all one issue" shrug.
