Last night a friend and I spent hours arguing about what actually grounds the laws of nature, and neither of us came away satisfied. She defended a Humean position close to David Lewis's best-system account: a generalization counts as a law just in case it is a theorem of whichever true deductive system best balances simplicity against strength, and there is nothing more to a regularity's being a law than that. I pushed an Armstrong-style objection: that this leaves the uniformity of nature a brute, unexplained coincidence, and that laws must instead consist in some further relation of natural necessitation between universals, one that actually makes the regularities hold rather than merely summarizing them after the fact. She replied with the standard rejoinder to Armstrong: that simply naming a relation "necessitation" does not give it the power to necessitate anything, so that positing it either adds nothing beyond the bare regularity, if the entailment from the necessitation fact to the first-order pattern is not itself guaranteed, or, if it is guaranteed, leaves unexplained why a merely contingent relation between universals should carry that guarantee in the first place. I did not have a good answer.

Could you compare the Humean best-system account of laws with Armstrong-style necessitarian anti-Humeanism specifically on this point? I would like to know whether positing necessitation between universals genuinely buys the anti-Humean an explanatory advantage over treating laws as mere regularities, or whether it just relocates the very mystery it was meant to solve one level up, and, if the latter, whether some other anti-Humean view, such as dispositional essentialism or primitivism about laws, is better placed to escape the objection.
