I'm a software engineer (mostly backend infra, Go and Kubernetes) at a mid-sized startup, and something from our last company offsite has been nagging at me. We always cap off the two-day retreat with a long, fairly boozy dinner, and this year our VP of Engineering admitted to a few of us, half-joking but clearly serious underneath it, that these dinners are one of the more useful signals he has when deciding who to nominate for a tech lead role. His reasoning went roughly like this: we already accept that watching someone handle a 3 a.m. production outage tells you something real about their judgment and self-control, because the pressure strips away their normal composure and shows you what's actually underneath; a few drinks just strip away a different set of filters, so why wouldn't that count as the same kind of evidence? He's since taken to quietly keeping the promotion candidates' glasses topped up at these dinners while nursing a single sparkling water himself so he can "keep an eye on things," and claims he's learned more about a couple of people this way than from a year of sprint retros.

A few colleagues and I argued about this over lunch afterward and couldn't quite pin down what, if anything, is wrong with it. It doesn't feel obviously different in kind from the on-call comparison he draws, and yet deliberately getting people a little drunk in order to study them feels manipulative in a way that watching them respond to a genuine outage doesn't — though none of us could articulate exactly where the disanalogy lies. I don't have any formal background in philosophy — I mostly just write code — but I suspect there's a real, well-developed question underneath our disagreement about whether conduct under deliberately induced disinhibition can be treated as evidence of, or even a way of training, someone's underlying character. Could you help us think through this properly, rather than just telling us whether our VP is being a jerk?
