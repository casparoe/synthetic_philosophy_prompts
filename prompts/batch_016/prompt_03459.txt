Over the past few months, my two siblings and I have been dissolving the small manufacturing business we inherited from our father, and rather than each retaining separate lawyers, we agreed to work with a mediator who has a graduate background in economics and specializes in business-dissolution disputes. She initially modeled the division of the proceeds as a Nash bargaining problem. Partway through, one sibling lined up an outside buyer willing to pay well above book value for his share alone, which should only have strengthened his position at the table; yet when the mediator recomputed the Nash-based split to account for this, a second sibling, whose own circumstances had not changed at all, ended up doing noticeably better than he had under the original numbers. This struck all three of us as backwards. The mediator agreed it was a known quirk of Nash's solution and proposed that we switch to the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution instead, since it satisfies a "monotonicity" axiom specifically designed to rule this kind of reversal out.

That was reassuring, until curiosity got the better of me. Having some background in mathematics, I tracked down Kalai and Smorodinsky's original 1975 paper, and then, following its later citations, came across a 1979 result of Alvin Roth's. If I am reading it correctly, it shows that once there are three or more bargainers at the table, no solution can simultaneously satisfy Pareto efficiency, symmetry, and that very same monotonicity property. Since there are three of us splitting the business, not two, this seems to directly undercut the guarantee our mediator was leaning on, and I would rather understand the actual tradeoff than simply be told the new numbers are "basically still fair."

Could you walk me through this in some technical depth? Specifically: (a) what exactly does the monotonicity axiom distinguishing Kalai-Smorodinsky from Nash require, and why can the Nash solution violate it in the first place; (b) what does Roth's impossibility result actually establish, and how does it bear on the usual way of extending the two-person Kalai-Smorodinsky construction -- the segment running from the disagreement point to the "ideal" or "utopia" point, extended until it meets the Pareto frontier -- once a third bargainer is added; and (c) in a practical case like ours, when a mediator says she is applying "the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution" to a three-way split, which of efficiency, symmetry, or monotonicity is most likely being quietly sacrificed, and is there any principled way to tell which compromise has been made just from the shape of the proposed division?
