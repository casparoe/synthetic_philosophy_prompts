I've spent a lot of time in rationalist-blog-and-forum land where Henry Sidgwick shows up constantly as the guy behind "the point of view of the universe" and the unresolved dualism of practical reason at the end of the *Methods of Ethics*, so I was surprised to learn recently that this same fairly buttoned-up Cambridge utilitarian spent decades as the founding president of the Society for Psychical Research, personally investigating mediums, telepathy experiments, and haunted houses. Curious, I tracked down his first Presidential Address to the Society, delivered on 17 July 1882 at its first general meeting, half-expecting either apologetic hedging or credulous enthusiasm. Instead I found a passage that seems to be doing real epistemological work, except I can't quite pin down what that work is. Here it is (I've cut a long stretch in the middle — mostly a historical digression about the changing reputation of mesmerism and Spiritualism over the previous thirty years — marked with [...], but otherwise left the wording and punctuation exactly as printed):

"...we are all agreed that the present state of things is a scandal to the enlightened age in which we live. That the dispute as to the reality of these marvellous phenomena — of which it is quite impossible to exaggerate the scientific importance, if only a tenth part of what has been alleged by generally credible witnesses could be shown to be true — I say it is a scandal that the dispute as to the reality of these phenomena should still be going on, that so many competent witnesses should have declared their belief in them, that so many others should be profoundly interested in having the question determined, and yet that the educated world, as a body, should still be simply in the attitude of incredulity.

Now the primary aim of our Society, the thing which we all unite to promote, whether as believers or non-believers, is to make a sustained and systematic attempt to remove this scandal in one way or another. [...] I do not mean to dispute — it is not now the time to dispute — with any individual who holds that reasonable persons, who have looked carefully into the evidence that has been so far obtained, ought to be convinced by that evidence; but the educated world, including many who have given much time and thought to this subject, are not yet convinced, and therefore we want more evidence.

If any one asks me what I mean by, or how I define, sufficient scientific proof of thought-reading, clairvoyance, or the phenomena called Spiritualistic, I should ask to be allowed to evade the difficulties of determining in the abstract what constitutes adequate evidence. What I mean by sufficient evidence is evidence that will convince the scientific world, and for that we obviously require a good deal more than we have so far obtained."

The bit I keep getting stuck on is that last move: "sufficient evidence is evidence that will convince the scientific world." Taken one way, that's a strikingly deflationary, almost sociological account of evidential sufficiency — it makes "there is enough evidence for X" a claim about the actual psychological state of a community, rather than a claim about the relation between evidence and hypothesis, which seems like exactly the kind of move an epistemologist should be suspicious of (a community could surely be collectively unreasonable in withholding belief, however much evidence accumulates). Taken more charitably, maybe "the scientific world" is functioning as a stand-in for something like an idealized set of competent, unbiased inquirers, so the definition is really gesturing at a regulative ideal rather than describing actual Victorian scientific opinion. I can't tell which reading he'd endorse, or whether he'd think there's a real difference between them. I'm also curious whether this is the same underlying epistemic move he relies on elsewhere: in the *Methods of Ethics* he leans heavily on the considered agreement of experts and of common sense as evidence bearing on the certainty of ethical first principles, and this passage has a similar shape, just applied to telepathy and clairvoyance instead of axioms of prudence and benevolence. Is that actually the same principle doing double duty in two domains, or is something importantly different (and maybe less defensible) going on here, given how much stronger the pull toward wishful thinking presumably is with this subject matter? And is there any real distance between what Sidgwick is doing here and the more demanding "ethics of belief" that Clifford had put on the table just five years earlier, or is Sidgwick already quietly working with something in that vicinity? I'd appreciate help reconstructing the most charitable version of Sidgwick's position and figuring out exactly where it's most vulnerable.
