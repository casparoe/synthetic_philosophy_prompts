Ok, this is a bit of a tangent from my actual dissertation (which is on Gödel's platonism about mathematical intuition, the usual Parsons/Maddy-adjacent stuff), but I fell down a rabbit hole this week reading about the letters he wrote to his mother Marianne in 1961, after she'd apparently asked him point-blank whether he believed they'd see each other again after death. I only have this in translation, quoted piecemeal in a secondary essay (the actual letters are sitting in an archive in Vienna and my German isn't good enough to go hunting through the Wienbibliothek catalogue myself), so what follows is stitched together from a couple of the August 1961 letters rather than one continuous passage - but here's the bit that's been bugging me and my officemate over lunch today:

"We not only don't even know whence and why we are here, but also don't know what we are (namely, in essence and seen from within)... According to the common conception, the question 'what am I' would be answered such that I am something that has absolutely no properties in its own right, something along the lines of a coat rack on which one can hang anything one pleases... one must imagine that the 'learning' occurs in great part first in the next world, namely, in that we remember our experiences from this world and come to understand them really for the first time, so that our this-worldly experiences are - so to speak - only the raw material for learning. Moreover one must of course assume that our understanding there will be substantially better than here, so that we can recognise everything of importance with the same infallible certainty as 2 x 2 = 4, where deception is objectively impossible."

Two things about this are tripping us up. First, what's actually supposed to be doing the logical work in the move from "rational learning-from-error is an essential, kind-specific property of human beings" to "therefore there must be a further existence in which this gets completed"? Is that basically a Leibnizian principle-of-sufficient-reason move in disguise (an unfulfilled essential property would just be inexplicable waste), or more of an Aristotelian final-causes argument from unrealized potential, or is it not really a valid inference at all and just dressed up to look like one? Second, that closing line about the afterlife delivering "infallible certainty as 2x2=4" - does that read to you as a genuine extension of his usual rationalist story about mathematical intuition (like the same quasi-perceptual faculty that supposedly grasps mathematical truth here just gets upgraded there), or is it a seperate, more ad hoc theological add-on that doesn't actually plug into the rest of his epistemology? I don't fully trust my own reading anymore since I've stared at this paragraph too long, so a close, skeptical unpacking of what's actually going on logically here (not just a gloss along the lines of "Gödel thought the world is rational so there's an afterlife") would be a huge help.
