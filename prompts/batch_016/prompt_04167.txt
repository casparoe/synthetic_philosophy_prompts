For my school's Effective Altruism club, a few of us have been reading Bostrom on anthropic reasoning for a session on existential-risk forecasting, and two members, Lena and Marcus, got stuck in an argument across two meetings that nobody settled. I mostly listened and would like a neutral read on each exchange.

1. Lena opened with a toy case: a machine flips a fair coin, making one observer on heads or a thousand (each alone in an identical room) on tails. She argued that finding yourself as one of these observers is a thousand times more likely under tails, so your credence in tails should update to 1000/1001, just as you'd infer which of two urns a ball came from if one holds a thousand times more balls.

2. Marcus countered with Bostrom's own "presumptuous philosopher": physicists in 2100, about to run a decisive experiment between two equally supported theories of everything, are told by a philosopher that it's unnecessary, since T1 implies a trillion trillion observers and T2 a trillion trillion trillion, meaning T2 must already be a trillion times more likely. He said Lena's logic commits her to exactly this absurdity -- the reason Bostrom invented the case.

3. Lena replied that the cases aren't parallel: the coin flip is a known chance process with exact populations, while the physics case compares rival theories about unknown laws, so an inference valid in one setting needn't transfer to the other -- and "it feels absurd" is weak evidence in a field as unintuitive as cosmology.

4. Marcus said the disanalogy blocks nothing, since her rule only tracks each hypothesis's ratio of observers, not its source; to exempt theory choice, she'd need a principled reason it tracks truth for coins but not physical laws, not just a description of the difference.

5. Lena pivoted to the alternative, the self-sampling assumption, noting it needs a stipulated "reference class" of similar observers with no principled way to draw the line -- so between two flawed options, she'd keep a well-defined rule with one weird consequence over an undefined one.

6. Marcus answered that well-defined isn't the same as correct: a rule that always favors whichever hypothesis posits more observers, regardless of evidence, isn't treating your existence as evidence at all -- it's a standing thumb on the scale, and SSA's flaws don't make that thumb disappear.

Could you go through this move by move and say who has the better of it at each point, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also like to know if there's a resolution to the SSA/SIA trade-off they're both missing.
