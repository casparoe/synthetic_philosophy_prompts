So I'm in the messy second-draft stage of a novel with a pretty big ensemble cast, and one subplot keeps tripping up my writing group. There's a regional ops manager character who reroutes his company's delivery trucks off a small town's Main Street to save on fuel costs; as a fully foreseen side effect, this quietly wrecks what's left of the town's retail strip. On the page his interior life is written as genuinely indifferent - not cruel, not sorry, he just doesn't factor the town into his thinking either way. But everyone in my group comes away convinced he's way more deliberate and blameworthy about the harm than I actually gave him credit for, like they're inferring intentions and knowledge I never wrote onto the page. One member with a philosophy background said I'd basically stumbled into the "Knobe effect" from experimental philosophy, and its been a research rabbit hole ever since.

I already know the classic chairman/environment case (help vs. harm side effect, identical indifferent psychology, wildly different intentionality and blame judgements from people), so you dont need to re-explain that one to me. What's actually got me stuck is the stuff past that - the extensions where the same asymmetry apparently shows up for causal judgements (whether he "caused" the harm), for whether someone "knew" the side effect was coming, and even for group agents like a corporate board making the same call collectively. And apparently nobody fully agrees on why it happens in the first place - Knobe's own idea that moral considerations are baked right into concepts like intentional action, versus a more general "any norm violation will do it" story, versus Alicke's culpable-control model where blame comes first and the intentionality judgement gets dragged along behind it, versus Sripada's "deep self" idea, versus Machery's trade-off hypothesis that the whole thing is really just cost-benefit reasoning wearing a moral costume.

Given all that, could you lay out the most important implications for how I actually write morally gray characters and manage reader sympathy across the book? I'm especially curious whether those different explanations would cash out in genuinely different advice for a novelist, and whether there's a comparably exploitable asymmetry on the "helping" side I could lean on to get readers to go easy on a character whose insides are just as indifferent as my trucking guy's, without the whole thing collapsing into cheap manipulation the moment a sharp reader clocks what I'm doing.
