A friend and I spent an entire dinner arguing about a tension in Grice's philosophy of language that neither of us could resolve. Grice defines speaker meaning (what he calls non-natural meaning) as a nested structure of three intentions: (1) the speaker intends to produce a belief in the hearer, (2) intends the hearer to recognize that very intention, and (3) intends this recognition itself to be the hearer's reason for forming the belief. He also treats whatever a speaker conversationally implicates as something meant in just this way. I argued that some implicatures - particularly ones designed to let a speaker insinuate something while retaining deniability - can't satisfy condition (3), since fully licensing the hearer to treat recognition of the speaker's intention as the reason for belief would destroy the very deniability the speaker is exploiting. My friend thinks this confuses insinuation with implicature proper, or else misreads condition (3).

Construct your own example (not Grice's philosophy-testimonial letter, and not a mafia-style veiled threat) of an utterance that clearly generates a conversational implicature, and use it to adjudicate our dispute: does the implicature in your example satisfy all three conditions of Grice's definition of speaker meaning? If not, say exactly which one fails and why; if so, explain what's wrong with the deniability worry.
