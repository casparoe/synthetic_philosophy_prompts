I'm teaching a proseminar on formal epistemology this term, and we just worked through the Hintikka chapters (Knowledge and Belief, 1962) and got stuck for basically a whole session on the problem of logical omniscience -- and I don't think I actually left my students, or myself if I'm honest, with a good gut-level feel for it. Everyone can rehearse the formal story fine: in a normal modal system you get logical omniscience for free just from the K axiom plus necessitation, so if K_a(p) holds at w (p is true in every world epistemically accessible from w) and p entails q, then q is true throughout that same set of worlds, so K_a(q) comes out true whether or not the agent ever entertained q, let alone derived it -- and necessitation alone gives you that every validity is automatically known too. Fine, I can put that derivation on the board. What I actually want is more like an intuitive diagnosis of why modeling an epistemic state as "true in a set of possible worlds" is the thing doing the damage here -- what is it about that particular way of representing knowledge that makes closure basically unavoidable short of ditching possible worlds altogether?

I'd also love your take on whether this is really one unified problem, or, like I vaguely remember Stalnaker arguing somewhere, more of a grab-bag of seperate failures that get lumped under one label -- not knowing a tautology seems like a different kind of failure from not having closed your beliefs under some inference you're perfectly capable of drawing, which seems different again from failing to realize two sentences express "the same" proposition. If you have opinions on which of the standard patches (impossible worlds, awareness logics, algorithmic/step-based knowledge, whatever) actually tracks something real about bounded reasoners rather than just being a fix bolted onto the semantics, I'd like to hear those too -- mostly trying to get past reciting the proof and toward actually feeling why its true.
