Okay so this is going to sound like a weird rabbit hole but bear with me. A while back I watched a bunch of pop-philosophy videos about David Lewis and this idea that there's literally a huge number of other possible worlds where things went differently, and the part that stuck with me is that Lewis apparently says "you" don't actually exist in any of those other worlds — there's just a "counterpart" of you there, someone similar but not actually, literally the same person as you. That felt off to me, like doesn't that make every "what if I had done X instead" question kind of pointless, since the guy who does X over there was never really you to begin with? Anyways I brought this up with a friend and he said this whole fight, over whether it's genuinely you showing up in another possible world versus just a stand-in (he said "transworld identity" is the term for the "genuinely you" side), is way older than Lewis and actually goes back to a fight Leibniz had with a theologian named Arnauld about whether God could've created a different Adam. So I dug up the actual letters, which are free on archive.org in an old 1902 translation, and holy cow it is dense going. Random Latin phrases just dropped in with zero explanation, sentences that never end, etc. Here's the specific bit that's been rattling around in my head for like a week now:

"This has given M. Arnaud opportunity to object, not without reason, that it is as little possible to conceive several Adams, understanding Adam as a particular nature, as to conceive of several me's. I agree, but yet, in speaking of several Adams, I do not take Adam for a determined individual. I must, therefore, explain... When we consider in Adam a part of his predicates, for example, that he was the first man, put into a garden of enjoyment, and that, from his side, God took a woman, and, if we consider similar things, conceived sub ratione generalitatis (that is to say, without mentioning Eve or Paradise, or the other circumstances which constitute his individuality), and if we call the person to whom these predicates are attributed Adam, all this does not suffice to determine the individual, for there might be an infinity of Adams, that is to say, of possible persons to whom these would apply who would, nevertheless, differ among themselves... it is not possible for two individuals to exist wholly alike, that is, differing solo numero. We must, therefore, not conceive of a vague Adam... but we must attribute to him a concept so complete that all which can be attributed to him may be derived from his... It follows, also, that if he had had other circumstances, this would not have been our Adam, but another, because nothing prevents us from saying that this would be another. He is, therefore, another."

I think I get the broad shape of it — "Adam" can mean either some generic sketch (first man, garden, wife made from his side, whatever) that a ton of different possible people would all fit equally well, or it can mean one fully nailed-down individual with literally every last detail fixed, and only the second one is the "real" Adam. What I can't pin down is what Leibniz thinks happens the moment you change even one of that fully-specific Adam's details. Is he saying that the actual Adam metaphysically just cannot turn up in any other possible history, period, so "another possible Adam" is automatically some other guy in the same way Lewis's counterparts are supposedly not you — or is he saying the real Adam could in principle exist elsewhere too, it's just that it happens none of his properties are actually changeable, so it comes out the same in practice? Those feel like genuinely different claims to me (one's about whether cross-world sameness is even a coherent possibility, the other's about how strict this one guy's essence is), but Leibniz seems to slide between them without ever flagging that there's a difference, and I definately can't tell if that's on me for missing something or if he's just not being careful. Side question, near the end where it says "may be derived from his" — from his what, grammatically? Could you help me work out what's actually going on here, tell me which of my two readings (or maybe some third option I haven't thought of) is closer to how people who study this stuff actually read Leibniz, and say whether it even matters for the modern counterpart-theory-vs-transworld-identity debate, or whether it's ultimately a distinction without a difference?
