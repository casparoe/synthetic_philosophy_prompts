So this actually happened after my constitutional history seminar last week (I'm a second-year in the history PhD program, and we'd just worked through the Foner anthology's chunk of Douglass speeches alongside some Garrison and excerpts of Spooner), and two people in my cohort, Nadia and Colin, got into this back-and-forth at the bar afterward that I haven't been able to stop chewing on. Nadia's in the legal-history track and Colin's more of an intellectual historian, if that matters. I was mostly just listening so I don't have a dog in the fight, but I've got a section response paper due on this exact question and would love a second opinion before I write it.

The argument was over whether Douglass's "text, not intentions" move in the 1860 Glasgow speech actually holds up. Roughly: (1) Nadia said the whole thing is a nice trick that doesn't survive contact with the historical record — the fugitive slave clause, the three-fifths clause, and the 1808 slave-trade clause were drafted and voted on by delegates who knew exactly what they were protecting (obvious enough once you read Madison's convention notes), and Douglass himself, before his freedom was purchased in '46, would've been fair game for recapture under this supposedly antislavery document. Calling it anti-slavery isn't interpretation, she said, its denial. (2) Colin shot back that this confuses what the framers privately wanted with what they actually enacted — we don't normally interpret statutes by psychoanalyzing legislators, we read the words as a reasonable reader would've, and the text never once says "slave" or "slavery," it says things like "person held to service or labour," precisely because delegates were too ashamed to name the thing directly. Read on its face, that clause isn't obviously restricted to chattel slaves at all. That courts and marshals later applied it to catch escaped slaves is a fact about what Douglass elsewhere calls the "American Government," not about what the Constitution as a text says. (3) Nadia countered that Douglass doesn't apply that government/text split evenhandedly — he happily reaches past the bare clauses to the Preamble's "blessings of liberty" and general appeals to justice when that helps him, but won't let an ambiguous clause be read in light of the near-universal contemporary understanding (including by the framers who wrote it) when that cuts the other way. Sounds like whichever interpretive tool gets the preferred answer, she said. (4) Colin replied there's a real difference between using purposes stated in the ratified document itself, which is text, and using private convention notes that nobody voting on ratification in the state conventions ever even saw — reading a clause in light of the document's own stated purpose isn't cheating, it's just ordinary legal interpretation. (5) Nadia's last word was that "how everyone at the time understood it" isn't only elite secret notes, its also how virtually every abolitionist (including Douglass himself before 1851), every slaveholder, and every court for seventy-odd years read those clauses — and that even fellow antislavery people at the time thought some of Douglass's specific clause-by-clause readings, the fugitive slave clause especially, were a stretch. When everyone affected by a text reads it the same way for seven decades, she said, a clever new reading starts looking less like discovering the true meaning and more like a great piece of advocacy for an independently good cause.

Could you go through moves (1) through (5) and say who's actually got the better of it at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'm especially curious whether Colin's textualism is internally consistent given move (3), and whether Nadia's "everyone read it this way" point in (5) is doing real interpretive work or is just a headcount dressed up as an argument. Flag it too if you think there's a good move neither of them made. And then, since I actually have to turn something in, give me your own bottom-line verdict: on balance, was Douglass's Glasgow argument a genuine, defensible reading of the constitutional text, or closer to motivated reasoning with excellent production values?
