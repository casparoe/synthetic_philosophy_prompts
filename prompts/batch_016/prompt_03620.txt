Been down a rabbit hole reading Augustine's actual treatise on lying (De Mendacio, "On Lying") for a piece I'm working on, instead of just recycling the two-line pop-philosophy version where he's "the guy who says you can never lie, even to the murderer at the door." Here's the snag that's stuck with me: Exodus has the Hebrew midwives lying to Pharaoh to save the Israelite baby boys, and God flat-out rewards them for it (Ex. 1:20-21). That's a real problem for Augustine, since his whole position is that once something counts as a lie, no motive or consequence can make it okay. His way out is to say the midwives weren't praised for the lie itself, just for moral progress relative to something worse — he compares it to how scripture calls Sodom "justified" next to Israel's worse sins elsewhere, i.e. better-then-terrible isn't the same as good. I don't want a survey of the debate, I want an argument: argue that this move fails, and that the midwives case really does commit Augustine, on his own terms, to admitting that at least some lies are good, not merely less bad.
