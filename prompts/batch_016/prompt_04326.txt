I spent my working life as an experimental physicist, on problems with no real connection to gravitation, and general relativity was always for me an elegant piece of differential geometry rather than something to interrogate metaphysically. Since retiring I have joined a small philosophy reading group, and we recently spent two sessions on "the hole argument": Einstein's own worry, dating from roughly 1913 to 1915, that if the points of the spacetime manifold are individuals with an identity independent of the metric field defined on them, then a generally covariant theory like his own is saddled with a strange indeterminism, since a diffeomorphism that is the identity outside some small region but differs inside it yields a distinct yet empirically indistinguishable solution to the field equations. I gather that Earman and Norton's 1987 paper "What Price Spacetime Substantivalism?", which revived the argument, is still treated as posing a serious difficulty for what is called "manifold substantivalism." Wondering whether Einstein's own way of dissolving the puzzle, the so-called "point-coincidence argument," had already filtered into the popular expositions of the day, I went back to my late father's copy of Eddington's Space, Time and Gravitation (1920). Sure enough, early in the chapter "Kinds of Space," he builds up to this:

"The irrelevance of the mesh-system to the laws of nature is sometimes expressed in a slightly different way. There is one type of observation which, we can scarcely doubt, must be independent of any possible circumstances of the observer, namely a complete coincidence in space and time. The track of a particle through four-dimensional space-time is called its world-line. Now, the world-lines of two particles either intersect or they do not intersect; the standpoint of the observer is not involved. In so far as our knowledge of nature is a knowledge of intersections of world-lines, it is absolute knowledge independent of the observer. If we examine the nature of our observations, distinguishing what is actually seen from what is merely inferred, we find that, at least in all exact measurements, our knowledge is primarily built up of intersections of world-lines of two or more entities, that is to say their coincidences. […] It would be extremely clumsy to describe the results of the simplest physical experiment entirely in terms of coincidence. The absolute observation is, whether or not the coincidence exists, not when or where or under what circumstances the coincidence exists; unless we are to resort to relative knowledge, the place, time and other circumstances must in their turn be described by reference to other coincidences. But it seems clear that if we could draw all the world-lines so as to show all the intersections in their proper order, but otherwise arbitrary, this would contain a complete history of the world, and nothing within reach of observation would be omitted.

"Let us draw such a picture, and imagine it embedded in a jelly. If we deform the jelly in any way, the intersections will still occur in the same order along each world-line and no additional intersections will be created. The deformed jelly will represent a history of the world, just as accurate as the one originally drawn; there can be no criterion for distinguishing which is the best representation."

This looks to me like the same move Einstein made at almost exactly the same time, when he wrote that "nothing is physically real but the totality of space-time point coincidences." What I cannot settle for myself is how much philosophical weight the jelly picture can actually bear against the more carefully stated modern hole argument. Does it show that the manifold substantivalist's indeterminism is somehow spurious, an artefact of a distinction between differently labelled but coincidence-preserving jellies that carries no physical content? Or does it merely restate a verificationist scruple that a stubborn substantivalist could grant in full while still insisting that there is a fact of the matter about which point is which, so that determinism genuinely fails? And if coincidences alone are left to carry all the physical weight, I worry the passage proves too much as well as too little: does it not threaten to make even metrical facts that are not reducible to bare intersections, such as distances, elapsed proper times, and curvature, just as philosophically idle as the identities of the bare points themselves? I would be very grateful for help thinking through where Eddington's picture actually lands among these options.
