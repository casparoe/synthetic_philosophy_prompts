I'm a third-year philosophy PhD student writing a dissertation chapter on the metaphysics of dispositions, and I'd like a sanity check on an argument I've been defending in my advisor's metaphysics seminar before I commit to it in the next draft. As I understand the literature, the simple conditional analysis of dispositions (x is disposed to give response r to stimulus s iff x would give r if it underwent s) faces two importantly different families of counterexamples. Finkish cases, like Martin's electro-fink wired to a dead wire, or Lewis's sorcerer who intervenes on a glass the instant it is struck, work by having the stimulus itself trigger the loss or gain of the disposition's causal basis. Masking cases, like Johnston's and Bird's fragile glass wrapped in protective packing, or a poison neutralized by a simultaneously administered antidote, involve no change to the basis at all: the basis is left completely intact, but something external stops it from doing its usual causal work. Lewis's fix in "Finkish Dispositions" was to require that x have some intrinsic basis B such that, if x underwent stimulus C and retained B throughout, C and B would jointly cause the manifestation M.

Here is the inference I want checked. Since Lewis's revised analysis already demands that C and B actually, causally suffice for M together, rather than merely that some bare counterfactual come out true, this same clause looks to me like it should dispose of masking as readily as finking. In the packaged-glass case, B is fully retained (nothing removes the glass's fragile microstructure), yet C and B manifestly fail to jointly cause breaking, since the packing intercepts the causal process; so Lewis's analysis should deliver the verdict that the glass, while wrapped, is not disposed to break, in just the way it correctly denies that a dead wire wired to an electro-fink is disposed to conduct. On this reasoning, the 1997 fix looks like a single, unified solution to both families of counterexamples. And yet whenever I've floated this, people talk as though masking remains a distinct, unsolved problem for conditional analyses, citing Bird, Fara, and Choi as though they had shown that Lewis's fix simply fails for masks in a way it doesn't fail for finks. Is there some real structural asymmetry between finks and masks that my argument is missing, which explains why the very same causal-sufficiency clause could succeed against one family of counterexamples and fail against the other? Or has the field just not caught up to the fact that the 1997 analysis already covers both cases?
