Ok so for the last year or so I've been really into stoicism -- Marcus Aurelius, Epictetus, the whole "you don't control outcomes, only your own judgments" thing, dichotomy of control, all of it. its genuinely helped me be less of a wreck about stuff I can't change. But a couple weeks ago I was at a used bookstore and right next to all the stoicism books there was this tiny book called "The Art of Happiness," and it turns out its basically excerpts from an essay called "Discourse on Happiness" by Emilie du Chatelet, the marquise who translated Newton into French and was Voltaire's partner/collaborator for like 15 years. Reading it kind of messed with my stoic practice, because she says almost the opposite of everything I've been trying to do: she flat out calls out the "moralists" who tell you to curb your passions and master your desires, saying they don't actually know the route to happiness, and argues you need real passions AND illusions to be happy. She even lists stuff like gambling and romantic love and the "glory" that comes from being absorbed in study as things to actively cultivate, and says you shouldn't let reason go backstage and look at the machinery (she's got this whole bit about not wanting to see the ropes and pulleys that make the flying effects work at the opera).

Here's the thing that's really messing with me though -- her own life reads like evidence for both sides. She taught herself to win at gambling as a teenager just to afford books, and then she died at 42 from complications of a pregnancy with a much younger lover she fell head over heels for late in life. You could spin that as "look what chasing passion actually costs you" or just as easily as "she lived a fuller life than any stoic sage ever will." And on top of that I found out the stoics basically have their own version of this exact essay -- Seneca wrote one literally called "On the Happy Life." So now I just go back and forth in my own head about this and get nowhere, and my roommate is sick of hearing about it.

So could you write me a dialogue between Seneca (or just "a Stoic," whoever you think can argue the position best) and Emilie du Chatelet where they actually hash this out: is cultivating strong passions and protecting your illusions about them a necessary ingredient of a happy life, or is it the main thing standing in the way of one? I don't want either of them to be a pushover -- the Stoic needs to seriously grapple with her specific point about illusions being necessary for pleasure and the love of study being the one passion fortune can't really touch, and she needs to seriously answer the classic stoic argument that passions rest on false judgments about what's actually good or bad and just leave you exposed to luck. I want real back and forth, where each one is responding to what the other just said, not just two people restating their own opening position over and over.
