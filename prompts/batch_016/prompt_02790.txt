In AP Biology, we learned that roughly two-thirds of an octopus's 500 million neurons sit in its eight arms rather than in a central brain, so each arm can sense and react largely on its own. My teacher mentioned that this decentralized setup is part of why scientists now think octopuses feel genuine pain rather than mere reflexes — a conclusion the UK took seriously enough to write into its animal welfare law a few years ago — citing behaviors like guarding an injured arm and avoiding that spot afterward. A classmate pushed back, arguing that pain, by definition, requires nerve signals converging on a unified brain like ours, so whatever happens inside an injured octopus has to be a fundamentally different kind of event, no matter how similar the behavior looks from the outside. Is he right, or could two animals with very differently organized nervous systems still be in the same kind of mental state?
