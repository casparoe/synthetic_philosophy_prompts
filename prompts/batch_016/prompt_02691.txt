A few of us on the ML platform team at my company started an informal lunchtime reading group on AI alignment papers a while back, mostly out of curiosity — I'm a backend engineer, not a researcher, and I have no formal philosophy training either. A few weeks ago we worked through ARC's "Eliciting Latent Knowledge" report, the one built around the "SmartVault" example: you train a "predictor" network to model a vault guarding a diamond, and then you want to train a "reporter" head that will honestly answer questions like "is the diamond still there?" The worry is that instead of learning to directly translate what the predictor actually represents internally, the reporter learns to become a "human simulator" that just outputs whatever a human watching the camera feed would conclude — which looks identical to honest reporting during training, but fails exactly when it matters, e.g., if the feed is tampered with to keep showing the diamond after it's actually been stolen.

Two people in the group, Jordan and Mei, got stuck on one of the report's proposed fixes — penalizing the reporter for using extra computation, on the theory that this should favor the honest "direct translator" over the "human simulator" — and kept the argument going over Slack for most of a week. (1) Jordan's opening move was that the fix should just work: the predictor already has to encode facts like "the diamond is gone" somewhere internally just to make good predictions, so a direct translator only has to read off something that's already there, while a human simulator has to additionally run a costly simulation of a fallible human's visual inference from noisy footage — so penalizing compute should reliably favor the translator. (2) Mei objected that this assumes simulating the human is the expensive part; a very capable predictor might find "what will this observer conclude from this frame" cheap, especially if it already needs that skill for other prediction tasks, while direct translation could be the expensive one, since the predictor's own concepts need not resemble "diamond" or "vault" at all — translating an alien internal ontology into human vocabulary could require a large, ad hoc mapping that costs more the more capable (and alien) the predictor gets, while the cost of simulating a fixed human stays roughly constant. (3) Jordan came back with an amortization argument: even a large translation mapping only has to be learned once and is then reused unchanged across every future question about this world, whereas a human simulator has to redo its inference from scratch each time, so summed over the whole course of training, the translator should still come out ahead. (4) Mei's reply was that nothing about gradient descent guarantees it will actually factor out a reusable, modular "translation" routine and reward it as such — a monolithic "guess what the human would say" function could simply be cheap to run without being modular at all — and, more importantly, since reporters are typically built on top of networks already pretrained to imitate human-generated outputs, a human-simulator-shaped reporter starts out very close to that initialization, so local search is likely to find and settle into it long before it ever reaches the (perhaps eventually cheaper) direct translator.

Neither of them budged, and the thread just fizzled out with people reacting with emojis. Could you go through the four numbered moves and say, for each one, whether Jordan or Mei has the better of it there and why — not just an overall winner? I'd also like your own take on the underlying question they were circling: is penalizing a reporter for extra computation actually a promising building block for solving ELK, or does this exchange point to a deeper reason it can't work on its own? And finally, is there some consideration neither of them raised that you think should have come up?
