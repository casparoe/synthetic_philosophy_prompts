For the past few years I have been teaching myself piano in the evenings, largely as a way of unwinding after work, and somewhere in the process I developed a conviction I haven't been able to shake: that instrumental music seems to be doing something categorically different from what a painting or a novel does. It doesn't seem to be "about" anything in the way a portrait is about a face, and yet it can affect me more powerfully than either. I mentioned this to a friend who studied music history at university, and she told me, more or less in passing, that Wagner became fixated on a philosopher named Schopenhauer for essentially this reason, and that there is an elaborate theory, buried in an old book of his called The World as Will and Idea (I gather it's sometimes translated as The World as Will and Representation), purporting to explain why music is supposedly unlike every other art. I have no background in philosophy whatsoever, but I found a free translation online, the Haldane and Kemp version from the 1880s, and worked my way through several hundred pages to reach the section on music near the end of the Third Book, since that seemed to be the part everyone keeps citing.

I can follow Schopenhauer well enough in outline: there is supposed to be some underlying "Will" behind everything, and both ordinary things and the other arts are said to relate to this Will only indirectly, by way of what he calls "Platonic Ideas." But then I reached the following paragraph and found myself unable to get any further:

"The (Platonic) Ideas are the adequate objectification of will. To excite or suggest the knowledge of these by means of the representation of particular things (for works of art themselves are always representations of particular things) is the end of all the other arts, which can only be attained by a corresponding change in the knowing subject. Thus all these arts objectify the will indirectly only by means of the Ideas; and since our world is nothing but the manifestation of the Ideas in multiplicity, though their entrance into the principium individuationis (the form of the knowledge possible for the individual as such), music also, since it passes over the Ideas, is entirely independent of the phenomenal world, ignores it altogether, could to a certain extent exist if there was no world at all, which cannot be said of the other arts. Music is as direct an objectification and copy of the whole will as the world itself, nay, even as the Ideas, whose multiplied manifestation constitutes the world of individual things. Music is thus by no means like the other arts, the copy of the Ideas, but the copy of the will itself, whose objectivity the Ideas are. This is why the effect of music is so much more powerful and penetrating than that of the other arts, for they speak only of shadows, but it speaks of the thing itself."

I have read this paragraph perhaps a dozen times, and while I can sense, vaguely, that it's gesturing at exactly the feeling I described above about music not depicting anything and yet moving me more than things that do, I can't actually reconstruct the argument in my own words, and I don't trust a mere feeling of recognition. Would you be able to walk me through what Schopenhauer is claiming here, step by step, in language suited to someone without any training in philosophy? I am especially puzzled by three things: first, what work the "Platonic Ideas" are doing in this system, and why he thinks painting, sculpture, and poetry can only reach the will second-hand, through them; second, what it could actually mean, in concrete terms, for something as abstract as a sequence of tones to be a "direct copy" of the will itself rather than a copy of an Idea, given that neither "copy" nor "direct" seem like they should apply literally to sound; and third, whether the claim that music "could to a certain extent exist if there was no world at all" is meant as a serious metaphysical proposition or is closer to a rhetorical flourish, and, if it is serious, what kind of consideration could possibly count for or against it.
