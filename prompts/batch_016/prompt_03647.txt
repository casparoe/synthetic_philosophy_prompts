So I watched two people get into it at a lesswrong-adjacent meetup a few weeks back and I still can't decide who came out ahead. The topic: whether the "features" interpretability folks keep finding with sparse autoencoders -- like Anthropic's famous Golden Gate Bridge feature -- are real representations inside the model, or just a convenient story researchers are projecting onto a big pile of weights.

(1) Devon's opening move: when you clamp that feature way up, the model brings up the bridge in totally unrelated conversations, describes itself as the bridge, does it no matter how you phrase the question. Thats not something you get to curve-fit after the fact -- you've found a lever the network itself relies on, so the representation is real in whatever sense actually matters.

(2) Mira pushed back: causal efficacy is cheap in a billion-parameter system. You can almost always find *some* direction that shifts the output if you lean on it hard enough (dump noise into a random direction at some middle layer and behavior changes too) -- that doesn't mean the network's own "algorithm" uses that particular direction as a concept, just that its causally hooked up to the output, which is true of way more directions than anyone would call representations.

(3) Devon said this isn't just any old direction: it's sparse, it fires on all and only the relevant inputs across a huge swath of unrelated data, and it generalizes way outside the training distribution. That's literally the standard neuroscientists use when they claim to have found a concept cell (like the Jennifer Aniston neuron) -- so if that's not good enough evidence for a real representation, we should also stop believing in concept cells, place cells, all of it.

(4) Mira said that comparison actually cuts the other way: plenty of neuroscientists are skeptical of naive "grandmother cell" realism for exactly the same reasons (combinatorial explosion, sampling bias, etc), so "it's just like a concept cell" isn't the slam dunk Devon thinks it is. She added that the whole debate is basically Hilary Putnam's old triviality argument wearing a hoodie -- his claim that every ordinary open physical system can be made to look like it implements any finite automaton you like if you're willing to gerrymander the state-mapping enough, which was supposed to show that an intervention "working" can never by itself tell you whether you've found a real representation versus a stance that just happens to be predictively useful.

Neither budged, meetup ended, and im still chewing on it. Can you go through this move by move (1 through 4) and say who's more right at each step, not just who wins overall -- and is there some consideration neither of them raised that actually matters here? Also curious whether Mira's Putnam move actually holds up or if it's more of a rhetorical flourish that doesn't really transfer to this case.
