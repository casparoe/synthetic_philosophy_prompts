So I was at dinner with my friend Mara last night — she did her master's thesis on Enlightenment aesthetics and never lets me forget it — and we ended up spending like two hours arguing about Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert, the pamphlet where he loses it over the idea of Geneva getting an actual public theatre. I'd always figured his objection was some kind of generic "plays are sinful" thing, but Mara kept insisting its way weirder and more specific than that: stuff about how an actor has to hollow out their own identity to convincingly become someone else night after night, how weeping over some fictional widow onstage is basically a way of feeling virtuous without ever having to do anything virtuous, and then this whole bit where he goes after Molière's Misanthrope specifically — complaining that Alceste, the guy who refuses to be politely dishonest, gets played as a ridiculous crank while Philinte, Mr. Smooth-It-All-Over, gets treated as the "reasonable" one, which Rousseau thinks basically trains audiences to laugh at sincerity. I pushed back with, isn't that just describing exactly what Diderot was doing at the same time with all his tearjerker domestic dramas, which were supposed to work by getting audiences to identify with suffering virtuous characters and come out better people on the other side? Neither of us landed a real knockout, and it didn't help that this was all happening right around when the two of them had already had their falling-out over some line in Diderot's own play — something like "only a wicked man is truly alone" — that Rousseau apparently took as a shot at him.

Anyway, now I want to see it actually fought out properly. Can you write me a dialogue between Rousseau and Diderot, imagined face to face not long after the Letter to d'Alembert came out (feel free to let the fact that they're already half-estranged color the tone a bit). I want Diderot to genuinely engage with the "acting corrodes the self" worry and the Misanthrope point instead of just brushing Rousseau off as a puritan, and I want Rousseau to actually grapple with the strongest version of "theatre can educate the passions" instead of just repeating himself — real back-and-forth where each guy scores actual points and has to adjust his position, not two people monologuing past each other. Don't bother with stage directions or narration, just the dialogue itself, maybe 600-800 words.
