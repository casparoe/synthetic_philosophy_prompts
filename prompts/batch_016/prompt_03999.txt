Now retired after a long career as a research chemist, I have spent much of the past year working through David Lewis's papers, having first been drawn to him by the systematic, almost mathematical rigor of On the Plurality of Worlds. Among his shorter and less-discussed pieces I came across "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?", where Lewis observes that almost no one accepts substitutionary punishment when the penalty is imprisonment or death (a devoted friend cannot simply serve a burglar's prison term), yet nearly everyone accepts it when the penalty is a serious fine, provided the offender consents. He considers, and rejects, the two obvious deflationary explanations for this asymmetry -- that such fines are really just fees in disguise, and that we merely tolerate fine-substitution because it is impractical to prevent -- and concludes that all of us, not only the Christians who read the Atonement as penal substitution, are of two minds on the matter, which he takes to be "some evidence that somehow they might both be right."

I would like you to resist that closing move. Please argue that the fine/imprisonment asymmetry Lewis documents in fact provides no genuine evidential support for the coherence of substitutionary punishment in general, and hence none for the doctrine of the Atonement in particular -- so that his tu quoque fails even on its own modest terms. In making your case, engage directly with Lewis's own rebuttals of the "mere fees" and "mere impracticality" explanations, rather than simply reasserting them.
