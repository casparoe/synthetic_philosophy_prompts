As a novelist, I rely constantly on the "test scene": the moment when pressure, temptation, or crisis is supposed to reveal what a character is really like underneath. I have lately been unsettled by the finks-and-masks literature surrounding the conditional analysis of dispositions in analytic metaphysics. C. B. Martin's electro-fink shows that a counterfactual such as "she would act bravely if put in danger" can be rendered true at the very last instant by some hidden mechanism, even though no stable disposition toward bravery was ever present; Mark Johnston's work on masking and Alexander Bird's on antidotes show the mirror-image case, in which a genuinely stable disposition, such as courage or cruelty, fails to manifest because something extraneous intervenes, while the disposition itself remains entirely intact. Please set out what you take to be the most important implications of this fink/mask problem for how a novelist should construct and interpret test scenes: whether any single such scene could ever count as decisive evidence of an underlying, stable trait, and whether deliberately "finkish" or "masked" reversals are a legitimate device for producing a twist without cheating the reader's sense of psychological consistency.
