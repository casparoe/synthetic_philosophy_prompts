I'm a novelist currently at work on a book about two former climbing partners, once inseparable, whose long friendship fractures after one of them turns back partway up a difficult route, abandoning a summit attempt the two had spent a year planning together, and leaving the other to finish the climb alone. My editor has pushed back on an early draft of the confrontation scene that follows, saying the wronged climber's anger reads as generic wounded pride rather than as something with real ethical weight. Trying to fix this sent me down a rabbit hole of reading on collective intentionality, where I got stuck on the disagreement between Michael Bratman's planning-theory account of shared intention and Margaret Gilbert's plural subject theory. On Bratman's reductive picture, a shared intention is ultimately built out of each party's own intention that "we act," held together by common knowledge, meshing sub-plans, and mutual responsiveness — so any residual obligation not to defect is just an ordinary norm against exploiting induced reliance. On Gilbert's non-reductive picture, by contrast, forming a joint intention creates a joint commitment that generates genuine, distinctive obligations and entitlements running between the parties, obligations that neither party can rescind alone, so that simply changing your mind and walking off doesn't end the commitment but violates it.

What I need from you is a scenario, rigorous enough to survive a philosopher's scrutiny, in which these two accounts would actually issue different verdicts about what has gone wrong when one partner quits on the other. You're welcome to build on my climbers or invent a different pair and a different joint venture, but please don't just hand me Gilbert's own "walking together" case in new clothes — I have already come across that example in my reading and want something engineered specifically for this purpose. I want a scenario constructed so that every condition in Bratman's story is satisfied (the common knowledge, the meshing sub-plans, the mutual responsiveness) and yet it remains a live question whether something further — some sui generis, joint-commitment-based obligation of the kind Gilbert describes — has been violated; or, if you think the more interesting case runs the other way, one that shows that instead. Either way, spell out exactly which feature of the scenario is doing the work of separating the two theories, and how a committed Bratmanian and a committed Gilbertian would each describe the wrong that has or hasn't occurred, so that I can tell which picture actually matches the moral texture I'm reaching for in my own scene.
