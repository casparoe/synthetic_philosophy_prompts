A friend of mine who works in AI alignment and I spent most of a dinner last week arguing about how much weight coherence arguments can actually bear. Her position was the familiar strong one: if an agent's preferences are incomplete or intransitive, a sufficiently clever counterparty can walk it through a sequence of trades — a money pump — that leaves it strictly worse off than where it started, purely by exploiting the gaps or cycles; so anything that survives long enough under competitive or optimization pressure will end up coherent in the Von Neumann–Morgenstern sense, with complete, transitive, independence-respecting preferences representable by a single utility function. I wasn't so sure.

I raised two cases against her. The first was Elliott Thornley's proposal for keeping advanced agents safely shutdownable: give the agent a genuine preferential gap between trajectories of different lengths, governed by what he calls a Timestep Dominance principle, so that it never has reason to manipulate its own shutdown button, while its preferences remain incomplete rather than completed into a single ranking. The second was the older, more purely philosophical idea of "resolute choice" from Edward McClennen, on which an agent that would otherwise have dynamically inconsistent preferences escapes the pump simply by committing in advance to a plan and sticking to it, rather than reconsidering at every choice node. My friend wasn't moved: she pointed out that Thornley's gaps have already been accused, by Wentworth and Lorell, of letting an agent be walked into a strictly dominated trajectory one step at a time, and argued on her own account that "resoluteness" just smuggles completeness back in, since committing to a fully specified plan requires having already settled every comparison the plan will ever call on. We went around in circles for an hour and neither of us budged.

I'd like your help making the case I was reaching for more rigorously than I managed that evening. Please argue for the position that money-pump arguments do not establish that a rational, or "sufficiently advanced," agent must have complete, transitive, independence-respecting preferences representable by a single utility function — that is, that there exist policies short of full VNM-rationality (incomplete-but-dominance-respecting preferences, resolute commitment to a plan, or anything else you think works) that block the exploitation a money pump relies on, without covertly presupposing the very completeness they are meant to do without. Please engage with the actual mechanics of the pump — what the exploiter needs at each step, and why backward induction is supposed to force the agent's hand — rather than resting the case on the general unrealism of the VNM axioms for bounded agents, and take on the strongest version of my friend's "smuggled-in completeness" worry directly.
