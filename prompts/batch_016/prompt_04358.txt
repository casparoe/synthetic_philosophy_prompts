Okay so for my philosophy of mind seminar (we're doing a whole unit on theories of mental representation -- Dretske, Fodor, all that) our professor brought in a friend of hers who does mechanistic interpretability for a living, and honestly it kind of broke my brain a little. She walked us through the Anthropic "Golden Gate Claude" example, where they trained a sparse autoencoder on the model's internal activations and found one specific direction that reliably fires whenever the model is dealing with the Golden Gate Bridge -- fires across languages, even fires on pictures of the bridge -- and when they turned that feature up super high the model got literally obsessed and started working the bridge into totally unrelated answers. She used it as her go-to example of why she thinks the "features" mech interp finds are real, causally load-bearing things inside the network, not just a story we tell after the fact to make ourselves feel better about a black box.

That's the part I can't shake. If you can (1) pull a concept straight out of the weights with a simple decoder, and (2) grab that exact direction and dial it up or down to reliably produce or suppress the behavior you'd expect if the concept were "really" in there, then hasn't the whole "do neural nets actually have representations or are they just doing statistics" debate basically been settled by data at this point? Like, what's even left for a Dretske-style skeptic to say once you've shown you can find the thing AND steer it like a knob? My roommate (CS major, zero patience for what he calls "vibes-based philosophy") thinks this is basically proof the philosophers lost this one and just haven't noticed yet, and part of me definately agrees with him, but something about "we found it and can turn the knob, therefore the metaphysics is settled" feels too fast. Is he right that this is basically a closed case now, or is he skipping over something important?
