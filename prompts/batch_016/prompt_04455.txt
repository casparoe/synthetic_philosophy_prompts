I teach a graduate seminar on early modern political philosophy, and this term we have been working slowly through Spinoza's Tractatus Politicus, chapter by chapter. Two weeks ago we reached the treatise's eleventh and final chapter, "Of Democracy" — and then, quite abruptly, the text simply stops. Spinoza died in February 1677 before he could finish it (some scholars think he had barely begun what was meant to be the first of two chapters on the subject), so what remains is, as far as anyone can tell, the last piece of sustained philosophical argument he ever wrote. Just before the manuscript breaks off, Spinoza has defined the democratic franchise so as to exclude foreigners and the "infamous," and then — more troublingly for a thinker usually so careful to derive political conclusions from general metaphysical principles rather than from unexamined custom — "women and slaves, who are under the authority of men and masters, and also children and wards." He anticipates an objection to the exclusion of women and, in the Elwes translation, answers it as follows:

"But, perhaps, someone will ask, whether women are under men's authority by nature or institution? For if it has been by mere institution, then we had no reason compelling us to exclude women from government. But if we consult experience itself, we shall find that the origin of it is in their weakness. For there has never been a case of men and women reigning together, but wherever on the earth men are found, there we see that men rule, and women are ruled, and that on this plan, both sexes live in harmony. But on the other hand, the Amazons, who are reported to have held rule of old, did not suffer men to stop in their country, but reared only their female children, killing the males to whom they gave birth. But if by nature women were equal to men, and were equally distinguished by force of character and ability, in which human power and therefore human right chiefly consist; surely among nations so many and different some would be found, where both sexes rule alike, and others, where men are ruled by women, and so brought up, that they can make less use of their abilities. And since this is nowhere the case, one may assert with perfect propriety, that women have not by nature equal right with men: but that they necessarily give way to men, and that thus it cannot happen, that both sexes should rule alike, much less that men should be ruled by women. But if we further reflect upon human passions, how men, in fact, generally love women merely from the passion of lust, and esteem their cleverness and wisdom in proportion to the excellence of their beauty, and also how very ill-disposed men are to suffer the women they love to show any sort of favour to others, and other facts of this kind, we shall easily see that men and women cannot rule alike without great hurt to peace. But of this enough."

And there the manuscript ends. When I put this passage to my seminar, several students wanted to set it aside as an unphilosophical lapse into the prejudice of Spinoza's age, but that struck me as too quick, given how insistently he otherwise grounds political conclusions in his official doctrine that natural right simply is the extent of one's power, rather than in appeals to custom or convention. I would like help reconstructing the argument on its own terms before we reconvene. As I read it, Spinoza wants to move from a purely historical premise — that no society has ever been observed in which women rule as equals — to a metaphysical conclusion about women's nature and their natural right. Could you help me spell out what additional premises would have to be doing the work to make that inference valid, and say whether Spinozism is actually entitled to them, given his usual insistence, especially in the Ethics, that apparent "natures" and incapacities are so often just artifacts of imagination, custom, and the play of the passions rather than fixed essences? I am also genuinely unsure what to make of the final sentence, where he shifts from talking about women's incapacity to talking about ordinary male jealousy as the real explanation for their exclusion from rule — does that sentence quietly undercut the "weakness" argument that precedes it, or is there a way of reading the two as parts of a single, consistent Spinozist account of why power in fact distributes as it does?
