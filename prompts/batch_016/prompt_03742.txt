So this came up after my ACX meetup's reading group last week — we'd just gone through the "surrogate goals" idea from the s-risk/CLR crowd (basically: an agent that might get blackmailed, including by another agent it can't causally interact with but which can accurately simulate/predict it — "acausal" blackmail — inoculates itself by also adopting some deliberately meaningless extra goal, like caring intensely about there never existing a platinum sphere of exactly 42.82cm diameter, and precommitting to respond to threats against that fake goal exactly as it would to real ones, so a rational blackmailer just threatens the sphere instead of whatever you actually value). Afterward, Owen and Mara kept going at it in the parking lot and neither budged. Roughly, from memory:

1. Owen: the whole scheme is reflectively unstable, so it doesn't really work. Once you've bolted on "I care about spheres" purely as bait, you know its bait, so the moment no threat is imminent you have every incentive to quietly strip it back out, since it's dead weight the rest of the time. A commitment you'll ditch whenever convenient isn't a commitment, it's a costume, and any threatener worth worrying about should see through costumes.

2. Mara: that argument proves too much. "You could always defect from your own precommitment" sinks every commitment strategy in decision theory, including plain old one-boxing on Newcomb's problem — if that move were fatal here there'd be no such thing as credible commitment anywhere, acausal or not. So this isn't a problem specific to surrogate goals.

3. Owen: there's a real disanalogy, though. One-boxing pays off from the inside, in expectation, so keeping it is just what a well-designed policy does. The surrogate goal is different: carrying "I terminally value spheres" around is pure overhead in every world where nobody threatens you, and the moment a threat does land, you have to actually act like sphere-destruction is catastrophic — which, from the inside, is indistinguishable from just valuing it. So you haven't dodged the cost, you've relocated it into quietly warping your own preferences forever.

4. Mara: that's not a flaw, it's the mechanism doing exactly what it's supposed to, and it's cheap precisely because spheres never come up outside of threat scenarios anyway. She added that Owen's real objection is a better one hiding inside a worse one: nobody actually has a story for how an agent gets its future self to keep treating a known-fake goal as terminal, without the fakeness either leaking to threateners or quietly stopping being fake.

Can you go through this move by move and say who's right at each step (not just who "wins" overall), and point out anything important that both of them are missing?
