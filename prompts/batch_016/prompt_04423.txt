The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem shows that any social choice function defined on the universal domain of strict preferences, with a range of at least three outcomes, is either manipulable or dictatorial. The literature has proposed a number of ways of living with, or getting around, this result:

1. Restrict preferences to a single-peaked domain (median-voter schemes)
2. Replace dominant-strategy incentive compatibility with Bayesian (interim) incentive compatibility
3. Introduce quasi-linear utility and monetary transfers (VCG/Groves mechanisms)
4. Randomize over outcomes (e.g., random dictatorship)
5. Restrict the outcome set to two alternatives (May's theorem)
6. Allow set-valued outcomes, i.e., social choice correspondences rather than single-valued functions
7. Tolerate manipulability in principle, but rely on its computational intractability in practice
8. Simply accept a dictator
