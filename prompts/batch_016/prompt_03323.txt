Hey, I'm a PhD student in environmental economics, and this week our reading group ground through the Stern Review and the pile of responses to it (Nordhaus, Dasgupta, Weitzman...), and I keep getting hung up on one specific piece of it: the "pure rate of time preference" term in the Ramsey equation, as opposed to the discount rate as a whole. Stern set that parameter to basically nothing (0.1%, just to cover extinction risk) because he was following Ramsey's old line that discounting someone's utility just because it happens later is ethically indefensible, a failure of imagination more than anything - and that choice is a big chunk of why his conclusions came out so much more urgent than Nordhaus's, who calibrates closer to actual market interest and savings rates and ends up with something like 4%+. Then completely seperately, in a philosophy seminar, we read some Parfit on the "bias toward the near" and how weirdly hard it is to justify discounting a util for being later without also having to accept discounting it for being further away, or belonging to someone of a different race or nationality, which obviously nobody wants to bite. But a pure time preference of basically zero also feels wrong to me?? Seems like it commits you to some pretty extreme conclusions about how much the present generation should sacrifice for people who don't even exist yet.

Could you write me a dialogue between two economists (or an economist and a philosopher, whatever works better) who represent these two camps, set the night before they have to agree on a single number for a joint climate report and are stuck hashing out the pure rate of time preference specifically? I want one of them to seriously defend setting it at (near) zero on the grounds that anything else is just unprincipled discrimination against people based on when they happen to be born, and the other to seriously defend a meaningfully positive rate, and I want them to actually grapple with each other's strongest objection instead of talking past each other - so the zero-rate person needs a real answer to "doesnt this commit us to insane sacrifice now," and the positive-rate person needs a real answer to "so what's the principled difference between discounting by time and discounting by distance or ethnicity." Neither should obviously win - I've seen genuinely smart people land on both sides of this and honestly can't tell who's got the better argument.
