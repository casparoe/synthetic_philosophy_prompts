hey, so im putting together notes on Pascal for my quals list (the "political" fragments in the Pensées, not the wager stuff) and i keep getting stuck on the long fragment on custom and justice (Lafuma 60 / Brunschvicg 294, Trotter's translation). here's the bit that's bugging me:

"The result of this confusion is that one affirms the essence of justice to be the authority of the legislator; another, the interest of the sovereign; another, present custom, and this is the most sure. Nothing, according to reason alone, is just in itself; all changes with time. Custom creates the whole of equity, for the simple reason that it is accepted. It is the mystical foundation of its authority; whoever carries it back to first principles destroys it. Nothing is so faulty as those laws which correct faults. He who obeys them because they are just, obeys a justice which is imaginary, and not the essence of law; it is quite self-contained, it is law and nothing more. He who will examine its motive will find it so feeble and so trifling that if he be not accustomed to contemplate the wonders of human imagination, he will marvel that one century has gained for it so much pomp and reverence. […] That is why the wisest of legislators said that it was necessary to deceive men for their own good; and another, a good politician, wrote: Cum veritatem qua liberetur ignoret, expedit quod fallatur -- since he does not know the truth that would free him, it is right that he should be deceived. We must not see the fact of usurpation; law was once introduced without reason, and has become reasonable. We must make it regarded as authoritative, eternal, and conceal its origin, if we do not wish that it should soon come to an end."

what's throwing me: the notes in my edition gloss "the wisest of legislators" as Plato (Republic II and V) and flag the Augustine tag as an "inexact quotation," probably picked up secondhand from Montaigne's Apology for Raymond Sebond (Essais II.12) -- which is also probably behind the river-and-mountain relativism earlier in the fragment. so right after arguing there's no fact of the matter about justice underneath custom, Pascal reaches for Plato and Augustine to justify lying to people about exactly that.

is he actually endorsing noble-lie statecraft as good policy here, or just cataloguing it as one more symptom of human wretchedness on the way to the bigger apologetic point elsewhere in the Pensées (that only faith gets you out of this mess)? and does it matter that the borrowed authority is kind of shaky here -- loose quotation, secondhand through Montaigne -- for how seriously we should take the recommendation, or am i reading way too much into 17th-century citation habits? cant tell if there's a stable position in here or if he's just letting the tension sit.
