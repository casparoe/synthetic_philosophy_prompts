Ok so for my history of ethics seminar this term we've been slogging through Bentham, and this week's reading was from that "Of Sexual Irregularities" volume of his collected works -- basically his never-published manuscripts where he goes after sodomy laws, calls "unnatural" just a fancy word for "uncommon," and argues that punishing people for private consensual sex that harms literally no one is just legislating other peoples squeamishness. Nobody in the room had heard of this stuff before so it kind of derailed the whole discussion section, and after class two people from my group, Owen and Naomi, kept arguing about it in the hallway for like 20 minutes. I wrote down the gist afterward because it was kind of a lot:

(1) Owen said the argument is basically airtight utilitarianism: nobody's directly harmed by what consenting adults do in private, so criminalizing it just adds suffering (the punishment itself) with nothing on the other side of the ledger, and Bentham's right that other peoples disgust shouldn't count as a real cost, it's prejudice dressed up as harm.
(2) Naomi pushed back that if Bentham's a genuine hedonist he doesn't get to just wave disgust away as "not real" -- pain is pain no matter the source, that's the whole premise of the felicific calculus. If enough people are grossed out badly enough, straightforward addition could tip the other way, so Owen's assuming a distinction between legitimate and illegitimate pain that bare hedonism doesn't hand you for free.
(3) Owen said Bentham's not just asserting this though: disgust reactions are all over the map depending on the act (someone's disgusted by X, plenty of others aren't), so treating any one group's squeamishness as the legal standard is arbitrary, not neutral counting. Plus the calculus already has dimensions like certainty and remoteness built in -- imagined disgust at something you'll never witness is a flimsier pain than the certain, intense pain of actual imprisonment or execution, which was literally the penalty on the table at the time.
(4) Naomi said fine, decent correction, but "extent" is also one of Bentham's own dimensions, and mass mild disgust multiplied across a whole population can mathematically swamp a smaller number of severe harms. So on Owen's own terms, tolerance ends up hostage to however the headcount happens to fall, meaning Bentham's shown that persecution doesn't currently pay, not that it's wrong -- which is a much weaker result than Owen's crediting him with.
(5) Owen answered that Bentham's got one more move: disgust founded on false beliefs (that the act is unnatural, or damns you, or whatever) isn't just another input to sum up, it's the kind of thing that's supposed to erode once people are better informed, so weighting it at face value like any other preference is a mistake.
(6) Naomi said that's honestly the most interesting point either of them made all night, but it requires some independent filter on which pains are "enlightened" enough to count, and the second you allow that you're not doing simple hedonic addition anymore, you're doing something more like value-laden preference-screening -- which is a bigger departure from strict Benthamite utilitarianism than either of them started out assuming.
