I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on early modern political thought, and I've been drafting a dissertation chapter on Spinoza's political philosophy centered on the Tractatus Politicus. Something has been bothering me about how the treatise actually ends. Throughout the work, Spinoza grounds political right in a purely naturalistic, and ostensibly universal, principle: each thing has exactly as much right (jus) as it has power (potentia) to exist and act (TP 2.3-4), a formula meant to follow, like everything else in his system, from the necessity of nature's laws rather than from convention or transcendent moral fact. Given how general that principle is supposed to be, I find the treatise's final argument, about who is entitled to a vote in a democracy, very hard to reconstruct in a way that's both charitable to Spinoza and consistent with his other commitments, and I'd appreciate help thinking it through.

Here is the passage, from the end of chapter 11 (TP 11.3-4; I'm quoting the old Elwes/Gosset translation, since it's what I have to hand, so apologies if the wording doesn't match whatever edition you'd otherwise reach for):

"...I added, besides, 'who are independent,' except in so far as they are under allegiance to the laws of the dominion, to exclude women and slaves, who are under the authority of men and masters, and also children and wards, as long as they are under the authority of parents and guardians... But, perhaps, someone will ask, whether women are under men's authority by nature or institution? For if it has been by mere institution, then we had no reason compelling us to exclude women from government. But if we consult experience itself, we shall find that the origin of it is in their weakness. For there has never been a case of men and women reigning together, but wherever on the earth men are found, there we see that men rule, and women are ruled, and that on this plan, both sexes live in harmony. But on the other hand, the Amazons, who are reported to have held rule of old, did not suffer men to stop in their country, but reared only their female children, killing the males to whom they gave birth. But if by nature women were equal to men, and were equally distinguished by force of character and ability, in which human power and therefore human right chiefly consist; surely among nations so many and different some would be found, where both sexes rule alike, and others, where men are ruled by women, and so brought up, that they can make less use of their abilities. And since this is nowhere the case, one may assert with perfect propriety, that women have not by nature equal right with men: but that they necessarily give way to men, and that thus it cannot happen, that both sexes should rule alike, much less that men should be ruled by women. But if we further reflect upon human passions, how men, in fact, generally love women merely from the passion of lust, and esteem their cleverness and wisdom in proportion to the excellence of their beauty, and also how very ill-disposed men are to suffer the women they love to show any sort of favour to others, and other facts of this kind, we shall easily see that men and women cannot rule alike without great hurt to peace. But of this enough."

As far as anyone can tell, that is the last passage of philosophy Spinoza ever wrote: the manuscript simply breaks off there, only four short sections into the chapter on democracy (a small fraction of the length he devotes to monarchy or aristocracy), and he died not long after, in February 1677, without ever returning to it. Two things about the argument itself puzzle me, and I'd welcome help with either. First, the move from the premise that no society has ever had joint or female rule to the conclusion that women therefore lack, by nature rather than by circumstance, the power/right that men have looks like it needs an additional, unstated premise: one that rules out the possibility that this historical uniformity has an ordinary contingent explanation (in child-rearing arrangements, physical coercion, custom, and so on) rather than tracking some more basic natural disparity in potentia. I don't see why a necessitarian, who thinks every social arrangement alike follows with strict necessity from finite causes, is entitled to treat this one historical regularity as diagnostic of nature specifically, rather than as just more deeply entrenched history. Second, I can't tell whether Spinoza is quietly trading on two different senses of "power": the deflationary, almost tautological sense doing the definitional work earlier in the treatise, on which whatever in fact exists or acts thereby has exactly the power (and so the right) to do so, versus a much thicker, empirically loaded sense of power as something like physical or social force, which is what the actual argument about women seems to need in order to go through. I know there's a substantial feminist literature on this passage, but rather than a survey of that debate, what I'd find most useful is your own reconstruction of the argument's logical structure and an assessment of whether it's really in tension with Spinoza's stated principles, or whether there's a reading on which it isn't. I'd also be curious what, if anything, you think should be made of the fact that the work happens to break off at exactly this point.
