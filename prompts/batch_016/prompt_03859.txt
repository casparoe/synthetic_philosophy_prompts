So we spent most of Thursday's seminar on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem and I feel like I'm the only person in the room who came out of it confused instead of impressed. The way our professor laid it out: if each person's preferences over risky options satisfy the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms, and society's preferences do too, and on top of that you accept a pretty weak-sounding Pareto condition (basically: if literally everyone is indifferent between two options, society has to be indifferent too), then it's a straight-up theorem that the social utility function comes out as a weighted sum of everybody's individual utility functions. Which sounds to me like Harsanyi basically derived utilitarianism out of a handful of axioms that almost nobody would want to reject — like, who's gonna say "no, I don't want my own preferences to be rational" or "no, society shouldn't care when literally every single person prefers A to B"?

That's basically my question: if this is a genuine proof, why isnt it treated as THE knockdown argument that ends the utilitarianism-vs-everyone-else debate? Why do Rawlsians, prioritarians, etc. get to just keep asserting their own view instead of having to point to the specific axiom they reject and defend rejecting it? My roommate (more the stats-y type) tried to tell me it's a big deal that the theorem doesn't actually pin down the weights on each person, but I don't really see why that saves anyone — even with unequal weights you'd still just be getting some weighted-average flavor of utilitarianism, not, like, Rawlsian maximin or whatever totally different thing critics actually believe. So am I missing a step where the theorem doesn't actually get you all the way to "you have to be a utilitarian," or is this really just a case of people not liking the conclusion and refusing to follow the math where it leads?
