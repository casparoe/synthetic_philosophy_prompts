I've been reading a biography of Georg Cantor for a piece I'm working on about mathematicians who basically broke their brains on infinity, and I got completely derailed by his letters to Cardinal Franzelin at the Vatican, where he's practically pleading with the Church to bless his transfinite numbers by carefully distinguishing them from the "Absolute Infinite" that belongs to God alone. What strikes me is that this wasn't just abstract math for Cantor - he genuinely believed completed, actual infinities were legitimate objects you could reason about with total rigor (ordinals, cardinals, the whole transfinite hierarchy), which went against basically the entire prior tradition since Aristotle saying infinity could only ever be "potential," never a finished, completed totality. Guys like Kronecker thought he was peddling dangerous nonsense.

Anyway, this got me thinking about Thomson's lamp, which didn't show up until 1954 (decades after Cantor died, so he obviously never heard of it). Quick version for context: philosopher James Thomson imagined a lamp switched on for a minute, off for half a minute, on for a quarter minute, and so on, halving the interval every time, so infinitely many switches happen inside a tidy two minutes - then he asks whether the lamp is on or off once the two minutes are up. I mentioned this to a mathematician friend and she just said "oh, Cantor would've loved that, he was pro-infinity," which felt like a cop-out answer. Given that Cantor spent his career insisting completed infinite totalities are perfectly coherent, do you think he'd actually see Thomson's lamp as a genuine problem for that view, something he'd have had to work to explain away? Or would his own toolkit - ordinals, order types, the fact that an omega-sequence just doesn't come with a "next" term the way Thomson's question assumes - hand him a clean way to dissolve the puzzle that a strict Aristotelian potentialist could never pull off? I'd love an answer that actually digs into his specific mathematical and theological commitments rather then just "he liked infinity so he'd be fine with it."
