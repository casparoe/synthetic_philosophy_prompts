A few of us in my metaphysics seminar got into an honest-to-god argument after class about the statue-and-clay case, specifically Gibbard's version with the statue "Goliath" and the lump of clay "Lumpl" that composes it -- we'd just read the paper for that week. His setup has a sculptor make the top and bottom halves seperately and then join them, so the statue and the lump come into existence at the exact same instant, and later the whole thing gets squashed into a ball, so they also go out of existence at the exact same instant. Two people in the group would not let it go, and I keep flip-flopping on who actually had the better of it, so I want to lay out the whole back-and-forth for someone less invested than me.

(1) He opened by saying that once you accept Kripke's proof that identity, when it holds, holds necessarily, the "contingent identity" reading Gibbard is famous for just doesn't make sense: either Lumpl and Goliath are the very same object, necessarily, whether we like it or not, or -- since squashing the clay into a ball would destroy the statue without destroying the lump -- they were never one object to begin with, and we've just got two things stacked on exactly the same matter, weird as that sounds. (2) She said that's exactly the bullet Gibbard didn't want to bite: two objects that are completely indistinguishable in shape, color, weight, and location for the entire time they coexist, differing only in what would've happened to them in situations that never actually occur, is the stranger view, and that the Lumpl/Goliath case was, for Gibbard, good enough reason to go back and reject a premise of Kripke's argument rather than just accept "two objects, one space." (3) He came back with: you don't need to touch Kripke's proof at all to get non-identity -- "can survive being squashed" and "can't" are properties the two things have right now, today, not just facts about some other possible world happening to a third thing, so Leibniz's Law alone (identical things share every property) gets you non-identity for free, no contingent-identity machinery required. (4) Her comeback was that this move quietly assumes exactly what's in dispute: whether "can survive being squashed" is really a property attaching to the lump-as-opposed-to-the-statue, rather than depending entirely on which of the two you'd already decided to track through the squashing, is the very question on the table, so it cant be used as a neutral premise against her. That's where it ended, more or less at a standstill, and honestly I think each of them scored real points at different moments, which is why I can't settle it myself.
