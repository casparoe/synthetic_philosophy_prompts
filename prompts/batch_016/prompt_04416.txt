I've been getting into effective altruism / longtermism stuff on and off for about a year now (no formal philosophy background, I'm just a curious person with too much time on youtube and reddit), and there's this thing called the "repugnant conclusion" that keeps coming up like it's some huge unsolved problem. As I understand it: if you just add up everyone's happiness (total utilitarianism), then a mind bogglingly huge population where everyone's life is barely worth living beats out a smaller population where everyone is thriving, just because you can multiply the tiny amount of good by enough people to out-total the smaller group. People talk about this like its kept philosophers up at night for decades, Parfit apparently died without ever fully solving it, and theres a whole missing "Theory X" that's supposed to fix everything but doesn't exist yet.

My confusion is this: isn't the fix just to average happiness instead of adding it up? If you divide total wellbeing by the number of people instead of summing it, then packing the world with barely-worth-living lives drags the average DOWN instead of up, so it stops looking "better" to do that. This feels so obvious that I brought it up with my roommate (who took one ethics class in college) and even she just said "it's not that simple" without explaining why. Every explainer I find online just breezes past this and starts talking about impossibility theorems and weird "critical level" theories instead of just saying "yeah, use the average, problem solved." Am I missing some reason this doesn't actually work, or is switching to average utilitarianism basically the answer and nobody just says so plainly?
