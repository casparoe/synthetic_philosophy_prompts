I'm writing a novel whose protagonist, a philosophy professor and lifelong Davidsonian, is invited onto a panel to debate whether a new large language model "means" anything by what it produces. I want her argument to be something Davidson could plausibly have made himself, not a pastiche that merely sounds right, so I need to work out his actual position before I put words in her mouth.

Davidson died in 2003, well before anything resembling a large language model existed, so he never confronted this question directly. Using only his documented views—radical interpretation, the principle of charity, and especially the triangulation requirement that content-bearing thought depends on causal interaction between at least two creatures and a shared world of objects they jointly respond to—work out the verdict he would most likely have reached. Does triangulation rule out, in principle, treating such a system as a genuine subject of interpretation, something with beliefs and meanings, or does it remain merely a very sophisticated pattern-completion device? Alternatively, could something relevantly like triangulation be reconstructed from the system's ongoing exchanges with the humans who use it?

Give my protagonist a specific, defensible verdict, and have her argue for it as rigorously as Davidson would have, anticipating the strongest objection a fellow panelist might raise.
