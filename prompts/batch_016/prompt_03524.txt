I've been chasing a feature on the arrow of time for a few months now, and a couple weeks back I tagged along to a physics-of-cosmology talk and then to the pub afterward, where two of my sources ended up having a much better argument about it than anything I've gotten on the record. One, a cosmology postdoc I'll call Marcus, was making the case that the arrow of time isn't really mysterious once you grant that the universe started in an extremely low-entropy state: (1) almost every microstate compatible with that early macrostate evolves toward higher entropy, so the "arrow" is just statistics riding on a boundary condition — he kept calling it "the Past Hypothesis," after Albert. Elena, who's in philosophy of physics, wasn't having it: (2) she said the dynamical laws are time-symmetric, so if you only conditionalize on some mid-history macrostate, plain statistical reasoning predicts entropy was probably higher in the past too (approaching equilibrium in both directions) — which is just false, we have records showing it was lower. So sticking "low entropy at the start" into the theory isn't free, its doing real work, and not explaining why that boundary condition holds just relocates the mystery rather then solving it.

Marcus's comeback: (3) it's not an unmotivated posit, because Penrose's Weyl curvature hypothesis gives independent reason to think gravitational entropy really was anomalously low at the big bang, compared to a generic, clumpy singularity like the one inside a black hole — so the postulate earns its keep empirically instead of just being tuned to give the answer he wants. Elena's reply brought in Boltzmann brains: (4) if the far future is an eternal de Sitter phase, random thermal fluctuations mint vastly more short-lived "brains" with false memories of a low-entropy past than there are observers who actually got here the long way, since a small fluctuation is astronomically more probable than a whole low-entropy universe — so pushed all the way, Marcus's "it's just statistics" move undercuts the very memories he's leaning on as evidence for the Past Hypothesis in the first place. Marcus's last word: (5) Boltzmann brain domination is a problem for specific cosmological models (stable eternal de Sitter, certain flavors of eternal inflation), not an objection to positing a low-entropy past as such — pick a cosmology where the far future doesn't sit in equilibrium forever and it doesn't arise. Elena thought that concession basically proved her point, since it means whether the arrow gets "explained" now hinges on unsettled cosmology, not on statistics-plus-a-boundary-condition the way Marcus opened with.

They tried to get me to referee on the walk back to the station and I had opinions but zero confidence in them, forgive me if I've garbled a technical term or two along the way. Could you go through moves (1) through (5) and say who's got the better of it at each one, not just who wins overall? I'm especially curious whether (2) is really as devastating as it sounded that night, whether (3) actually rescues the Past Hypothesis from being "brute" or just relocates that same problem, and whether there's a connection between (2) and (4) that neither of them said out loud but should have. End with one line telling me, on balance, who came out ahead: Marcus, Elena, or a genuine draw.
