I'm plotting a detective novel and have hit a snag that feels more philosophical than literary. Early on, my detective notes, without much interest, that the victim's study window was latched from the inside -- she has no theory yet, so the detail just sits there. Two hundred pages later, once she has become convinced the death was a murder staged to look like a suicide, she treats that same latched window as one of her strongest proofs, reasoning that only the killer would have bothered to latch it before slipping out by the door. No new observation intervenes between these two moments: it is the identical fact, doing very different evidential work before and after she has a theory to fit it into.

An early reader flagged the scene as a cheat, arguing that she is simply dressing up a conclusion reached on other grounds and calling an old detail "proof" after the fact. I'm not convinced he's wrong, but I'm not convinced he's right either. Is there a principled difference between genuinely drawing support for a theory from a fact one already knew and merely rationalizing a theory one already believed on other grounds? And if there is, what would need to be true of the window, or of her reasoning about it, for hers to belong to the first kind rather than the second?
