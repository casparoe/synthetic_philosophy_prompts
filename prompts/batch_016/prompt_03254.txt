I've been rereading Sidgwick's Methods of Ethics, and I keep coming back to how he grounds utilitarianism in an axiom of rational benevolence: the good of any one person counts, from what he calls "the point of view of the universe," no more than the good of any other. He also breaks with Mill by insisting that pleasures and pains differ only in quantity, never in quality, since a hedonism that admitted incommensurable qualities could no longer function as a method for summing outcomes across people. Given that combination of commitments, how do you think Sidgwick would have come down on Yudkowsky's torture-versus-dust-specks dilemma: fifty years of torture for one person versus a single momentary dust speck in the eyes of 3^^^3 people (an almost incomprehensibly large number)? Would his official axioms simply commit him to calling torture the lesser aggregate evil, or would his characteristic deference to common-sense moral intuition as philosophical evidence — the same instinct that left him admitting an unresolved "dualism of practical reason" rather than forcing a resolution between egoism and utilitarianism — push him to treat our revulsion at that conclusion as itself evidence against unrestricted aggregation?
