I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on philosophy of mathematics, and I sit in on a weekly set theory reading group that otherwise consists entirely of people from the math department. We had just finished the chapter on the axiom of choice, Zorn's lemma, and the Banach–Tarski paradox in our slow group read-through of Jech when two of the regulars, Renata and Oskar, got into an argument over drinks afterward that I found much harder to referee than I expected, given how much technical ground they actually agreed on.

Renata opened by arguing that we should be suspicious of full choice and restrict ourselves to something like ZF plus dependent choice (DC): DC already supplies whatever choice principle is doing the real work in ordinary analysis, so full AC only buys extra trouble on top of that, in the form of "monsters" like the Banach–Tarski decomposition and a well-ordering of the reals that nobody can ever actually exhibit; and, she pointed out, there exist perfectly consistent models, like Solovay's, in which DC holds, full choice fails, and every set of reals turns out Lebesgue measurable, so these monsters simply don't arise there. Oskar replied that Banach–Tarski is not actually evidence against the truth of AC, since the pieces in the decomposition are non-measurable — the theorem shows that measure cannot be extended to every subset of space, not that a solid ball can literally be duplicated — and that Renata was underselling what dropping full choice costs, since results she surely wants to keep, such as every vector space having a basis or arbitrary products of compact spaces being compact, are not merely consequences of AC but are outright equivalent to it, so there is no discount version of choice that saves those theorems while still avoiding the paradoxes. Renata came back that relocating the strangeness onto "some sets simply have no measure" does not dissolve it, only relabels it, and that in any case most working mathematicians outside of general topology and a corner of functional analysis never invoke choice beyond its countable or dependent forms, so the disruption of giving up full AC is smaller in ordinary practice than Oskar's examples suggest. Oskar's closing point was that Renata cannot have it both ways: if convenience for existing practice is not supposed to settle what is true, then mere intuitive oddness should not settle it either, especially given that our intuitions about infinite sets were already being flouted by Cantor's results long before choice ever entered the picture.

Neither of them budged, and I have gone back and forth in my own head ever since without reaching a verdict. Could you go through the exchange move by move — Renata's opening, Oskar's reply, Renata's rejoinder, and Oskar's closing point — and say who has the better of it at each individual step, rather than simply declaring an overall winner? I would also be curious whether you think there is some consideration that both of them are overlooking entirely.
