At a family gathering, my aunt and uncle spent the better part of an hour arguing about how my late grandmother's estate should have been split between my cousins Mara and Dev. Mara has a lifelong condition that requires expensive ongoing care just for her to live and work independently. Dev has no medical needs, but has spent years cultivating expensive tastes, and insists that cheap wine, food, and travel genuinely satisfy him less than they would someone without his palate.

My aunt said the estate should simply be split evenly in dollars, since anything else means the executor deciding whose preferences deserve more weight than whose. My uncle replied that equal dollars isn't equal treatment here, since Mara needs a large share of hers just to reach the baseline that Dev starts at for free — real equality has to track quality of life, not currency. My aunt answered that by the same logic, Dev's expensive tastes should count too. My uncle said the cases aren't parallel: Mara never chose her condition, while Dev's tastes are things he cultivated and could, in principle, live without. My aunt came back with the point that Dev didn't choose to become someone who cares this much about wine and travel any more than Mara chose her condition — both are products of a history neither of them fully controlled. My uncle's response was that even a fuzzy line is still a line, and that Mara's case is about as far from the blurry part of it as any case could be.

Could you go through this move by move and say who has the better of it at each step? I'd also like to know whether there's a better way of thinking about what equality between Mara and Dev would even mean — something both of them seem to be missing.
