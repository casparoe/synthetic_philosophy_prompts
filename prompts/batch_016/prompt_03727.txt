So I fell down a bit of a rabbit hole this weekend. I was watching some videos on the different interpretations of quantum mechanics (Copenhagen, many-worlds, pilot wave, QBism, etc) and what struck me is that apparently all of these make the exact same experimental predictions - there's no experiment you could ever run that tells you which one is "true," its purely a matter of which underlying picture you find appealing. I'm a backend dev with zero physics background, but it reminded me a lot of when two totally different refactors both make your test suite pass and you have no real way of knowing which one actually matches whats going on under the hood.

That sent me down a wiki hole into a French physicist/philosopher named Pierre Duhem, who apparently made basically this exact argument decades before quantum mechanics was even formulated - that you can never test one hypothesis in isolation because there's always a pile of background assumptions bundled in with it, so more than one theory (or patched-up version of a theory) can always be made to fit the same data. Everyone online just shorthands this as "the Duhem-Quine thesis" and moves on, but skimming a couple summaries of his actual book it seems like his specific reasons for believing it, and what he thought it implied about what physics is even for, were a lot more particular to him than the one-line version suggests. (Also apparently he was on the losing side of the whole atoms-are-real debate, which makes me wonder if that's a point against him or actually pretty consistent with his whole outlook.)

He died in 1916, so he never saw QM mature, let alone the interpretations mess, or stuff like string theory's landscape problem, where the theory allegedly allows something like 10^500 possible variants that all fit our observed universe equally well. Based on what he actually argued, not just the textbook one-liner - what do you think Duhem would say if he saw the state of physics today? Would the interpretations situation look to him like exactly what his own arguments predicted, something he'd have been totally comfortable with, or do you think he'd have drawn a line somewhere before that? And would something like string theory even count as legitimate physics by his own standards, given that it doesn't spit out unique testable predictions?
