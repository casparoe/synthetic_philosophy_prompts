In my philosophy of opera seminar, we've been working through Bernard Williams's essays in On Opera, including his discussion of Mozart's Così fan tutte. Williams's central claim is that the music Mozart gives the two disguised suitors during the seduction scenes is far too tender and sincere for a plot that is, on its face, just a cynical wager about whether anyone's fidelity is real, and that this is precisely what makes the opera's tidy ending — where the original couples are simply reunited and life resumes as before — feel evasive rather than genuinely resolving. I'm skeptical that Williams is entitled to treat the music's emotional intensity as evidence that the characters' feelings during the seduction are sincere. Please argue that the beauty of that music tells us nothing about the sincerity of what the characters feel, so that Così's return to the status quo is not the uneasy compromise Williams takes it to be, but a perfectly adequate resolution.
