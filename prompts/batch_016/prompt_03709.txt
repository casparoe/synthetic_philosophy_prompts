So this turned into a two hour argument at dinner last night and i still can't decide who came out ahead. My cousin Danny was best man at his college roommate's wedding this past weekend. Turns out his estranged dad, who he hasn't really spoken to in a decade, was having risky heart surgery that same weekend and asked Danny to come sit with him the night before, just in case. Danny went to the wedding instead. Says it was the right call, but also says he still feels like he owes his dad something. When he told us the story, two of my friends, Marcus and Ellie, would not let it go.

1. Marcus: says this is a genuine moral dilemma, full stop - two real obligations, no option that doesn't wrong someone, not every conflict resolves cleanly.
2. Ellie: says no - the wedding role was replaceable (another groomsman could've stepped in), but a maybe-final request from your dying dad can't be handed off to anyone. Once you see that, one obligation clearly outranks the other - there was a right answer, Danny just didn't take it.
3. Marcus: says "look closer and one side always wins" is a dodge you can run on literally any conflict after the fact. That's not discovering a fact about the situation, it's defining dilemmas out of existence by fiat.
4. Ellie: says fine, real test then - does the loosing obligation just evaporate once it's outweighed, like reasons normally do, or does it leave a residue? It leaves one, sure, Danny feels bad. But that's just regret over a cost, like feeling bad about skipping a friend's birthday for a work trip. Doesn't mean two things were genuinely required of him at once.
5. Marcus: says it's not generic regret though - Danny feels like he owes his dad an apology specifically, like he wronged him, not just that he missed out on something good. You don't apologize for correctly ranking mere preferences, only for failing someone who had a real claim on you.
6. Ellie: says feeling like you owe an apology doesn't make it true - people feel guilty about stuff that isn't their fault all the time (survivor's guilt, etc), so Danny's guilt might just mean he's a conscientious person, not that he actually wronged anyone.

Neither of them gave an inch. Can you go through moves 1 through 6 and say who's got the better argument at each one, not just who wins overall, and flag anything you think they're both missing?
