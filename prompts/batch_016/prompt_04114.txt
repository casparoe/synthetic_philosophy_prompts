Owen's opening move was that this is a textbook instance of what Arendt, writing about the Pentagon Papers, describes as the liar coming to believe his own lies -- ordinary self-deception, just laundered through a spreadsheet instead of a press release. Maya pushed back hard: she said this is exactly the situation in which Arendt says self-deception is "no satisfactory answer," because the transit officials look much more like her "problem-solvers" than her image-makers, and the whole force of that passage is that self-deception still requires somebody, somewhere, to be suppressing a truth they in some sense already have -- whereas the planners were never in contact with the relevant facts to begin with, since their confidence rested on the model's internal arithmetic rather than on the ground conditions the model was supposed to track. Owen replied that this looked like a distinction without a difference: somebody had to type realistic base rates for things like utility relocation or contractor delays into that model in the first place, and if they kept re-entering falsely optimistic inputs long after experience had shown those inputs to be wrong, that just is motivated reasoning -- self-deception -- one step removed; "defactualization," on his reading, is simply a more impressive word for the same failing. Maya answered that where the fact-suppression is supposed to be located is exactly what's at issue: Arendt's problem-solvers "did not judge, they calculated," and what makes their case different in kind rather than just in degree is that once the machinery of calculation was running, nobody remained answerable to any fact the model had not already formalized -- so the relevant test isn't whether accurate numbers ever existed upstream, but whether contradicting field reports could still register as relevant to anyone in the room, and on her account of the transit case, they couldn't.
