So this happened at dinner on Saturday and I haven't been able to let it go. Backstory: our friend Theo just started at a tiny startup, works something like 70-hour weeks, is genuinely obsessed with the product, and got a small equity grant (like 0.25%) thrown into his offer. Someone brought this up and my other two friends, Ben and Nadia (who minored in philosophy and will bring up Marx at any opportunity), ended up in a full-on argument about whether Theo is being "exploited" in the Marxist sense. It went on long enough that I was taking notes on my phone under the table.

Roughly how it went: (1) Ben said obviously Theo isn't exploited — nobody forced him into the job, he loves it, and he even owns a tiny piece of the company, so he gets some cut of whatever surplus he helps create. (2) Nadia said thats not what exploitation means for Marx: it's basically a ratio, the surplus value Theo's labor creates over and above the value of his labor power (roughly what it costs to keep him fed, housed, and able to keep showing up), and that ratio doesn't care one bit whether he consents or feels good about it. (3) Ben shot back that if "exploited" applies just as much to a happy employee with equity as to a 19th-century mill worker, the word has basically stopped doing any work — it just means "employee." (4) Nadia said yeah, that IS the point — Marx thinks the wage relation is exploitative as such, good treatment and all, that's not a flaw in the definition, that's the actual thesis. Then it pivoted: (5) Ben said fine, but atleast Theo can't be alienated, since alienation is about feeling estranged from your work and Theo has never felt more into anything in his life. (6) Nadia said that's a misreading too — alienation for Marx is about not controlling the product, the process, or the conditions of your own labor, structurally, regardless of how you feel, and she added that Theo's total passion might just be the ideological form alienation takes now, i.e. "do what you love" is exactly what gets people to accept worse terms without noticing. (7) Ben's closer was that if exploitation and alienation can both be true of someone no matter how they feel or what evidence they could point to, the theory can basically never be wrong about anyone, ever, and that should worry Nadia more than it seemed to.

I don't know Marx well enough to referee this myself and it's been bugging me.
