In the philosophical literature on dispositions, I've been working through the debate over the "simple conditional analysis" with a reading group, and we keep getting tangled on how its two classic families of counterexamples are supposed to relate to one another. The simple conditional analysis holds that an object is disposed to M when C just in case it would M if C were the case -- a glass is fragile, on this view, just in case it would break if struck. C. B. Martin's 1994 "finks" exploit cases where the very stimulus that would trigger a disposition's manifestation is also what causes the disposition to be gained or lost: a wire, ordinarily dead, is hooked up to a device that makes it live an instant before any conductor touches it, so the counterfactual "it would conduct electricity if touched" comes out true of a wire that isn't actually live.

Separately, Mark Johnston had already described a different-looking problem in 1992, calling it "masking": a fragile glass carefully protected by packing material is still fragile, he argued, even though it's false that it would break if struck, because the packing material blocks the process from stimulus to manifestation without touching the underlying disposition at all. When David Lewis proposed his 1997 "reformed conditional analysis," tying manifestation to the persistence of an intrinsic causal basis through the stimulus, it was quickly credited with handling Martin's finks -- but Alexander Bird pushed back the following year, aiming the very same packing-material case (which he renamed an "antidote") squarely at Lewis's repair, since a masked glass keeps its causal basis completely intact right through the stimulus while still failing to break.

Write an essay that works through this dialectic and reaches a definite verdict on the question at its center: can any version of the conditional analysis of dispositions -- simple, reformed, or further patched -- survive finks and masks/antidotes simultaneously, or do the two problems, taken together, show that dispositions resist reductive analysis in conditional terms altogether? Explain concretely why finks and masks pose genuinely distinct kinds of problem rather than variants of the same one, engage seriously with at least one real attempt in the literature to rescue the conditional analysis from both at once (for instance, building an "absence of finks and antidotes" clause into the stimulus condition, or replacing the strict conditional with a proportion-of-cases analysis), and close by committing to a specific answer rather than simply weighing considerations on each side.
