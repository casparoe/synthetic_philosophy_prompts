I'm a backend engineer who got into Bayesian reasoning and forecasting as a side hobby — I do calibration training and dabble on prediction-market sites like Metaculus and Manifold, mostly by way of blogs and forums in the LessWrong-adjacent corner of the internet. That's where I first ran into the Dutch book argument for "probabilism," the claim that your credences (degrees of belief) must satisfy the probability axioms or else you're being irrational. The version that convinced me was the synchronic one: if, say, your probability for two mutually exclusive outcomes doesn't add up to your probability for "one or the other," a bookie who knows your betting quotients can sell you a set of bets that each look fair to you individually but that are jointly guaranteed to lose you money no matter which outcome occurs. That struck me as a genuinely damning result rather than a rhetorical trick.

More recently, someone in a comment thread ran what they presented as the exact same argument for Bayesian updating. The claim was: if you learn some evidence E and your new credence in a hypothesis H doesn't equal your old credence in H conditional on E — i.e., if you don't "conditionalize" — a bookie can again line up a set of bets, some sold before you see E and some after, that guarantees you a loss whatever happens. Everyone in the thread took this as showing that conditionalization is forced on you in exactly the way the probability axioms are, just spread across time instead of confined to a single moment. That inference felt too fast to me — revising your beliefs over time seems like it ought to involve different commitments than merely having consistent odds right now — but I don't have the philosophical background to pin down where the disanalogy actually lies, and nobody else in the thread pushed back.

Could you argue against generalizing from the synchronic Dutch book argument for probabilism to the diachronic Dutch book argument for conditionalization as the uniquely rational updating rule? I'm especially interested in whether sure-loss vulnerability really indicates the same kind of irrationality in the two cases, and in exactly which extra assumptions the standard diachronic version needs that the synchronic one doesn't.
