hey — working on a piece about the philosophy of "dispositions" (why we say a glass is fragile even when its never broken, that sort of puzzle) and I went back to Locke since he's often credited as the guy who really kicked off the modern conversation about "powers." Got stuck on the opening of his chapter "Of Power" (Essay Concerning Human Understanding, Book II, ch. 21, 1689) — the first sentence alone is a nightmare to diagram:

"The mind being every day informed, by the senses, of the alteration of those simple ideas it observes in things without; and taking notice how one comes to an end, and ceases to be, and another begins to exist which was not before; reflecting also on what passes within itself, and observing a constant change of its ideas, sometimes by the impression of outward objects on the senses, and sometimes by the determination of its own choice; and concluding from what it has so constantly observed to have been, that the like changes will for the future be made in the same things, by like agents, and by the like ways, considers in one thing the possibility of having any of its simple ideas changed, and in another the possibility of making that change; and so comes by that idea which we call power."

He goes on: "Thus we say, fire has a power to melt gold, i.e., to destroy the consistency of its insensible parts, and consequently its hardness, and make it fluid … In which, and the like cases, the power we consider is in reference to the change of perceivable ideas. For we cannot observe any alteration to be made in, or operation upon anything, but by the observable change of its sensible ideas." And a bit later: "Power thus considered is two-fold, viz. as able to make, or able to receive any change. The one may be called active, and the other passive power."

Can you help me untangle that first sentence, and then tell me if im onto something here: it sounds like Locke thinks a power is basically known and individuated by the change it does or would produce. If so, doesnt that commit him to exactly the kind of naive "if touched, then it melts/shocks/breaks" analysis that "finks" and "masks" were later invented to blow up? (quick gloss in case it helps — a mask is when a disposition is genuinely there but something's stopping it from showing itself, like bubble wrap around a fragile vase; a fink is when the disposition itself switches on or off at exactly the instant of the test, like C.B. Martin's live wire that goes dead the moment before you touch it.) Is there anything in Locke's empiricism that lets him dodge that problem, or is he just walking straight into it 300 years early?
