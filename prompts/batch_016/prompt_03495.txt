I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, writing a dissertation chapter on Integrated Information Theory (IIT) of consciousness, and there's a specific objection to it that a committee member keeps pushing me to address — something more precise than the usual "consciousness can't just be information" complaints. IIT ties experience to a system's recurrent causal structure rather than to what it computes: on the theory, a purely feedforward network has essentially zero integrated information (Φ) no matter how sophisticated its behavior, which is why proponents invoke it to explain why the cerebellum — wired in a mostly feedforward, modular fashion despite containing more neurons than the entire cortex — contributes essentially nothing to experience, while the densely recurrent cortex does. What I can't shake is the so-called "unfolding argument" (Doerig, Schurger, Hess & Herzog, 2019): since any recurrent network can, in principle, be "unfolded" through time into a feedforward network computing exactly the same input-output function, IIT seems committed to saying that two systems could be perfectly indistinguishable in behavior and self-report — the same words, the same laughter, the same autobiographical detail — while one has a rich, unified inner life and the other has essentially none.

I'd like you to write a short story built around this specific bind, rather than the generic "could a machine be conscious" premise. Imagine a near-future patient undergoing a staged cortical prosthesis procedure — to replace tissue destroyed by intractable epilepsy or some degenerative disease — whose replacement implant, for some plausible in-world engineering reason, ends up feedforward rather than recurrent, despite being exhaustively validated to reproduce the original tissue's input-output behavior. Nothing observable changes afterward: the patient still tells the same jokes, still remembers proposing to their spouse the same way, still insists they feel entirely themselves. Write from the perspective of someone — the patient, a partner, or the engineer who signed off on the design — who has actually worked through what IIT implies about the replaced region, and can't decide whether that implication is a discovery worth grieving or a reductio that shows the theory was never tracking anything real. I'd rather the story sit inside that uncertainty than resolve it neatly; feel free to invent whatever medical and technical texture makes the scenario feel lived-in.
