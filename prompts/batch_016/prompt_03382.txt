I'm teaching a graduate seminar on decision theory this term, and last week we covered updateless decision theory, using counterfactual mugging as the running example -- Omega flips a coin, tells you it landed tails, and asks you for $100, which would only have paid off for you in the counterfactual scenario where the coin landed heads and Omega rewards agents who'd have payed up. I told the class that what makes UDT distinctively "updateless," as opposed to causal or evidential decision theory, is that the agent's choice of action doesn't depend on what it actually observes: an updateless agent just carries out whatever policy was optimal relative to its original prior, full stop, rather than conditioning on the fact that its now looking at "tails" and re-deriving what to do from there. On my telling, that's the whole reason UDT agents pay up in cases like this -- they've essentially agreed in advance to be unmoved by whatever evidence later turns up, which is also supposedly why UDT outperforms CDT and EDT here: it trades responsiveness to evidence for better performance averaged across possible worlds.

One of my students pushed back afterward with what I thought was a pretty good question: if that's right, isn't UDT less a theory of rational choice than a theory of rational precommitment, since a genuinely updateless agent couldn't adjust its behavior in light of new information even when it obviously should? I didn't have a great answer on the spot. So, is my gloss on what "updateless" means here basically right, and if so how do UDT's defenders block the charge that this makes it normatively bizarre -- or have I gotten the theory wrong somewhere along the way?
