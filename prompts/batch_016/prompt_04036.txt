Ok this is going to sound like a weirdly specific question but it's been bugging me for like two weeks so here goes. I'm a 2nd year PhD student working on emergent communication in multi-agent RL — basically we train populations of neural nets to invent their own signaling protocols from scratch to solve cooperative tasks. Almost every paper in this subfield cites David Lewis's book Convention as the theoretical backbone (his signaling games are literally the template half our experiments are built on), so my advisor finally made the reading group sit down and read the actual book instead of just the two-sentence summary everyone recycles in their intro sections. That's when things got confusing for me.

Lewis's whole definition of convention leans really hard on common knowledge — a regularity only counts as a convention if everyone conforms, expects everyone else to conform, and all of this is common knowledge in the population (built up through his "indication" machinery, this whole "I know that you know that I know" business). But then I went down a rabbit hole into Brian Skyrms's evolutionary stuff (Evolution of the Social Contract, the replicator dynamics models, populations of simple reinforcement learners converging on signaling systems), and none of his agents ever get anywhere near common knowledge of anything — they just reinforce whatever paid off last round and a convention pops out the other end. Which, now that I think about it, is basically also whats happening in our deep RL setups: the agents aren't modelling each other's beliefs about beliefs, they're just doing gradient descent on a shared reward.

So here's what I actually want: can you compare Lewis's account and Skyrms's account head to head, specifically on this common-knowledge question? Like, does the evolutionary/learning story actually vindicate Lewis somehow (by showing how something functionally equivalent to common knowledge can build up over repeated interaction), or does it show that common knowledge was never really doing the necessary work he thought it was doing? I'd rather have an actual verdict with reasoning than a "both perspectives have merit" type answer — which account actually gives the better picture of what's going on when a population (biological or artificial) converges on a convention?
