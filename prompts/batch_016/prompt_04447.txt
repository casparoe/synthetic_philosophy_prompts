I don't have any formal background in math or philosophy, but a chess habit pulled me into a puzzle I can't quite let go of. I once watched a chess engine confidently give one side a 70 percent chance of winning a position that, it turned out, was actually a forced mate in nineteen moves for the other side. That fact had been true from the moment the position arose; the engine simply hadn't searched deep enough yet to see it. That got me wondering how anyone, human or machine, is supposed to assign a sensible degree of confidence to a mathematical fact, such as whether a specific huge number is prime, whether some program eventually halts, or whether a long-unsolved conjecture is true, before a proof settles the matter one way or the other. Ordinary probability seems like the wrong tool here, since the fact in question isn't "random" the way a coin flip is: the answer is already fixed, we simply haven't finished checking it, yet insisting on 100 percent or 0 percent confidence right up until the proof arrives feels wrong too. When I've raised this with friends, they usually just say "wait for the proof," which misses the point, because sometimes, like a trading algorithm or an engine mid-game, you have to act on a belief now, before the calculation is finished.

Digging through old forum threads, I've come across roughly five different families of answer to this puzzle. I'll call the first the market idea: treat the situation like a financial market, where many small, computable trading strategies continuously buy and sell shares in the statement's truth, and whatever price emerges after enough rounds of trading becomes the credence, with strategies that keep getting it right gaining more influence over time. The second I'll call the search-effort idea: gauge confidence from how a systematic proof search is progressing, for instance by noting that no counterexample has turned up after checking trillions of cases, or that any proof short enough to be found in reasonable time does not seem to exist. The third I'll call the provability idea: set the original claim aside and instead reason about provability itself, treating something like "a proof of length under N exists" as the real object of belief, using the formal logic of what can and cannot be proved as a guide. The fourth I'll call the track-record idea: lean on analogy, so that if almost all past conjectures resembling this one in the relevant respects turned out to be true, the new one is assigned a similarly high credence, revised as more get settled. The fifth I'll call the coin-flip idea: refuse to play favorites and give every unverified claim a flat 50 percent, on the grounds that anything else would amount to pretending to knowledge you do not actually have.
