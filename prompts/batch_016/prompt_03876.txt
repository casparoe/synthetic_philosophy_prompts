For my early modern philosophy seminar we just spent two weeks on Leibniz's Theodicy, and I think I finally understand the actual argument behind "best of all possible worlds" instead of the cartoon Pangloss version everyone makes fun of. Like, the real argument has actual machinery to it — the compossibility thing, where not all perfections can jointly exist in one world so God is stuck choosing the maximally compossible combo, plus the idea that "best" specifically means the world with the best ratio of variety/richness of phenomena to simplicity of the laws producing it. My prof kept emphasizing that this whole apparatus was built to answer Bayle, who argued you basically can't square an all-good, all-powerful God with the evils we actually see.

Here's what's been bugging me since though: Leibniz died in 1716, so he built all this without any notion of evolution by natural selection. His hard cases were stuff like earthquakes and human sin. But Darwin later showed that the insane diversity of life — literally the kind of "variety" Leibniz treats as a good-making feature of a world — is generated by a mechanism of predation, parasitism, and mass extinction grinding away for hundreds of millions of years, not just occasionally marred by evil on the side. Darwin himself apparently got genuinely disturbed thinking about parasitic wasps that lay eggs inside living caterpillars, writing to a friend that he "cannot persuade myself that a beneficent and omnipotent God would have designedly created" them for that purpose. A guy in my study group thinks this basically breaks Leibniz's whole system, since now the "greater variety" isn't just accompanied by evil, its the product of it, but i'm not totally convinced that's fatal.

So genuinely curious what you think: using Leibniz's own principles (not just generic theodicy hand-waving about "greater goods"), how do you think he'd actually respond to the Darwinian picture? Could the variety/simplicity trade-off let him bite the bullet and say suffering-as-engine-of-diversity is just the cost of the richest possible world, or does the fact that suffering is the mechanism itself (rather than an occasional side effect) break something more fundamental in his framework than what he was originally defending against?
