I'm a backend engineer who's been lurking on AI safety forums for a while, and a couple of weekends ago I fell down a rabbit hole reading old Center on Long-Term Risk posts about "surrogate goals" as a defense against threats — including acausal threats, where a sufficiently good predictor could threaten you without ever causally interacting with you at all, just by simulating what you'd do under the threat. The pitch, as I understood it, is that an agent worried about extortion can inoculate itself by bolting on some deliberately silly, low-stakes goal it doesn't actually care about intrinsically — Tobias Baumann's original example is an all-consuming preference about the diameter of a platinum sphere — in the hope that threateners will target that decoy instead of whatever the agent truly values, so that even a "successful" threat only destroys something worthless.

What's been bugging me, and what I couldn't fully resolve arguing about it with my housemate (who's more into decision theory than I am), is a tension Baumann himself raises: for the surrogate goal to actually deflect threats, the threatener has to find it credible that the agent cares about it, and credibility seems to come from the agent's revealed willingness to pay real costs to protect it — but if the agent really is disposed to pay costs to protect a goal it supposedly doesn't "truly" hold, in what sense is that goal still a disposable decoy rather than just a second real value it now has to defend? My housemate thinks this is a solvable calibration problem; I keep thinking it's closer to Kavka's toxin puzzle than to an engineering detail — a case where the very thing that would make the "fake" commitment work is also the thing that would stop it from being fake.

Could you write a dialogue dramatizing this disagreement — one side defending surrogate goals as a genuine, implementable improvement, the other pressing the worry that the scheme can't stay coherent under the exact pressure that makes it useful? It doesn't have to be us specifically, just two equally sharp representatives of each position; a page or so is plenty. I'd like them to actually engage with each other's specific moves rather than talk past one another, and I'd rather it end in a real concession or an honest impasse than a tidy resolution, since I don't think this is actually a settled question.
