For my seminar on formal epistemology, I've been comparing David Lewis's Principal Principle—that credence in a proposition should match its known objective chance—with Bas van Fraassen's Reflection Principle, which requires current credence to defer to one's own anticipated future credence. Several commentators treat these as two instances of a single, more general norm of "expert deference," and I'd like your help assessing that claim: what, precisely, does each principle add to the bare idea of deferring to an expert, and are the two really on a par? In particular, memory-loss cases such as Sleeping-Beauty-style scenarios are often presented as counterexamples to Reflection without posing any obvious analogous threat to the Principal Principle. Can you explain this asymmetry, and say whether it reflects a genuinely different justification behind each principle, or only a technical difference in how they are formulated?
