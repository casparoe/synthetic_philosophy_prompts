Random tangent, but I've been re-reading Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" for a piece I'm putting together on the early history of AI, and there's a section near the end I don't think I ever properly clocked before: one of his listed objections to the imitation game is literally the argument from extrasensory perception. He's dead serious about it too - reckons the statistical evidence for telepathy is basically overwhelming, then spends a paragraph working out that a telepathic contestant could out-guess a machine on which suit of card the interrogator's holding, and that the machine's own random-number generator might start guessing right more than chance if the interrogator's psychokinesis is somehow acting on it too. His deadpan fix is that you'd need to put both contestants into a "telepathy-proof room" to keep the test fair. Showed it to a physicist friend of mine last night and she nearly spat out her wine.

It's such a weird little crack in an otherwise hyper-rational document, and I cant stop thinking about that room as an actual object - like, what would it even mean to build something telepathy-proof the way a Faraday cage is EM-proof? Could you write me a short story (a page or two is plenty) that takes the room seriously as a real piece of engineering, or institutional history? Doesn't have to be set in 1950 - jump forward to whenever you like and pick whatever POV works best - but I'd love it if it sat with the actual problem underneath the joke: once you admit a mind might reach outside its own skull, how do you ever design a clean test for another kind of mind again, machine or otherwise. Doesn't need to be a downer, but I think it lands better a little unsettling than played for laughs.
