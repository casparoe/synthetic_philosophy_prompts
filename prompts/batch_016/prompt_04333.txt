I'm several chapters into a novel set at Rudolf II's court in Prague, with Johannes Kepler as one of two viewpoint characters, and I've reached a scene where he has to defend his own, rather strange, version of astrology to a skeptical court philosopher. I'd like to avoid the line every popular biography reaches for — astrology as astronomy's "foolish daughter" — since it's become a kind of tic in Kepler fiction, so instead I've been sitting with his 1601 tract *De Fundamentis Astrologiae Certioribus* (fittingly, addressed "to philosophers" on its own title page), working from Frisch's nineteenth-century *Opera Omnia*. I've gotten stuck on two of its numbered theses that seem to be doing real argumentative work rather than just placating patrons, and I honestly can't tell whether I'm failing to understand them or whether they simply don't cohere.

Thesis XX opens: "Mihi Alteritas, in creatis nulla aliunde esse videtur, quam ex materia, aut occasione materiae; at ubi materia, ibi geometria," and closes a little later with "Cum ergo Geometria toti mundo creando praebuerit exemplar, non ineptè haec Geometrica contrarietas ad ornatum mundi, qui consistit in variatis planetarum viribus, concurret." As best I can render it: "To me, otherness among created things seems to come from nowhere else than from matter, or from the occasion of matter; [but/that is,] where there is matter, there is geometry … Since, then, Geometry furnished the pattern for the creation of the whole world, it is not inapt that this geometrical contrariety should contribute to the world's adornment, which consists in the varied powers of the planets." (Irritatingly, I've found that middle connective transcribed as "at" — "but" — in the Opera Omnia text, and as "aut" — "or, that is" — in a modern discussion of the passage, which seems to change its logic more than a single letter should.) Twenty theses on, in Thesis XL — right after arguing that an aspect can affect earthly affairs only once a perceiving soul is added to the picture — Kepler writes: "Scilicet omnis animalis facultas est imago Dei γεωμετροῦντος in creatione, excitaturque ad opus suum hac caelesti Aspectuum Geometria, seu Harmonia," which I take to mean roughly "every faculty of soul, then, is the image of the geometrizing God in creation, and is roused to its proper work by this celestial Geometry, or Harmony, of the Aspects" (I'm fairly, though not perfectly, confident about that Greek participle glossing Dei).

What I can't reconstruct well enough to put in a character's mouth is how these two theses are meant to lock together into a single argument, and whether what results actually explains anything. Is the thought something like this: geometrical archetypes are eternal in God's mind; individuated instances of them appear wherever matter appears; and a soul that itself bears the same "image" can therefore be causally roused on encountering those archetypes again in the harmonic angles among planetary rays — in which case the soul's "recognition" is doing genuine explanatory work? Or is invoking a geometrizing, image-bearing soul just a more dignified way of stipulating that aspects affect us, without supplying any independent handle on why they should do so? I'd also value your sense of where this move sits relative to more familiar positions — Plotinian emanation, Ficino's astral magic, something more like a proto-Kantian claim about forms supplied by the perceiving mind itself — so that my Kepler can argue like a thinker with a genuine philosophical lineage, rather than a mouthpiece for whatever is dramatically convenient.
