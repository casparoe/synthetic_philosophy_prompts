Since retiring some years ago from a career in experimental physics — the last two decades of it spent on precision metrology, which turns out to be more relevant to what follows than I would have expected. I have taken up auditing philosophy courses at the university near where I live, and reading rather more widely than the syllabi strictly require. One thread I followed, out of some reading on the epistemology of artificial reasoners, led me to what is apparently called the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents: a result, ultimately owed to Löb's theorem in provability logic, that seems to block a sufficiently powerful formal reasoner from ever fully certifying, from the inside, that it (or a successor built to reason just as it does) can be relied upon.

A few of us from the audit group meet monthly to workshop short pieces on whatever we have lately been chewing over, and I would like to bring the note below, in which I tried to set the obstacle out in my own words, largely as a test of whether I actually follow it. I circulated an earlier draft to an old colleague, a mathematician, who remarked only that I might be asking Löb's theorem to carry more weight than it can bear, and then left for a conference before he could explain what he meant. I have not managed to reach him since, but I would like a second opinion before the group meets. Here is where the draft currently stands:

Any agent that revises its own code, or that must eventually hand control to a future version of itself, runs into a puzzle that sounds technical but is really about the limits of self-knowledge. Suppose an agent A only performs an action once it has proved, within some fixed formal system S rich enough to reason about its own proofs, that the action is safe. Suppose further that A is about to defer to a successor, or its own later self, A', which reasons within that very same system S. Before doing so, A would like some assurance that A' is trustworthy in a fairly demanding sense: that whenever A' proves an action safe, the action really is safe. The natural way to state this assurance is as a schema, one instance for every sentence P expressible in S: "if S proves P, then P." Call this local reflection.

This is exactly where Löb's theorem intervenes. It says that for any sentence P, if S proves "if S proves P, then P," then S already proves P outright. So A cannot establish local reflection for some P without S having, in effect, already settled P on its own — which defeats the point of appealing to A' at all, and is in any case impossible across the board for any consistent S with real expressive power, since no such S proves everything. (Taking P to be a flat contradiction is instructive: "if S proves a contradiction, then a contradiction holds" just restates that S is consistent, and Löb's theorem then says that if S proves its own consistency, S proves a contradiction — that is, S is inconsistent. This is Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, recovered as a special case.) So the very attempt, mounted from within S, to certify that "whatever S proves is true" collapses the distinction between S proving P and S merely proving that something proves P.

I find it helps, coming from my own background, to think of this on analogy with a measuring instrument that cannot certify its own accuracy by consulting only itself; certification has to come from some independent, and presumably better-calibrated, standard. In the logical case, though, there is no obvious place to stand outside the tower: a strictly stronger system S' capable of certifying S runs into the identical obstacle one level up, on pain of an infinite regress of ever-stronger systems, each vouching only for the one beneath it and none for itself.

The proposals I have come across for living with this: something like a "waterfall" of successively weaker systems, each trusting only the next; indexing a successor's proof system to a shrinking parameter rather than demanding that one system vouch for its exact twin; or abandoning proof altogether for the calibrated, self-correcting credences of an approach sometimes called logical induction, strike me less as solutions than as ways of trading an outright impossibility for a weaker, graded kind of self-trust.

I would be grateful for comments on three things in particular. First, whether I have the logical core right, especially the claim that local reflection collapses into proving P outright, and the way I have folded Gödel's second incompleteness theorem in as a special case. Second, whether the calibration analogy in the third paragraph genuinely illuminates the obstacle or instead papers over a disanalogy I am not seeing — this is also where I suspect my colleague's objection was aimed, though I would welcome your own independent judgment on it rather than a guess at his. Third, whether the note's closing paragraph is a fair, non-tendentious summary of how people have tried to soften the obstacle, or whether I have made the proposed fixes sound more, or less, adequate than they actually are. Any comments on where the writing itself would lose a philosophically literate but non-mathematical audience would be useful as well.
