My grandmother has moderate dementia and keeps asking where my grandfather is; he died two years ago, but she does not retain that fact from one conversation to the next. Her care home practices what staff call validation therapy: instead of correcting her, which reliably re-triggers a grief she will not remember an hour later, they gently say he is out running an errand, and by every visible measure she is calmer for it. My father thinks this is obviously right; my aunt thinks the staff are, in effect, running a small experience machine on their mother, and that something is missing even though she will never know it. Neither has budged, which is what has sent me back to Nozick for the first time since college.

Write the dialogue they might have, with my father defending a hedonistic theory of well-being and my aunt an objective-list view on which an authentic connection to the people we love matters independently of how believing in it feels. Have each engage seriously with Nozick's own stated reasons for not plugging into the experience machine, and dispute whether those reasons still hold when the "machine" is local — a single false belief embedded in an otherwise real life — unchosen, and irreversible. Each should press the other on the least comfortable implication of their own view; neither should have the easier case, and both should respond to what the other has actually just said rather than simply restate their opening position.
