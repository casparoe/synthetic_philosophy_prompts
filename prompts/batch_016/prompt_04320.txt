Fell down a research rabbit hole on this and landed on the actual philosophy for it: David Lewis's counterpart theory vs. the "transworld identity" view people associate with Kripke, including Kripke's gripe that on Lewis's view, saying "Humphrey could have won the election" isn't even about something that could've happened to Humphrey, just to some other guy in a different world who happens to resemble him a lot, which feels like changing the subject.

Here's the bit that actually made me want to bug you about it: apparently Leibniz was already having this exact argument with himself two hundred-something years before either of those guys was born, and nobody mentioned this to me in undergrad. There's that strange scene near the end of the Theodicy where a goddess walks the priest Theodorus through a pyramid-shaped palace where every room is its own possible world with a slightly different Sextus Tarquinius living out a different life — which sure sounds like these Sextuses are different individuals, not one guy on different paths. Seperately, in the letters to Arnauld, Leibniz talks about there being an infinity of possible Adams (all distinct people) and says a Caesar who didn't cross the Rubicon just plain wouldn't be our Caesar. I guess theres a whole scholarly fight about whether that makes Leibniz a proto-counterpart-theorist (someone named Mondadori apparently wrote a paper back in the 70s arguing exactly that) or whether that's not quite fair to him, since he supposedly despised essentialism and might not want to be caught siding with Lewis.
