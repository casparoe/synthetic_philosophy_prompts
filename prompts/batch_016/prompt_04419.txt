I spent about 35 years as a field ecologist before I retired, and it's only in the last year or so, with time on my hands, that I've actually sat down and read real philosophy instead of just grumbling about reviewer comments. I joined a little Tuesday-night discussion group at the community college, mostly other retirees, and we've been slowly working through Popper. Last session we got to his writing on Darwinism and it turned into a genuine argument between two other members — Nadia (retired chemistry teacher) and Walt (retired patent attorney) — that I haven't been able to stop turning over since, because I honestly can't decide who came out ahead.

Nadia opened by saying Popper's original tautology complaint basically still holds up: "survival of the fittest" tells you nothing unless you can specify what counts as fit before you already know who survived, and in practice most adaptive stories get reconstructed after the outcome they're meant to explain. Walt shot back that Popper definately recanted exactly this in 1978, in so many words admitting he'd changed his mind about the testability of natural selection — so resurrecting the tautology charge decades later just ignores the man's own correction. Nadia came back with something I hadn't known myself: that in the very same "recantation," Popper still called Darwinism a metaphysical research programme and never actually said the general principle was non-tautological, only that certain specific selective hypotheses (particular predation or resistance mechanisms, say) could be made testable — so on her reading he recanted almost nothing about the abstract schema, just softened his tone about applications of it. Walt said that isn't a retreat at all, that's just how theories work in general: nobody tests "F equals ma" in the abstract either, you test specific force laws built on top of it, and needing auxiliary detail to become testable was never treated as disqualifying for Newton, so it's a double standard to treat it as disqualifying for Darwin. Nadia's next move was to point out that biologists themselves apparently felt the need to invent a whole new account of what "fitness" even means — some probabilistic, dispositional notion instead of just reading fitness off of who happened to survive — specifically to escape this circularity, and she thinks the fact that this took real philosophical work shows Popper's nose was onto something, not just a misunderstanding on his part. Walt answered that concepts get sharpened like that constantly in perfectly respectable sciences (he brought up "species" and "gene") without anyone concluding the earlier theory had been mere metaphysics all along. Then I mentioned an old interview I'd read about where Popper, years after the recantation, apparently still grumbled that the theory left him unsatisfied and said people ought to keep hunting for alternatives — Nadia took that as proof he never believed his own recantation, Walt said that's just Popper being Popper, treating every theory, his own past pronouncements included, as fair game, which isn't the same thing as secretly still holding the old view.

Nobody budged, and we honestly just ran out of wine before it could get resolved. I'd love an outside read, since I keep flip-flopping on it myself. Could you go through the exchange move by move — Nadia's opening tautology claim, Walt's recantation point, Nadia's "narrow recantation" rejoinder, Walt's F=ma analogy, Nadia's point about the fitness fix, Walt's "concepts get refined" reply, and the dueling reads of that later interview — and say who has the better of it at each individual step, not just who wins overall? And seperately, is there a version of Popper's original worry that actually survives all of Walt's replies, or is Nadia mostly just trading on how loosely "recant" gets used?
