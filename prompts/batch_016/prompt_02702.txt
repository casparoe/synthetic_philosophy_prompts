Over dinner last week, a good friend of mine and I ended up arguing for nearly two hours about a claim he had picked up somewhere: that once autonomous AI agents can inspect each other's source code, or some verified summary of it, before acting, the old game-theoretic pessimism about one-shot Prisoner's Dilemmas simply dissolves, and such agents could reliably cooperate on high-stakes, one-off deals without needing contracts, courts, or reputational tracking to back them up. He works at a small logistics startup that is piloting AI agents to negotiate shipping terms with suppliers, so the question isn't purely academic for him. I countered that he was essentially describing what game theorists call "program equilibrium," in the sense Tennenholtz originally formalized it, and that the mere existence of a cooperative equilibrium in that idealized setting, or even of cleverer constructions like the Löbian FairBot, tells us little about whether real, gradient-trained systems could ever achieve anything like verified mutual transparency. I also pointed out that the same commitment devices that can support fair cooperation could, in principle, just as easily be used to make credible, one-sided demands.

Neither of us managed to convince the other, partly because we kept sliding between the formal theory and our own intuitions about deployed systems without clearly separating the two. I would like a more careful outside perspective: what would you say are the most important implications of program equilibrium, as it has actually been developed by game theorists and, later, by AI-safety researchers working on multi-agent cooperation, for the question of whether mutual code or policy transparency between autonomous agents could realistically substitute for the external enforcement that contracts, courts, and reputations currently provide in high-stakes, one-off negotiations of the kind my friend has in mind? I would especially value your judgment on which idealizations in the formal results are doing the most work and seem least likely to survive contact with real deployed systems, and on whether, on reflection, the theory should leave us more worried than reassured about what such transparency would actually secure.
