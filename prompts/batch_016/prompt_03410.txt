I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but I've been reading about weakness of will and keep running into the claim, popular in productivity and behavioral-economics writing, that hyperbolic discounting basically solves the puzzle: we discount near-term rewards far less steeply than a time-consistent exponential curve would predict (the same kind of curve used for the discount factor in reinforcement learning), so our ranking of two options flips once the smaller, sooner reward gets close, and we backslide. Reading Donald Davidson's "How Is Weakness of the Will Possible?" left me doubting this actually answers his question: Davidson argues the akratic agent forms an unconditional, "all-out" judgment favoring the worse action while still holding a conditional, all-things-considered judgment that the other action is better, and that what makes this genuinely irrational, rather than a mere change of mind, is that the agent has no reason at all for the all-out judgment winning out over the all-things-considered one — a violation of what he calls the principle of continence.

Compare Davidson's account with George Ainslie's picoeconomic model, where hyperbolic discounting produces temporary preference reversals that get negotiated through ongoing bargaining among an agent's own "successive selves," with personal rules and bundled commitments serving as the main tools by which more far-sighted interests win that bargain. Does Ainslie's model explain the same phenomenon Davidson is analyzing, such that it could be read as supplying the causal mechanism behind his all-out judgment, or does it quietly redefine weakness of will as a mere preference reversal, dissolving rather than answering Davidson's puzzle about irrationality? Defend your answer with reference to the specific structure of both accounts, not just their general reputations.
