so this is the kind of argument that shows up a lot in rationalist/AI-safety corners of the internet - that any agent whose preferences can't be written as maximizing some expected utility function is exploitable via money-pumps, so sufficiently capable agents will end up "coherent" in that sense, which is part of why people expect advanced AI to behave like a goal-directed EU maximizer. two people in a discord i'm in got into a whole back-and-forth about this and it was way more interesting than either of them probably realized at the time. roughly it went:

Ben: if your preferences aren't complete and transitive, someone can offer you a sequence of trades that leaves you strictly worse off for sure, and you'll take every single one. so any agent smart enough to notice the pattern will eventually iron itself into having complete, transitive prefs - that's basically the whole case for expecting coherence out of advanced agents.

Mara: the pump only works if the agent keeps making the same "mistake" every round though. it can dodge the whole think just by adopting a policy of never accepting a trade that lands it back on an option it already turned down earlier in the sequence. that alone blocks the exploitation, no completeness required.

Ben: that's a dodge, not a refutation. if what you're willing to accept now depends on what you've already rejected, you've just smuggled in preferences over (outcome, history) pairs instead of outcomes - and once you allow that, literally any pattern of choices can be redescribed as "maximizing some utility function." so your counterexample doesnt block the theorem, it just makes the theorem unfalsifiable.

Mara: that's exactly my point though. if "coherent EU maximizer" is compatible with any behavior once you let history into the outcome space, then the claim that actually matters - that advanced agents converge on maximizing utility over the states of the world we actually care about - has no content left. dodging via a history-sensitive policy is a real counterexample to that claim. it's only a non-counterexample to a version of the theorem so watered down it couldn't have been wrong in the first place.
