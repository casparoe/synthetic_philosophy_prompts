Been down a total rabbit hole this week because a friend in my metaphysics reading group brought up Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument, and now I can't stop thinking about how weirdly relevant 17th century philosophy is to it. Quick version for context: if the physical domain is causally closed (every physical event with a cause has a sufficient physical cause) and you rule out systematic overdetermination, then any mental property that isn't literally identical to some physical property gets "excluded" as a cause of behavior — it just rides along epiphenomenally, doing no real work. Kim thinks this leaves non-reductive physicalists stuck: either reduce the mental to the physical, accept that mental causation isn't genuine, or swallow a ton of causal overdetermination that seems absurd.

My friend's point was that Spinoza basically pre-empted this whole mess by construction. In Ethics part 2, the modes of each attribute are caused only by other modes of that same attribute (extension causes extension, thought causes thought, nothing crosses over), and then the "order and connection of ideas is the same as the order and connection of things" line guarantees the two series always march in lockstep, because their actually the same underlying modes, just conceived under different attributes. So there's never a competition for the same causal slot to begin with — no closure-vs-mental standoff, since the mental was never trying to push around physical stuff directly in the first place.

Don't give me the shallow "haha Spinoza invented parallelism, mind blown" answer — actually work through where his specific commitments would hold up or break down against Kim's specific premises.
