As a software engineer, I spend a good deal of my time on product analytics: reading A/B test results, engagement dashboards, and recommendation-ranking metrics. Whenever a feature debate gets heated, someone eventually argues that whatever keeps people clicking, watching, or scrolling longer must be what they "really want," since their behavior is the clearest evidence of their preferences, and that this settles what's good for users. That argument leans on the economic notion of revealed preference, where choices are treated as the basis for inferring preference and, from there, utility. Please describe the most important implications of revealed preference theory, including its well-known limitations as an account of utility or welfare, for how much weight my team should give engagement metrics as evidence of what's actually good for users, as opposed to signals like surveys or churn. I'd also value your take on whether manipulative design patterns, such as autoplay and infinite scroll, undermine the very idea of a "revealed" preference in this setting.
