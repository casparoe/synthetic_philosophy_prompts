Over the past few weeks I have been reading Leibniz for a piece on how today's multiverse talk in physics echoes far older debates about possible worlds, and I keep returning to a passage most people skip past: the strange, almost science-fictional coda to the Theodicy in which the priest Theodorus is led by Pallas Athena into a pyramid of infinite chambers, each one a complete possible world containing its own variant of Sextus Tarquinius, with the actual world — the best one — shining at the apex. It is a far more rigorous image than the "everything happens for the best" cliché usually attached to Leibniz, since it is really an attempt to dramatize compossibility: the claim that one cannot assemble the best world by combining the most appealing features of many worlds, because each possible individual's complete concept comes bundled with an entire cosmos of consequences.

I would like to see that apparatus taken seriously as fiction rather than as a philosophy-lecture illustration. Please write a short story, roughly 800 to 1200 words, set in whatever time and idiom you choose, that puts real weight on two ideas rather than merely decorating around them: first, why a benevolent chooser cannot simply splice together the best elements of different possible worlds into some superior hybrid; and second, the worry that there may be no single best world to find in the first place, since for any candidate one can seemingly always conceive of a slightly better one. The philosophical machinery should be doing genuine work in the plot, not sitting in the background.
