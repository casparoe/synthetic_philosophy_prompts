I don't have any formal training in philosophy, but for the last few months I've been quietly obsessing over a problem I ran into while reading around effective altruism and cosmology forums: what happens to ordinary "add up all the value in the world and maximize it" ethics if the universe turns out to be infinite. I eventually found my way to Nick Bostrom's paper on this, called "Infinite Ethics," and the core problem, as far as I can tell, is genuinely troubling: if space is infinite, it probably contains infinitely many happy lives and infinitely many miserable ones, infinitely many kind acts and infinitely many cruel ones. But once you're adding up an infinite amount of good and an infinite amount of bad, no finite act of yours — feeding a village, committing a massacre, whatever — changes the total, because a finite quantity added to or subtracted from an infinite one leaves the infinite quantity exactly as it was. Bostrom calls this "infinitarian paralysis," and what struck me is that it doesn't just threaten hardcore total utilitarianism — it threatens almost any view on which what you ought to do depends even partly on the overall amount of good and bad you bring about.

The paper walks through several proposed fixes, and I've spent a while trying to decide for myself which of them actually earn their keep rather than just relabeling the problem. As I understood them: (1) "extensionist" theories, which only declare one world better than another when it stays ahead in every sufficiently large expanding region around some reference point, and simply refuse to rank the many cases where that never stabilizes — call this "extensionist"; (2) comparing worlds by their average value per unit of space-time rather than their undefined raw total, which gives you an ordinary finite number to compare even though both totals are infinite — call this "value density"; (3) assigning worlds "hyperreal" values from non-standard analysis, which can represent different sizes of infinity and stay sensitive to finite changes, except that in the hard cases the answer depends on an arbitrary technical choice (a "free ultrafilter") with no principled way to make it — call this "hyperreal"; (4) giving up on valuing the universe as a whole and only ever evaluating the finite slice of it you can actually cause, such as your future light cone, writing everything else off the books — call this "causal restriction"; (5) an "extended decision rule" that, when finite tweaks can't move the infinite total, has you first maximize your shot at tipping the balance between an infinite-good universe and an infinite-bad one, falling back on ordinary finite reasoning only as a tiebreaker — which has the uncomfortable side effect that a trillionth-of-a-percent shift in that probability could justify sacrificing every finite good there is — call this "EDR"; and (6) just accepting the paralysis: conceding that add-it-all-up consequentialism genuinely has nothing to say once the universe is infinite — call this "bite the bullet."

I'd like your own independent judgment on these six, evaluated against four things I think any adequate fix needs: it should actually make different acts come out differently rather than smuggling the paralysis back in; it shouldn't collapse into "fanaticism," where a razor-thin shift in the odds of an infinite outcome swamps every finite consideration; it shouldn't quietly abandon the impartial, add-everything-up spirit that made this kind of ethics attractive in the first place; and it shouldn't produce verdicts that hinge on some arbitrary technical artifact unrelated to anything morally relevant.
