I am a novelist currently at work on a book set in a fictional Midlands town in the 1860s, whose central figure is a clergyman's daughter who loses her faith over the course of the narrative, and in trying to get the intellectual texture of that loss right—rather than reaching for something anachronistically modern—I have been rereading George Eliot, both the novels and what I can piece together of her intellectual biography. Two things about her keep pulling at me. The first is that before she wrote a word of fiction, she had translated Strauss's Life of Jesus and Feuerbach's Essence of Christianity, and, less famously, produced a complete translation of Spinoza's Ethics that went unpublished in her lifetime; her break with orthodox Christianity seems to have been bound up with a Feuerbachian redirection of religious feeling toward humanity itself, and with something close to a Spinozist picture of a world ruled by impersonal, necessary law rather than providence. The second is the well-known anecdote of F. W. H. Myers recalling her, late in life, taking as her text the words "God, Immortality, Duty" and pronouncing the first inconceivable, the second unbelievable, and the third nonetheless peremptory and absolute—a performance Myers himself glossed as affirming "the sovereignty of impersonal and unrecompensing Law."

Here is what I cannot resolve. Eliot's fiction seems to want to dramatize exactly that impersonal, unrecompensing law: her narrators reach for words like "nemesis" rather than sin or judgment to describe how a person's past catches up with them, as when Bulstrode's history unravels in Middlemarch or when Tito Melema is finally strangled in Romola by the very father he betrayed and abandoned. And yet the actual architecture of her plots looks a great deal like moral bookkeeping with the divine bookkeeper edited out: the callous and self-serving are exposed and ruined, while the sympathetic and self-renouncing—Dorothea, Silas Marner, even Hetty Sorrel, whose sentence for infanticide is commuted from the gallows to transportation at the last possible moment—are granted some form of grace, meaning, or narrative mercy that looks suspiciously like just deserts, however grim. So my question is this: did George Eliot actually succeed, in her stated views and in the moral architecture of her novels alike, in building an ethical order that is genuinely secular and naturalistic—one that owes nothing to the providential picture she explicitly rejected—or does her fiction quietly reintroduce a religious logic of reward and punishment under cover of scientific-sounding words like "nemesis" and "consequence"? I would like an essay that takes this tension seriously, weighs the strongest considerations on each side rather than splitting the difference between them, and closes with a conclusion you are willing to commit to and defend as your own reading of her, rather than a survey of how others have answered it.
