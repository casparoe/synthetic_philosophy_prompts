I'm a retired biologist finally getting round to reading philosophy properly, and this month it's Descartes. The bit that's really grabbed me is the argument near the end of Meditation 6, where he says he can clearly and distinctly conceive of his mind existing without his body, and since God can create anything that matches a clear and distinct conception, mind and body must be genuinely separate substances. That sent me down a rabbit hole into David Chalmers' philosophical zombies -- something physically identical to a person, but with nobody home -- which is basically the same move minus the God part, used to argue consciousness can't be purely physical. The standard rebuttal these days seems to be that conceiving of something doesn't guarantee its possible (people like to point out that you could once have 'conceived' of water turning out not to be H2O, before the chemistry was known, even though its actually necessarily H2O). So here's what I keep wondering: if you sat Descartes down in front of this whole modern debate, what would he actually make of the zombie argument? Does his God-backed version escape the Kripke-style objection in a way Chalmers' secular one can't -- and, given that he thought animals were just mindless machines, would he even grant that a 'zombie' counts as clearly and distinctly conceivable in his own sense? I want his real considered position, not just a textbook summary of what conceivability arguments are.
