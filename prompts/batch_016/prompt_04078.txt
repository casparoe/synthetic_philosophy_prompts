In my graduate proseminar on philosophy of mathematics this term, we spent a session on the 2010–2011 episode in which Vladimir Voevodsky gave a public lecture explaining why he found neither of the standard consistency proofs for Peano Arithmetic convincing, and said he was seriously suspicious that an inconsistency might eventually turn up. About a year later, Edward Nelson — after some twenty-five years of work on the problem — announced that he had actually found one, locating it not in some exotic extension of PA but in a small fragment of primitive recursive arithmetic. Nelson withdrew the claim within days once Terence Tao and Daniel Tausk, working independently, identified the flaw in his argument, but by his own account this left the consistency of PA an open problem rather than something he was now prepared to affirm. Two of my students got into a genuine argument about what to make of all this. One insisted the episode shows nothing philosophically interesting: an idiosyncratic proof was floated and refuted exactly the way the system is supposed to work, and no one's justified confidence in PA's consistency ever really wavered. The other insisted that the very fact that it took two first-rate logicians real effort to locate the error, rather than the flaw being obvious on its face, shows that the sense in which PA's consistency is "obviously" secure is doing much less work than mathematicians like to think, and that what we actually have is an extremely strong but thoroughly defeasible track record, not fundamentally different in kind from our confidence in a well-confirmed empirical theory.

I'd like you to help me do justice to both sides of that disagreement by writing a dialogue between two mathematical logicians of equal sophistication who pick it up where my students left off. One should press the case that our confidence in the consistency of PA — and, further up the hierarchy, of ZFC — is at bottom empirical or sociological in character: a matter of a very long failure to find a contradiction, resting on essentially inductive grounds, and in principle revisable the way any well-supported empirical claim is. The other should try to articulate what a distinctively mathematical (rather than merely inductive) ground for that confidence would even look like — for instance by appeal to our grasp of the standard model of arithmetic, to relative consistency results, or to what Gödel's second incompleteness theorem does and does not actually show — and should explain why, on reflection, an episode like Nelson's doesn't really damage that ground. Please have them genuinely engage with each other's strongest moves rather than talk past one another, and don't be afraid to let the dialogue get into real technical weeds — feasibility worries about induction, the status of relative consistency proofs, and so on — where that's the natural way for the conversation to make progress.
