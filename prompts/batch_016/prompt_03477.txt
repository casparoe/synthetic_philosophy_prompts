As a political theory PhD student working on Pascal's account of legal authority, I keep running into an unresolved tension in the "Justice and the Reason of Effects" section of the Pensées. Pascal wants justice and might united, and treats their divorce as a kind of tragic compromise: unable to make what is just strong, "we have made what is strong just" (Brunschvicg 298). Yet a few fragments later he warns that publicly tracing laws back to their forceful, arbitrary origins — "sounding them even to their source" — is reckless (294), and that telling people their laws are unjust breeds sedition without producing anything juster (326); the wise, he says, should keep this insight secret while outwardly complying (336).

Holding both strands together, how do you think Pascal would judge an institution like the International Criminal Court or a UN commission of inquiry — bodies built specifically to establish, in legal terms, that powerful states or individuals have acted unjustly, despite having no power to coerce them into compliance? Would he treat this as a genuine, if partial, step toward binding force to justice, or as exactly the kind of destabilizing exposure of law's "usurpation" that his own politics warns against? Defend your answer with reference to specific fragments.
