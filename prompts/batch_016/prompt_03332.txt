I've spent the last few weeks reporting a piece on the strange afterlife of Rawls's original position, and in re-reading the relevant sections of A Theory of Justice I got stuck on something that most popular treatments of the veil of ignorance gloss over entirely. Rawls has the parties behind the veil reason their way to the difference principle via maximin — securing the best worst-case outcome — and he is explicit that this is not because he thinks people are constitutionally risk-averse; he denies that outright. Instead he leans on features of the choice situation itself: its irreversibility, the fact that it is a single, unrepeatable decision rather than an iterated gamble, and the impossibility of renegotiating once the veil lifts. Harsanyi, however, starting from what looks like essentially the same setup — nobody knows which position in society they will end up occupying — reasons in exactly the opposite direction, using an equiprobability assumption across social positions together with ordinary expected-utility maximization to arrive at average utilitarianism instead.

What I would like is to actually understand where these two arguments part ways, rather than simply noting that they do. Rawls lays out a short list of conditions under which he takes maximin reasoning to be rationally required rather than merely one permissible option among others, and my understanding is that he later downplayed one of those conditions — the claim that the parties have no usable basis for assigning probabilities at all — while continuing to lean heavily on the other two. If that is right, I am not sure his position is as far from an expected-utility approach as it first appears, since it starts to sound like a claim about how much weight to place on catastrophic downside risk rather than a wholesale rejection of probabilistic reasoning as such. Could you walk through exactly what is doing the work in each thinker's argument, and then offer your own verdict: does Rawls have a principled basis for treating the original position as an exception to ordinary expected-utility reasoning, or is Harsanyi right that this is really just risk-aversion reintroduced through the back door? I would also be curious whether Rawls's separate "strains of commitment" argument is doing independent work here, or is ultimately just the maximin logic restated in the language of moral psychology.
