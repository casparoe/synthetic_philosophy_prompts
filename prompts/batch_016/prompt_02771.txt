I'm about two-thirds of the way through a novel whose narrator, a hospice volunteer named Elena, is quietly troubled by her own goodness. She sits with dying strangers, holds their hands, telephones their estranged children — and yet, in journal entries that interrupt the third-person narration, she confesses that she has never once, in ten years of this work, thought of any of it as her duty. She does it, she writes, purely because she cannot bear to imagine these people dying alone, and she suspects that if that feeling ever deserted her, she would stop showing up. My writing group split down the middle over whether this makes Elena more or less admirable than a version of the same character who did identical work from a stern, self-conscious sense of obligation, and the argument sent me back into the philosophical literature on "moral worth" — the question of when an agent deserves credit for doing the right thing, rather than merely doing it.

I've now accumulated more theoretical apparatus than I quite know what to do with. There is Kant's original distinction between acting "from duty" and merely "in accordance with" it, illustrated in every textbook by the honest shopkeeper who doesn't cheat inexperienced customers only because scrupulous honesty happens to be good for business. There is Nomy Arpaly's case against Kant, built around Huckleberry Finn, who helps Jim escape slavery precisely while sincerely believing, per his upbringing, that doing so is sinful; Arpaly takes this to show that moral worth tracks an agent's unreflective responsiveness to whatever features actually make an act right or wrong, not any explicit thought about duty. And there is Julia Markovits's rival "Coincident Reasons Thesis," on which an act has moral worth just in case the reasons that actually move the agent to act are the very same reasons that make the act the right one to perform — whether or not the agent conceives of those reasons in moral terms at all.

What would actually help me, rather than another pass through the shopkeeper or Huck Finn, is a scenario invented fresh for the occasion — concrete enough that I could transplant it directly into a scene — in which Kant's acting-from-duty requirement and Markovits's Coincident Reasons Thesis hand down different verdicts about whether the agent's action has moral worth: one theory awarding it, the other withholding it, or the two disagreeing sharply about degree. Please describe the scenario itself (who the agent is, what they do, and exactly what is passing through their mind as they do it), and then say precisely which feature of that psychology is doing the work of pulling the two theories apart. If there's room, I'd also be curious how Arpaly's notion of "moral concern" would size up the very same agent, since I suspect it might split the difference between the other two theories in a way that would suit Elena rather well.
