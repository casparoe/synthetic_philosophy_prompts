In a seminar I'm taking on the semantics of conditionals, we recently covered David Lewis's 1976 argument that no proposition can have its probability equal the corresponding conditional probability across every reasonable credence function without trivializing that function (forcing every proposition to come out probabilistically independent of every other one, which is absurd). Since Lewis is equally well known for the possible-worlds account of counterfactuals he developed in his 1973 book Counterfactuals, on which "if A had occurred, B would have occurred" is true just in case B holds throughout the closest worlds where A holds, I can't see why this isn't a case of Lewis refuting his own earlier theory: isn't the closest-worlds analysis just another way of fixing a conditional's truth value using the same sort of information that a conditional probability captures, which his own later proof shows cannot be done in general? None of our assigned readings address this directly, and I haven't been able to work out on my own where my reasoning goes wrong. Could you help?
