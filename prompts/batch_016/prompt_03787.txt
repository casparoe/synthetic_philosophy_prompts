I've spent the past few weeks working through Scott Garrabrant's "Geometric Rationality" sequence on LessWrong, alongside the older Kelly-criterion literature (Zvi Mowshowitz's reference post is still the one I send people) and Ole Peters' work on ergodicity economics. The essay below tries to pin down something that's been bothering me about how these get lumped together, and I'd like your comments before I try to turn it into an actual post. I'm mainly interested in whether the central distinction I draw is fair to the two research programs, and whether I've missed an argument that would collapse it.

Working title: "Geometric Rationality: One Argument or Two?"

Garrabrant points out, correctly I think, that a recurring mathematical shape keeps appearing across seemingly unrelated tools: Kelly betting, Nash bargaining, the log scoring rule, and, he argues, Thompson sampling and Bayesian updating all come out as maximizing an expected logarithm. His diagnosis is that this isn't coincidence but reflects a genuine primitive, the geometric mean, which we normally only reach by routing through logarithms and additive reasoning instead of treating multiplication as having its own native notion of "average." I find this half of the case genuinely persuasive as mathematics. What I'm not persuaded of is that it licenses treating two very different normative claims as though they were a single discovery.

The first claim, call it the Kelly claim, concerns one agent facing a sequence of multiplicative bets. Peters' ergodicity economics diagnoses naive expected-wealth maximization as confusing an ensemble average, across counterfactual copies of the agent, with the time average actually realized along a single trajectory, and recovers the Kelly criterion as the strategy that maximizes the latter. This gets presented, in a lot of the popular write-ups, as a refutation of expected utility theory. But nothing here requires rejecting a single von Neumann-Morgenstern axiom: an ordinary expected-utility maximizer with logarithmic Bernoulli utility over wealth already recommends the Kelly fraction. So the substantive content of the Kelly claim, once you strip away the framing, is an argument about which cardinal utility function is normatively (or evolutionarily) privileged for an agent whose wealth compounds, not an argument that arithmetic expectation is generally the wrong tool. I recall this exact complaint being aimed at Peters before, in a post on the blog Philosophical Multicore with a title something like "Against Ergodicity Economics": that ergodicity economics smuggles log utility in through the back door while denying there's a front door at all.

The second claim, Garrabrant's own, looks to me like a genuine rejection of independence rather than a relabeled utility function. His example involves a couple choosing where to settle between two cities, whose preference between those two options flips once a third, unlikely but much-better option is added to the menu, which is exactly the kind of reversal the independence axiom forbids. He doesn't deny the reversal; he defends it by appeal to updatelessness, on the grounds that the couple is honoring a policy fixed in advance rather than re-optimizing from inside whichever branch they land in. Whatever one thinks of that defense, the argument doesn't turn on anything essentially logarithmic. It turns on treating the couple's joint preference as an aggregate built from something other than a linear, Harsanyi-style social welfare function, and I'd guess a similar independence violation could be manufactured from other non-additive bargaining solutions that have nothing to do with geometric means specifically.

So here is my suspicion: "geometric rationality" is a label that makes a narrow, orthodoxy-compatible claim about the right utility function (Kelly) and a genuinely heterodox claim about aggregation and commitment (bargaining) look like one unified alternative to expected-value reasoning, when what actually unifies them is that both computations happen to bottom out in a logarithm. Garrabrant would presumably respond that I'm underrating the direct connection he draws elsewhere in the sequence between Nash bargaining and Kelly betting, where he treats them as two instances of one underlying operation rather than as look-alikes that merely share notation. I haven't been able to decide whether that connection does real work at the normative level, or only at the level of the math.

I'd value your comments on three things: whether this split between the two claims survives scrutiny, or whether Garrabrant's "geometric mean as primitive" move actually bridges them at a deeper level than I'm crediting it for; whether the Kelly claim really is fully recoverable inside ordinary expected utility theory, or whether that "domestication" move itself misses whatever Peters thinks the actual point is; and whether you can think of a cleaner test case than mine that would settle the question either way.
