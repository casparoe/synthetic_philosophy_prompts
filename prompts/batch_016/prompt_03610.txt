Ok this is going to be a pretty in-the-weeds ask but bear with me. I teach a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and last week we spent two whole sessions on the Fitch–Church knowability paradox — the proof that if you accept the seemingly harmless "knowability principle" that every truth is knowable (p → possibly Kp), plus some pretty minimal facts about the logic of knowledge (that it distributes over conjunction, and that its factive, i.e. Kp → p), you can derive that every truth is actually, already known. Which is absurd. Fun fact I threw at the class to make them feel old: the core proof wasn't even really Fitch's, it came from an anonymous referee report on one of his papers back in 1945, and it took until 2005 for anyone to work out that the referee had been Alonzo Church. Fitch himself apparently didn't even think the result was that big a deal when he finally published it in '63.

Anyway, the class got surprisingly heated about which of the standard "fixes" for the paradox actually deserve to be taken seriously, versus which ones are just technical patches that dodge the real problem. Off the top of my head there's: restricting the knowability principle syntactically to some privileged class of statements (Tennant's "Cartesian" restriction, or Dummett's considerably stricter "basic statements" version), going the actuality-operator / situation-semantics route instead (Edgington, developed further by Rabinowicz & Segerberg), accusing Fitch of an illicit substitution of a non-rigid designator into a modal context (Kvanvig's move), revising the underlying logic to intuitionistic logic to block the crucial double-negation step (the route Williamson himself floated early on, even though he's since been the harshest critic of basically every other proposed fix), going paraconsistent and just learning to live with a true contradiction somewhere in our theory of knowledge (Beall, building on Routley), or simply denying distribution/factivity instead of the knowability principle. I've taught this every year for a while now and honestly don't trust my own ranking anymore, so I want a more disinterested second opinion before I inflict my slides on next year's cohort.

Could you rank the major responses to Fitch's paradox (the ones above, plus any others you think belong) by how philosophically successful they are — basically, how well each one resolves the paradox without either (a) being an ad hoc patch designed just to get the desired result, or (b) quietly abandoning the anti-realist/verificationist project the knowability principle was meant to capture in the first place.
