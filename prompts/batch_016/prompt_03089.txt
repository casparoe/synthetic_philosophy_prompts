Last month, prompted by my mother's cancer diagnosis, I took a direct-to-consumer genetic test. It reported that, because I carry a particular gene variant associated with the disease, I have a 34 percent lifetime risk of developing it myself — a figure derived from a cohort study of people who carry the same variant. I have not been able to work out what this actually tells me about my own future: I do not get to live my life over and over, developing the disease in roughly a third of those lives; I will simply get it or not, once. My physician treats the number as a perfectly good basis for planning my screening, but a friend who works in statistics insists that, strictly speaking, a probability only describes a pattern across many repeated cases, so that attaching one to a single, unrepeatable event like my own health is close to meaningless. Is my friend right that the figure cannot really apply to me individually, or is there a defensible sense in which a single person can have a genuine probability of a one-time future event?
