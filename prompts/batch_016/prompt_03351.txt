A close friend of mine, Mara, died last spring after a long illness. A few weeks before she passed, she asked me to promise that I would go to her apartment before her family cleared it out and destroy, unread, a shoebox of old journals and letters she had kept since university. I promised, and I did retrieve the box, but between a move and a demanding new job it has sat sealed in my closet for nearly a year now. Last month another friend, Daniel, found out about this arrangement, and we ended up in a long and occasionally heated argument over dinner about whether I am still bound to destroy the letters, given that Mara is no longer around to know, care, or be disappointed either way.

Daniel, who did a philosophy minor and enjoys reminding people of it, pressed on me Scanlon's account of promising from What We Owe to Each Other: what he calls Principle F, according to which the wrong in breaking a promise consists in voluntarily and intentionally leading someone to form an expectation of performance that they want to be assured of, and then failing either to fulfill that expectation or to secure a release from it. Daniel's point was that several of the principle's conditions are explicitly about the promisee's ongoing knowledge, desire for assurance, and awareness of my intentions -- and a dead person has none of these states anymore, so whatever obligation the principle ever generated has, on his reading, simply lapsed along with Mara's capacity to expect or be assured of anything. I objected that this gets the case backwards: if anything, promises made near someone's death strike most people as especially binding, not especially fragile, so a theory with this consequence looks like it is misdescribing the case rather than vindicating his conclusion. Neither of us could settle the matter, partly because neither of us was sure whether the principle's conditions are meant to describe a state of affairs that must persist right up until performance is due, or a completed transaction that, once satisfied at the time of promising, generates a standing duty regardless of what subsequently happens to the promisee.

I would like a better handle on this than either of us managed that evening. Could you lay out the most important implications of Scanlon's Principle F for cases like mine, where the promisee has died or otherwise become permanently incapable of expecting, wanting, or being assured of anything? I am especially interested in whether a careful reading of the principle actually forces Daniel's conclusion, or whether there is a defensible reading on which the obligation survives; and, more broadly, in whether this is a difficulty peculiar to Scanlon's expectation-based approach or whether accounts that seem better placed to avoid it in principle -- such as the normative-power views of Raz or Owens, which locate the source of the obligation in the promisor's own act rather than in the promisee's mental states -- turn out, on closer inspection, to face some version of the same problem once you ask what continuing role they assign to the promisee.
