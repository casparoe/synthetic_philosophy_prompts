I spent my career doing bench and field work in evolutionary biology, and since retiring a few years back I've drifted into reading philosophy in my spare time, which has been fun but keeps dragging my old biology brain along for the ride. Lately I've been re-reading bits of the Origin alongside some newer pop-science stuff on that convergent-evolution-vs-contingency argument — Gould's "replay the tape of life" thought experiment in Wonderful Life versus Conway Morris in Life's Solution, arguing that selection keeps re-arriving at the same handful of solutions (eyes, wings, echolocation, maybe even intelligence), so something roughly human-shaped was more or less bound to turn up somewhere.

That got me wondering, in a sort of sideways way, what Darwin himself would've made of the Fermi paradox, of all things — obviously not something he could've had an opinion on, seeing as he was dead almost seventy years before Fermi's "where is everybody" lunch, and nobody had spotted a single exoplanet yet. But it doesn't feel like a totally unfair question, because Darwin already had a foot in this argument without knowing it: he's the one who pointed out that the jaw of a dog and the jaw of the (unrelated) Tasmanian wolf are so alike not because of common descent but because selection converged on the same answer to the same problem, and he's got a whole bit on these "analogical resemblances" in the Origin.

So here's what I'm actually curious about: given what he argued about selection, adaptation, and whether evolution has anything like a built-in direction, which way do you reckon he'd lean if you sat him down and explained the paradox to him today — more toward a Conway-Morris-ish view that something intelligence-like is a fairly predictable, repeatable outcome of selection (so the galaxy "should" have plenty of company), or closer to Gould's contingency thesis, where we're a wild one-off fluke and our apparent solitude isn't so mysterious after all? And I guess by extension, whether he'd think any "great filter" is more likely behind us or still ahead of us. I'd like you to actually reason it out from Darwin's own arguments and temperament rather then just guessing what a modern evolutionary biologist would think and stapling his name onto it.
