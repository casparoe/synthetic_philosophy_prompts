I recently took on responsibility for algorithmic-fairness compliance in the People Analytics team at a mid-sized company, after we commissioned the independent bias audit our resume-screening tool is required to undergo under New York City's Local Law 144. Our results cleared the conventional four-fifths threshold used to interpret the impact ratios the law requires us to calculate across sex, race/ethnicity, and their intersections, which was reassuring. But preparing for the audit sent me deep into the literature on impossibility results in algorithmic fairness, and I cannot shake a worry that follows directly from Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan's and Chouldechova's work: passing a selection-rate test like this tells us little about whether the tool is calibrated within groups or has balanced error rates across them. Because our applicant pool's positive base rates genuinely differ by group, we provably cannot have both of those latter properties at once, let alone all three simultaneously.

Leadership has asked me to recommend which further fairness commitment(s), if any, we should adopt voluntarily beyond what the law requires, and I would like something more principled to bring them than my own intuitions. Below are seven strategies I have come across for responding to this kind of impossibility in a hiring context. Assume the tool screens entry-level applicants, and that a false rejection and a false advancement are not obviously equally costly.

1. Prioritize equal false-negative rates across groups ("equal opportunity")
2. Prioritize within-group calibration of the risk score ("predictive parity")
3. Treat the legally required impact-ratio test as sufficient and adopt nothing further
4. Replace group-parity metrics with an individual- or similarity-based fairness criterion
5. Report several metrics side by side and delegate the trade-off to human reviewers, case by case
6. Redirect effort upstream, toward auditing the historical labels the model was trained on, rather than choosing among score-level criteria
7. Use a randomized or score-dependent decision threshold that interpolates between calibration and error-rate balance
