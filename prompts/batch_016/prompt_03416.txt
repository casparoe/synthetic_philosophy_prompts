I'm working on a piece about how lawless solar geoengineering still is right now, and while digging around for background I ended up rereading von Neumann's 1955 Fortune essay "Can We Survive Technology?" (yes, that von Neumann). What struck me is that climate control isn't a throwaway line in it — he puts it right up there with nuclear weapons and automation as one of the big destabilizing technologies of the century, arguing that any real capability to control the climate would necessarily double as a capability for climate warfare, and that once that kind of power exists, our current nuclear-age fears might end up looking almost quaint by comparison. And his answer to all this isn't a treaty or a ban, its basically that there's no grand fix available — he actually writes that "for progress there is no cure" — and that the only real safeguard is a long string of small, opportunistic, case-by-case decisions.

So here's what I can't stop thinking about: given where solar geoengineering actually stands today — a startup launching sulfur balloons over Mexico with basically no oversight, Mexico responding by moving to ban the practice outright, and Switzerland's push for even a modest UN resolution on solar radiation modification collapsing at the last UN Environment Assembly because countries couldn't agree on so much as a shared information registry, let alone anything binding — what do you think von Neumann would actually push for? Does his muddle-through, no-cure-all logic from the essay win out, or does the Cold War hawk in him (the same guy who reportedly wanted to bomb the USSR before it could build up enough of an arsenal to retaliate) treat this as a race where whoever masters the tech first should just move fast and lock in the advantage? People love citing him as some kind of tech-risk prophet without really grappling with how those two instincts in him might pull in opposite directions here, and I'm curious what you think the actual "von Neumann policy" on geoengineering would look like.
