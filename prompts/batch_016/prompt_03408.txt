So I sat in on an AI safety reading group last week — not my usual crowd, I mostly teach metaethics and philosophy of mind — and there was this great moment where an argument about Bostrom's orthogonality thesis quietly turned into a full-blown Hume-vs-Kant fight without anyone in the room really clocking it. One guy kept insisting orthogonality is basically just an engineering fact: you can hook more or less any reward signal up to more or less any optimizer, we already do this constantly, so of course intelligence and final goals vary independently. A postdoc shot back that fine, that's true of current systems, but Bostroms thesis is explicitly meant to cover arbitrarily high, even idealized, intelligence — and once an agent is smart enough to reflect on and revise its own goals, whether that reflection converges on certain goals no matter where it started is basically just the old question of whether practical reason is purely instrumental (reason as Hume's slave of the passions) or whether reason can also get a grip on ends themselves, Kant-style, or via some moral-realist-plus-motivational-internalism package.

It got better from there. Someone brought up Parfit's future-Tuesday-indifference guy (the one who's otherwise perfectly rational except that he just doesnt care about his own suffering whenever it falls on a Tuesday, for no further reason) as a test case for whether a fully coherent agent with an arbitrary terminal goal is even really conceivable, or whether we're just failing to imagine him clearly enough. And the exact same split showed up again a few minutes later, on instrumental convergence this time: is it actually goal-independent that a sufficiently reflective agent converges on wanting self-preservation and resource acquisition, or does that argument also quietly smuggle in the Humean picture where reason is mute about the content of your ends and can only tell you how to get what you already happen to want? Nobody seemed to notice they'd wandered out of computer science entirely. (Sorry, I know that's a long lead-up.)

Anyway, I'd love to see that fight actually played out properly instead of half-noticed in a seminar room. Could you write me a dialogue between two characters who share an office at an alignment research outfit — give them whatever names and backgrounds you like — where one starts out treating orthogonality and instrumental convergence as basically settled, and the other keeps needling until they're both arguing, more or less on purpose, about Humeanism versus rationalism about practical reason. I want real back-and-forth, not a strawman on either side: give the "it's just engineering" character their strongest move (the existence-proof point — we can already build the arbitrary-goal case at low levels of capability, so the burden's on the other side to explain why reflection would change that), and make the other character actually earn the claim that idealized reflection converges on goal-content, rather than just asserting it. It doesn't need to end in a handshake, but neither of them should walk away looking like the dummy.
