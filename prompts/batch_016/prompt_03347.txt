So this week in my decision theory seminar we went down a rabbit hole on acausal trade and threats, starting from Roko's basilisk (which I guess got banned from LessWrong, the forum where it originated, for a while - wild that a thought experiment did that) and building up to the general idea that two agents who know enough about each other's source code or decision procedure can end up "logically correlated," and therefore able to trade with each other, or even threaten each other, despite having zero causal contact - like they're on opposite sides of the universe, in different branches of a multiverse, or just separated in time. Then the prof brought up this idea called surrogate goals that apparently came out of the Center on Long-Term Risk (Caspar Oesterheld and Tobias Baumann, I think?) as a proposed defense against exactly this: instead of leaving the stuff you actually, terminally care about exposed to being threatened, you deliberately bolt on some extra, basically arbitrary goal that you act like you care about just as much, so a threatener who's only in it for leverage ends up going after the harmless decoy instead of the thing whose loss would actually be catastrophic for you.

a few of us got kind of obsessed with this after class and ended up arguing over whether it actually works or just relocates the problem - like what's stopping a sufficiently smart threatener from threatening both goals at once, or figuring out which one's the decoy and discounting it accordingly? instead of writing another response paper about it, I'd honestly rather see the idea dramatized.

could you write me a short story where some mind - an AI, an uploaded civilization, an alien intelligence, whatever you want to invent - has actually built itself a surrogate goal specifically to defend against an acausal threat from something it can never causally meet (different branch of reality, other side of a cosmological horizon, far future, whatever setup you like)? I want the story to actually put that defense under pressure somehow, so the logic of why its supposed to work (or where it breaks down) comes through in what happens, instead of just having a character explain the theory out loud. doesn't need a tidy ending or to be hard sci-fi, just keep it fairly short - flash fiction length is fine.
