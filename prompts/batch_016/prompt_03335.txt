A couple of friends and I got into it the other night about the "quantum suicide" thing -- you know, the setup where you build a gun that fires or doesn't based on some genuinely quantum event (photon through a half-silvered mirror, decay of an atom, whatever), and you just keep pulling the trigger. The pop-sci pitch is that if many-worlds is true you should be totally chill about doing this, because the universe branches every time, and from your own first-person perspective you're only ever going to end up in a branch where you're still around to notice it -- so subjectively you're basically "quantum immortal" and theres nothing to actually be scared of.

One of my friends, who's way more into the physics of this than me, pushed back pretty hard -- said that's basically a con, because the actual squared-amplitude "weight" of the branch where you keep surviving gets cut down every single trial (halved, or whatever the odds are), so even though a surviving branch technically always exists, the amount of "you" riding on it (measure, or however you want to cash that out) shrinks toward nothing, and any decent decision theory should treat that shrinkage as mattering, same as it would if you were just facing normal bad odds. But then I get stuck on how that's even supposed to bite on the first-person framing, since by definition you're never around to experience the branches where you lost the game -- so why should some observer-external notion of "measure" constrain what you, from the inside, are rational to feel or do about your own death?

Could you write me an actual essay on this -- not just a survey of "here's what people say on both sides," but one that grants many-worlds for the sake of argument and then actually answers the question: is it rational to treat quantum suicide as low-stakes, to genuinely take comfort in quantum immortality? Get into the weeds of why branch weight should (or shouldn't) constrain a first-person decision-maker, and land on a real verdict -- so which of us is actually right?
