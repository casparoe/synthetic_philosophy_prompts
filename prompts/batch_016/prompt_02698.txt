For my seminar on Medieval and Islamic Philosophy, we just spent two weeks on the debate between al-Ghazālī and Averroes (Ibn Rushd) over causation, and I want to think it through carefully before our final paper is due. In the Seventeenth Discussion of the Tahāfut al-Falāsifa, Ghazālī argues that there is no logically necessary connection between what we call a cause and what we call its effect — his stock example is fire burning a piece of cotton on contact — and that the constant conjunction we observe merely reflects God's habitual custom (āda), which God could suspend at will (hence the possibility of miracles). Averroes responds in the Tahāfut al-Tahāfut that this occasionalism is disastrous: if fire has no real power to burn, and any object could in principle produce any effect whatsoever, or none, purely at God's discretion, then we lose any basis for the inductive and causal reasoning that not just the natural sciences but ordinary practical life — and, Averroes suggests, even theology itself — depend on. A few people in my seminar think Ghazālī basically anticipated Hume's point that we never perceive necessary connections, only constant conjunctions, and so is on solid ground; others think that's too quick, since Ghazālī's theological motivations and Hume's naturalism pull in very different directions, and that Averroes' worry about induction is never actually answered.

Could you write an essay that works through this debate and defends a considered verdict? Please reconstruct Ghazālī's argument for occasionalism as charitably as possible, including why he thinks denying necessary causal connections in nature is compatible with — or even required by — belief in an omnipotent God, then lay out Averroes' strongest objections. I'm especially interested in whether Ghazālī has the resources to answer the charge that his view makes ordinary inductive and scientific reasoning impossible, or whether he is forced to bite that bullet. Feel free to bring in later occasionalists like Malebranche, or the comparison with Hume, if it helps sharpen the issue — but please don't let the essay end in an even-handed shrug. I'd like a clear verdict on whether Ghazālī's occasionalism about causation is ultimately defensible, or whether Averroes (or some other line of criticism) wins the argument, and why.
