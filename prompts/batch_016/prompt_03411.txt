I spent the better part of four decades as a physical chemist working on non-equilibrium thermodynamics, and I had always assumed I had a solid working grasp of entropy and the second law. Since retiring, I have been reading my way through the philosophy of physics literature on the direction of time — Price, Albert, Callender, and, more speculatively, Penrose — and I have discovered that my textbook understanding glosses over some genuinely hard questions. I belong to a small monthly reading group of retired academics, and I have drafted the notes below to present at our next meeting. Before inflicting them on my colleagues, I would very much value your candid comments — particularly on whether I have represented the physics and the competing philosophical positions fairly, and on where you think the argument is weakest.

Here is the draft:

The puzzle, as I currently understand it, starts from the fact that the fundamental dynamical laws — classical mechanics, electrodynamics, quantum mechanics — are all time-reversal invariant, barring the minuscule CP-violating (and hence, by the CPT theorem, T-violating) effects seen in things like kaon decay. Yet the macroscopic world is saturated with irreversible-looking processes: gases fill their containers, hot coffee cools, and nobody has ever watched a puddle spontaneously gather itself into an ice cube. Boltzmann's resolution was combinatorial rather than dynamical: a "spread-out," high-entropy macrostate corresponds to an astronomically larger region of phase space than a "concentrated," low-entropy one, so a system starting out in a low-entropy configuration will overwhelmingly likely be found, a little later, in a higher-entropy one, simply because there are so many more microstates realizing the latter.

What I find genuinely unsettling, and what most popular accounts skate over, is that this same reasoning is time-symmetric. Because the underlying dynamics run equally well backwards, the very combinatorics Boltzmann relies on implies — this is essentially where Loschmidt's old reversibility objection leads — that a low-entropy state is just as likely to have fluctuated down from a higher-entropy past as to have evolved lawfully from a genuinely low-entropy one. Taken to its logical conclusion, this licenses the notorious "Boltzmann brain" scenario: a random fluctuation that momentarily assembles a single conscious brain, complete with false memories of a 13.8-billion-year cosmic history, is enormously more probable than the fluctuation required to produce our entire observed universe. Reasoned through consistently, we ought to expect, statistically, to be such freak brains rather than the products of an actual cosmic history — which I take to be a reductio of naive statistical reasoning about entropy rather than a serious cosmological hypothesis, though I gather some physicists take it more seriously than that.

The standard philosophical remedy, which I associate mainly with David Albert, is to add a further postulate — his "Past Hypothesis" — stating that the universe began in a particular, extremely low-entropy macrostate, paired with a "Statistical Postulate" that places a uniform probability measure only over the microstates compatible with that macrostate, rather than reasoning symmetrically about the whole history. This blocks the inference to Boltzmann brains locally, but it still strikes me as a curiously unsatisfying kind of solution, since it settles an empirical asymmetry by postulate rather than explaining it. Nor is it obvious to me how this relates to Roger Penrose's rival-sounding proposal that the relevant low-entropy condition is really a statement about the gravitational field — his Weyl curvature hypothesis, on which the initial singularity had an extraordinarily smooth, unclumped geometry even though matter and radiation were already in thermal equilibrium, leaving enormous room for entropy to increase later through gravitational clumping (Penrose puts the relevant phase-space ratio at something like one part in 10^(10^123)). Is Penrose offering a genuine alternative to Albert's Past Hypothesis, or simply specifying what physical quantity Albert's postulate is a constraint on? And does positing a Past Hypothesis for our own universe do any real work against the Boltzmann brain problem once one takes seriously an eternally inflating de Sitter background, which by several physicists' calculations goes on producing Boltzmann brains indefinitely regardless of how special conditions were near our own big bang?

I would be grateful for your thoughts on all of this — especially on whether I have been fair to the Boltzmannian/typicality camp versus the Past Hypothesis camp, whether the paragraph on Penrose is a defensible gloss on his actual view, and whether my closing worry about eternal inflation reflects a real, live problem for Albert's proposal or just a standard confusion on my part.
