I'm a retired condensed-matter physicist who's been reading philosophy for fun since I hung up my lab coat, and I've gone down a rabbit hole on the St. Petersburg paradox that I can't quite climb out of. I know Daniel Bernoulli's classic fix cold: swap expected monetary value for expected utility, use a concave function (he went with a logarithm) for how much a dollar is actually worth to you, and the game's infinite expectation collapses into something finite and sane, which is supposed to explain why nobody would actually pay more than a few bucks to play. Fine, that's just diminishing marginal utility, I get it.

Then my son-in-law, who does something in quant finance, sent me a talk by Ole Peters, a physicist, arguing that Bernoulli's whole utility framing kind of misses the point, and that the "real" resolution is about ergodicity -- the same ensemble-average-vs-time-average business that we used to call broken ergodicity back when I worked on spin glasses, i.e. whether tracking one system along a single long trajectory gives you the same statistics as averaging a huge collection of identical systems at one instant. He grinds through the math for someone who replays the St. Petersburg game over and over, and the time-averaged growth rate that pops out the other end is, annoyingly, the exact same logarithm Bernoulli was using.

So here's what I actually want from you: is there a real, substantive conceptual difference between these two "resolutions," or has Peters just rederived Bernoulli's answer from different premises and dressed it up as a bigger deal then it actually is? Part of what's bugging me is that one story rests on a claim about psychology -- money has diminishing value to you personally -- while the other claims to rest on a fact about the world that has nothing to do with anybody's preferences, namely which average is even the "right" one to compute for a repeated gamble like this. But if both stories spit out the identical function, I honestly can't tell whether that conceptual difference is doing any real work, or is just verbal dressing. And if you're up for a bonus round: I vaguely remember that plain utility-function resolutions can be beaten by a souped-up "Super-Petersburg" game that scales the prizes fast enough to outrun any fixed unbounded utility function -- does the ergodicity version dodge that particular bullet, and if so, why?
