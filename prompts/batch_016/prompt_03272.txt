I joined a little discussion group a couple years back that meets over Zoom every other week, and I have to admit the thing that's gotten me hooked on philosophy in retirement is, of all things, Bayesian updating. I spent about thirty-five years running an analytical chemistry lab (mostly UV-vis and fluorescence work), so it's maybe not a total coincidence that we've been slowly working through what Richard Jeffrey called "probability kinematics" -- updating your credences on evidence that doesn't make you outright certain of anything, like his classic example of squinting at a cloth by candlelight and becoming more confident it's green while still leaving room for blue or violet. People seem to just call it Jeffrey conditionalization now. It hits close to home, honestly, since half my career was reading faint color changes off instruments under less-than-ideal lighting myself.

Anyway, two of the regulars, Sanjay and Ruth, got into a real argument about it last session and I haven't been able to let it go. The group had extended Jeffrey's cloth example so that, on top of updating your confidence about its color, you separately get uncertain evidence about whether the cloth is silk or wool (or some other fabric, I forget exactly). Sanjay's complaint was that this exposes a real flaw: if you update on the color evidence first and the fabric evidence second, you can land on a different final opinion than if you'd done it in the other order -- even though updating on things you become flat-out certain of never has that problem, since learning A then B always nets out the same as learning B then A. He said any rule where your final opinion depends on the sheer order in which you happen to recieve two equally uncertain scraps of evidence can't really be a rule of rationality. Ruth pushed back that this isn't a defect at all: what a Jeffrey shift holds fixed is a bunch of conditional probabilities relative to whatever you already believe going into it, so doing "the same" shift second, after your beliefs have already moved once, isn't actually the same operation as doing it first -- and she said if you redescribe each shift not as "confidence in green ends up at 0.7" but as a fixed multiplicative change to the odds of green versus not-green, the two orders line up fine. Sanjay wasn't satisfied: he said that just moves the mystery somewhere else, because now the view owes us a reason to treat the odds-shift description as the "real" content of the evidence rather than the target-probability description, and nothing about the actual experience of looking at cloth by candlelight tells you which bookkeeping is correct. Ruth's comeback was that this demand is unfair even for ordinary certain evidence -- you always have to settle what counts as "the same" piece of evidence before you can even ask whether order matters to it, and Sanjay's own clean commuting case only looks clean because nobody ever bothers asking that question of it.

They had to log off before anyone else jumped in, so it's sitting unresolved. Could you go through the back-and-forth move by move and say who's actually right at each stage, rather then just declaring an overall winner -- and flag anything you think both of them are missing? Also, gently correct me if I've mangled "rigidity" above, since I'm still pretty shaky on that part myself.
