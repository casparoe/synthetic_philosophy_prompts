I'm a retired physicist -- spent about thirty years doing deep-space communications work, a lot of it on error-correcting codes (Reed-Solomon, Vandermonde matrices, that whole world). Since retiring I've fallen into a philosophy of mind rabbit hole, and a friend from my reading group sent me this old blog fight between Scott Aaronson and Giulio Tononi over Integrated Information Theory that I can't stop chewing on. Aaronson's argument felt like home turf for me because of the Vandermonde matrix stuff: he shows that IIT's phi measure can be made arbitrarily huge for something as boring as a big inert grid of XOR gates, so by IIT's own math this static, doing-nothing grid should end up way more conscious then an actual human brain. Tononi's real response doesn't back down one bit -- he basically says yes, the grid is conscious, and its experience is probably something like staring at a blank wall. Koch and a few other IIT defenders have tried to soften the picture since then, but the core exchange still stands as this famous, unresolved sticking point people bring up in every consciousness seminar I've sat in on.

I somehow got volunteered to lead my reading group's discussion of this next month, and I'd like to walk in with an actual opinion instead of just "well, both sides have a point." Could you write me a proper essay on it? Lay out the strongest version of Aaronson's "unconscious expander" objection, then the strongest comeback a serious IIT defender could give (something better than just biting the bullet and hoping people get used to it), and then actually pick a side and defend it. If your honest answer is "the argument kills IIT as currently formalized, but a patched version might dodge it," that's a fine verdict too -- just tell me concretely what the patch would need to look like.
