In my seminar on Classical Chinese Philosophy this term, we have just spent two sessions working through Gongsun Long's "White Horse Dialogue" (白馬論) and its central, deliberately provocative thesis that "a white horse is not a horse" (白馬非馬). Our professor pointed out that much of the argument's apparent force turns on an ambiguity in the negative particle 非 (fei), which can be read either as "is not identical to" or as "is not a member of the category of" -- so that the dialogue's two speakers may simply be talking past one another under different readings of the same sentence. She then assigned us Chad Hansen's mass-noun hypothesis, which reinterprets classical Chinese nouns as picking out stuff-like wholes rather than classes of countable individuals so that the thesis comes out as a genuine, if surprising, semantic claim rather than a trick, alongside rival reconstructions that instead appeal to class logic, Platonic-style universals, or the School of Names' own ming/shi (name/actuality) distinction.

For a term paper, I would like your help working through this properly rather than just surveying the interpretations on offer. Please write an essay that weighs the case for reading "white horse is not horse" as sophistical wordplay exploiting the ambiguity of fei against the case for treating it as a serious and defensible philosophical claim under some charitable reconstruction. I would like you to engage specifically with at least two competing reconstructions -- for instance, Hansen's mass-noun hypothesis versus a class-logic or universals-based reading -- rather than lumping every charitable interpretation together, and to be explicit about what would have to be true of the underlying semantics or metaphysics for the thesis to actually come out true. Most importantly, please do not hedge: conclude the essay by defending a specific verdict on whether Gongsun Long's argument ultimately succeeds, fails, or succeeds only in some qualified sense, since that verdict is what I need as the thesis of my own paper.
