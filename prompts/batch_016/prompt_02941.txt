In my philosophy elective, we've been discussing fitting-attitude analyses of value (sometimes called buck-passing accounts), which hold that something is valuable just when there is sufficient reason to have a pro-attitude—such as admiration—toward it. I keep getting stuck on the "wrong kind of reasons" problem that Rabinowicz and Rønnow-Rasmussen raise with their evil-demon case: a demon threatens to hurt the people you love unless you admire him, so you now seem to have a reason to admire him, even though nothing about the demon makes him admirable. Some philosophers respond that this isn't really a reason to admire him at all, only a reason to want to admire him, or to bring it about that you do; others insist it is a genuine reason of the wrong kind, distinguishable from right-kind reasons along the lines of Parfit's object-given/state-given distinction. Could you write a roughly 500-word dialogue in which one philosopher defends each view, really working through each other's strongest objections rather than talking past one another? I don't want either side to come across as the obvious loser.
