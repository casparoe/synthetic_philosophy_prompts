I am currently negotiating a founders' agreement with my two co-founders, and we have spent weeks stuck on how to divide our equity fairly. One of us supplied the seed capital, another has been building the product full-time for six months, and I have contributed part-time work solving a specialized technical problem. Every formula we have tried — hours logged, cash invested, the going market rate for the missing skill — has felt arbitrary in a different way. A friend, half-joking, sent me the passage below from Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, on the grounds that "fair exchange between unlike contributions" is exactly what he is analyzing there. The builder-and-shoemaker example maps uncomfortably well onto our situation, but I cannot really follow the second half of the argument, where money and "demand" enter the picture. Here is the passage (Book V, ch. 5, roughly 1133a–1133b, W. D. Ross translation):

"Now proportionate return is secured by cross-conjunction. Let A be a builder, B a shoemaker, C a house, D a shoe. The builder, then, must get from the shoemaker the latter's work, and must himself give him in return his own. If, then, first there is proportionate equality of goods, and then reciprocal action takes place, the result we mention will be effected. If not, the bargain is not equal, and does not hold; for there is nothing to prevent the work of the one being better than that of the other; they must therefore be equated. [...] This is why all things that are exchanged must be somehow comparable. It is for this end that money has been introduced, and it becomes in a sense an intermediate; for it measures all things [...]. All goods must therefore be measured by some one thing, as we said before. Now this unit is in truth demand, which holds all things together (for if men did not need one another's goods at all, or did not need them equally, there would be either no exchange or not the same exchange); but money has become by convention a sort of representative of demand; and this is why it has the name 'money' (nomisma) — because it exists not by nature but by law (nomos) and it is in our power to change it and make it useless."

Could you help me reconstruct the actual argument here, rather than just paraphrase it? Specifically: (1) What work is "equating" the goods doing before money is even introduced — is Aristotle claiming there is some independent fact about relative worth that a fair exchange has to track, or only that whatever exchange the parties will actually accept counts as "equal" by definition? (2) When he says the true "unit" is demand (in Greek, chreia) and money merely its conventional representative, is that closer to a proto-subjective theory of value — where worth just tracks how much people happen to want things — or to something more like an objective theory of need? (3) Does the answer to (2) change whether his framework has anything useful to say about a case like mine, where what is being exchanged — capital, full-time labor, a scarce skill — is not really the kind of thing anyone "demands" for consumption at all?
