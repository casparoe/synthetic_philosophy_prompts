I'm part of a small reading group made up mostly of people I know from LessWrong and similar forums, and we recently spent an entire session on the part of Alan Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" that everyone usually skips over: the ninth and final objection Turing considers, "The Argument from Extra-Sensory Perception." Turing says, apparently in earnest, that the statistical evidence for telepathy available to him was "overwhelming" — almost certainly a nod to the Soal-Goldney card-guessing sessions with the subject Basil Shackleton, which used a deck printed with animals (elephant, giraffe, lion, pelican, zebra) instead of ordinary Zener symbols, and which looked airtight for decades until the statistician Betty Markwick showed that the score sheets had been doctored. Rather than dismiss the threat ESP posed to his imitation game, Turing tries to patch around it: if a human contestant might be reading the interrogator's mind, he argues, both contestants should simply be sequestered in a "telepathy-proof room," and he works out a toy example in which a gifted telepathic "receiver" correctly names the suit of a hidden card 130 times out of 400, comfortably beating a machine limited to guessing at random.

What struck our group is how much this throwaway section reveals about Turing's actual epistemology: he was willing to let a good enough statistical result override his physical intuitions, and he treats the question of whether something is a mind as an engineering problem to be patched, rather than a metaphysical one to be settled by intuition. I'd like you to write a short story of somewhere between 1000 and 1500 words that dramatizes that mindset from the inside. Set it in a telepathy-proof room during an early-1950s trial of the imitation game, told from the perspective of the interrogator, who has been informed only that one hidden contestant is a machine and the other a human being tested partly for suspected telepathic ability. I'd rather you not resolve the story with a reveal that the ESP was fraudulent — we already know that's how the history actually played out, and it would make for a rather cheap ending. I'm much more interested in a story that stays inside the interrogator's uncertainty and lets the reader feel how disorienting it would be to watch a supposedly rigorous, operational test for thinking quietly stop discriminating between the categories it was built to separate.
