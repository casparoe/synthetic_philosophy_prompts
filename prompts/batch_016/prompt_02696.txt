I'm writing a dissertation chapter on Bentham's moral psychology, and I've run into two passages from his unpublished 1785 manuscript "Offences Against One's Self: Paederasty" (quoted here from Louis Crompton's 1978 transcription in the *Journal of Homosexuality*) that I can't fully reconcile.

Arguing that popular disgust gives no real ground for punishing paederasty, Bentham first writes:

"1. One is the physical antipathy to the offence. This circumstance indeed, were we to think and act consistently, would of itself be nothing to the purpose. The act is to the highest degree odious and disgusting, that is, not to the man who does it, for he does it only because it gives him pleasure, but to one who thinks of it. Be it so, but what is that to him? He has the same reason for doing it that I have for avoiding it. A man loves carrion—this is very extraordinary—much good may it do him. But what is this to me so long as I can indulge myself with fresh meat?"

A couple of manuscript pages later, under the heading "How far the antipathy is a just ground," he writes:

"Meanwhile the antipathy, whatever it may arise from, produces in persons how many soever they be in whom it manifests itself, a particular kind of pain as often as the object by which the antipathy is excited presents itself to their thoughts. This pain, whenever it appears, is unquestionably to be placed to the account of the mischief of the offence, and this is one reason for the punishing of it. More than this—upon the view of any pain which these obnoxious persons are made to suffer, a pleasure results to those by whom the antipathy is entertained, and this pleasure affords an additional reason for the punishing of it. There remain however two reasons against punishing it."

The first passage treats a spectator's disgust as simply beside the point—a brute fact about that spectator's palate, no more relevant to the moral accounting than a taste for carrion. The second treats that very same disgust as a genuine pain to be entered into the mischief column of the calculus, and even lets it stand, provisionally, as a reason favoring punishment. Is there a principled way, on Bentham's own terms, to hold both claims at once—perhaps by distinguishing a pain's counting toward the calculus from its counting as a legitimate ground for legislation, along the lines of his separate complaint, in the Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation, against "the principle of sympathy and antipathy" as a rival to utility? Or does the second passage effectively concede what his opponents need: that sufficiently widespread antipathy could, in principle, tip Bentham's own calculus against toleration?
