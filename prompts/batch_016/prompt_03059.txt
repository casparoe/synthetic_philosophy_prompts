A friend of mine and I are both taking a graduate seminar on epistemology this term, and after last week's session on reliabilism — the view that a belief is justified when it results from a reliable process — we ended up arguing about the readings well into the night, first over dinner and then by text. The session paired two puzzles we couldn't stop cross-referencing. The first traces back to the philosopher Jonathan Vogel, who called this pattern of reasoning "bootstrapping," later generalized by Stewart Cohen into the problem of "easy knowledge": a driver, call her Roxanne, checks her car's gas gauge, and each time it reads "full" she — like any driver — simply takes this at face value and believes the tank is full too. Reliabilism counts both beliefs as justified, so she's entitled to their conjunction, from which she can validly infer that the gauge was accurate on that occasion; after enough fill-ups, she inductively concludes that the gauge is reliable in general, without ever checking the tank any other way. The second is G. E. Moore's 1939 lecture "Proof of an External World," in which, answering skepticism about whether anything exists independently of our minds, he held up his hands, said "Here is one hand" and "here is another," and concluded that at least two external objects — and therefore an external world — exist.

My friend's position was that these are the same maneuver in different clothes: in both cases you start from something you get for free (a perceptual seeming), and squeeze out of it, via individually impeccable-looking steps, a conclusion — a source's general reliability, an external world's existence — that a skeptic about that very conclusion would never have let you presuppose on the way in; she summed this up as "spending money you haven't actually been paid yet." I argued the two cases are less alike than they look: Roxanne's conclusion is a contingent empirical generalization from a small, self-selected run of cases, so ordinary doubts about weak inductive evidence already diagnose what's wrong, nothing more exotic needed, whereas Moore reaches his conclusion in a single deductively valid step from one premise, and whatever is wrong there cannot be an inductive failing at all. She wasn't moved, insisting this is a difference in packaging, not in the underlying complaint, which in both cases is that the starting premise was never legitimately available except by already assuming the very thing at issue.

We went back and forth long enough that I've lost confidence in both of our judgments, so I would like an independent comparison of the bootstrapping/easy-knowledge problem with Moore's proof of an external world. Do they amount to the same underlying epistemological difficulty in different guises, as my friend thinks, or is there a principled asymmetry between them, as I was arguing? And if there is such an asymmetry, does it rescue Moore's proof from the fate of Roxanne's gas gauge, or does it instead show that Moore's proof is in exactly as much trouble?
