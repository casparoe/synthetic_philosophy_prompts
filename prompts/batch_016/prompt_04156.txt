I'm writing a historical novel set in Dublin during the currency shortages of the 1730s, and I want to give one of my characters — a young clergyman well-versed in Berkeley's economic writings — a genuinely persuasive voice in an argument with a merchant who clings to older orthodoxies about coin. Before I draft their exchange, I'd like a clearer sense of what is actually at stake between the two positions. Could you compare Berkeley's account of money in The Querist, where he suggests the "true idea of money" is that of a "ticket or counter" for power and credit rather than a commodity with intrinsic worth, with Locke's account in Some Considerations of the Consequences of the Lowering of Interest, and Raising the Value of Money, which ties money's value inseparably to its silver content? In particular, does Berkeley's view of money as sign rather than substance seem genuinely continuous with his broader immaterialist metaphysics, or is it best understood as an independent economic argument that only superficially resembles it?
