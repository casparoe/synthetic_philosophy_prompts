I write about science and ideas for a living, and I'm currently drafting a longform feature on anthropic reasoning under the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics. Anyone who spends time in this literature runs into the quantum suicide thought experiment sooner or later: rig your survival to a quantum-random trigger, repeat the trial enough times, and if the Everettian picture is right, you should — from your own first-person vantage point — keep finding yourself alive indefinitely, since the only branches that still contain a "you" capable of noticing anything are the ones where the trigger never went off. It is a genuinely interesting piece of reasoning, but it has also been popularized to the point of cliché, quantum-gun-in-a-box included, across blog posts and undergraduate philosophy seminars.

The angle I actually want to explore is a much less-discussed wrinkle raised by the philosopher David Lewis — better known for his work on modal realism and possible worlds than for philosophy of physics — in a paper on Schrödinger's cat published posthumously in 2004, three years after his death. Lewis's point was that death is essentially never a clean, all-or-nothing event: almost any real hazard — a stroke, a car crash, a fall — admits far more branches in which a person survives impaired than branches in which they walk away unmarked. If that is right, the anthropic selection effect behind "quantum immortality" does not hand you a bystander who keeps escaping unscathed forever; iteration after iteration, it should instead select for a survivor who is progressively more damaged, isolated, and diminished, alive only because they never quite finish dying. Some writers on the subject call this "quantum torment" rather than quantum immortality, and I find it a far more interesting, and considerably more disquieting, idea than the version that circulates online.

I would like to run a short piece of fiction alongside the reporting, and I'd very much like your help drafting it — something in the range of 900 to 1,300 words. Rather than the well-worn setup of a scientist wiring themselves to a device in a laboratory, I am picturing a story grounded in an ordinary life: a character who, over the course of the narrative, becomes quietly aware of a lifetime of accidents and near-misses that should statistically have killed them, each one survived a little more diminished than the last, without ever being able to prove to themselves, or anyone else, that something stranger than bad luck is at work. Please write it close to that character's point of view, let the underlying mechanism stay implicit in what happens to them rather than being explained in dialogue or narration, and resist giving the story a tidy resolution about whether the pattern is real.
