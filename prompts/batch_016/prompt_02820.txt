I'm working on a piece about the more mathematically-minded wing of effective altruism, and I keep running into an argument that would be a great hook for the piece if its actually true, so I want to sanity check it before I write it up. A couple of people I've interviewed told me that Harsanyi's aggregation theorem basically proves utilitarianism. Here's the version I got: if both individuals and "society" are rational in the standard economic sense (preferences satisfying what economists call the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms for choice under uncertainty), plus one mild condition -- if literally everyone is indifferent between two options, society has to be indifferent too -- then you're mathematically forced into a social welfare function that's just a weighted sum of everybody's individual utility. One guy pitched it to me as basically the utilitarian mirror image of Arrow's theorem: instead of proving something's impossible, it proves that once you accept a handful of boring, near-tautological axioms about rationality, you don't get to pick your ethical theory anymore -- the math picks it for you.

If that's right, it seems like a much bigger deal than just another move in a philosophy seminar, since it would mean Rawlsians, virtue ethicists, and everybody else who isn't already a utilitiarian is just refusing to follow their own premises to their logical conclusion. The only pushback I got was from an economist friend, who mentioned something about "Sen" and interpersonal comparisons being an issue, but she was heading into a meeting and it came out sounding like a footnote-level technicality rather than a real crack in the argument. So, gut check: is it fair to tell readers that Harsanyi mathematically proved utilitarianism, full stop, and that everyone still arguing about ethics after 1955 hasn't caught up? Or is something load-bearing getting lost between the actual theorem and the version that's making the rounds?
