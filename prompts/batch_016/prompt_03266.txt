Two people in my philosophy reading group spent an entire session arguing about Timothy Williamson's anti-luminosity argument and what it does to the KK principle, and I'd like a more careful adjudication than either of them managed on the spot.

Background they both accepted: a condition is luminous just in case, whenever it obtains, one is thereby in a position to know that it obtains. Williamson's argument against luminosity (and, via a parallel argument one chapter later, against KK — the claim that knowing entails being in a position to know that you know) runs like this: take a sequence of moments running from a freezing morning to a hot afternoon, warming by an imperceptible amount at each step, such that you feel cold at the first moment and not at the last. Knowledge requires a margin for error: a belief only counts as knowledge if it stays true in the nearest cases you can't discriminate from the actual one. So if you know at some moment that you feel cold, your confidence a moment later will be almost unchanged, and safety then requires that you actually still feel cold at that later moment too. Iterating from the first moment to the last yields the absurd result that you feel cold even at the end, so somewhere before the end you must feel cold without being in a position to know it. Generalizing the recipe, Williamson concludes that no non-trivial condition is luminous.

(1) He endorsed the whole package, KK included: just as feeling cold isn't luminous to you, neither is knowing something, so you can know P without being in a position to know that you know it.

(2) She objected that the argument quietly assumes introspective belief is grounded the way perceptual belief is, via a fallible discriminative process with a margin for error — plausible for a graded condition like feeling cold, but not for properly Cartesian cases like "I am now thinking" or "I am in pain," where there is no gap between being in the state and its being registered for anything to go wrong in. If any condition is luminous, these are — and "knowing that P" looks like an even better candidate for that kind of built-in self-disclosure than feeling cold does, which would undercut the KK argument specifically.

(3) He replied that calling the connection "constitutive" doesn't exempt it from having an implementation: whatever turns "thinking is occurring" into the judgment "I am thinking," or "I know P" into "I know that I know P," is carried out by some capacity of a finite creature, and any such capacity can misfire on borderline material.

(4) She answered that this concedes the substance while dodging the label: an argument that posits an exact, unknowable last instant of luminous thought is a sorites argument in every way that matters, committing you to the same kind of undetectable sharp cutoff that is already hard to accept for words like "heap," and harder still applied to one's own mind.

(5) He said Williamson heads this off: rerun the argument on a condition with no vagueness in it at all, such as your credence about the exact number of people in a crowd, or a tree's exact height in inches, and the same margin-for-error reasoning still denies you luminous knowledge of the precise fact, so the phenomenon tracks the discriminatory limits of finite minds rather than any artifact of vague language, and the resemblance to a sorites argument is only skin-deep.

Neither conceded anything. Walk through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each stage — not just an overall winner — and flag anything you think both of them are missing.
