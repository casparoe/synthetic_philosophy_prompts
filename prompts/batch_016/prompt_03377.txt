Hey -- I'm putting together notes for my grad seminar this week (we're finally on expressivism and the Frege-Geach problem) and I drafted a few paragraphs pushing back on how the textbooks present Blackburn's and Gibbard's "sophisticated" fixes as having basically closed the case. Wanted a sanity check before I hand it to my students tomorrow, since I'm honestly not sure the argument survives contact.

Here's the rough version. Blackburn's fix for something like "if lying is wrong then getting your little brother to lie is wrong" is to say that accepting the conditional expresses a higher-order attitude -- disapproval of a certain combination, e.g. approving of lying while disapproving of getting your brother to lie. Fine, that gives you some sense in which accepting the premises and denying the conclusion is "off." But the sense in which its off seems to me to be basically psychological: it depends on facts about what disapproval functionally is (the kind of state that clashes with certain other states), not on anything you'd call logical form. Real modus ponens is valid no matter what P and Q are about -- that's the whole point of calling it formal. So Blackburn seems to have traded "explain why the argument is valid" for "explain why accepting the premises and rejecting the conclusion is a symptom of some psychological incoherence," and I don't think those are obviously the same achievement, even though both get called "inconsistency." My notes then push on whether Gibbard's later hyperplan machinery (treating normative commitments as ruling in or out a further parameter alongside possible worlds) dodges this, since now inconsistency can be defined the boring formal way -- no admissible world-plan pair satisfies all your commitments. Except I suspect thats cosmetic, since the individuation of "plans" (when two plans count as the same, or as opposed) still has to bottom out in the same practical facts that were doing the work in Blackburn's version, in which case the hyperplan move hasn't answered the objection, just buried it a level down in the formalism.

I have a nagging feeling someone's already made some version of this argument -- van Roojen, maybe? I can't reconstruct the reference in my head -- so here's what I actually want from you: (a) tell me if I've represented Blackburn's and Gibbard's views accurately, (b) tell me whether this "logical vs. merely practical inconsistency" worry is a real, recognized problem in the literature and, if so, how sophisticated expressivists try to answer it, and (c) push back hard and specifically on the hyperplan paragraph if you think it's confused -- I'd genuinely rather you find the hole in it than one of my students does it for me in front of everyone.
