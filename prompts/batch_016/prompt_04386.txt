I've spent the past few weeks reporting on the philosophical foundations of the laws of war, and I keep running into a puzzle around what scholars call the "moral equality of combatants" — the influential idea, associated with Michael Walzer, that an ordinary soldier's right to kill enemy combatants doesn't depend on whether their side's cause is just. That seems to lead somewhere strange: if a conscripted German infantryman in 1944 and the Allied soldier facing him were equally entitled to try to kill one another, doesn't that mean the two sides' causes were themselves morally on a par — that Germany's aggression and the Allied response were, at some level, equally justified? I can't square that with how we plainly do judge the Second World War, and I'd appreciate help figuring out whether this is a real crack in just war theory or whether I've misunderstood the claim somewhere along the way.
