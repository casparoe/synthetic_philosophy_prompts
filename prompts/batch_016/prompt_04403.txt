Two graduate students in my philosophy of economics seminar got into a running argument after class about the centipede game. In this two-player game, a shared pot grows each round that both players keep passing, and at any turn the player to move can instead take, ending the game and claiming the larger immediate share; both players would do strictly better if play continued all the way to the last round than either does by taking early, yet standard backward induction reasoning says the first mover should take immediately anyway. Here, roughly, is how the disagreement unfolded.

Marcus (1): Backward induction settles it. At the last node, the mover strictly prefers to take rather than pass, since passing there buys them nothing. Anticipating that, the second-to-last mover should take rather than pass into a node where they'll just be exploited, and unwinding that logic all the way back, the uniquely rational move is to take on move one. Subjects who keep passing for several rounds in the lab aren't refuting the theory -- they're just being irrational.

Elena (2): That argument undercuts itself. The case for taking at move 6 assumes it's still common knowledge that both of you are rational from here on. But if you really were rational, and this were common knowledge, play would never have gotten past move one -- so merely reaching move 6 already falsifies the very premise the argument needs in order to apply at move 6.

Marcus (3): You're confusing a track record with a disposition. Being rational just means you'd take at whatever node you found yourself at -- it doesn't require that the node was reached without any deviation. Aumann's proof treats off-path nodes as probability-zero events precisely so they needn't disturb anyone's belief in the other's continued rationality; the counterfactual can be common knowledge even though, on the equilibrium path, it's never triggered.

Elena (4): But that's the problem, not the solution. If reaching move 6 truly has probability zero under common knowledge of rationality, Bayes' rule gives no instruction at all about what to conclude once it happens anyway. Choosing to read your opponent's deviation as a harmless "tremble," rather than as evidence that they're reasoning differently than you assumed, is an extra modeling decision -- not something common knowledge of rationality hands you for free. That's exactly what makes backward induction here question-begging rather than proven.

I wasn't able to settle this myself. Could you assess moves (1) through (4) individually -- saying who has the better of it at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner -- and then say whether you think both parties are missing something important about backward induction's status as a requirement of rationality?
