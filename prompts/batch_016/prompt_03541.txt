I spent nearly forty years as an exploration geophysicist, and in retirement I have been working through introductory epistemology, where I have run into a puzzle drawn from my own field that I cannot quite resolve. Early in my career I recorded a small, unexplained gravity anomaly during a regional survey and, lacking any explanation for it at the time, set the reading aside as an oddity. A former colleague has recently proposed a model of a buried salt structure in that same region, and her model predicts an anomaly of precisely the magnitude and shape I measured decades ago. She now describes my old survey reading as one of the strongest pieces of evidence for her model, which strikes me as odd: I was already entirely confident in the accuracy of that reading long before her model existed, so consulting my own decades-old field notes again hardly feels like learning something new. Yet it also seems wrong to say the reading has no bearing on her theory, since a differently shaped anomaly would surely have counted against it. I cannot work out how the very same numbers can be, from where I sit, both old and unsurprising, and at the same time genuine evidence for a hypothesis that did not exist when I recorded them. Was my colleague right to lean on my old measurement the way she did, and if so, what exactly is happening when a long-settled observation starts supporting a theory proposed only afterward?
