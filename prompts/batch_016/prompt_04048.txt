Since retiring from a career studying the population dynamics of wild animals, I have been reading my way into philosophy, currently the interpretation of probability. I ran into a passage in John Venn's The Logic of Chance (3rd edn., Macmillan, 1888; 1st edn. 1866) that I would like help thinking through, rather than simply paraphrased.

A little context: Venn has been asking how we decide which statistical "series," or class, to refer an individual to when assigning him a probability. His example is one "John Smith," who might be classed by his age, his nationality, his health, and so on, each classification carrying its own statistics. He calls it a "mild" difficulty when the relevant classes nest neatly one inside another, since we can then simply prefer the narrowest class for which the statistics are still stable. He then turns to a more "aggravated" version of the same difficulty:

22. So much for what may be called the mild form in which the ambiguity occurs; but there is an aggravated form in which it may show itself, and which at first sight seems to place us in far greater perplexity. Suppose that the different classes mentioned above are not included successively one within the other. We may then be quite at a loss which of the statistical tables to employ. Let us assume, for example, that nine out of ten Englishmen are injured by residence in Madeira, but that nine out of ten consumptive persons are benefited by such a residence. These statistics, though fanciful, are conceivable and perfectly compatible. John Smith is a consumptive Englishman; are we to recommend a visit to Madeira in his case or not? In other words, what inferences are we to draw about the probability of his death? Both of the statistical tables apply to his case, but they would lead us to directly contradictory conclusions. This does not mean, of course, contradictory precisely in the logical sense of that word, for one of these propositions does not assert that an event must happen and the other deny that it must; but contradictory in the sense that one would cause us in some considerable degree to believe what the other would cause us in some considerable degree to disbelieve. This refers, of course, to the individual events; the statistics are by supposition in no degree contradictory. Without further data, therefore, we can come to no decision.

23. Practically, of course, if we were forced to a decision with only these data before us, we should make our choice by the consideration that the state of a man's lungs has probably more to do with his health than the place of his birth has; that is, we should conclude that the duration of life of consumptive Englishmen corresponds much more closely with that of consumptive persons in general than with that of their healthy countrymen. But this is, of course, to import empirical considerations into the question. The data, as they are given to us, and if we confine ourselves to them, leave us in absolute uncertainty upon the point. [...] The problem is, therefore, left absolutely indeterminate, for we cannot here appeal to any general rule so simple and so obviously applicable as that which, in a former case, recommended us always to prefer the more special statistics, when sufficiently extensive, to those which are wider and more general. We have no means here of knowing whether one set is more special than the other.

Venn immediately adds that an insurance office would feel none of this perplexity: it could act on either statistic and, spread across enough clients, "all will come right" for the office in the long run.

I'd value your help with three things. First, state precisely what breaks down in the "prefer the narrower class" rule once nested classes give way to merely overlapping ones like "Englishman" and "consumptive" -- why does overlap, rather than nesting, make the choice genuinely undecidable on Venn's own terms, and not just harder? Second, does the appeal to the insurance office really dissolve the difficulty for the single case -- John Smith's own chance of benefiting from the move to Madeira -- or does it quietly substitute a different, aggregate-level question that the office can answer while leaving the original one just as indeterminate as before? Third, has anyone since Venn -- frequentist, propensity theorist, or Bayesian -- found a non-arbitrary way to pick a reference class, or do modern treatments of what's now called the reference class problem essentially concede his point and manage it rather than solve it?

I ask partly because Venn's "aggravated" case is uncomfortably close to something that used to bother me in my own work: a tagged animal typically belongs to several cohorts at once -- sex, age class, breeding status, local population -- each with its own survival curve, and nothing in the data itself says which curve should govern our expectation for that one animal.
