I'm a PhD student in political theory working on a dissertation chapter about nineteenth- and early twentieth-century theories of "the public" as a distinctively modern, and distinctly puzzling, kind of social entity — the sort of thing Tarde treats as categorically different from a crowd, and that Lippmann, writing in the 1920s, would independently describe as a "phantom" too (a verbal coincidence that's part of why I've ended up pairing the two in this chapter). Kierkegaard is my earliest case study: the material comes from the closing section of his 1846 literary review Two Ages, which Alexander Dru excerpted and translated on its own under the title The Present Age. I've worked through this section closely several times now, but there's one short stretch — right after Kierkegaard introduces the image of the public keeping a dog for its amusement (a stand-in for the sensationalist press of his day, clearly modeled on The Corsair, the paper that had just lampooned him) — that I still can't parse logically, no matter how often I return to it.

Here is the passage, in Dru's translation:

"The dog goes for him, snapping and tearing at his coat-tails, allowing itself every possible ill-mannered familiarity — until the public tires, and says it may stop. That is an example of how the public levels. Their betters and superiors in strength are mishandled — and the dog remains a dog which even the public despises. The levelling is therefore done by a third party; a nonexistent public levelling with the help of a third party which in its insignificance is less than nothing, being already more than levelled. And so the public is unrepentant, for it was after all not the public that acted but the dog; just as one says to children — the cat's mother did it."

What trips me up is "the third party." On one reading it just renames the dog, restated for emphasis. But the clause "a nonexistent public levelling with the help of a third party" makes it sound as though the third party is a further, distinct link in the chain — neither the public itself nor quite the dog either. I'm even more stuck on the pairing of "less than nothing" with "already more than levelled": these read as though they should pull against each other, yet Kierkegaard clearly means them to cooperate in explaining why responsibility never lands anywhere. Could you help me reconstruct the logical structure of this passage step by step — who or what occupies each role (the public, the dog, the third party, the victim) — and spell out how exactly the "less than nothing / already more than levelled" formulation does the actual work of dissolving accountability, rather than just gesturing at the idea that nobody is to blame?
