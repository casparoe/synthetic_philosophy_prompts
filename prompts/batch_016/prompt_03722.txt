I've been falling down a rabbit hole researching Otto Neurath for a piece on the history of infographics (my editor keeps asking when it's due, but I keep getting distracted by tangents like this one), and there's a question I can't quite work out myself. Neurath was oddly strict about what his Isotype picture-language from the 1920s-30s was and wasn't allowed to do. To show "twice as many" of something you had to repeat the same little icon side by side, never just draw one icon bigger, because he thought resizing distorted people's sense of scale (he'd hate basically every bar-chart-with-oversized-icons infographic you see online now). He was also adamant that picture-language was a "helper language" running alongside words, not a replacement for them -- his own line was "words divide, pictures unite" -- and that pictures were only suited to plain, standardized, factual or statistical content, not anything ambiguous, ironic, or emotional. Given he was a founding member of the Vienna Circle and cared a lot about unambiguous, verifiable communication generally, that all seems to hang together.

So naturally I got stuck thinking about emoji. On one hand they're the closest thing we've got to Neurath's dream of a standardized picture-language anyone on earth can read regardless of what they speak. On the other hand they're constantly doing exactly the stuff he thought pictures couldn't or shouldn't handle -- sarcasm, hedging, entire emotional registers, a wink that flips the meaning of the sentence before it -- and they're not even that standardized, theres a whole study from a few years back showing the same emoji rendered differently enough across iPhone, Android and Windows that people read totally different emotions into it depending on the platform. So which way do you think Neurath would actually jump here: would emoji look to him like proof his project basically won, or like a cautionary tale about why pictures can't stay that "pure" once ordinary people get their hands on them? I'd love an answer that really wrestles with his specific reasoning about what pictures can and can't do, rather than just a vague "here's what some 1930s intellectual would think of texting" take.
