I'm reporting on why panels of equally credentialed experts—forecasters, clinicians, economists—so rarely converge even once each side knows exactly where and why the other disagrees, and I'd like to open the piece with a short philosophical dialogue that dramatizes the sharpest problem underlying that debate.

Write a dialogue, set backstage after a conference session on the epistemology of disagreement, between a conciliationist (who holds that discovering a disagreement with someone she regards as an epistemic peer should move her credence substantially toward his) and a steadfaster (who denies this). Give both the strongest version of their position — neither should be a strawman — and skip the standard restaurant-bill-splitting illustrations that appear in every introduction to the topic. Instead, have the two realize, partway through, that they are themselves epistemic peers on the very question of how to respond to peer disagreement, and have each apply their own theory to that fact in real time. The steadfaster should press the charge that conciliationism is self-undermining precisely in a case like this one, and the conciliationist needs to do more than simply bite the bullet. Neither should just restate an opening position — each move should respond to what the other has actually just said — and the dialogue should close with either a genuine resolution or a precisely diagnosed impasse, rather than trailing off inconclusively.
