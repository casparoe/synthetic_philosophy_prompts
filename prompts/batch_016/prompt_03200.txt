I'm a philosophy student taking a graduate course on mathematical logic and the philosophy of mathematics this term, and we recently finished a unit on the Löwenheim–Skolem theorems, which led into a discussion of Skolem's paradox. In class, our professor presented what I take to be the standard resolution: because a countable model M of ZFC is only countable "from the outside," the bijection that would witness some internally "uncountable" set actually being countable simply isn't an element of M itself, so there's no genuine contradiction — just a gap between which bijections the model has access to and which ones exist in the wider universe of sets. That resolution makes sense to me mechanically, but afterward I read through Skolem's original 1922 paper out of curiosity, and he clearly didn't think the matter ended there. He used the result to argue for what he called a relativity of set-theoretic notions, which he treated as a genuine problem for treating axiomatic set theory as a foundation for mathematics.

What I'd like help with is understanding what Skolem's complaint actually amounts to, beyond the internal/external bijection point. In particular, I want to understand why it's supposed to be more interesting than the comparatively boring fact that specific objects simply get relabeled across different models (some object other than "the empty set" in one model might play that role in another) — since, as I understand it, plenty of cardinality notions, like being empty or having exactly seventeen members, come out the same in every model of the axioms, so "uncountable" is apparently the first place this invariance breaks down. Why should that particular breakdown carry the philosophical weight that Skolem — and later, in a different way, Putnam, in his argument against there being any single "intended" model of set theory — wanted to put on it, rather than being just one more curious fact of first-order model theory? If you can also say a little about why Putnam's extension of the idea has struck many commentators as overreaching, that would be helpful, though the relativity question itself is my main interest.
