A few weeks ago my team ran a postmortem after a fairly bad production outage, and the final write-up named the root cause as "the on-call engineer's failure to roll back the bad deploy within the fifteen-minute SLA window." That sentence stuck with me, because a great many other people also failed to roll back that deploy within fifteen minutes: I did, our director did, and so did an engineer on a completely unrelated team who had never even heard of the service in question. None of us would ever be listed as a cause of the outage, yet strictly speaking our inaction satisfied exactly the same counterfactual test as the on-call engineer's — if any of us had acted, the outage would have been shorter. That asymmetry bothered me enough that I looked into it, and it turns out I'd stumbled onto a genuine, unresolved dispute in metaphysics about whether absences, omissions, and failures to act can be causes at all, rather than just a question of how engineering orgs like to assign blame.

As far as I can tell, the standard counterfactual theory of causation, most associated with David Lewis, is committed to saying that omissions can indeed be genuine causes, since the counterfactual test doesn't distinguish between an absent action and a present one; Lewis apparently even wrote a late paper called "Void and Object" extending his account to exactly this kind of case, effectively accepting that it multiplies causes well beyond what common sense would pick out (on this view my director's failure to roll back is just as much a "cause" as the on-call engineer's, even though nobody would say so out loud). Against this are philosophers who hold that genuine causation requires some physical process or transfer connecting cause and effect — Phil Dowe's work seems to be the reference point here — and who conclude that omissions are never causes in the strict sense, only what Dowe calls "quasi-causes," close enough to the real thing to fool our intuitions without actually being it. And then there's apparently a third camp (Sarah McGrath's name came up) that tries to rescue ordinary talk by making causation-by-omission relative to a norm or statistical default, so that an absence counts as a cause only when it departs from how things were supposed to go — which would explain why we pin the outage on the on-call engineer, who had a defined duty to act, and not on the rest of us, who didn't.

I'd like you to write me a properly argued essay on this question: are omissions ever causes in the full, literal sense, or is a sentence like "the missing rollback caused the outage" always, underneath the surface grammar, doing something other than describing genuine causation — expressing a normative judgment, assigning responsibility, or just speaking loosely for convenience? Please engage seriously with the profligacy problem I ran into above (treating omissions as causes seems to force you to count vast numbers of irrelevant non-events as causes too) and show how each side actually tries to handle it, rather than just surveying the positions. Above all, don't leave me with a shrug at the end: come down on one side, or on a precise qualified position — something like "omissions are causes, but only relative to a contextually fixed normative baseline, and here is why that's principled rather than a cop-out" would count as a real verdict — and defend it against the strongest objections you raised along the way. If you have room, I'd also be curious whether your answer should make us more suspicious of naming any single "root cause" in a postmortem at all, but that's secondary to the main metaphysical question.
