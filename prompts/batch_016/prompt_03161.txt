Ok, this has been bugging me for weeks and I cant tell if I'm onto something or just confused. I reread Inadequate Equilibria over the break -- the whole "modest epistemology" vs "inadequacy analysis" thing, where Yudkowsky argues that sometimes you should trust your own inside-view reasoning over expert or crowd consensus, even once you already know the general base rate that people who think "I'm the exception" are usually wrong. Around the same time a friend sent me Christensen's paper on epistemic akrasia (the one arguing it isn't automatically irrational), which defines the akratic combination as roughly: believing P while also believing, given your total evidence, that P isn't the rational thing to believe.

Once I had both of those in my head at the same time I couldn't unsee the overlap. If you already know the statistical fact that people in your reference class who override expert consensus are usually wrong, and you keep believing your object-level view anyway "because this time I've got a good reason," isn't that just conceding that your evidence supports the outside view while continuing to believe the inside view -- textbook epistemic akrasia, just dressed up as principled disagreement? I brought this up with a friend from my rationalist meetup and she insisted there's a real distinction between updating on higher-order evidence about your own reliability and just plain disagreeing with a heuristic, but when I pushed on where exactly the line is supposed to be, she couldnt quite say, and honestly neither could I. Is there an actual principled distinction here, or does Yudkowsky's whole "sometimes trust the inside view" move just collapse into a fancy license for akrasia?
