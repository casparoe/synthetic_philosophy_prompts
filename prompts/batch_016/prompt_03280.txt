I write backend infrastructure for a living, but in my spare time I've gotten pretty into prediction markets (Polymarket, Kalshi, and so on), mostly as a way of aggregating dispersed information. Some reading on the theory side has left me unsettled, though. Milgrom and Stokey's 1982 no-trade theorem, building on Aumann's earlier result that two Bayesians with the same prior can't knowingly agree to disagree, seems to show something quite strong: if two rational people share a common prior and it becomes common knowledge that each is willing to take one side of a purely speculative bet, that mutual willingness is itself evidence they should update on, and the perceived gain from trading should unravel before any money changes hands. Traders sometimes call this the "Groucho Marx problem" — you shouldn't want to bet against anyone willing to bet against you. Yet obviously enormous volumes of purely speculative trading happen every day, on markets whose prices we otherwise trust precisely because trading is supposed to pull them toward the truth.

Write a dialogue between Alice and Bob, two equally sharp traders staring at a live order book moments before executing a large speculative bet against each other. Bob has just remembered the no-trade theorem and hesitates, arguing that Alice's continued willingness to take the other side should already have talked him out of it; Alice insists the trade is still fully rational. Have them genuinely engage with each other's strongest moves — on whether their priors are really "common," what common knowledge of willingness-to-trade would actually require, and whether hedging or liquidity motives change the picture — and let the dialogue arrive at some real verdict on whether the trade, and the epistemic trust we place in market prices generally, can survive the logic Bob has raised.
