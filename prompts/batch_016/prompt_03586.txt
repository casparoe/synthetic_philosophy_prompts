For my high school elective on world philosophy, we're each writing a short reflection paper on a non-Western philosophical text, and I picked a passage from the Zhuangzi that our teacher didn't actually cover in class. I found it while looking through James Legge's 1891 translation because I wanted to work with something other than the wife's-funeral story that shows up in basically every summary of Zhuangzi on death. The passage is from Book VI of the Inner Chapters, "The Great and Most Honoured Master." I understand the general Daoist background from our textbook, but I'm stuck on the specifics of this passage and would really appreciate help working through it:

> Dze-sze, Dze-yü, Dze-lî, and Dze-lâi, these four men, were talking together, when some one said, "Who can suppose the head to be made from nothing, the spine from life, and the rump-bone from death? Who knows how death and birth, living on and disappearing, compose the one body?—I would be friends with him." The four men looked at one another and laughed, but no one seized with his mind the drift of the questions. All, however, were friends together.
>
> Not long after Dze-yü fell ill, and Dze-sze went to inquire for him. "How great," said the sufferer, "is the Creator! That He should have made me the deformed object that I am!" He was a crooked hunchback; his five viscera were squeezed into the upper part of his body; his chin bent over his navel; his shoulder was higher than his crown; on his crown was an ulcer pointing to the sky; his breath came and went in gasps:—yet he was easy in his mind, and made no trouble of his condition. He limped to a well, looked at himself in it, and said, "Alas that the Creator should have made me the deformed object that I am!" Dze said, "Do you dislike your condition?" He replied, "No, why should I dislike it? If He were to transform my left arm into a cock, I should be watching with it the time of the night; if He were to transform my right arm into a cross-bow, I should then be looking for a hsiâo to bring down and roast; if He were to transform my rump-bone into a wheel, and my spirit into a horse, I should then be mounting it, and would not change it for another steed... This would be what the ancients called loosing the cord by which the life is suspended. But one hung up cannot loose himself;—he is held fast by his bonds."
>
> Before long Dze-lâi fell ill, and lay gasping at the point of death, while his wife and children stood around him wailing. Dze-lî went to ask for him, and said to them, "Hush! Get out of the way! Do not disturb him as he is passing through his change." Then, leaning against the door, he said to the dying man, "Great indeed is the Creator! What will He now make you to become? Where will He take you to? Will He make you the liver of a rat, or the arm of an insect?"
>
> Dze-lâi replied, "Wherever a parent tells a son to go, east, west, south, or north, he simply follows the command. The Yin and Yang are more to a man than his parents are. If they are hastening my death, and I do not quietly submit to them, I shall be obstinate and rebellious... Here now is a great founder, casting his metal. If the metal were to leap up in the pot, and say, 'I must be made into a sword like the Mo-yeh,' the great founder would be sure to regard it as uncanny. So, again, when a form is being fashioned in the mould of the womb, if it were to say, 'I must become a man; I must become a man,' the Creator would be sure to regard it as uncanny. When we once understand that heaven and earth are a great melting-pot, and the Creator a great founder, where can we have to go to that shall not be right for us? We are born as from a quiet sleep, and we die to a calm awaking."

A few things I can't figure out:

1. Legge keeps calling whatever is responsible for these transformations "the Creator," capitalized, and has Ziyu and Zilai talk about what "He" will do with them next. Does that make Zhuangzi sound like he's positing a personal being with a plan for each of us, in a way that doesn't actually fit Daoist metaphysics — or is that a defensible way to translate the underlying idea?
2. Ziyu doesn't just say he accepts his fate in the abstract — he imagines quite specific transformations (his arm as a crossbow, his rump-bone and spirit as a wheel and horse). Is there an actual argument about personal identity buried in these images, or are they just vivid illustrations of an attitude, with no real claim about whether "he" would survive as those things?
3. The passage gives two images for death — "loosing the cord" and dying as "a calm awaking" — that both suggest death is a release rather than a loss. Is Zhuangzi making a metaphysical claim that death isn't actually bad for the person who dies, or just recommending an attitude to take regardless of whether it's bad? How would I even tell those two readings apart using only this text?
