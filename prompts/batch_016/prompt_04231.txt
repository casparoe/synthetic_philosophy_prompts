I recently retired after a career as an experimental physicist working mainly on acoustics and vibration in layered materials, and in retirement I have finally had time to read the philosophy I always meant to get to. A handful of former colleagues and I have formed an informal reading circle, and we are currently working through Schopenhauer's *The World as Will and Representation* in the somewhat creaky old Haldane and Kemp translation, which renders the title as *The World as Will and Idea*. Everyone else in the group is delighted by the famous excursus on music in the Third Book, §52, but I confess I am stuck on it in a way I suspect only someone with a physics background would be, because at a crucial point Schopenhauer reaches for the physical facts of acoustics to underwrite a metaphysical claim, and I cannot tell whether that reach is supposed to be doing real argumentative work or is just illustrative colour.

Just before the passage that troubles me, Schopenhauer has insisted that music is unlike every other art: it does not copy the Platonic Ideas at all, but is a direct copy of the Will itself, and for that reason "passes over the Ideas" and could, in some sense, exist even "if there was no world at all." Then he writes:

"I recognise in the deepest tones of harmony, in the bass, the lowest grades of the objectification of will, unorganised nature, the mass of the planet. It is well known that all the high notes which are easily sounded, and die away more quickly, are produced by the vibration in their vicinity of the deep bass-notes... and it is a law of harmony that only those high notes may accompany a bass-note which actually already sound along with it of themselves (its *sons harmoniques*) on account of its vibration. This is analogous to the fact that the whole of the bodies and organisations of nature must be regarded as having come into existence through gradual development out of the mass of the planet... Bass is thus, for us, in harmony what unorganised nature, the crudest mass, upon which all rests, and from which everything originates and develops, is in the world. Now, further, in the whole of the complemental parts which make up the harmony between the bass and the leading voice singing the melody, I recognise the whole gradation of the Ideas in which the will objectifies itself. Those nearer to the bass are the lower of these grades, the still unorganised, but yet manifold phenomenal things; the higher represent to me the world of plants and beasts... Lastly, in the melody, in the high, singing, principal voice leading the whole and progressing with unrestrained freedom, in the unbroken significant connection of one thought from beginning to end representing a whole, I recognise the highest grade of the objectification of will, the intellectual life and effort of man."

Two things leave me unsure how charitably to read him. First, having just told us that music bypasses the Ideas entirely, he now describes that same music as containing "the whole gradation of the Ideas" – which sounds like precisely the mediated relationship he had ruled out a page earlier. Is there a way of taking "gradation of the Ideas" here that is consistent with the earlier claim, perhaps as a mere structural parallel rather than any real dependence, or is this simply a point where the exposition slips against his own official doctrine? Second, as someone who has spent a career around resonance and overtone phenomena, I am wary of how much weight he puts on the physical fact that upper partials sound faintly along with a fundamental in order to justify treating high voices as "higher" grades of nature and the bass as "lower" – this looks like it trades on a spatial pun between pitch-height and rank in a hierarchy of being that the acoustics itself does nothing to support. Am I right to be suspicious here, or is there a more charitable way of understanding what the overtone series is actually supposed to be contributing to the analogy, beyond a suggestive coincidence of vocabulary? I would be grateful for a close reading that takes both worries seriously rather than one that simply restates that Schopenhauer thought music was metaphysically special.
