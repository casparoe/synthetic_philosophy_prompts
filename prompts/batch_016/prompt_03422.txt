During one of the bad COVID surges a couple of years ago, I ended up as one of two non-medical community members on our regional hospital network's ethics advisory panel, which had been put together to help finalize a policy for what to do if we ever ran short of ventilators. I have no background in medicine or philosophy at all — I run a small landscaping business, and a neighbor who knew I'd been active in our town's emergency-preparedness group put my name forward — so for the first few meetings I mostly just listened. But one part of the discussion has stayed with me. The policy that was eventually adopted said, in effect, that if you ever have to choose between giving your remaining ventilators to one patient or using the same resources to save several other patients who would otherwise die, you save the larger group, no exceptions. Everyone on the panel treated this as obvious, and at the time I went along with it, but it has bothered me ever since. It seems to say that the one patient's claim on our help simply stops counting for anything the moment there happen to be more people needing help elsewhere in the building — as though five separate tragedies just stack up into one bigger tragedy that automatically outweighs the smaller one, rather than each of those deaths being its own complete loss to that one person, exactly as total for the lone patient as for any single member of the group of five.

What really threw me was a side conversation with the panel's bioethicist, who mentioned that some ethicists have proposed a weighted lottery instead of a flat rule — giving the larger group a proportionally better chance at the ventilators, say five chances in six, rather than saving them automatically, on the idea that a straight coin flip ignores the numbers entirely while an automatic rule ignores the individual entirely. She said this compromise has never really satisfied anyone in the debate, on either side. I can't tell whether that's because it's a genuine third option that both the "always save more" rule and a plain coin flip are missing, or whether it's just a way of splitting the difference that avoids answering the actual question rather than resolving it. I keep going back and forth on it myself and would genuinely like to know what you make of it: is there a real argument for why the sheer number of people on one side of a rescue shouldn't just settle the matter on its own, or is my discomfort with the rule really just a failure to accept that, in a tragic situation, saving more lives is simply the better outcome?
