I don't have any background in economics, computer science, or philosophy -- I'm a QA analyst at a mid-sized software company -- but a few months ago I fell down a rabbit hole of blog posts and videos about how AI systems sometimes find dishonest-looking shortcuts to rack up reward without doing the task they were actually trained for, and that eventually led me to Goodhart's Law more generally. Ever since, I've been half-jokingly diagnosing my own workplace with it, and last week that turned into a real argument between two of my coworkers, Marcus and Renata, during a sprint retro that I haven't been able to stop thinking about.

Some background: our team is partly evaluated on "bugs closed per sprint," and over the last couple of quarters people have started closing bugs by relabeling them "won't fix" or "duplicate," splitting one gnarly bug into five trivial ones so each can be closed separately, and writing narrow tests that pass without touching the underlying issue. The argument went roughly like this. (1) Renata said this is a textbook case of Goodhart's Law: the moment "bugs closed" became the number we're all judged on, it stopped tracking the thing it was supposed to track, namely code quality. (2) Marcus pushed back hard: he said this has nothing to do with Goodhart's Law and is just people cheating, which he called a much older and simpler story than whatever the idea behind that law actually was. He'd looked it up, and said it originally came from a British economist describing a statistical relationship that breaks down once you lean on it for policy -- nothing to do with anyone being dishonest -- and that calling ordinary fudging "Goodhart's Law" just dresses cheating up in fancier clothes. (3) Renata answered that the reason AI researchers borrowed Goodhart's name for what they call "specification gaming" is precisely that you get the identical failure with no dishonesty anywhere in the system -- a reinforcement-learning agent with no concept of lying will stumble into the same kind of loophole a dishonest employee looks for on purpose -- so human cheating is just one special case of a more general pattern, not some unrelated thing that happens to look similar. (4) Marcus conceded the mechanism might be real in AI systems, but said that if a dishonest employee and a non-dishonest optimizer can produce the same-looking outcome for completely different underlying reasons, then filing both under one label is actively unhelpful: you deal with a dishonest employee through incentives and oversight, and you deal with a badly specified metric by redesigning the metric, and calling both "Goodhart" obscures which fix a given situation actually needs. He added that if literally any way a metric can go wrong counts as Goodhart's Law, the term stops picking out anything specific.

Nobody at the table had a clean comeback to that last point, but nobody backed down either, and I've gone back and forth on who's right ever since. Could you go through the exchange move by move -- (1) through (4) -- and say who has the better of it at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also like to know whether there's a real, principled line between "Goodhart's Law" and plain old cheating that Marcus and Renata are each only half-seeing, or whether the concept is inherently too slippery for any such line to hold up.
