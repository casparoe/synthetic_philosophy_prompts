Last semester I took a graduate seminar on the epistemic foundations of game theory, and one week the instructor had us actually play the centipede game for money before we got to the theory behind it. Pairs faced a pot that started at two dollars and doubled each round; at their turn, a player could stop the game and take seventy-five percent of the current pot, leaving the rest for their partner, or pass and let the pot grow, with anything remaining after six rounds simply split evenly. Almost nobody defected on the very first move, and most pairs let the pot grow for several rounds before someone cashed out, which the instructor said matches how these experiments typically go. Afterward, two classmates of mine, Marcus and Elena, got into a long argument over what the exercise actually showed, and I've been turning it over ever since without reaching a verdict of my own.

Marcus's opening claim was that the exercise was behaviorally interesting but normatively irrelevant: if it's common knowledge that both players are rational, the player moving at the last real decision node will always defect, so the mover just before them should defect too rather than hand over a better position only to lose more the round after, and this unravels all the way back to move one — a genuinely rational first mover, on his view, defects immediately. Elena objected that the argument quietly undercuts itself: to run it, you need a view about what a rational opponent would do at, say, round four, but round four is a node that, on the very premises generating Marcus's conclusion, a rational pair should never reach; if you actually get there and your partner has passed three times, that's evidence against the assumption that was supposed to be common knowledge between you, and Marcus hadn't explained why he was entitled to keep assuming rationality resumes on schedule for the rest of the game rather than concluding that the common-knowledge assumption had simply broken down. Marcus replied that this conflates two separate questions: a fully specified strategy has to say what you'd do at every node, including ones never reached in equilibrium, and it's a perfectly coherent modeling choice to hold fixed that your partner will play out the remainder of the game exactly as backward induction dictates, whatever turns out to explain their earlier passing. Elena's comeback was that this "coherent modeling choice" needs just as much defense as the alternative: if a single deviation is enough to make Marcus doubt that Elena is the fully rational agent his argument required, he owed her some reason why that doubt should stay narrowly confined to predicting her future moves rather than also spreading to his own confidence that he was still required to defect, given that the whole prediction rested on common knowledge running all the way down and not just on her end of the table. Marcus's closing point was that Elena's picture proves too much: on her view, essentially no off-path move could ever count as a mistake, since any deviation could always be redescribed as new information overturning whichever theory had labeled it a mistake in the first place, and a notion of rationality flexible enough to absorb any evidence in that way isn't so much refuted as drained of content.

Could you go through their five moves in order and, for each one, say who has the better of it and why, rather than just awarding an overall winner? I'd also value your view on whether the underlying disagreement points to a genuinely open problem with treating backward induction as binding on rational agents in a game like this one, or whether one of them has already defused the other's objection without being credited for it.
