I'm a retired physical chemist -- spent about 35 years doing corrosion and failure analysis work before I hung up the lab coat -- and a few months ago I started sitting in on a philosophy discussion group at the library, mostly just to keep my brain from turning to mush in retirement (it's been alot of fun, actually). A couple weeks back the session was on dispositions -- the "conditional analysis" business, Martin's finkish wires, and then the masking/antidote stuff from Johnston and Bird. Since corrosion was basically my whole career I couldn't help chiming in with galvanized nails as a test case, and it kicked off an argument between two of the other regulars, Warren and Diane, that never really got resolved and has been nagging at me since.

Warren's take: a galvanized nail is a textbook mask, not a fink. The bare steel's disposition to rust is just a fact about the iron -- it oxidizes in the presence of water and oxygen, period -- and that hasn't changed one bit once you dip it in zinc. What changes is that the zinc gets in the way and reacts first, so the rusting reaction never actually gets triggered. He compared it to bubble-wrapping a vase: the fragility is still in there, you've just cut off the road from stimulus to manifestation. Diane pushed back that this leaves out the electrochemistry: the zinc isn't just a passive barrier, it also makes the iron the cathode of a little galvanic cell, so that even at a scratch where bare steel is sitting right there in the water, that steel still won't rust, because electrons are being fed into it from the zinc the whole time. That's not blocking a pathway after the fact, she said, that's altering an actual causal property of the iron right at the moment the "stimulus" (water plus oxygen) shows up -- which sounds a lot more like a fink to her than a mask. Warren's comeback was that by that logic almost any working antidote would count as finkish, since Bird's own poison-and-antidote case, his go-to example of masking, works by the antidote getting into your bloodstream and changing your chemistry too -- so if "alters some causal parameter" is enough to make something a fink, the fink/mask distinction basically evaporates. Diane's reply was that timing, not just whether some parameter changes, is what should matter: an antidote intervenes downstream, after the poison's already on board, whereas cathodic protection changes the iron's reactivity right at, or even before, the moment oxygen and water arrive, which is a lot closer to Martin's original wire case, where the disposition itself flips exactly at the moment of stimulus. Neither of them gave any ground, and we ran out of time before anyone raised whether "the moment of stimulus" even makes sense for a slow, continuous process like corrosion the way it clearly does for touching a wire or swallowing a pill.

Could you go through this move by move and say who's got the better argument at each stage, rather then just declaring an overall winner? I'd also be curious whether you think the nail case actually exposes something broken in the fink/mask distinction itself, or whether there's a principled way to sort it that both Warren and Diane are missing.
