so this has been bugging me since it came up in an ACX open thread a few weeks back, and I want someone to actually steelman it instead of doing the thing everyone (including past-me) does, which is wave it away in one sentence. You know the Kripke/Harman dogmatism paradox: if I know that h is true, then I already know that any future evidence against h would have to be misleading (since evidence against something true is, by definition, misleading), so it looks like I'm licensed to write off that evidence in advance, before I've even seen it. The standard move whenever this comes up in rationalist circles is "sure, but that's only a problem for weird idealized agents who assign probability 1 to things -- real Bayesians follow Cromwell's rule, never fully certain, so there's always room left to update." I found that satisfying for years.

Here's what's been nagging at me though: conservation of expected evidence doesn't say anything about how your credence has to move on any particular piece of evidence, it only constrains the average over everything you might see. So nothing stops a Bayesian sitting at, say, 99.5% credence in h from ALSO holding a correspondingly high credence that any given piece of apparent counter-evidence is measurement error, fraud, or noise rather than signal, and pre-committing to discount that whole reference class in advance, all without ever touching probability 1 or violating conservation of expected evidence anywhere. That looks to me like it recreates basically the entire structure of the paradox Kripke's pointing at, just wearing a Bayesian costume -- and "well technically he's at 99.5%, not 100%" feels like a distinction that isn't earning its keep. It's also uncomfortably close to how actually dogmatic people (cranks, but honestly also just very confident people generally) seem to reason about disconfirming evidence.

Can you build the strongest possible case that this is a real problem rather than me just being confused -- i.e. argue that something like the dogmatism paradox survives, more or less intact, the move from certainty to high-but-sub-1 credence, and that "just don't assign probability 1" is a much weaker fix then people (me, two weeks ago) give it credit for? Please actually commit to the thesis rather than hedging into a survey of considerations on both sides -- I can generate the wishy-washy version myself, that's not what I'm looking for here.
