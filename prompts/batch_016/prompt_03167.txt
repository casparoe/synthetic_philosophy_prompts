I keep seeing 'spandrel' invoked in the rationalist blogs and forums I read as a rebuttal to overconfident evolutionary-psychology just-so stories, but tracing it back to Gould and Lewontin's 1979 paper complicated things for me. Gould later acknowledged that their flagship example -- the triangular spaces beneath San Marco's domes in Venice -- had drawn two lines of attack: a terminological one (they are technically pendentives, not spandrels) and a more damaging conceptual one, namely that architectural historians think the builders may have chosen the pendentive solution partly for the decorative surfaces it would create, making it an ordinary design choice rather than an unplanned byproduct. Separately, within three years of that paper, Gould and Vrba felt they needed an entirely new term, 'exaptation,' because 'spandrel' had blurred together two questions that needed to be kept apart.

Could you reconstruct what was being blurred? I want a precise account of the distinction between why a trait originated and what it is currently useful for -- precise enough to explain why present fittingness for a purpose can never, by itself, show that a trait was built by selection for that purpose rather than merely inherited and later put to use, and why even Gould's own textbook example stayed genuinely contestable on exactly this point.
