In my IB Biology HL class, we recently finished a unit on sexual selection, and my teacher used Zahavi's handicap principle to explain ornaments like the peacock's tail: because carrying around such a large, metabolically expensive tail makes a male slower and more visible to predators, only a male in truly excellent condition could afford to grow one without paying a fatal price, so peahens can trust the tail as an honest signal of quality that a weaker male could never fake convincingly. She also mentioned that economists and philosophers use very similar reasoning to explain why costly displays in general, like elaborate wedding ceremonies or wasteful advertising, can work as credible signals of hidden quality, which I found fascinating. Over the weekend I read the relevant chapter of Matt Ridley's The Red Queen for fun, and it left me stuck on something the book never really resolved for me.

If what makes a signal trustworthy is precisely that it is costly enough that a lower-quality individual could not afford to produce it, then it seems like this logic should always favor whichever version of a trait is more extreme and more costly, since a more extravagant tail would be even harder for a weak male to fake than a merely large one. So why doesn't this reasoning push traits like the peacock's tail to become larger and more burdensome every single generation, escalating indefinitely, rather than settling at roughly the size we actually observe? Relatedly, does the same logic imply that a comparatively cheap trait, like a small patch of distinctively colored feathers, could never function as a genuinely honest, evolutionarily stable signal, since a low-quality cheater could presumably always afford to grow one too? I would really appreciate help figuring out exactly where this reasoning goes wrong, because as it stands, the handicap principle seems to predict an unstoppable arms race rather than the kind of stable, moderate signals we actually observe in nature.
