I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years now—LessWrong, Overcoming Bias, and their various descendants—and Aumann's agreement theorem is a recurring reference point in that world, going back to the sort of thing Robin Hanson used to write about constantly. My understanding of the result is that if two Bayesian reasoners are honest with each other and fully share the evidence and reasoning behind their views, their posteriors are mathematically guaranteed to converge: genuinely "agreeing to disagree" isn't something two truth-seeking rational agents can coherently do once each knows where the other stands. This stopped being just an academic point for me last month: a friend of mine, who takes this material just as seriously as I do, and I spent an entire evening running a full "double crux" on whether a proposed rent-control ordinance would backfire, laying out our cruxes explicitly and updating openly on each other's points in real time. We still ended the night with meaningfully different credences on the object-level question.

Neither of us was withholding evidence or arguing in bad faith, and since this is supposed to be an actual theorem rather than a rule of thumb, it seems to follow that one of us must have been reasoning badly at some point during that conversation. Given that both of us were sincere and neither could spot an obvious lapse in the other's reasoning, how would you recommend we go about identifying which of us made the mistake, and roughly where in an exchange like ours that kind of error tends to creep in?
