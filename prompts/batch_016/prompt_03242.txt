I'm a software engineer at a fintech company (my background is mostly in distributed systems, not philosophy or ethics), and I recently got pulled into a fairness review of one of our lending-risk models after a regulator flagged disparities in approval rates across demographic groups. To get up to speed, I read through the old ProPublica investigation of the COMPAS recidivism tool, Northpointe's rebuttal, and a couple of the computer science papers that came out of that dispute (I believe one was by Chouldechova, another by Kleinberg and coauthors). What struck me is that these papers don't just take a side — they apparently prove, as a matter of mathematics, that whenever two groups have different base rates for the outcome being predicted, no model can be simultaneously "calibrated" (a given score corresponds to the same real-world probability of the outcome in every group) and have "balanced error rates" (equal false-positive and false-negative rates across groups), except in degenerate cases like a perfect predictor. So it looks like Northpointe's calibration defense and ProPublica's error-rate critique were both correct on their own terms, and also jointly impossible to satisfy, given that recidivism rates differed by race in the underlying data.

I relayed this to my team as a reason to stop agonizing so much over our own audit: if it's a mathematical certainty that no scoring model can satisfy every fairness criterion at once whenever base rates differ, doesn't that mean there's no principled, non-arbitrary fact of the matter about whether our model is "biased" in the first place — just a choice of which metric you'd already decided to care about, with any sufficiently motivated critic able to find some statistic that makes us look discriminatory no matter what we do? Our head of data science pushed back immediately but couldn't fully articulate why in the meeting, and I'd genuinely like to know whether my reasoning from the impossibility result to that conclusion actually holds up, or whether I'm sneaking in an unjustified step somewhere.
