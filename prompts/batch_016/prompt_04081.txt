I'm a novelist, and a scene from my work-in-progress set off a disagreement in my writers' group that I haven't been able to settle myself. In the scene, my protagonist Marcus -- who had an affair a decade ago but has been faithful ever since -- furiously confronts his brother for cheating on his wife. Two people in the group got into it afterward over whether the scene actually works. (1) Dana said the confrontation rings false, because Marcus has no standing to judge his brother for the very thing he himself once did. (2) Leo replied that standing is beside the point: infidelity is wrong no matter who says so, and the brother's affair isn't excused just because Marcus has a past. (3) Dana agreed that Marcus's history doesn't make infidelity any less wrong, but held that being right and being entitled to say so are different things -- a fraudster can correctly tell someone their tax return is dishonest and still have no business saying it; she added that Marcus's fury carries an unspoken "I would never," which is false, and that this false claim to be above the thing he did, rather than the moral point itself, is what makes hypocrisy a distinct offense. (4) Leo answered that this can't be the whole story, since it would mean nobody who has ever done wrong could criticize that wrong again -- standing forfeited for life. Marcus reformed years ago, he said; a decade of fidelity has to earn back at least the "I would never," or reform means nothing. So what, if anything, is left of Dana's objection?

Could you go through their exchange move by move, saying who has the stronger argument at each step, and note any distinction one of them needs but doesn't reach for? I'd rather have the philosophy right than have a side declared the winner, since I still have to decide whether to rewrite the scene.
