So I'm about a third of the way into a novel with a subplot set in Newton's rooms at Trinity in the 1690s - not the Principia years, the later, wierder stretch where he's mostly holed up doing biblical chronology and alchemy and trying not to get caught holding heretical views. I've been reading through his prophecy manuscripts for texture (the "Observations upon the Prophecies of Daniel, and the Apocalypse of St. John," which got compiled from decades of his notes), partly just to steal some good period dialogue, and I ran across this passage that stopped me in my tracks:

"For understanding the Prophecies, we are, in the first place, to acquaint our-selves with the figurative language of the Prophets. This language is taken from the analogy between the world natural, and an empire or kingdom considered as a world politic." Then he spends a paragraph establishing that in this scheme "heaven" maps onto thrones and rulers and "earth" onto the common people below, before getting concrete: "In the heavens, the Sun and Moon are, by interpreters of dreams, put for the persons of Kings and Queens; but in sacred Prophecy, which regards not single persons, the Sun is put for the whole species and race of Kings, in the kingdom or kingdoms of the world politic, shining with regal power and glory; the Moon for the body of the common people, considered as the King's wife; the Stars for subordinate Princes and great men, or for Bishops and Rulers of the people of God, when the Sun is Christ; light for the glory, truth, and knowledge, wherewith great and good men shine and illuminate others; darkness for obscurity of condition, and for error, blindness and ignorance; darkning, smiting, or setting of the Sun, Moon, and Stars, for the ceasing of a kingdom, or for the desolation thereof, proportional to the darkness; darkning the Sun, turning the Moon into blood, and falling of the Stars, for the same; new Moons, for the return of a dispersed people into a body politic or ecclesiastic."

He goes on like this for pages, basically laying out a whole dictionary of what every image in scripture supposedly really means, stated with the same flat confidence you'd expect from him stating a law of motion. What I can't quite get a handle on, for the sake of getting the character right, is what this is actually supposed to be, in his own mind. Is it just allegorical exegesis in a tradition he inherited from earlier commentators, dressed up with more system than usual? Or did he genuinely believe there was a fixed, quasi-lawlike code buried in scripture that could be pinned down with real rigor, so that a passage like this is basically his version of doing science on the Book of Revelation? Basically: is the guy writing this a seperate person, intellectually, from the guy who wrote the Principia, or is there some deeper continuity of method underneath both that a modern reader is likely to miss?
