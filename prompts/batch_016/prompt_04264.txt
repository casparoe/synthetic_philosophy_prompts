For the past four months, two senior engineers on my team have been unable to agree on the cause of an intermittent bug in our order-processing service: roughly once every few thousand transactions, a customer's cart is priced using stale data from an upstream pricing service. One engineer is convinced this is a race condition in how we invalidate a local cache under high concurrency. The other is equally convinced it's a network issue: brief, hard-to-detect partitions between our service and the pricing service that cause reads to silently fall back to a stale replica. What I find strange is that every attempt we've made to settle the question empirically has ended up compatible with both accounts. When we added fine-grained tracing to try to catch the bug directly, it stopped occurring for three weeks; the first engineer said this was because the added logging overhead closed the timing window for the race, while the second said it was simply because the network happened to behave itself during that period. When we later disabled the cache entirely for a week, as a more decisive test, the bug still appeared twice. The first engineer concluded that some subtler race condition must be at play elsewhere in the concurrency model, rather than treating this as a reason to abandon his diagnosis; the second treated the recurrence as clean confirmation of the network theory, and in the same breath explained away the rest of that otherwise quiet week as simply the network behaving itself for once, exactly as you would expect from an intermittent fault.

We've now spent a fair amount of engineering time on this, leadership wants a resolution, and the team has half-jokingly suggested just flipping a coin or deferring to whichever engineer is more senior. That feels wrong to me, though I have trouble putting into words exactly why, beyond a sense that we're supposed to let the evidence decide these things. I'm not asking you to adjudicate the actual bug -- I know I haven't given you nearly enough to go on for that. What I'd like help thinking through is the general pattern: is it just coincidence, or a sign that one of these two people is arguing in bad faith, that every test we devise ends up being explainable by both theories after the fact? Or is there something more structural going on here that makes this kind of standoff hard to avoid? And if it's the latter, is there any principled way to design a test that would actually be harder to explain away than the ones we've tried so far, or to otherwise make real progress, short of simply picking an engineer to believe?
