During my career as an experimental physicist I relied constantly on the law of large numbers, so now that I have taken up philosophy in retirement, I was delighted to discover that Condorcet applied essentially the same reasoning to collective decision-making two centuries earlier. His Jury Theorem shows that if voters judge independently and each is individually more likely right than wrong, then enlarging the electorate drives the probability of a correct majority verdict toward certainty - and, by the same logic, toward zero once either condition fails.

I would like your help imagining how Condorcet himself might reassess this result if he were briefed on today's information environment. Social media feeds and recommendation algorithms now expose enormous numbers of citizens to correlated, sometimes deliberately engineered, content, so that what looks like millions of separate judgments may really amount to a much smaller number of judgments echoed many times over. Bearing in mind that Condorcet was not merely a mathematician but a constitutional designer who tried to build representative institutions on this very theorem, and who treated public education as the chief guarantor of citizens' independent judgment, how do you think he would react? Would he conclude that the theorem's optimistic result is simply inapplicable to mass democracy today, propose institutional remedies in the spirit of his own constitutional drafting, or argue that the theorem still holds once we ask at what level independence should properly be measured?
