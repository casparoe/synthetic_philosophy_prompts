I don't have any formal background in philosophy, but I've been turning the Sleeping Beauty problem over in my head ever since a colleague sent it to me as a brain-teaser a few months back. For anyone unfamiliar: Sleeping Beauty agrees to take part in an experiment. On Sunday she is put to sleep, and a fair coin is tossed. If it lands heads, she is woken once, on Monday, and the experiment ends there. If it lands tails, she is woken on Monday and again on Tuesday, except her memory of the Monday waking is erased before Tuesday, so on neither day can she tell which one it is. Each time she wakes, she is asked the same question: "What is your credence that the coin landed heads?" I already knew that people split into 'halfers,' who say 1/2, and 'thirders,' who say 1/3, roughly depending on whether simply waking up counts as evidence. What I didn't expect was how heated things would get when I raised a variant with two friends of mine, Owen and Marisol, over dinner: instead of two possible wakings on tails, suppose there were a thousand -- if the coin lands tails, Beauty is woken once a day for a thousand days running, memory wiped every night, and still only once if it lands heads. I asked what her credence in heads should be on any ordinary waking, and the two of them argued about it for the rest of the meal.

(1) Owen said the answer has to be something close to 1/1001: if you imagine the whole thousand-day setup repeated many times over, only one waking out of every 1001 would be a heads-waking, and since Beauty has no way to distinguish her current waking from any other, she should spread her belief evenly across all of them and conclude she is almost certainly living through a tails run. (2) Marisol said that can't be right, because the coin itself is fair and nothing about the experience of waking up gives her any actual information about how it landed -- she was guaranteed to wake up at least once regardless of the outcome, so however many times that waking gets repeated afterward, it isn't evidence of anything; if you had asked her right after the coin was tossed, before any wiping had happened, her answer would obviously be 1/2, and nothing since then has given her a reason to move off that number. (3) Owen replied that Marisol was missing what makes this a different sort of question from an ordinary one: nobody is disputing that the coin toss itself was fair, only that once Beauty knows she is one of many otherwise identical wakings, she has to work out which of those wakings she is likely to be in, the same way she would in the original two-day version -- and Marisol had already agreed that version gives 1/3, so unless she could say why a thousand wakings differ in kind rather than just in number, consistency required her to follow the logic all the way through. (4) Marisol said the thousand-day version was exactly what had turned her against the 1/3 answer generally: if adding more wakings can push credence in a fair coin toward one in a thousand, or one in a million, purely by changing a memory-wiping schedule, that shows the reasoning proves too much rather than confirming it. She said she would rather keep her credence at 1/2 and treat separately the fact that if Beauty is being rewarded per waking instead of per coin toss, the smart strategy is still to guess tails every time -- but that, she insisted, is a fact about how the payoffs are counted, not about what she should actually believe.

Neither of them would budge, and I honestly can't tell which parts of this are right. Could you go through the four moves one at a time rather than just declaring an overall winner?
