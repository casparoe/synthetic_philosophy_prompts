Random one from my formal epistemology reading group. Van Fraassen's Reflection Principle says your credence now in A, conditional on your future credence in A being x, should equal x -- and he didn't just posit this, he backed it with a diachronic Dutch book argument. But there's a well-known family of counterexamples where deferring to your future self seems crazy: Christensen's case of someone who's just taken a drug that they know will make them falsely certain in an hour that they can fly, or Talbott's case about forgetting what you had for dinner a year ago. The standard fix is to just restrict Reflection to future selves you have no reason to think are epistemicaly compromised. Argue that this restriction is basically ad hoc: that any version precise enough to actually rule out the compromised-future-self cases ends up quietly doing all its work via ordinary trust in your future evidence-gathering, so Reflection adds nothing over and above conditionalization plus that background trust -- contra what van Fraassen wanted the dutch book argument to establish. Please actually work through at least one concrete case rather then staying abstract the whole way.
