Okay, this is a weirdly personal research question but here goes. My grandfather is a pretty serious traditionalist Catholic, and at a family dinner a while back we got into an argument about political violence (there's so much of it in the news lately) where he kept insisting that "the Church has always permitted killing a tyrant" and threw out Aquinas's name like that settled it. I went to Catholic school for like a decade and I still couldn't push back on that with anything specific, which bugged me, so afterward I actually went and dug up the passage he was probably gesturing at (Summa Theologiae, II-II, Q.42, the question on sedition). Except now that I'm reading it myself I'm more confused than before, because scholastic prose is its own beast and I honestly can't tell if it says what he thinks it says.

Here's the bit, from the article asking "whether sedition is always a mortal sin":

Objection 3: "it is praiseworthy to deliver a multitude from a tyrannical rule. Yet this cannot easily be done without some dissension in the multitude, if one part of the multitude seeks to retain the tyrant, while the rest strive to dethrone him. Therefore there can be sedition without mortal sin."

Reply to Objection 3: "A tyrannical government is not just, because it is directed, not to the common good, but to the private good of the ruler, as the Philosopher states (Polit. iii, 5; Ethic. viii, 10). Consequently there is no sedition in disturbing a government of this kind, unless indeed the tyrant's rule be disturbed so inordinately, that his subjects suffer greater harm from the consequent disturbance than from the tyrant's government. Indeed it is the tyrant rather that is guilty of sedition, since he encourages discord and sedition among his subjects, that he may lord over them more securely; for this is tyranny, being conducive to the private good of the ruler, and to the injury of the multitude."

A few things are genuinely tripping me up here and I can't find anyone laying them out in plain terms. First, is Aquinas's move basically definitional - resisting a tyrant can't count as "sedition" because sedition by definition attacks a real, common-good-oriented unity, and a tyranny never had that kind of unity to begin with - or is it actually smuggling in a seperate cost-benefit test with that "unless" clause, which sounds like it's just weighing harms against each other? Those feel like two different kinds of justification to me and I don't get how they're supposed to work together in a single answer. Second, nowhere in this passage does Aquinas say anything about killing anybody, just "disturbing" the government and "delivering" the multitude - so how does this specific text end up being the go-to citation for "Aquinas endorsed tyrannicide" that I keep seeing thrown around online? Am I missing an inference, or is that just people overreading it? And third, that closing move where he flips it around and says the tyrant himself is "guilty of sedition" - is that doing real philosophical work, or is it just a rhetorical trick to make the conclusion feel less radical than it actually is? I'd really appreciate someone walking through the actual logical structure here instead of giving me the general "Aquinas thought tyrants could sometimes be resisted" takeaway, since that part I already knew going in.
