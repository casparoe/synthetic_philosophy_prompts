Over dinner last week, a friend and I fell into a long-running argument about whether it is ever literally true that a neural network "represents" something, or whether such talk is always a convenient fiction we impose on weights and activations after the fact. The case that set us off was Othello-GPT: a transformer trained only to predict legal next moves in the board game Othello, given nothing but sequences of past moves, which turns out to carry decodable information about the full board state at every point in the game despite never being given the board directly. My friend kept deflating this with the standard comparison to calling a thermostat a device that "believes" the room is cold — on his view, "representation" here is just an explanatorily idle label we hang on a system we could describe just as completely in purely causal terms. I suspect he's wrong about this particular case, but I went home unable to say exactly why, and it's been nagging at me since.

Here is the detail I think does the real work. Kenneth Li and colleagues could only extract the board state using non-linear probes, and had to run gradient descent against those probes to show the extracted state was doing any causal work — for instance, editing a model's internal "board" through optimization and getting it to subsequently play legal moves on a configuration that could never actually arise from legal play. That result already invites my friend's deflationary reading, since enough optimization pressure against a rich enough activation space can arguably be made to find almost any correlate you like. What struck me as harder to dismiss was Neel Nanda's follow-up: he found the same information is linearly decodable, one direction per square, once you stop asking whether a square is "black" or "white" and ask instead whether it holds the current player's own piece or the opponent's, since the same weights play both colors over a game. A single, simple intervention on that one direction, with no further optimization involved, was enough to make the model subsequently play out legal moves consistent with the edited square.

I'd like your help turning that hunch into an actual argument. Please argue, as rigorously as you can, that a case like this genuinely supports realism about neural network representations — that at least some internal variables of trained networks are representations in a full-blooded sense, not merely useful-as-if descriptions — and that it withstands my friend's thermostat-style deflation. I would especially value it if you engaged with William Ramsey's "job description challenge" from Representation Reconsidered: his complaint that neuroscientists and connectionists too readily call something a representation just because it reliably detects or correlates with a feature (his "receptor notion"). Ramsey goes on to argue that even showing the detected state is causally exploited by some downstream system may not be enough to rescue it, on pain of making "representation" apply to any causal intermediary whatsoever, thermostats included; his own preferred alternative instead ties representational status to something more like being a model that a system reuses across many different tasks, rather than a state that just trips one switch. So instead of resting the case on the word "causal" by itself, please say what, specifically, the Othello "mine/theirs" direction would need to do to count as that kind of structure-exploiting model, and then argue for whether the evidence above already shows it.
