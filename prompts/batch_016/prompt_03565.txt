Ok so we just covered the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem in my mechanism design class and its kind of messing with my head. From what I got out of lecture, it says that for any voting rule where atleast three different outcomes can actually end up winning, and the rule has to spit out one single winner no matter what preference rankings people report, that rule is either a flat-out dictatorship or there's some preference profile where at least one voter does better by misreporting their true ranking. My professor basically framed it as one of those "sorry, everything is broken" results, kind of the strategic-voting equivalent of Arrow's theorem.

Except a couple weeks later she's teaching us about second-price auctions and the deferred-acceptance algorithm (the one used for stuff like matching students to schools and residents to hospitals), and calling both of them strategy-proof in the sense that truthful reporting is a dominant strategy for everyone, no dictator required. That feels like a direct contradiction of what Gibbard-Satterthwaite supposedly proved?? A friend in my study group thinks economists must just be using a weaker definition of "strategy-proof" than the one in the theorem, but that explanation feels like a cop-out to me. Am I missing something about what the theorem actually applies to, or is my friend right that there's some sleight of hand going on with definitions?
