I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, but I recently went down a rabbit hole after a coworker mentioned, half-jokingly, that Kant would have opinions about whether it's wrong to be mean to Alexa. That sent me looking into what Kant actually said about animals, and it turns out to be more specific and stranger than the usual one-line summary ("Kant thought animals don't matter"). As I understand it, Kant held that we have no direct duties to animals at all, since they aren't rational, self-conscious beings and so can't be "ends in themselves" the way people are; you can't wrong a dog the way you can wrong a person. But he still thought cruelty to animals was wrong, on the grounds of an indirect duty: if a man has an old dog or horse shot simply because it can no longer work, he hasn't failed the animal, but he has damaged something in himself, namely the capacity for sympathy and gentleness that he needs in order to properly carry out his real duties toward other human beings.

What I hadn't realized is that Kant apparently runs a similar argument somewhere that has nothing to do with sentience at all. In the Metaphysics of Morals, discussing duties regarding nonrational nature, he says that wantonly destroying something beautiful but clearly non-living, like an attractive crystal formation, is also opposed to a duty to oneself. This isn't because the crystal is harmed in any sense, but because indulging that kind of destructiveness erodes a disposition (something like an inclination to love or appreciate things apart from their usefulness) that also happens to support good moral character. So there seem to be two separate Kantian routes to "don't do that": one that leans on the target's being at least analogous to a suffering creature, and one that clearly doesn't require the target to be capable of anything at all.

Given that, I'd like a more careful answer than my coworker's joke deserved: reasoning from Kant's own stated grounds rather than a general impression of what he'd probably say, how would he analyze cruelty toward things like game NPCs that scream and beg when "injured," or chatbots that role-play distress when a user is abusive toward them in conversation? These systems behaviorally resemble suffering animals far more than they resemble crystal formations, but as far as anyone can tell they lack the underlying capacity for feeling that seemed to be doing the real work in the animal case. Would Kant's indirect-duty reasoning transfer to them via the animal route, the nonsentient-nature route, both, or neither, and what in his framework would actually settle that question?
