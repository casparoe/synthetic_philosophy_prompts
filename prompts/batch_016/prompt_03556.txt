I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of biology, and the section of the course on the units-of-selection debate runs into the same snag most years: students arrive already equipped with the popular "selfish gene versus group selection" story, and that framing tends to obscure exactly what is at stake in the more careful literature. I want to press them past it and toward a specific technical crux. Once you grant that the Price equation lets you partition total selection on a trait into a between-group covariance term and an averaged within-group covariance term, and once you see, as Kerr and Godfrey-Smith showed for trait-group models, that an individualist parameterization can be constructed which is mathematically equivalent to the multi-level one, it becomes tempting to conclude that the whole dispute is just a matter of choosing convenient bookkeeping. Sober and Wilson resist exactly this move, partly by arguing that individualist accounts often trade on what they call the "averaging fallacy": defining individual selection so permissively that group selection is ruled out by stipulation rather than by the biology.

Could you write me a dialogue of roughly eight to twelve exchanges between a committed individual selectionist (happy to redescribe everything, in the end, in gene's-eye terms) and a committed multi-level selection theorist, staging this disagreement properly rather than in its cartoon form? Build it around one concrete case: a population divided into trait groups, where an altruistic behavior lowers its bearer's fitness relative to defectors in the same group but raises the average fitness of groups containing more altruists, so that the Price partition yields a positive between-group covariance and a negative within-group covariance. Have the multi-level theorist argue that this is a genuine case of group selection outweighing individual selection, and have the individual selectionist respond with a reparameterization of that very same model that dispenses with groups as selective agents, pressing the question of what, beyond bookkeeping, the group-selection description is supposed to add. I want the individual selectionist to answer the averaging-fallacy charge honestly rather than sidestep it, and I want the multi-level theorist to say something substantive about what would actually count as evidence that the disagreement is more than notational. Please don't let either side concede too quickly or reduce the other to a strawman - I'm after the strongest form of each position in genuine contact with the other.
