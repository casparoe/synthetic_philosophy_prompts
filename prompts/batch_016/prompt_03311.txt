Random rabbit hole I fell into this week: my evo-psych prof brought up the handicap principle (peacock tails, gazelles stotting at predators, all that) as the standard explanation for costly signalling, and afterward a friend of mine said Veblen was basically describing the same "cost equals honesty" logic almost 80 years earlier in Theory of the Leisure Class, just applied to rich people instead of animals. So I dug up the actual text and now I cant tell if that's really the same argument or if we're just pattern-matching two things that sound alike on the surface.

Here's the bit that's tripping me up, from chapter 4 ("Conspicuous Consumption"):

"In order to gain and to hold the esteem of men it is not sufficient merely to possess wealth or power. The wealth or power must be put in evidence, for esteem is awarded only on evidence."

and then a bit further on:

"From the foregoing survey of the growth of conspicuous leisure and consumption, it appears that the utility of both alike for the purposes of reputability lies in the element of waste that is common to both. In the one case it is a waste of time and effort, in the other it is a waste of goods. Both are methods of demonstrating the possession of wealth, and the two are conventionally accepted as equivalents. The choice between them is a question of advertising expediency simply."

What I can't figure out is whether Veblen is actually making Zahavi's argument here — i.e., that the cost has to be real and disproportionately hard for a "cheater" to fake, so that it works as a guarantee — or whether he's making a much weaker claim: that waste is just whatever convention a given society happens to land on as the visible marker of wealth, with no particular reason it couldn't have been something cheap instead, so long as everyone agreed to read it the same way. That "conventionally accepted as equivalents" line especially throws me, since he seems totally fine with leisure and goods being interchangeable stand-ins for each other, which sounds more like an arbitrary sign system than an evolutionarily stable handicap. Is there something in his actual argument (here or elsewhere in the book) that supplies the "can't be faked by people who don't really have the wealth" mechanism the way the biology does, or is that whole equilibrium/mimicry piece genuinely missing from Veblen, and people just over-credit him with having anticipated an idea he only gestured at?
