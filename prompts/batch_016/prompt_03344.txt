Hey, I'm working on a piece about the "you should always want more information, it literally cannot hurt you" mentality that's kind of become gospel among the quantified-self / rationalist crowd. A source I interviewed kept citing something called Good's theorem — from I.J. Good, the statistician who worked with Turing — which he says is a genuine mathematical proof that a rational Bayesian can never be made worse off in expectation by free information before a decision, so any kind of deliberate ignorance or "epistemic caution" is just irrational, full stop. He said it's literally why he never turns off notifications and reads every test result the second it's in.

But for the same piece I talked to an economist who brought up an older result, I think from the 70s, by a guy named Hirshleifer (or somthing close to that spelling) — showing that if information about a risk gets revealed publicly before people have a chance to insure against it, it can destroy the whole insurance market for that risk and leave everyone worse off than if nobody had found out yet. That sounds to me like a flat contradiction: one says info literally can't make you worse off, the other is a case where more info demonstrably does, for basically everyone involved. Am I missing something, or do economists and decision theorists just quietly disagree with each other on this one? Trying to sort it out before I put a line like "the math says ignorance is never bliss" into the piece.
