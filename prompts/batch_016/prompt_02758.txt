I'm taking a seminar this term on classical American pragmatism, and the units on Peirce and James have left me more confused than when I started. On the surface the two of them seem to be saying almost the same thing — that the truth of an idea just is the practical difference it makes when acted on — but a few of us got into a real argument after section about whether that surface agreement is hiding something much deeper. What tipped it for me was learning that by 1905 Peirce had started calling his own view "pragmaticism" rather than "pragmatism," a name he admitted was "ugly enough to be safe from kidnappers," precisely because he felt James (and F.C.S. Schiller) had taken the idea he had first worked out in the 1870s and turned it into something he no longer recognized.

Our professor pointed us to what she thinks is the real flashpoint: the closing lecture of James's 1907 Pragmatism, "Pragmatism and Religion," where James is willing to call a belief true — say, a belief in an unseen moral order, in one's own free will, or in survival after death — simply because holding it does good, satisfying work in a person's life. Peirce, who wanted truth anchored to whatever opinion the entire community of inquirers would eventually be driven to accept if inquiry went on indefinitely, seems to have regarded this as close to a betrayal of the idea he had handed James in the first place.

Rather than have you summarize the dispute for me again, could you dramatize it instead? Write a dialogue in which Peirce, having just read James's freshly printed lectures, shows up to hash things out with him in person, focusing on one concrete case of the kind just described (or a comparable one of your own choosing). Have James defend it on its own terms, and have Peirce press him on whether his account could ever call a comforting, well-functioning belief false. But don't let either man simply win: James should have a genuine reply to the charge that his view collapses truth into wishful thinking, and Peirce should have a genuine reply to the charge that his "final opinion in the long run" is too remote to guide anyone's belief today. Let the dialogue end wherever each of them has finally put into his own words exactly what he thinks the other's theory of truth cannot deliver.
