I'm chipping away at a novel that has a secondary character — an appellate judge a couple years from retirement — who's quietly obsessed with Pascal and keeps a beat-up copy of the Pensées on his nightstand. I want a scene where he and his old clerk get into it over one of the "justice and force" fragments, but NOT the one everyone already has on a poster ("justice without force is powerless, force without justice is tyrannical" or however people phrase it). I found one a bit further into that section that I like better for the scene, but honestly I only half-follow the actual argument and I don't want my judge to say something Pascal didn't earn.

Here's the fragment, from the old W. F. Trotter translation (the one with the T.S. Eliot introduction), numbered 298 in that edition:

"Justice, might.—It is right that what is just should be obeyed; it is necessary that what is strongest should be obeyed. Justice without might is helpless; might without justice is tyrannical. Justice without might is gainsaid, because there are always offenders; might without justice is condemned. We must then combine justice and might, and for this end make what is just strong, or what is strong just.

Justice is subject to dispute; might is easily recognised and is not disputed. So we cannot give might to justice, because might has gainsaid justice, and has declared that it is she herself who is just. And thus being unable to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just."

The first paragraph I feel like I get okay, its the second one that trips me up — specifically "might has gainsaid justice, and has declared that it is she herself who is just." Is Pascal just being cynical there, like, might wins and then simply rebrands itself as justice, end of story — or is there a more specific move being made about why it's justice's disputability in particular (as opposed to its slowness, its dependence on interpretation, whatever) that dooms the whole project of ever making the just side strong instead of the strong side just? I'm also curious how this squares with the much more famous bit a page or two earlier about truth switching sides depending which bank of the Pyrenees you're standing on — is the "custom varies by geography" complaint and the "might declares itself justice" complaint the same underlying diagnosis wearing two different hats, or genuinely separate arguments that just happen to sit near each other in his notes? Would love a slow, careful reading rather than the general gist, since my judge is supposed to have worked this out for himself over thirty years on the bench and I want his version of it to feel earned rather than sound like he skimmed a summary.
