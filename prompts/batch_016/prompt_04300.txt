In my AP European History class, we spent a day on the Scientific Revolution, and my teacher mentioned, almost in passing, that Johannes Kepler — the same Kepler who formulated the laws of planetary motion — spent years working as a professional astrologer and even wrote an entire book, Tertius Interveniens (1610), defending astrology against a physician named Feselius who wanted it rejected outright. I found this quite surprising and looked into it further on my own, partly because a couple of my friends are really into horoscopes and zodiac-sign compatibility, and I wanted to know whether Kepler's astrology resembled theirs at all.

As it turns out, it really doesn't. From what I can tell, Kepler discarded most of traditional astrology outright: the zodiac signs, the houses, and the assumption that a single planet could influence anything on its own. The one piece he kept was the system of "aspects," the angles planets form with each other, such as the trine or the square, and he even added a few new ones of his own, like the quintile, while rejecting others, such as a hypothetical seventh-of-a-circle aspect, because they fail to correspond to a harmonious, geometrically constructible angle the way the traditional aspects supposedly do. Rather than proposing any mechanical force behind all this, he explained it by claiming that the soul directly and instinctively recognizes these geometric "archetypes," much as it recognizes consonant musical intervals, and he even thought the Earth has a soul of its own that reacts to the aspects and produces weather as a result.

I would like to see this position argued out loud rather than just summarized. Could you write a dialogue between Kepler and an equally sharp contemporary critic — you could model this figure loosely on Feselius, perhaps confronting Kepler shortly after Tertius Interveniens was published, or simply invent a comparable skeptic — who wants astrology rejected in its entirety, aspects included? I don't want the critic to be an easy target: they should press Kepler both on the general lack of any testable mechanism behind "archetypes recognized by the soul" and on the more specific charge that his boundary between the aspects he keeps and the rest of astrology he throws out is just as arbitrary as the tradition he claims to be reforming. I would then like Kepler to respond with his actual reasoning rather than a vague appeal to authority, with both characters genuinely responding to whatever the other has just said rather than simply reiterating their own position.
