I work as a software engineer, not as a historian, but I've spent a good chunk of my evenings over the past couple of months reading primary sources from the American abolitionist movement -- one of those hobbies that started with a podcast and got a little out of hand. I recently landed on the well-documented split between Frederick Douglass and William Lloyd Garrison over how to read the U.S. Constitution, and I haven't been able to put it down since. Garrison and his allies held that the Constitution was, as a resolution he championed at the time put it, "a covenant with death, and an agreement with hell" -- citing things like the three-fifths clause, the fugitive slave clause, and the clause shielding the slave trade until 1808 -- and concluded that a genuine abolitionist should refuse to vote, hold office, or otherwise work within a political system built on that foundation. Douglass broke from that position in 1851 and spent much of the rest of his career arguing the reverse: that the words "slave" and "slavery" appear nowhere in the actual text, that a legal document has to be read by what it says rather than by what some of the men who wrote it may have privately intended, and that, as he put it in 1852, "interpreted as it ought to be interpreted, the Constitution is a glorious liberty document." What gets me is that both camps were staring at the very same clauses and the same convention records, and neither side was naive about how slavery actually worked -- so it's hard for me to write either one off as simply not paying attention.

Could you write a dialogue between a committed Garrisonian abolitionist and Douglass, set just after one of the speeches in which he laid out his change of mind, where the two of them argue it out directly -- each responding to what the other has actually just said rather than delivering prepared speeches past each other? I'd like both sides handled by someone genuinely capable: the Garrisonian shouldn't come across as dogmatic for its own sake, and Douglass shouldn't win simply because he gets the last word. If it fits naturally into the exchange, I'd also like the two of them to poke at a question I can't resolve on my own: whether they are really disagreeing about how a legal text ought to be interpreted, or whether that's just the vocabulary in which a more basic disagreement about political strategy -- moral witness and disunion versus working for change inside the existing system -- is actually being fought out. I'd rather watch them work through that themselves than have you settle it for me afterward.
