I'm reporting a feature on the evolution of extravagant animal ornaments — peacock trains, the exaggerated eyestalks of stalk-eyed flies, that sort of thing — and I keep running into an origin story for the underlying theory that I suspect is more contested than it's usually presented as. The standard version goes: Amotz Zahavi argued in the 1970s that a costly, seemingly wasteful trait can function as an honest signal of quality precisely because only high-quality individuals can afford to bear its cost, and although this "handicap principle" was initially dismissed by theoreticians as unworkable, it was supposedly vindicated once Alan Grafen built a rigorous evolutionarily stable strategy model of it in 1990. While prepping for an interview, though, a behavioral ecologist pointed me to more recent critical literature — I believe it's a 2020 paper by Penn and Számadó in Biological Reviews — arguing that Grafen's model doesn't actually reconstruct Zahavi's idea at all: it shows that honesty can be maintained through differential marginal costs at equilibrium, which is perfectly compatible with a signal being cheap, or even efficient, overall, whereas Zahavi's own writing repeatedly insisted that real, absolute waste was doing the explanatory work.

Could you help me think this through before I write it up? I'd like you to compare what Zahavi's original handicap principle actually committed him to — especially the idea that waste is intrinsic rather than incidental to honest signaling — against what Grafen's strategic-choice model actually establishes, and to be precise about where the two substantively part ways rather than treating "marginal cost" as a mere technical footnote. I'm also curious for your own verdict: is it more accurate to say Grafen produced a distinct, efficiency-compatible theory of honest signaling that has been mistaken for thirty years as a proof of Zahavi's stronger and stranger idea, or is there a charitable enough reading of Zahavi on which the two turn out to be reconcilable after all?
