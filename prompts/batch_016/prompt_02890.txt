I've been reading about the philosopher Henry Sidgwick, and something about him puzzles me. He's held up as one of the nineteenth century's most rigorous ethical thinkers, author of The Methods of Ethics, yet the same man spent decades helping found and lead the Society for Psychical Research, investigating mediums and séances for hard evidence of life after death. From what I understand, his ethics runs into a problem he never resolved, sometimes called the 'dualism of practical reason': he could find no strictly rational reason to always choose the general good over his own interest when the two conflict, short of assuming some further order — a benevolent God, an afterlife — that makes duty and self-interest coincide. Unlike Kant, who was content to treat this as a postulate of practical reason, Sidgwick apparently wanted actual proof, which I suspect is what drew him into psychical research. Could you compare these two sides of his work: how exactly does the unresolved problem in his ethics motivate the psychical research, and, judged by the standards of rigor he applied to each, which project would you say he pursued more successfully?
