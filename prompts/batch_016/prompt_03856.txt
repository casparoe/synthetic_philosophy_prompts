I spent my career as an experimental physicist, working mostly on precision measurements of fundamental constants, and only since retiring have I begun reading philosophy seriously -- currently working through the literature on the fine-tuning argument. Two members of a discussion group I've joined, Marta (a cosmologist) and Felix (who teaches philosophy of science), got into a disagreement about it that spanned two meetings and that I still haven't been able to settle in my own mind.

(1) Marta defended the standard multiverse response to fine-tuning: the constants of nature look absurdly well-suited to life, but if there are vastly many universes with different constants, that stops being surprising, since we could hardly find ourselves in one of the sterile ones. Allow enough universes, she said, and fine-tuning stops being a puzzle -- it's just what a large, varied ensemble guarantees somewhere.

(2) Felix objected that this is the inverse gambler's fallacy in a lab coat. If you walk into a casino and see someone roll double sixes, you aren't entitled to conclude there must be a hundred other tables rolling dice elsewhere in the building: the odds of that particular roll don't change no matter how many other rolls happen out of sight. Multiplying universes doesn't make our universe's constants one bit less improbable; you've just added a lot of dice you can't go check.

(3) Marta replied that Felix was skating past the one disanalogy that matters. The gambler would be standing at that table to see any roll come up, sixes or not, whereas we would not be here, full stop, in a universe that wasn't life-permitting. Our observing anything at all is filtered through our own existence in a way the gambler's isn't, and that kind of filter is exactly what licenses inferring a large or varied ensemble -- the same move a biologist makes in explaining why we evolved on a habitable planet rather than a barren one, without needing to suppose anyone aimed at that outcome.

(4) Felix pushed back that even if there were only a single universe which, by sheer luck, happened to be life-permitting, we would be just as guaranteed to observe fine-tuning in that world as in any multiverse. So the observation that we see fine-tuning doesn't actually discriminate between one lucky universe and countless universes -- we were certain to make it either way. Marta, he said, hadn't earned the extra universes from the evidence; she had assumed them and then credited the theory for predicting what was already guaranteed.

(5) Marta answered that this proves too much, since the identical move sinks the design hypothesis as well. If a designer had wanted a life-permitting universe, we would be exactly as guaranteed to observe fine-tuning as under sheer luck or a multiverse. If "we were bound to see this regardless" kills the multiverse explanation, she said, it kills design too, leaving Felix with a principle on which fine-tuning can't be evidence for anything about its own cause.

Neither budged, and I'm told a third meeting is scheduled. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each stage, not just who wins overall? I'd also value your judgment on whether Marta's closing move at (5) establishes a genuine parity between design and the multiverse, or whether the two are on different footing here -- and if so, exactly why.
