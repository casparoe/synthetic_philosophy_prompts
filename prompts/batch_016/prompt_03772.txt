I'm a senior, and something that happened during my college applications this fall has been bothering me long after the immediate stress of it wore off. My school's guidance counselor was supposed to upload my recommendation letter to the Common App portal before the early-decision deadline, and she simply forgot. Because the letter never appeared, my application was marked incomplete and was never even considered for the scholarship I'd been hoping for. My parents were furious and wanted to file a formal complaint against her, arguing that her forgetting to upload one document is what cost me the scholarship.

When my parents brought this up with the school's dean of students, though, he pushed back in a way that has stuck with me since. He pointed out that plenty of other people also failed to do things that would have prevented the outcome: I could have checked my application status a week earlier and caught the missing letter myself, the school's system could have flagged incomplete files automatically and didn't, and a friend of mine who does office work-study could have noticed my folder was missing something and mentioned it but didn't think to. His point was that it seems arbitrary to single out the counselor's forgetting as "the" reason this happened, when so many other non-actions were, in exactly the same sense, necessary for things to go wrong.

I can't quite decide whether the dean is being unfair to my parents or whether he's actually onto something important. It seems obviously true that people can be responsible for things they failed to do and not just for things they did, but once you start listing everyone who failed to do something that would have helped, it stops being clear why any one absence deserves to be picked out as the cause rather than just being treated as one more missing piece among many. A couple of friends and I argued about this for a while without getting anywhere. I'd genuinely like your take: is there a real, principled basis for saying the counselor's forgetting specifically caused this, rather than it just being the failure that's easiest for us to blame?
