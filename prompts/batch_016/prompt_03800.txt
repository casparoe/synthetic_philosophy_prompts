I'm a philosophy PhD student preparing a seminar paper on the School of Names (名家), and I keep getting stuck on the opening moves of Gongsun Long's White Horse Dialogue (白馬論). Here is the exchange, in a rendering I've put together from the standard translations:

Objector: Can it be maintained that "a white horse is not (非) a horse"?
Advocate: It can.
Objector: On what grounds?
Advocate: "Horse" is the name for a shape; "white" is the name for a colour. Naming a colour is not naming a shape. Hence: a white horse is not a horse.
Objector: If there is a white horse, one cannot say there is no horse. And if one cannot say there is no horse, isn't that just to say there is a horse? Having a white horse just is having a horse — so how can the white one fail to be a horse?
Advocate: Ask for a horse, and a yellow or a black horse will do. Ask for a white horse, and a yellow or a black horse will not do. Now, if a white horse simply were a horse, what is asked for in the two cases would be one and the same thing. And if it were the same thing, "white horse" would be no different from "horse" — so why is it that yellow and black horses count as acceptable answers in one case and not the other? That "acceptable" and "unacceptable" are opposed to one another is plain. Hence a yellow or a black horse alike can answer to "there is a horse" but cannot answer to "there is a white horse." That is why a white horse is not a horse.

I can more or less follow the Advocate's first move, from shape and colour: it seems to license only the modest conclusion that "horse" and "white horse" are not synonymous, which is hardly controversial. What I can't get straight is the second move, the one about asking for a horse versus asking for a white horse. Could you lay out its logical structure explicitly — premises, the inference actually being drawn, and the conclusion it is entitled to — and tell me whether it trades on a slide between "is not" meaning "is not identical to" and "is not" meaning "is not a member of"? And if it does slide between the two, is the first argument innocent of that same move, or does it quietly depend on it as well?
