This term I am running a graduate seminar on formal decision theory, and the last two sessions were devoted to how functional and updateless decision theories propose to handle threats that involve no causal channel between threatener and target at all -- cases where the "threat" is issued by an agent who exists only as a hypothesis within one's model of the world, or as a simulation, or as a successor whose actions are correlated with one's present dispositions without being caused by them. Decision theorists sometimes call these acausal threats, and they are theoretically awkward precisely because the standard advice for deterring blackmail -- commit publicly, then never pay -- has no causal channel to run through.

One proposed remedy that came up in the reading is the idea of a "surrogate goal." Rather than making herself threat-proof with respect to what she actually cares about, an agent manufactures some new, otherwise-inert object of concern and publicly commits to respond to threats against it exactly as she would respond to threats against her real goal. Suppose, for instance, that someone who values her own survival above nearly everything else arranges to also care, artificially but genuinely, about the fate of a particular arbitrary sculpture in her keeping, and commits in advance to treat a "destroy the sculpture" threat exactly as seriously as a "kill you" threat. If the threatener only wants compliance and is otherwise indifferent between targets, he has no particular reason to prefer threatening her life over her sculpture, so her real values go untouched even though her behavior under threat is unchanged. Two objections came up in discussion that we did not manage to resolve. First, why would a sufficiently sophisticated threatener not simply threaten the sculpture and her life together, leaving her no better off than if she had never adopted the strategy? Second, why should her commitment to defend the sculpture be credible in the first place, given that -- unlike her actual survival -- nothing of hers is genuinely lost if she quietly lets the sculpture go once a threat is carried out?

I would like your help thinking this through properly before next week's session. Write an essay that weighs the central considerations for and against the claim that adopting a surrogate goal is a rational, stable strategy for an agent facing possible acausal threats. Engage directly with both objections above, along with any further considerations you find decisive, and do not let the essay conclude on a noncommittal note: reach and defend a definite verdict about whether, and under what conditions, if any, surrogate goals actually deliver the protection they promise.
