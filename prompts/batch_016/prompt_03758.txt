I'm taking a course on decision theory this term, and we've spent a while on Piccione and Rubinstein's absent-minded driver problem. A driver on a highway passes two intersections, X then Y, that look identical to him, and he can never tell whether he has already gone through one. Exiting at X pays 0, continuing past X to exit at Y pays 4, and missing both exits pays 1. The plan that maximizes expected payoff when fixed in advance is to continue with probability 2/3 (expected payoff 4/3) — and that very plan implies that, on actually reaching an intersection, the driver should believe he is at X with probability 3/5 and at Y with probability 2/5.

Piccione and Rubinstein use this example to argue that something genuinely unresolved remains: a driver who takes that belief at face value and, standing at the intersection, directly recomputes the continuation probability that maximizes his expected payoff given it, is pulled toward continuing with probability 4/9 instead — a strictly worse strategy, yet apparently the natural upshot of maximizing expected utility given what he currently believes. Aumann, Hart, and Perry deny that this shows anything troubling: once the driver's momentary selves are modeled as separate players who take each other's behavior as fixed and hold beliefs consistent with the strategy actually being played (their "modified multiself" approach), the 2/3 plan is recovered exactly, and the pull toward 4/9 turns out to be an artifact of an ill-posed calculation rather than a rival form of rationality.

Write a dialogue of around eight exchanges between a proponent of each position, standing together at the first intersection while the driver's car idles between them. They should argue over whether the driver, having actually reached the intersection, faces any genuine open question about what to do, or whether the modified-multiself treatment fully closes the case. Each should respond directly to the other's strongest point rather than restate their own.
