I've started using the Dutch book argument in my formal epistemology seminar to motivate probabilism, and I think I've found a nice real-world illustration, though I'd like to sanity-check the logic before I put it in front of students. Bookmakers routinely post odds on a two-horse race whose implied probabilities sum to noticeably more than 100 percent—what the industry calls the "overround"—which looks like a textbook case of what de Finetti called incoherence: a violation of the additivity axiom that, per the Dutch book theorem, should leave whoever holds those credences open to a guaranteed loss. Since the practice is ubiquitous and entirely deliberate, doesn't it follow that the entire bookmaking industry is, in effect, built on exploitable, irrational credences? I'd welcome your view on whether this is a sound way to motivate the argument for my students, or whether the analogy breaks down somewhere I'm not seeing.
