In my high school's "Philosophy of Mind" elective, we moved from Descartes' Meditations straight into David Chalmers' philosophical zombie argument, and our teacher mentioned, almost as an aside, that the two arguments share the same basic logical skeleton: you conceive of some scenario clearly and distinctly, infer that it is therefore metaphysically possible, and then use that possibility to establish a substantive conclusion about the mind — that it is really distinct from the body, in Descartes' case, or that physicalism is false, in Chalmers' case. A friend of mine, who is more interested in philosophy of language than philosophy of mind, argued afterward that Chalmers has not really added anything beyond more technical vocabulary, and that his argument should be just as vulnerable as Descartes' to the standard objection that not everything conceivable is possible (the "water could have failed to be H2O" sort of case). I was not sure whether to agree with her.

Trying to work this out on my own, I have read that Chalmers tries to get around this problem by appealing to two-dimensional semantics, distinguishing primary from secondary intensions so that he can explain why some conceivable scenarios, like water not being H2O, turn out to be impossible, while insisting that the zombie scenario is not like that. I do not fully understand, though, why phenomenal concepts are supposed to be immune to the same treatment, and I would like to check my own reasoning against someone who actually knows the literature well before I commit to a position in my final essay.

Could you compare Descartes' conceivability argument for mind-body dualism with Chalmers' zombie conceivability argument against physicalism, focusing specifically on whether Chalmers' two-dimensional apparatus genuinely closes the gap that a posteriori necessities, like the water/H2O case, open up in the simpler Cartesian version, or whether the zombie argument remains vulnerable to essentially the same kind of objection? Please be explicit about where, in your view, the two arguments actually diverge, rather than simply asserting that Chalmers' version is "more sophisticated" without explaining what that extra sophistication amounts to.
