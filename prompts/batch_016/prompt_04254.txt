I am finishing a chapter on personal identity for an edited volume on the metaphysics of persons, and one of my central arguments leans heavily on the split-brain (commissurotomy) literature. The claim I want to defend is fairly deflationary: that the unity of consciousness is not a metaphysical bedrock fact about what it is to be a subject, but a contingent, and evidently quite fragile, achievement of ordinary interhemispheric wiring — one that a surgeon's scalpel can apparently undo. My evidence is the familiar set of dissociations: when two different words are flashed simultaneously to a patient's left and right visual fields, each hand will independently retrieve the object corresponding to "its own" word; an emotionally striking image shown only to the left visual field can make the patient blush or laugh in a way that the patient's speaking hemisphere cannot account for, though it will happily confabulate a plausible-sounding reason on the spot.

I have been treating this as more than a popular over-simplification, since it seems to have genuine scientific and philosophical pedigree behind it. Sperry himself, in the work for which he won the 1981 Nobel Prize, was willing to describe these patients as harboring two independent conscious minds operating side by side within a single brain, and my recollection of Nagel's classic 1971 discussion of these cases is that, having canvassed the available descriptions of what is going on, he more or less settles on some version of the two-minds hypothesis as the least objectionable one on offer.

Before I commit any of this to print, I would like you to pressure-test it. First, does my recollection of where Nagel ultimately lands match what he actually argues in that paper, or have I misremembered the shape of his conclusion? Second, and more importantly for the chapter: is it accurate to say that the classic behavioral dissociations establish, rather than merely being consistent with, the existence of two separate centers of subjective experience in these patients? If that overstates the case, I would like you to explain carefully where the inference breaks down, drawing on whatever you know about how later philosophical or experimental work has complicated the simple two-minds picture I have been assuming.
