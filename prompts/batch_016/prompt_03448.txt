I've spent the past few weeks reporting a piece on "functional decision theory," the alternative account of rational choice that Nate Soares and Eliezer Yudkowsky at the Machine Intelligence Research Institute (MIRI) have developed together with the decision theorist Ben Levinstein, aimed at agents — human or artificial — whose choices might be predicted, simulated, or copied by other agents. Tracing the older philosophical puzzles that motivate this work, I kept running into a decision-theory thought experiment called "Death in Damascus," introduced by Allan Gibbard and William Harper in 1978 as an adaptation of the older "Appointment in Samarra" fable that Somerset Maugham had popularized several decades earlier. A man meets Death in Damascus, who tells him he is coming for him tomorrow; the man flees that night to Aleppo, only to find Death already waiting there, since Death works from an appointment book, compiled in advance from highly accurate forecasts of people's movements, that always correctly states where a person will be. Because Death's location is fixed by a prediction of the man's own decision, whichever city he picks, the very fact of having picked it looks like evidence that Death foresaw it and is already there; there seems to be no choice he could make and then be glad, on reflection, that he had made it.

That structure has stuck with me, because it echoes something far more mundane than the philosophers had in mind: the low-grade paranoia I suspect many of us now feel toward pricing and recommendation systems that seem to have modeled us so well that any attempt to act differently — booking flights at odd hours, browsing in a private window, deliberately choosing the option we'd normally skip — feels somehow pre-empted before we've made it. Could you set out the most important implications of the Death in Damascus puzzle for situations like that, where someone suspects a fairly reliable predictor, whether a person or an algorithm, has already conditioned its behavior on a forecast of what they are about to choose? I'm especially curious whether decision theory offers any principled way out of the "regret it either way" bind the puzzle describes, or whether it instead shows that the whole project of trying to outwit a sufficiently good predictor is confused from the start — and what either answer would mean for how much effort it's rational to spend trying to make oneself less predictable.
