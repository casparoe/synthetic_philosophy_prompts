I'm a retired physicist, and a couple years back I started going to a little philosophy discussion group that meets monthly at our local library — mostly other retirees, plus a couple of grad students who wander in when they feel like it. Last month we went through the classic split-brain material (Sperry and Gazzaniga's experiments, plus we'd all read Nagel's old paper beforehand), and it kicked off a real argument between two regulars, Warren and Alice, that's been rattling around in my head since.

Here's roughly how it went, move by move:

(1) Warren said the callosotomy patients literally have two separate minds in one skull. His example was the chicken-claw/shovel test: flash a chicken claw to the left hemisphere and a snow scene to the right, and the patient's right hand points to a chicken while the left hand points to a shovel — then the talking hemisphere calmly announces the shovel's obviously for cleaning out the chicken coop, no idea its making that up. Warren's line: anything that perceives its own thing, acts on its own thing, and doesn't even notice another part of the system inventing a cover story for it, is a seperate subject of experience. Full stop.

(2) Alice said that confuses an information bottleneck with a second self — ordinary people confabulate reasons for choices all the time, for causes they have zero conscious access to, and nobody concludes there's a second person in there. She also pointed out these patients act like one coordinated person the moment you let them move their eyes and head freely — one ongoing set of plans for the afternoon. The "two minds" thing only ever shows up under the contrived lab setup (an image flashed for a split second to one fixed half of the visual field), which she said is a fact about the test, not about the patient.

(3) Warren replied that gets the order of explanation backwards — unrestricted daily life doesn't prove there's one mind, it just supplies enough shared cues that two minds can fake being one. The lab conditions don't create the split, they just remove the crutches. If you can experimentally dissociate independent perceiving-and-acting like that, the duality had to be there already.

(4) Alice said that "reveal vs. create" distinction isn't itself something the data can settle — which was exactly Nagel's point, that our ordinary assumption that there's some definite number of minds per skull might just stop applying here. So it cuts against Warren's confident "two" just as much as against her own "one."

(5) Warren's comeback: fine, then explain the case where researchers asked one hemisphere what career it wanted, and it spelled out with the left hand "automobile racer," while the other hemisphere said, out loud, "draftsman." Two standing, incompatible life goals in one head sounds a lot more like two people to him than like a concept simply running out of steam.

Nobody at the meeting could settle it, and someone mentioned there's newer research complicating the whole informational-isolation picture even further, but none of us knew enough to say more.

Could you referee this properly — go move by move (1 through 5) and say who's got the better of it at each step, not just who wins overall? I'd love it if you'd actually score each move from -2 (clearly wrong) to +2 (clearly right), old habits die hard. And separately: is there some third answer to "one mind or two" that neither of them is even considering?
