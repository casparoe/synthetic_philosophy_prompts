This term I'm taking a graduate seminar on self-knowledge, and this week we worked through the anti-luminosity argument in chapter 4 of Timothy Williamson's *Knowledge and Its Limits*. I've followed the mechanics well enough: Williamson has us imagine someone who feels freezing cold at dawn and hot by noon, with the intervening hours chopped into cases α₀ through αₙ at one-millisecond intervals, spaced so finely that no one could discriminate case i from case i+1 by introspection alone. Using a safety-style premise — that knowing that p requires a belief that couldn't easily have been false — he argues that if you know you feel cold in case i, you must still feel cold in case i+1. Add the assumption that feeling cold is luminous (that you're always in a position to know you feel cold whenever you do), and you can chain your way from the true claim that you feel cold at dawn to the false claim that you feel cold at noon. Since the chaining premise looks secure, luminosity is what has to give — and because knowing that p itself seems like a condition that can be gradually gained or lost, the same reasoning is supposed to take down the KK principle — the thesis that knowing p puts you in a position to know that you know p — as a special case.

Where I get stuck is turning this from a recipe I can execute into something I actually understand. Two classmates and I argued about it over coffee after class and couldn't settle either point. First, why isn't the obvious move just to concede a sharp, unknowable cutoff — some exact case k after which one stops feeling cold — especially since Williamson is independently committed, as an epistemicist about vagueness, to exactly this sort of unknowable sharp boundary elsewhere? What does positing the boundary fail to buy you here that it buys you there? Second, Williamson insists this isn't just a sorites paradox dressed up in epistemic language, even though the small-steps-to-absurdity shape looks identical — so what is the extra premise actually doing, and why think it transfers so cleanly from a comparative sensation like feeling cold to something as structurally different as knowing a proposition? I can reproduce the derivation from lecture notes; what I'd like is an explanation pitched at the level of whatever picture of knowledge and introspection has to be true for the argument to have any bite at all, rather than one that just re-runs the formal steps.
