Had a long, kind of rediculous argument with my friend Theo last night over drinks about whether Daniel Bernoulli's famous fix for the St. Petersburg paradox actually holds up in general, or whether it's more of a one-off patch that got lucky. We're both fine with the classic move: cap the craziness of the infinite expected value by saying utility is logarithmic in wealth, so "moral expectation" converges even though the expected cash payout doesn't. But then Theo (who did some grad work on formal decision theory before switching fields) brought up this thing I'd never heard of called the Pasadena paradox -- Nover & Hajek, I think 2004? The setup: you flip a fair coin repeatedly until it lands heads for the first time; if that happens on toss n, you win 2^n/n units of utility when n is odd, and you have to pay 2^n/n units when n is even. Because that's just the alternating harmonic series, it only conditionally converges, so depending on how you group or reorder the very same terms you can make the "expected utility" come out to literally any number you like, including plus or minus infinity, even though nothing about the underlying gamble has changed at all. Theo's whole point was that becuase the payoffs are stipulated directly in utils instead of dollars, Bernoulli's "just apply a concave function to the prize" move isn't even available this time, so this is a way deeper problem for expected utility theory than St. Petersburg ever was. I didn't have a great comeback tbh, and it's been bugging me since.

So here's what I actually want from you (yes I know Bernoulli died in 1782 and this is totally anachronistic, that's the point): if someone sat him down today and walked him through the whole thing -- the Pasadena game, the conditionally convergent series, the bit where literally just re-shuffling the order the payoff cards are stacked in changes the "answer" -- what do you think he'd actually say? Would he treat it as a genuine refutation of using expected utility as the criterion of rational choice, something that forces him to give up ground, or do you think he'd have some way of squirming out of it -- denying the game is a legitimate object of choice at all, insisting some ordering of the sum is privileged, rejecting unbounded utility scales, something else entirely? I'd like you to actually ground this in what we know of his real reasoning and commitments from the Specimen Theoriae Novae paper, rather than just telling me what a generically sensible modern decision theorist would say if you stuck a powdered wig on them.
