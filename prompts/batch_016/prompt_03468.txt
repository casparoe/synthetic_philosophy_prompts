For a seminar on classical Chinese philosophy, we've just finished a unit on how the Zhuangzi handles death. Two passages stood out to me: Master Lai's speech in "The Great and Venerable Teacher," where he compares himself to metal that has no business insisting it must be forged into the legendary sword Moye, and a passage near the end of the text, in the chapter "Lie Yukou," where the dying Zhuangzi rebuffs his disciples' plans for an elaborate burial by pointing out that leaving his corpse to the crows and kites above ground is no different from leaving it to the mole crickets and ants below.

For my response paper, I want to move past the vague claim that "Daoism just accepts death" and figure out where Zhuangzi would actually land on a practice he never anticipated: cryonics, the freezing of the newly dead in the hope of eventual revival. Given his views on transformation and the futility of clinging to any one fixed form, would he most plausibly regard cryonics as an understandable, if misguided, human impulse, or as close to the purest possible instance of the very error he diagnoses in others? Please defend a specific verdict rather than hedging between the two, and engage with the strongest textual evidence that could be used against your reading.
