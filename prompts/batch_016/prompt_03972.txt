I never studied philosophy formally, but over the past year I've gotten interested in Buddhist metaphysics and started reading primary texts instead of just summaries. Right now I'm slowly working through Jay Garfield's translation of Nagarjuna's Mulamadhyamakakarika, and I've hit a wall in Chapter 22, "Examination of the Tathagata."

Nagarjuna runs the Tathagata (a fully awakened Buddha) through the same fivefold search used elsewhere for the self: he is not identical to the aggregates, not different from them, does not contain them, is not contained in them, and does not possess them. Having failed to locate a Tathagata anywhere in that search, he then says we aren't even entitled to call the Tathagata "empty" or "non-empty," or both, or neither, since those are only used as a manner of speaking, and a little later asks, "How can the tetralemma of permanent and impermanent, etc., be true of the peaceful?" On this basis he concludes that it isn't even appropriate to think that the Buddha exists or doesn't exist after passing into nirvana. But then the chapter's very last verse seems to turn around and assert something after all: that whatever the Tathagata's essence is, that is also the essence of the world, and that neither has any essence whatsoever.

What I can't work out is how that closing move is allowed. If refusing all four options about the Tathagata's existence is motivated by the idea that any assertion already smuggles in the kind of independent, self-standing existence Nagarjuna is trying to dismantle, then isn't "the Tathagata and the world are equally without essence" itself a further assertion that should fall under the same ban? Is this a genuine inconsistency on Nagarjuna's part, or is there a recognized way of reading the chapter's ending that keeps it from simply contradicting everything that came before?
