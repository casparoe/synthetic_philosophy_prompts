A friend of mine—we're both second-year econ PhD students—and I spent a long train ride last week arguing about the Ellsberg paradox, and neither of us budged by the time we arrived. You'll recall the classic three-color version: an urn holds 90 balls, 30 known to be red and the other 60 some unknown mix of black and yellow. Most people prefer betting on red over black, yet also prefer betting on "black-or-yellow" over "red-or-yellow"—a pair of preferences that cannot be rationalized by any single subjective probability over the urn's contents, and that jointly violate Savage's sure-thing principle. My friend defended the position you'll find in most decision theory courses: that this pattern reveals ambiguity aversion as a genuine, sui generis attitude toward unknown probabilities, one that forces us to abandon strict subjective expected utility maximization in favor of something like Schmeidler's Choquet expected utility or Gilboa and Schmeidler's maxmin expected utility over a set of priors. I argued the more deflationary line: that an entirely orthodox, single-prior expected-utility maximizer who simply takes seriously the possibility that the experimenter has both superior information about the urn's contents and some incentive to exploit it would rationally shade away from the ambiguous bet in both choices, reproducing the whole pattern without touching Savage's axioms or positing any exotic, non-additive beliefs.

I'd like you to build the strongest possible version of my side of the argument. Please defend the claim that the classic Ellsberg choice pattern can be fully rationalized within orthodox subjective expected utility theory once the decision maker's beliefs about the strategic process generating the "ambiguous" urn are modeled explicitly, so that ambiguity aversion need not be treated as an independent primitive of rational preference at all. I'd especially like you to confront, rather than sidestep, the strongest evidence against this deflationary strategy: the well-documented finding that ambiguity aversion persists even in designs constructed specifically to remove any interested party from the picture—for instance, when the unknown proportion is fixed by a transparent, non-strategic randomizing device, or concerns a real-world quantity with no experimenter standing behind it at all. Tell me plainly whether, and to what extent, your reconstructed argument survives that objection, even if the honest conclusion is that it needs to be narrowed considerably.
