I am a novelist assembling a collection of short fiction organized around scenes of radical incomprehension, and for the piece I am currently stuck on, I would like your help translating a specific idea from philosophy of language into narrative rather than exposition. In reading around Donald Davidson's notion of radical interpretation this past year, what has stayed with me is not the familiar image of a stranger who speaks no English, but the harder claim underneath it: that an interpreter can never fix what someone believes and what their words mean as two separate steps, since the evidence available for each is inseparable from assumptions about the other, and the whole undertaking only gets off the ground if the interpreter assumes—not as a courtesy but as something closer to a precondition of the enterprise—that the speaker is largely rational and largely right about the world (Davidson's principle of charity).

Please write a complete short story—not an outline or a commentary—of roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words, built on the following situation. A woman in her forties returns to care for her father, a retired cryptographer, after a stroke leaves his reasoning apparently undamaged but severs the old mapping between his thoughts and his words, so that he speaks fluently and often urgently in sentences that are grammatical yet no longer track ordinary English usage. No notebook, cipher key, or surviving colleague can serve as a bridge; whatever sense his daughter makes of him, she must build herself, out of nothing but his utterances, his gestures, and the circumstances in which he offers them. Narrate the story in close third person from her point of view, dramatizing how she works out, in tandem rather than in sequence, what he seems to believe and what his private idiom seems to mean, and include at least one moment where her charitable assumption of his good sense is nearly broken by something he says that resists any reading on which it comes out true or reasonable. He spent his working life as a codebreaker, but please resist the pull of turning this into a puzzle with a hidden, determinate solution. The gap the story dramatizes should feel like Davidson's kind of interpretive indeterminacy rather than a cipher waiting to be cracked, and it need not, and probably should not, close completely by the final page.
