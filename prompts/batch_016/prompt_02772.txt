During my working life as a physicist I used Bayesian reasoning all the time, but only in a rough, practical way, without ever worrying much about its philosophical foundations. Since retiring I have been reading more systematically in philosophy of science, and a few weeks ago, in a local reading group, we spent an entire evening on Clark Glymour's "problem of old evidence." As I now understand it, the puzzle runs as follows: on the standard Bayesian picture, a piece of evidence E confirms a hypothesis H for an agent only by raising that agent's credence in H, which in turn requires the agent's prior credence in E to have been less than one. Yet the textbook illustration, which Glymour himself used, happens to be drawn from physics: the anomalous excess precession of Mercury's perihelion — about 43 arcseconds per century, by modern measurement — which Le Verrier had already isolated in 1859, more than fifty years before Einstein's general relativity accounted for it in 1915. Physicists still routinely describe this episode as one of the most striking confirmations general relativity ever received, yet on a strict Bayesian reading a scientist who already assigns the perihelion data probability 1 cannot have their credence in general relativity moved by that data at all.

I gather that the proposed solutions fall into a few families: some, following Garber and Jeffrey, relax the assumption that agents are logically omniscient, so that discovering that the theory entails the evidence becomes itself a piece of genuine news; others, in the spirit of Howson, instead ask us to reason counterfactually about what our credence in the evidence would have been had we not already possessed it. None of this was on my radar as a working scientist, and it unsettles me a little, since I had always treated cases like Mercury's perihelion as paradigms of sound inductive practice, and I gather that similar "old evidence" worries resurface whenever a newer theory is praised for reproducing results that were never really in doubt. Could you set out what you take to be the most important implications of the problem of old evidence, both for how someone with a physicist's working notion of confirmation should now think about historical episodes like Mercury's perihelion, and for whether it is Bayesian confirmation theory itself, rather than our scientific practice or our intuitions about such cases, that ought to give way?
