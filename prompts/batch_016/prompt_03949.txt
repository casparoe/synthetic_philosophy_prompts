I'm about 60k words into a generation-ship novel where the crew's AI eventually proves, to everyone's satisfaction, that the universe is genuinely spatially infinite rather than just really really big, and I want the last act to hinge on what that does to the ship's ethics. I went down a research rabbit hole on infinite ethics so i wouldn't put nonsense in my characters mouths, and I think I've found the exact argument I want my antagonist (a brilliant, burnt-out philosopher-engineer) to make. It goes like this: an infinite universe already contains infinite good and infinite suffering, and since adding or subtracting any finite quantity from infinity leaves it unchanged, no possible action -- yours, mine, humanity's whole future -- changes the total value of the universe by even a little. Saving your kid from a fire and doing nothing to stop a genocide come out mathematically identical -- zero net effect on the cosmic total. There's apparently even a name for this in the literature, "infinitarian paralysis." He's also got a nastier follow-up for when the ship's ethicist tries the obvious "well surely a universe where everyone is happier has to be better" move: picture two infinite futures, one where every person who'll ever live gets 2 units of well-being and one where they all get only 1 -- intuitively the first has to be better, but summed out to infinity both totals are "equal," so apparently even that intuition doesn't survive contact with the math.

My actual problem is writing the ethicist's comeback. I don't want her to win by just refusing to accept a valid argument because it feels bad -- that's cheap writing -- but I can't find an actual hole in the logic either, so as of my current draft her best line is basically "fine, you're technically right, but we have to act as if it matters anyway," a kind of dignified surrender dressed up as pragmatism. Before I commit to that being the emotional climax of the book, I guess what I'm asking is: is dignified surrender really the intellectually honest place for a rigorous character to land here, or is there a sharper, more technical objection a philosopher who actually works on this stuff would have ready, that I'm just not aware of?
