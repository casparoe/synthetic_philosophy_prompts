I don't have any formal background in philosophy or social science, but I've been turning over a disagreement I keep having with friends about whether things like "the economy," "public opinion," or "company culture" are real forces in their own right, over and above whatever individual people happen to be doing and choosing, or whether that kind of talk is just a convenient shorthand that could, in principle, be spelled out entirely in terms of individuals. A friend who studied sociology told me this is basically a version of the old "methodological individualism versus holism" debate, so I went looking for where the individualist side of it was first argued carefully, and that led me to John Stuart Mill's 1843 book A System of Logic, Book VI, Chapter 7, titled "Of the Chemical, or Experimental, Method in the Social Sciences." There is a short passage near the start of that chapter which I must have read ten times now without feeling like I've really understood the argument, so I would appreciate some help working through it.

Here is the passage:

"The laws of the phenomena of society are, and can be, nothing but the laws of the actions and passions of human beings united together in the social state. Men, however, in a state of society, are still men; their actions and passions are obedient to the laws of individual human nature. Men are not, when brought together, converted into another kind of substance, with different properties; as hydrogen and oxygen are different from water, or as hydrogen, oxygen, carbon, and azote are different from nerves, muscles, and tendons. Human beings in society have no properties but those which are derived from, and may be resolved into, the laws of the nature of individual man. In social phenomena the Composition of Causes is the universal law."

What trips me up is the chemistry comparison. Mill seems to be saying that combining people into a society is not like combining hydrogen and oxygen into water, where the result is a genuinely new substance with properties you could not have predicted just from the ingredients; instead, society is supposed to work more like adding up a set of forces that remain fundamentally the same kind of thing throughout, which he calls "the Composition of Causes." But he does not spend much time in this particular passage justifying why social combination has to be the additive kind rather than the water kind, and that concerns me, because I have since come across secondhand summaries of Durkheim making almost the mirror-image move a few decades later, using that very same chemistry idea (a living cell being more than a bag of chemicals, a society being more than a set of individuals) to argue for exactly the opposite conclusion. So I would like help pinning down what is actually doing the argumentative work here: is there a principled basis, perhaps elsewhere in Mill's account of causation, for putting social phenomena on the "mechanical" rather than the "chemical" side of that distinction, or is he really just asserting, via a well-chosen analogy, the conclusion that his individualism already needs? And stepping back, does this passage amount to a real argument for methodological individualism, or more of a promissory note that the reduction to individual psychology can eventually be carried out?
