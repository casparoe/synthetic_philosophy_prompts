I've been working through Henry Sidgwick's Methods of Ethics for a seminar on Victorian moral philosophy, and a footnote about his other life as a psychical researcher sent me down a rabbit hole I haven't quite climbed out of. Methods of Ethics famously ends without full resolution: Sidgwick argues that rational egoism and universal benevolence (utilitarianism) are both self-evident principles of practical reason, yet they can conflict in application, and no further rational principle is available to adjudicate between them when they do. This is his "dualism of practical reason," which he warns will reduce the "Cosmos of Duty" to a "Chaos" unless it can somehow be resolved. The one resolution he keeps returning to, in a broadly Kantian spirit, is to postulate that there is a moral order to the universe -- underwritten, for instance, by personal immortality -- that ultimately harmonizes duty and self-interest.

What strikes me is that Sidgwick did not leave this as a mere thought experiment. He spent decades trying to establish the very same conclusion empirically, as co-founder and repeated president of the Society for Psychical Research: overseeing telepathy experiments, the large-scale Census of Hallucinations, and sittings with mediums such as Leonora Piper, all in search of scientific evidence bearing on survival after death. By most accounts he was also the most careful and skeptical member of his own circle, repeatedly declining to draw the stronger conclusions about survival that convinced close collaborators such as Myers, and he never really reached a settled view. In an 1887 retrospective, he wondered whether, if this "search" turned out to be "a failure," he could still act "consistently" with his "whole view of truth" and make the postulate at all -- worrying that, if not, he might be left with no ethical system whatsoever.

I'd like you to compare these two sides of Sidgwick's project: the philosophical move of postulating a moral order to resolve the dualism of practical reason, and the empirical research program he pursued in the hope of actually establishing that order. Are these best understood as a single undertaking pursued by rationalist and empiricist means respectively, or do they rest on two different, perhaps incompatible, standards of justification -- one willing to assume what practical life seems to require, the other refusing to believe anything not yet adequately proven? Given how inconclusive Sidgwick himself judged the evidence to be by the end of his life, do you think his own standards of intellectual honesty should have obliged him to abandon the postulate of immortality, and with it his proposed way out of the dualism? Justify your answer with reference to the specific methodological commitments he brought to each of the two projects.
