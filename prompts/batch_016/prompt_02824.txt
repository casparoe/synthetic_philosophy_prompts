I write horror novels, so Colin Radford's old puzzle about crying over Anna Karenina has been bugging me for months, especially since I fell down a rabbit hole into Kendall Walton's reply to it. Walton's argument turns on a guy named Charles watching a slime monster on screen: Walton says Charles isn't actually scared, hes got 'quasi-fear' cooked up by a game of make-believe, since a genuinely scared person would run for the exit or call the cops. Thing is, when my own beta readers tell me they had to put the book down and turn a light on, that doesn't feel to them like some watered-down quasi version of fear -- it feels like the real thing. Then theres Lamarque's 'thought theory' crowd, who think Walton overcomplicated it, and that just entertaining the thought of Anna's situation is enough to trigger an ordinary, non-quasi emotion, no make-believe required. And Radford himself basically just shrugged and said fine, we're being a little incoherent, that's what being human is like. I don't want the textbook rundown of the paradox, I've already got that part -- what I want is the gut-level version of why so many philosophers treat Radford's shrug as unacceptable, and whether Walton and Lamarque are actually disagreeing about something real or just putting different labels on the same underlying thing.
