I lead a small team at work that builds LLM-based agents which autonomously chain together tool calls (database lookups, code execution, web requests) to complete multi-step tasks with minimal human supervision. We keep running into a specific, recurring failure mode: the agent takes some action, and several steps later something in the environment has quietly changed in a way that invalidates a plan or assumption formed earlier, but the agent has no principled way of noticing this short of re-verifying everything after every step, which is both expensive and doesn't really solve the problem so much as relocate it. When I described this at a team lunch, a colleague who'd done a philosophy minor said we had basically rediscovered "the frame problem" from classical AI, and pointed me toward McCarthy and Hayes's original 1969 paper.

Since then I've read a bit further and noticed that "the frame problem" seems to refer to at least two related but distinct things: the narrow, logical problem McCarthy and Hayes posed about efficiently representing which facts a formal action doesn't change, and the much broader epistemological problem Dennett lays out in his 1984 essay "Cognitive Wheels," about how any finite reasoner manages to bound the space of things it needs to treat as potentially relevant, without that bounding process itself requiring unbounded reasoning. These strike me as quite different problems that happen to share a name, and I can't tell whether the literature treats them as the same issue at different levels of description or as genuinely separate problems that get run together in casual usage.

Against that background, I'd like your take on the most important implications of the frame problem, in both of these senses, for the design of production LLM agents like the ones we're building. Concretely: is the failure mode we're seeing best understood as an instance of the narrow logical problem, such that better state-tracking or more explicit formal representations of "what changed" could genuinely fix it, or is it closer to Dennett's broader relevance problem, in which case I'd worry that bigger context windows, more retrieval, and more self-reflection or re-planning prompts are just more elaborate ways of not solving it? If it's closer to the latter, I'd be curious whether anything in the philosophy of mind literature after Dennett offers even a partial answer as to what a genuine solution would have to look like, or whether the consensus leans toward this being the kind of problem that gets dissolved by sufficiently different architectures rather than ever solved outright on its own terms.
