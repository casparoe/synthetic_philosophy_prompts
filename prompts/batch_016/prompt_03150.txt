Kripke's skeptical interpretation of Wittgenstein on rule-following (sometimes called "Kripkenstein") has always struck me as one of the more unsettling arguments in analytic philosophy: the idea that no fact about my past behavior, dispositions, or mental states can determine whether, in using "+," I have ever meant addition rather than some deviant "quus"-like function that agrees with addition on every pair of numbers I have actually added but diverges on pairs I have not. I'd like you to write a short story that treats this paradox as a genuine plot engine rather than as background color—closer in spirit to how Borges's "Funes the Memorious" dramatizes the trouble with subsuming particulars under a general concept than to a story that merely name-drops the idea. It should turn on a character, human or otherwise, being confronted with real indeterminacy about which rule they (or someone else) have actually been following all along, and it should take Kripke's "skeptical solution"—that what settles such matters, if anything does, is communal agreement rather than any private fact—seriously, rather than resolving that indeterminacy by authorial fiat.
