I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but I've been reading about the "School of Names" in classical Chinese philosophy and got stuck on Gongsun Long's "White Horse Dialogue" (白馬論, a chapter of the Gongsun Longzi, roughly third century BCE), in which two speakers debate whether "a white horse is not a horse" can be asserted. Below is a paraphrase of the core exchange, not a quote from any single translation, since the original is classical Chinese and translators disagree on the details:

A: Can it be maintained that a white horse is not a horse?
B: It can.
A: On what grounds?
B: "Horse" is the term by which we name a shape; "white" is the term by which we name a color. Naming a color is not the same act as naming a shape, so the compound term "white horse" cannot simply be equated with the bare term "horse."
A: But surely if a white horse exists, a horse exists — a white horse just is one of the horses there are. How can the white one fail to be a horse?
B: Consider what happens when someone asks you for "a horse": a yellow horse or a black horse will do equally well. Now suppose someone asks you for "a white horse": neither a yellow horse nor a black horse will do. If "white horse" meant exactly what "horse" means, the two requests would be interchangeable, and a yellow or black horse ought to satisfy both equally. It plainly satisfies only one. Since what is accepted in one case is refused in the other, the two terms cannot be picking out the same thing. That is what I mean in saying a white horse is not a horse.

B's first move strikes me as little more than a pun: naming a color and naming a shape being "different acts" no more shows that white horses aren't horses than "even" and "prime" naming different properties shows that 2 isn't a number with both. The second argument, concerning requests, is harder for me to dismiss the same way. I think in terms of type hierarchies, so my instinct is that "white horse" is simply a subtype of "horse" — but B's request argument seems to rest on a genuine logical principle rather than mere equivocation, and I can't tell whether it actually holds up. Could you lay out B's second argument as an explicit, premise-by-premise argument and tell me whether it's valid? And, assuming a white horse really is a horse, which premise or step should be rejected — and why is that a principled objection rather than just a way of dodging an unwelcome conclusion?
