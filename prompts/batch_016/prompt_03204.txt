As a novelist, I recently agreed to serve on a five-person jury for a regional fiction prize, with four novels on the shortlist: call them The Salt House, Orchard Light, A Quiet Defection, and The Cartographer's Wife. Each of us ranked all four from best to worst, and we combined the rankings using a simple points system to declare a winner. Afterward, out of curiosity, another judge and I checked the result by comparing the novels head-to-head instead: The Salt House beat Orchard Light, Orchard Light beat A Quiet Defection, yet A Quiet Defection beat The Salt House, so no single novel was actually preferred by a majority to every other one. Stranger still, when we reran the points system after setting aside The Cartographer's Wife, since one judge felt embarrassed to have nominated it, the relative standing of the other three reversed completely, even though nobody's opinion of those three books had changed at all. Most of my fellow judges insist we simply used a flawed method, and that some way of tallying our rankings must exist that captures the jury's true collective judgment. I suspect something deeper is going on. What is actually happening here, and how would you advise us to choose next year's winner?
