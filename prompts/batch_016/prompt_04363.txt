Since retiring after nearly forty years as an experimental physicist working mainly in industrial semiconductor research, I have finally had the leisure to read philosophy properly, and a small reading group of former colleagues has spent the last several months working through Hobbes. One claim of his has stayed with me longer than I expected. In the Six Lessons to the Professors of the Mathematiques, he argues that geometry is demonstrable because the lines and figures we reason about "are drawn and described by ourselves," and that civil philosophy is demonstrable "because we make the commonwealth ourselves." Genuine scientia, on this account, requires knowledge of a thing's actual causes, and such knowledge is available to us only where we ourselves are the makers; everywhere else, including in natural philosophy, we are confined to reasoning from effects back to merely possible causes. This is presumably also behind his remark in Leviathan that geometry is "the only science that it hath pleased God hitherto to bestow on mankind."

A complication has occurred to me that our reading group has not managed to resolve. A good deal of my own work involved fabricating the very samples I then went on to measure, so in some loose sense I "made" my objects of study much as a geometer makes a triangle by drawing it, which none of my colleagues who did field astronomy or seismology could ever say of theirs. Does that bring experimental physics of this kind any closer to Hobbes's demonstrable sciences than he seems to have allowed, or does his argument really turn on something deeper, such as knowledge of matter's ultimate causes, that fabricating one's own samples cannot supply?

Setting that particular puzzle within the larger picture, I would like your view on the most important implications of Hobbes's maker's-knowledge argument, and of the sharp line it draws between geometry and civil philosophy on one side and natural philosophy on the other, for how someone in my position should now regard the epistemic standing of the physics to which I devoted a career. Please commit to and defend the two or three implications you judge most important rather than surveying every possible reading, and say something, too, about whether Hobbes's decades-long insistence to the mathematician John Wallis that he had squared the circle ought to lower our confidence in his instincts about what makes a science certain.
