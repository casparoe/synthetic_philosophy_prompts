I'm auditing a graduate seminar on value theory this term (a friend talked me into it, I'm not even enrolled), and this week's session was about the history of "incommensurable values" as an idea, tracing back way further than the usual Isaiah Berlin / Joseph Raz starting points. The professor had us sit with a passage from Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, Book V, chapter 5 — the section on justice in exchange — as a kind of ancient ancestor of the whole debate. Problem is, my Greek is nonexistent so im stuck working from the (kind of clunky) standard English translation, and I honestly can't tell what Aristotle is committing himself to. Here's the bit we were given:

"This is why all things that are exchanged must be somehow comparable. It is for this end that money has been introduced, and it becomes in a sense an intermediate; for it measures all things, and therefore the excess and the defect — how many shoes are equal to a house or to a given amount of food... All goods must therefore be measured by some one thing, as we said before. Now this unit is in truth demand, which holds all things together... but money has become by convention a sort of representative of demand; and this is why it has the name 'money' (nomisma) — because it exists not by nature but by law (nomos) and it is in our power to change it and make it useless... Money, then, acting as a measure, makes goods commensurate and equates them; for neither would there have been association if there were not exchange, nor exchange if there were not equality, nor equality if there were not commensurability. Now in truth it is impossible that things differing so much should become commensurate, but with reference to demand they may become so sufficiently. There must, then, be a unit, and that fixed by agreement (for which reason it is called money); for it is this that makes all things commensurate, since all things are measured by money."

What's throwing me is that one sentence in the middle: "it is impossible that things differing so much should become commensurate, but with reference to demand they may become so sufficiently." That reads like Aristotle conceding that money doesn't really make radically different goods commensurable in any strict sense, it's just a workable stand-in via demand. But the sentences right before and after keep talking as if money really does make all things commensurate, full stop, no qualification. So which is it? Is Aristotle basically saying value is one-dimensional and money proves it, or is he closer to the modern "hard choices" side (Chang, Raz, that whole literature) where a lot of value comparisons resist any single common scale and money/demand is just a rough patch over that fact? And seperately — is "commensurable" doing the same technical job here that it does in the contemporary incommensurability literature, or is Aristotle making a narrower point specifically about market prices that later readers have projected way more onto? Would love help untangling the actual logical structure of the passage rather than just a general gloss on "Aristotle thought money was a measure of value."
