As a novelist, I keep returning to Borges more than to any other writer, and I've become preoccupied with what looks like a genuine fault line in how he treats time across one of his essays and his most famous story. In "A New Refutation of Time," he borrows Hume's bundle theory of the self to argue that if a given perception recurs in exactly the same way — to one man on separate occasions, or to two different men — nothing is left to certify it as a second, later moment rather than a literal repetition of the first; and once any moment can turn out to be identical with any other, the notion of a single linear succession collapses. Yet the essay ends, famously, with "And yet, and yet..." — an admission that the argument offers him no consolation, since he confesses to still feeling swept along by the very time he has just dissolved. "The Garden of Forking Paths" seems to run in the opposite direction: rather than dissolving succession, Ts'ui Pên's labyrinth-novel has every branch of every decision actually taken, so that incompatible futures accumulate side by side instead of one being chosen and the rest eliminated.

Write a dialogue between two longtime readers of Borges, arguing over coffee after a lecture, who disagree about whether these two pieces express one coherent view of time or two incompatible ones. Have the first contend that the forking-paths proliferation is exactly what the New Refutation's argument looks like once it is dramatized rather than merely asserted — the positive image left over once no single sequence is treated as privileged. Have the second contend that dissolving succession altogether and multiplying it into every possible branch are not two faces of one thesis but opposite metaphysical instincts, so that Borges is best read as trying on each without being answerable to either. Both readers should be equally sharp: have them take up each other's strongest point directly rather than restate their own view, and don't let either side win too easily.
