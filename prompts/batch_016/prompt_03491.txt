While preparing a presentation on the philosophy of language for my school's philosophy club, I came across David Lewis's 1976 triviality result, which shows that no proposition can have its probability equal to the corresponding conditional probability except in degenerate cases. The main line of resistance to this result, originating with Ernest Adams and developed further by Dorothy Edgington, denies that indicative conditionals express propositions at all, restricting the equation between a conditional's probability and the relevant conditional probability to conditionals that appear unembedded, as the main connective of a sentence — since there is no settled probabilistic treatment of conditionals nested under negation, disjunction, or further conditionals. Please argue that this restriction to unembedded conditionals is not a minor technical fix but effectively concedes the original debate: an account of "if" that cannot compositionally handle such embeddings has abandoned any claim to be a general semantics for the English conditional, rather than a semantics for just a limited fragment of it.
