I've spent a long time reading rationalist blogs and forums, and until recently the only Ramsey I knew was the decision-theory Ramsey — the Ramsey–de Finetti representation theorem, Dutch-book arguments for coherent credences, that whole cluster of results. A small reading group I'm part of, most of us originally connected through old LessWrong meetups, decided a few months ago to look past that Ramsey and read the actual "Epilogue" he delivered to the Cambridge Apostles in February 1925 — a few days after he turned twenty-two — later printed as the closing piece of The Foundations of Mathematics and Other Logical Essays. It's the passage where he explains why he isn't troubled by the eventual heat death of the universe, or by human smallness on a cosmic scale. Two people in the group, Owen and Farah, got into a real argument about whether the closing paragraph actually holds together, and having reread it several times since, I still can't settle it myself.

Owen's opening claim was that Ramsey is making a sharp and basically defensible point: cosmic scale has no bearing on value, since, as Ramsey puts it, "the stars may be large, but they cannot think or love," and neither the thrilled nor the depressed reaction to the universe's size and eventual death is more accurate than the other — which is exactly why Ramsey says "neither can correspond to the fact." Farah objected that Ramsey doesn't actually hold that line: in the very next sentences, having just disclaimed that either mood corresponds to the fact, he says "I have reason and you have none," and adds that he can "pity you with reason" — which sounds like a claim that his own reaction is objectively better-founded, the very thing he'd supposedly ruled out a moment earlier. Owen replied that there's no real contradiction, just two senses of "reason" being run together: Ramsey can deny that either mood is theoretically correct, in the sense of getting some fact about the universe right, while still holding that his mood is practically more reasonable, since he explicitly grounds the pity in its being "not merely pleasanter but better for all one's activities" — ordinary practical rationality, not a hidden factual claim. Farah's rejoinder was that this concedes too much to save too little: if all Ramsey is entitled to is that his mood is more pleasant and more useful, that's a fact about his temperament, not an answer to someone who thinks the depressive reaction is the fitting one regardless of its costs — she compared it to thinking grief is the fitting response to a death even though grief is neither pleasant nor useful — and she didn't see what licenses "pity... with reason" on grounds that thin.

Neither of them budged, and I don't think either was being unreasonable. Could you go through the four moves in order and say who has the better of it at each one, rather than just picking an overall winner? I'd also be curious whether Farah's charge of inconsistency survives Owen's theoretical/practical distinction, and whether there's a way of reading Ramsey's paragraph that resolves the tension without reducing his famous cheerfulness to something as deflationary as "optimism feels nice and helps me get more done."
