I spent about 35 years as a forecaster with the weather service before I retired, and lately I've fallen down a rabbit hole reading philosophy, mostly formal epistemology. Funny thing - I keep running into my old friend the Brier score, except its doing philosophical work now that I never expected. Brier cooked that scoring rule up in 1950 basically so forecasters couldn't game their verification stats (say 70% when you really believe 55%, whatever makes your record look better). Now I find Jim Joyce and everyone after him using essentially the same rule (well, any "strictly proper" one) to argue that having probabilistically coherent credences is the only rational option, and they bill it as a "nonpragmatic" vindication of probabilism - accuracy alone doing the work, no decision-theoretic considerations allowed in.

Here's what's bugging me. The whole accuracy-dominance argument only goes through once you've restricted attention to strictly proper scoring rules and thrown out the improper ones as illegitimate. But "strict propriety," as far as I can tell, was never a purely alethic notion - it's a device for rewarding honest reporting, a pragmatic idea straight out of forecast verification, where you obviously don't want forecasters hedging or gaming the system. So doesn't picking out the "legitimate" measures of accuracy on propriety grounds just smuggle the pragmatic considerations back in through the side door, after they'd supposedly been shown out the front?

Tell me if there's an obvious response I'm missing here, or if this is an actual soft spot in the program. Also flag anywhere I've mangled the history or the technical bits - I'm mostly self-taught on the probability axiom side of things. I'd rather know now then keep embarrassing myself at my philosophy reading group.
