I teach a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of dispositions, and this past week we worked through the Martin/Lewis/Bird sequence on finkish dispositions and their relatives. For the following session I asked everyone to invent a test case of their own, mainly to see whether the distinction between the two main kinds of counterexample to the simple conditional analysis had actually sunk in: a fink, where the very stimulus that would trigger a manifestation instead removes or alters the disposition's causal basis (as in Lewis's own sorcerer, who stands ready to change a fragile glass's molecular structure the instant it's about to be knocked over), versus a mask or antidote, where the causal basis is left completely untouched and something else simply intercepts the process on its way to the manifestation (as in the standard Johnston/Bird case of a fragile glass safely packed in protective material). What came back was not tidy homework but a live, unresolved argument between two of my strongest students, Lena and Marco, that has now been running for a week over email, and both have asked me to settle it.

Here is the case that started it, and the four moves that followed. Lena's example: a wine glass is blown from thin, intrinsically brittle glass, but its outer surface is coated in a shear-thickening polymer, the kind used in some protective sports padding, which sits completely inert under ordinary handling and only stiffens -- absorbing the shock for the few milliseconds after an impact -- when the glass is actually struck or dropped, going soft again immediately afterward. (1) Lena classified this as a mask: just as in the Johnston/Bird case, the glass's brittle microstructure, which is the causal basis of its fragility, is entirely unaffected at every moment, before, during, and after any impact, and the coating merely blocks the causal process between striking and shattering, the way an antidote blocks the process between ingesting a poison and dying without changing the fact that a fatal dose was ingested. (2) Marco objected that it can't be a mask, because antidotes and packing material are standing interferers, already doing their protective work independently of the very event that would trigger the manifestation, whereas Lena's coating only springs into action because of, and at the same instant as, the impact -- exactly the temporal signature of Martin's electro-fink and Lewis's sorcerer, both of which work by intervening precisely when, and because, the stimulus occurs. On his view, that alone settles the case for "fink." (3) Lena replied that timing can't be the relevant test, since Lewis and Bird individuate finks and antidotes by what is altered rather than by when the intervener acts: finks change the causal basis, antidotes leave the basis alone and interfere further downstream, and by that test her glass is unambiguously masked, since its molecular structure never changes at all. (4) Marco's comeback was that "never changes" is doing too much work: if the coating's reaction measurably stiffens the glass's outer layers for precisely the interval when it matters, then for that interval, however brief, the glass's dispositional basis is different from what it was a moment before -- and a momentary fink is still a fink, which, he pointed out, was exactly why Martin insisted that his own electro-fink could act instantaneously in the first place.

Neither has budged, and both are now drafting increasingly baroque variants to force the issue, which I'd like to head off before it swallows the rest of the term. Could you go through moves (1) through (4) and say, for each, who has the better of it, or whether it's a genuine draw -- and then say what you think both of them are still missing, either about how the fink/mask distinction is supposed to work in general, or about what, if anything, could actually settle a borderline case like this one?
