I'm a software engineer, and a few months ago I fell down a rabbit hole of AI-safety-adjacent writing on decision theory, starting with Eliezer Yudkowsky's essay "Coherent decisions imply consistent utilities." The core argument seemed clean to me: if your preferences over some set of goods are cyclical (you'll pay a small amount to trade A for B, B for C, and then C back for A), someone can construct a sequence of trades that extracts money from you forever, and this "money-pump" is supposed to be a big part of why we should expect sufficiently rational agents — including future AI systems — to behave like expected-utility maximizers rather than follow some looser policy.

Digging through the comments and citations, though, I found a strand of pushback I hadn't expected, tracing back to a philosopher named Edward McClennen and picked up by others more recently: the pump only works against a "naive" agent who takes whichever trade looks best in the moment. An agent who can see the whole sequence coming, the argument goes, can simply resolve in advance to refuse the very first trade (or to stick to whatever plan it originally settled on), and thereby keep every last cent without its underlying preferences ever becoming acyclic. I also gather that people have since tried designing subtler, "sweetened" or "soured" versions of the trades specifically to catch agents with merely incomplete (rather than cyclical) preferences, though I couldn't really follow why that's supposed to be a harder case to defend against. Having no formal philosophy training, I can't tell whether this "resolute choice" move is a genuine rebuttal to coherence arguments or a kind of dodge.

Could you write me a dialogue — featuring two equally sharp speakers, a Coherence Theorist and a Resolute Choice Defender — that stages this disagreement properly, using a concrete money-pump scenario of your choosing? I want both sides to actually engage with each other's last point rather than talk past one another: in particular, I'd like the Coherence Theorist to press the question of whether "resolving in advance" to refuse a trade you'd otherwise take isn't just smuggling a higher-order kind of consistency back in through the side door, and I'd like the Resolute Choice Defender to have a real answer to that rather than concede it outright. If there's a natural opening for it, I'd also be curious whether the Defender's position is supposed to survive those subtler incomplete-preference pump attempts — but only if it fits organically; I'd rather have a tight, substantive exchange than a comprehensive survey of the literature.
