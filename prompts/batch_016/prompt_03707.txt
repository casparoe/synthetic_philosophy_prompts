So I'm a linguistics PhD student (semantics/pragmatics side of things) and I've been sitting in on a philosophy of language reading group that just spent two sessions on slurs, and honestly I'm still chewing on it. My professor kept calling it "a Hom-vs-Potts split, more or less" — the gist is that the "content" camp says the derogatory force of a slur is just part of its truth-conditional meaning, i.e. the word semantically predicates some ugly, complex property of whoever it's applied to, so a sentence like "X is a [slur]" is literally true or false depending on whether X has that property (Hom's actual version says it always comes out false, since nobody really has the property, but that's a side point). The other camp is more of a conventional-implicature move: the slur and it's neutral counterpart have identical truth conditions and pick out exactly the same people, and all the ugliness sits on a separate, non-at-issue dimension that rides along with the utterance without being part of "what's said."

What I can't get past is that each camp has a data point that's genuinely awkward for it. The content camp has to explain why the derogation survives when you negate the sentence, question it, or embed it under "John thinks that..." — if the ugly part were really at-issue content, embedding should be able to target and neutralize it the way it does for ordinary assertions, but it stubbornly doesn't. The expressivist camp, meanwhile, has to explain reclamation: how a word whose whole job is to conventionally implicate contempt can get picked up by in-group speakers and used with basically the opposite, affiliative force, without that just amounting to a totally different word that happens to be spelled the same.

Could you write me a dialogue — maybe 12-16 exchanges — between a philosopher defending the content view and one defending the expressivist view, hashing this out over drinks after a workshop panel on pejoratives? I don't want either of them to just restate their thesis; I want the content theorist to actually take a real swing at projection without quietly importing implicature-style machinery, and the expressivist to take a real swing at reclamation without just asserting that the implicature "flips" for in-group speakers. Bonus points if one of them brings up cancellability and the other points out that conventional implicatures aren't supposed to be cancellable anyway, so that alone doesn't settle anything. I'd honestly rather they end up sharpening exactly where they disagree than have either one "win," since that feels closer to where the actual literature is stuck.
