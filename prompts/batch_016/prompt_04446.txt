I spent nearly forty years as an experimental condensed-matter physicist (mostly low-temperature transport measurements) before retiring, and it is only in the last few years, free of grant applications and committee work, that I have had the time to read philosophy with any real care. I have joined a small reading group at the local university, and this term we have been working through decision theory, including Newcomb's Problem. I understand the basic lay of the land: the causal decision theorist takes both boxes, since whatever the predictor has already placed in the opaque box is causally fixed by the time you choose, so the extra thousand dollars in the transparent box is simply free money; the evidential decision theorist takes only the opaque box, on the grounds that one-boxing is strong evidence of being the sort of person the predictor rewards with a million. Neither answer has ever fully satisfied me, and ever since the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics went to Aspect, Clauser and Zeilinger for their experiments on Bell's inequalities, I have found myself thinking about the problem in terms I have not encountered in anything we have been assigned.

Below are the notes I have drafted for next week's session, and I would be grateful for your comments before I embarrass myself in front of the group. One way physicists have tried to make peace with the strange correlations found in Bell experiments, without invoking any faster-than-light influence, is to deny what is called measurement independence: the assumption that the hidden state of a particle pair is statistically independent of the experimenter's later, freely chosen measurement setting. Give up that assumption, allow the particles and the choice of setting to share a common cause somewhere in their joint past, and the correlations stop looking mysterious - this is the so-called superdeterminism loophole, unpopular among physicists but logically available. It struck me that Newcomb's predictor is exploiting exactly this kind of structure. Even setting aside whether a literally infallible predictor is coherent, a predictor reliable enough to make the puzzle bite - right, say, in the high nineties out of a hundred - can, on my view, only be that good if the brain-state that will generate your "decision" is already largely fixed at the moment of prediction, so that predictor and chooser are reading off two correlated facts about one and the same history, rather than one causing the other. If that is right, the two-boxer's dominance argument is not wrong exactly, but question-begging: it treats the box's contents as settled while quietly treating your choice as still metaphysically open, when on this picture the two are equally fixed in advance. Taken seriously, this seems to leave one-boxing as the only action compatible with the single, actual history in which the money is really there, so that the puzzle dissolves rather than getting solved, rather as I gather some physicists think the mystique surrounding the "observer" in quantum measurement dissolves once you stop granting experimenters some special exemption from the laws they are testing.

What I would most like your comments on is whether this line of thought earns its keep, or whether I have merely dressed up a familiar confusion in unfamiliar clothes. Does treating a highly reliable predictor as evidence of something like superdeterminism actually support one-boxing, or have I smuggled the conclusion in somewhere along the way? Is the analogy with the measurement-independence loophole doing genuine philosophical work, or is it merely a physicist's redescription of the correlation that evidential decision theorists already lean on? And is there some standard objection that a committed causal decision theorist would raise at once, one I am simply not yet equipped to see? I would much rather be corrected now than at the seminar table.
