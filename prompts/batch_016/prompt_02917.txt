I'm a phil of mind PhD student and for a seminar on the Turing test I actually read all the way through section 6 of the 1950 paper instead of stopping at the imitation game setup like everyone does — turns out objection #9 on Turings own list of objections is literally "The Argument from Extrasensory Perception." He's dead serious about it too: says the statistical evidence for telepathy is "overwhelming," worries that a telepathic participant could ace a guess-the-suit-of-the-card type question and throw the whole test off, and floats fixing this by locking everyone in a "telepathy-proof room." I brought it up with a couple people in my cohort and literally nobody had heard of this bit, which is wild given how canonical the rest of the paper is.

I'd love to see someone actually run with this instead of just treating it as a historical curiosity — could you write me a short story built around it? Think along the lines of how Asimov's robot stories poke at the seams of the Three Laws by constructing edge cases, except here the edge case is: what does an imitation-game-style test for machine intelligence even mean once telepathy turns out to be a real, working phenomenon that the machine obviously doesn't have but a human competitor might. Feel free to set it in Turing's original three-person imitation game or update it to something more modern, whatever makes for a better story. I'm less interested in a tidy resolution and more in watching the mechanics of the test actually strain or break down once minds can leak into each other a little. A page or two is plenty.
