I've been teaching myself Lean over the past few months, mainly to formalize some algorithms for a side project at work, and a couple of weeks ago I went down a rabbit hole I still haven't fully climbed out of. I noticed that a version of the axiom of choice apparently comes "for free" as a provable theorem in dependent type theory, via what's basically a mechanical reshuffling of dependent sum and product types, whereas in ordinary set theory choice is this famous, independent axiom that mathematicians hold surprisingly strong opinions about. Following that thread led me to Errett Bishop's constructive analysis, and from there to something called Diaconescu's theorem, which shows that in a constructive setting, assuming the full axiom of choice lets you derive the law of excluded middle — the very principle constructivists are supposed to reject. Apparently Bishop himself posed a version of this as an exercise in his 1967 book, years before Diaconescu proved it outright in 1975, so it doesn't seem like some obscure edge case that constructive mathematicians could plausibly have overlooked. This strikes me as a far more specific, and more disturbing, objection to choice than the one I already knew about (the Banach–Tarski paradox), and I'd like to understand why it doesn't equally sink the weaker choice principles, like countable or dependent choice, that constructivists seem perfectly happy to keep using.

Rather than a dry explanation, I think I'd get more out of seeing the disagreement actually played out. Could you write a dialogue between a classical mathematician, who accepts the full axiom of choice without reservation, and a constructivist in the Bishop tradition, who restricts or rejects it — say, the two of them run into one another at a blackboard after a seminar and end up arguing well past the point where either has somewhere else to be. I'd like the classical mathematician to lean on the indispensability of full choice (Zorn's lemma giving every vector space a basis and every ring a maximal ideal, the well-ordering theorem, Tychonoff's theorem, and so on) and to press the constructivist on whether their cutoff at countable or dependent choice is really principled, or just a pragmatic line drawn to keep ordinary analysis workable. I'd like the constructivist to bring up Diaconescu's argument directly, rather than leaning on Banach–Tarski, and to give a real technical account of why it threatens full choice without equally threatening the weaker principles they rely on. Neither character should be a pushover or a strawman for the other; I want them genuinely responding to each other's strongest points, even if the conversation doesn't end in a tidy resolution.
