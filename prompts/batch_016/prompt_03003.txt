I'm several chapters into a novel set in London in 1880, and I've reached a scene I don't quite trust myself on. A minor but important character, Edith Growcott, a draper's clerk who has educated herself largely through public lectures and borrowed volumes, belongs to a small radical debating club that meets above a public house in Clerkenwell. The plot requires her to read the club a short paper arguing that Mill, dead some seven years by then, gave away the substance of his own liberalism in his late writings on socialism, above all the Chapters on Socialism that had run in the Fortnightly Review the year before. I drafted her paper mostly from secondary accounts and half-remembered university reading rather than a fresh look at Mill himself, and a friend with a soft spot for Mill took one look and called it "the chapter's weak floorboard," which was not the reassurance I was hoping for. I'd like a more disinterested opinion before I build further scenes on top of it.

Her paper, in full, reads:

"Mr. Mill spent thirty years teaching us that society has no business dictating how a man shall dispose of his own labour and his own capital, so long as he wrongs no one else by it. Yet in his last writings he confesses to Socialist leanings, and looks forward to a time when the labourer works not for himself but for the common stock, and is content to be paid according to his need rather than his exertion. I cannot see how a man may hold both opinions at once. If the distribution of what we produce is to be settled by a plan agreed upon beforehand, rather than by each person's free bargaining in the market, then the sovereignty over himself that he prized so highly has already been surrendered, whatever gradual and voluntary character he attaches to the surrender. His cooperative workshops are only Socialism with better manners: the principle conceded is the same one Owen and the Continental Socialists conceded, and a principle once conceded cannot be walked back merely by insisting that the change come slowly and by consent."

I'd welcome comments on three things in particular: whether Edith's central claim, that the "qualified socialism" of Mill's final decade sits in real contradiction with the liberty principle rather than growing out of it in some considered way, is a position a sharp, well-read contemporary could honestly have reached, or whether it rests on a conflation that a careful reader of the actual chapters would have caught; whether I have the substance of Chapters on Socialism right, independent of what Edith does with it, particularly her lumping together of Owen, the Fourierists, and the revolutionary continental socialists as having conceded "the same principle"; and whether her voice reads as an earnest autodidact wrestling with a genuinely difficult text rather than a modern summary dressed up in bonnet and shawl.
