Three weeks ago my law firm offered our entire document-review team a buyout after concluding that an AI system could now do in hours what used to take a team of associates months; I accepted, and at forty-four I find myself with a comfortable income and no professional obligations for the foreseeable future. Trying to work out what to do with that time, I have been reading Moritz Schlick's 1927 essay "On the Meaning of Life," where he argues that meaning is never found in purposive activity performed as a means to some further end, but only in self-contained activity he calls play, a disposition he associates with youth and which he thought could occasionally fuse with a vocation, as in art or scientific inquiry; he also directly addresses the objection that such a play-centered life reduces us to mere animal existence. Schlick was killed in 1936, decades before anyone could have imagined software capable of replacing complex professional judgment. Reasoning strictly from the actual structure of his argument, how do you think he would assess a world in which automation frees large numbers of trained professionals from purposive labor: would he see it as history finally delivering the conditions for his ideal of a meaningful life, or does his own essay already contain the resources to predict that freedom arrived at this way would fail to produce genuine play?
