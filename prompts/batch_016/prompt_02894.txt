I'm working on a novel set around the 1940 hearing that cost Bertrand Russell the philosophy professorship that the City College of New York's Board of Higher Education had just awarded him, after a parent, Jean Kay, sued on the grounds that her daughter might be morally corrupted by studying under him -- notwithstanding that CCNY at the time admitted no women at all. The judge, John E. McGeehan, obliged her, ruling, among other things, that views expressed in Marriage and Morals and three of Russell's other books rendered him unfit to teach. Russell himself was barred from appearing in the actual proceedings, so the scene I'm building -- a fictional junior member of the philosophy department, Alden Warfield, who was to have been Russell's assistant, testifying in his place and facing a hostile trustee under cross-examination -- has no basis in the historical record beyond its premise. The night before, Warfield drafts a statement in Russell's defense, partly to rehearse it and partly to talk himself into delivering it. I've written the closing paragraph of that draft and would value your comments before I write the hearing scene proper, which turns on whether his position survives contact with a skilled cross-examiner.

Here is the paragraph, in Warfield's voice:

"If tomorrow I am asked to defend the passages that so alarm the Board, I will say this. Russell's critics mistake a vice for a convention, when the truth runs the other way: it is the jealous husband, not the faithless wife, who is the product of custom. Trace the history of marital fidelity far enough back and the whole edifice rests on one anxious question -- whose child is this? -- and on a man's wish to be sure that the heir he raises and the estate he leaves are truly his own; that is why a wife's infidelity was always reckoned the graver offense, since only she could confuse the line of inheritance. Reliable contraception, for the first time in our species' history, severs the act from its consequence, and with it severs the one argument that ever gave marital fidelity its urgency. What remains, once the ledger of legitimate heirs no longer needs balancing, is a smaller and more human question -- not whose seed took root, but how to give children, once they exist, a settled and loving household. On that question Russell has never been anything but conservative. It is only on the question of adult jealousy that he is radical, and his radicalism amounts to this: that a feeling we are trained from the cradle to call natural and inevitable is no more natural or inevitable than the horror our grandmothers were trained to feel at an uncovered ankle, and a generation raised without the training would no more feel it than we feel that."

There are three things I'd welcome your candid comments on. First, fidelity to the source: is this a fair compression of Russell's actual argument in Marriage and Morals, or does Warfield flatten a genealogy that Russell himself weaves from property, Christian asceticism, and evolutionary psychology into a single-strand story about legitimate heirs -- and does he claim an outright disappearance of jealousy that the book itself is more cautious about? Second, independent of exegesis: is the argument any good on its own terms, or does the contraception point do less work than Warfield thinks it does, and is the ankle analogy illuminating or simply question-begging against anyone who suspects jealousy has roots deeper than custom? Third, I intend to have the trustee confront Warfield with the fact that Russell's own second marriage collapsed in no small part because he discovered he could not, in practice, live with his wife Dora's long relationship and two children with the journalist Griffin Barry, for all that he had spent years arguing in print that this particular jealousy was nothing but a prejudice to be outgrown. Is that a genuine philosophical objection to the position Warfield is defending, or an ad hominem wearing an argument's clothes -- and either way, what is the strongest reply available to him? I would rather learn now that the paragraph doesn't hold up than discover it in a second draft.
