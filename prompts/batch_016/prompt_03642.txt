Longtime SSC/ACX lurker here, finally posting something instead of just reading. I've been slowly working through Wealth of Nations this year with a little reading group that spun out of our local rationalist meetup (yes, we are exactly that kind of nerds), and we just hit the "Of Colonies" chapter in Book IV. Someone in the group had hyped it up as the part where Smith basically calls the entire British empire a money-losing vanity project that only benefits a handful of merchants, and it mostly delivers on that. But there's one paragraph near the end that I think is supposed to be the rhetorical climax of the whole argument, and I can feel that Smith is making a sharp, specific point, but I can't quite pin the logic down well enough to restate it in my own words. Hoping someone can walk me through it.

Here's the bit, copied straight from an online edition so the odd 18th-century commas are preserved: "To found a great empire for the sole purpose of raising up a people of customers, may at first sight, appear a project fit only for a nation of shopkeepers. It is, however, a project altogether unfit for a nation of shopkeepers, but extremely fit for a nation whose government is influenced by shopkeepers. Such statesmen, and such statesmen only, are capable of fancying that they will find some advantage in employing the blood and treasure of their fellow-citizens, to found and maintain such an empire. Say to a shopkeeper, Buy me a good estate, and I shall always buy my clothes at your shop, even though I should pay somewhat dearer than what I can have them for at other shops; and you will not find him very forward to embrace your proposal. But should any other person buy you such an estate, the shopkeeper will be much obliged to your benefactor if he would enjoin you to buy all your clothes at his shop."

The part that's got me stuck is the pivot in that second sentence, from "unfit for a nation of shopkeepers" to "fit for a nation whose government is influenced by shopkeepers" — those read like they should basically be the same claim, but Smith is obviously treating them as opposites, and it doesn't feel like he's just being cute with the parallelism. Then the little parable about buying someone an estate in exchange for their shop-loyalty seems to be where the actual argument is hiding, but I keep failing to cash it out into a clean, literal statement of what's going on. My best guess, and tell me if im overreading this, is that its basically a "concentrated benefits, diffuse costs" argument before that phrase existed — no individual shopkeeper would ever pay for a customer's loyalty out of his own pocket, but he's delighted to have the government buy it for him using the "blood and treasure" of people who never see the profit. Is that a fair reading, or am I smuggling in too much 20th-century public-choice framing? Would love a sentence-by-sentence unpacking of that passage, plus maybe how it connects to the bit later on where he flatly says Britain "derives nothing but loss" from the colonies even though they're clearly great for what he elsewhere calls a "particular order of men."
