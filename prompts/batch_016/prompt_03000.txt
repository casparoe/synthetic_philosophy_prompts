After thirty-eight years running an analytical chemistry laboratory, I retired some years ago and have since taken up philosophy as a serious hobby, gravitating mostly toward epistemology and metaphysics, subjects I never had the leisure to pursue during my working life. A conversation with a former colleague at a reunion dinner last month has been nagging at me ever since, and I would value your perspective on it.

Before automated titrators became standard equipment, we used to perform titrations by hand: adding titrant drop by drop from a burette while watching an indicator (phenolphthalein, for instance) shift gradually from colorless to pink, and stopping once we judged the endpoint had been reached. Anyone who has done this many times knows that the color change is never abrupt; it develops so gradually that no single drop looks appreciably different from the one before it, and yet by the twentieth drop past the true endpoint the flask is unmistakably pink. Over coffee, my colleague and I fell into an argument about when, exactly, during that stretch of drops, one can be said to know that the endpoint has already been passed. His position was that if I really know, at some particular drop, that the endpoint has been reached, then I ought also to be able to tell, right there and then, that I know it; otherwise, he insisted, what I have is not knowledge but merely a hunch that later turns out to be correct, and by that standard I never strictly know the endpoint has passed until well after it is obvious to anyone. This struck me as far too strong a conclusion to draw from an everyday laboratory judgment, but I could not immediately say where the argument breaks down — and it seemed to be something more specific than the old puzzle about heaps and vague boundaries, since his complaint was not that "the endpoint" is an ill-defined notion, but that knowing a thing seems to require being able to know, on the spot, that one knows it.

Was my colleague right that my inability to certify my own knowledge in the moment shows that I never really possessed it? Or can one coherently come to know a gradually approached fact of this sort without always being in a position to know that one knows it? I would be most interested in your assessment of the underlying argument itself, rather than in any comments on titration technique as such.
