Im working on a piece about this idea philosophers call "moral trade" -- basically, if two people disagree about ethics they can still cut a deal that makes the world better by *both* of their own lights, without ever agreeing on who's actually right. Classic setup: someone who thinks factory farming is a serious wrong (but isn't too fussed about foreign aid) offers to put $500 toward an anti-malaria charity if a friend -- who thinks global health is the cause that actually matters, and eats meat without a second thought -- agrees to go vegetarian for six months. Nobody has to concede anything philosophically, they each just get more of what they already wanted.

I tried this example on two friends of mine, Nadia and Ben, over dinner last week and it derailed the whole evening. Roughly how it went:

1. Nadia: seems fine to me, its just gains from trade applied to ethics.
2. Ben: no, it's closer to selling indulgences -- if the meat-eating friend really thinks factory farming's not his problem, paying him to stop for six months isn't moral progress, it's just buying compliance.
3. Nadia: we accept mixed motives everywhere though -- tax breaks for charitable giving, companies paying cash to employees who quit smoking -- and we don't say the resulting good doesn't count just because there was an incentive attached.
4. Ben: sure, but here the "buyer" is literally pricing how much more wrongdoing they're willing to tolerate. If you actually thought something was a serious wrong, you wouldn't haggle over the dollar figure for six more months of it.
5. Nadia: that's not pricing the wrongness, it's being realistic about what changes behavior once moral arguments alone haven't worked -- how is that different from a plea deal, or reparations, which also put a price on wrongdoing and which we treat as legitimate?
6. Ben: the difference is leverage. Whoever has more money gets to buy the version of morality they want, so at scale this just lets rich people purchase moral cover while everyone else's convictions are up for auction.

Neither of them gave any ground and honestly I could see both sides. Can you go through this move by move -- who's got the better argument at each step, not just who wins overall -- and flag anything you think both Nadia and Ben are missing?
