Here's a kind of niche bit of bargaining theory I want an argument on. In some recent game theory / AI-safety-adjacent work there's a notion of a "safe Pareto improvement" (SPI): a way two parties can change how they handle a dispute so that no matter what their original strategies were, they both end up weakly better off -- e.g. agreeing that if negotiations break down they'll settle things via a cheap simulated conflict instead of an actual costly one, without touching anyone's actual demands. People pitch these as a big deal because, unlike classic bargaining solutions (Nash bargaining and friends), SPIs supposedly let you get a Pareto improvement without the two side ever having to agree on some notion of what's "fair."

Except for almost any real dispute there's more than one SPI you could apply, and nothing guarantees both parties land on the same one -- this is sometimes called the SPI selection problem.

Argue that this selection problem is actually fatal to the "don't need to agree on fairness" selling point of SPIs: that choosing which SPI to adopt is itself just the original fairness/coordination problem showing back up, so SPIs don't dodge that problem, they just relabel it one level up.
