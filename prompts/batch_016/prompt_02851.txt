I'm a retired analytical chemist (35 years in the lab, give or take) and now that I've got free time i've finally started reading actual philosophy instead of just method papers. Been going through Benjamin Franklin's autobiography and got kind of hung up on the "moral perfection" chapter - hung up in a good way. Wrote up some notes on it and would love a second opinion before I take this any further - don't just be nice about it, tell me if I'm off base.

Here's the gist of what I've got:

Franklin's "bold and arduous project of arriving at moral perfection" is basically a self-experiment run with lab discipline. Thirteen virtues, each given a one-line operational definition (Temperance: "eat not to dullness; drink not to elevation"), deliberately sequenced so mastering one is supposed to make the next easier. A little notebook ruled into a grid, days across the top, virtues down the side, a black mark for every failure, one virtue getting concentrated attention per week so the whole 13-week course repeats four times a year. Quantified self-tracking, two and a half centuries early.

What actually grabbed me isn't the method though, it's the moment he catches himself gaming it. Order was the virtue he could never nail, and he tells a story about a man having an axe ground who gives up halfway through and decides he likes it "speckled" rather than fully bright. Franklin offers this as a cautionary tale about people who quit improving themselves - and then admits the same "reasoning" was working on him: that perfect orderliness might be "a kind of foppery in morals," that a flawless character risks "being envied and hated," and that a benevolent man should allow himself a few faults "to keep his friends in countenance." He seems to only half-trust this as genuine reasoning rather than an excuse, and yet he goes along with it anyway, at least for Order.

My hunch is that Franklin backed into something like Susan Wolf's "moral saints" argument - the idea that relentlessly optimizing your own moral character might actually make you a worse, less likeable person - about two hundred years before she wrote it up properly.

Does that comparison actually hold, or am I bolting together two things that don't belong? Is there a more standard philosophical reading of the "speckled axe" bit that I'm missing, something about practical wisdom or satisficing rather than Wolf's point specifically? And is my summary of what Franklin was doing even fair, or am I flattening something important?
