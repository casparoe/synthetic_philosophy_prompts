Last night, a friend and I got into a long argument about how to read a cluster of Pascal's fragments on justice and force in the Pensées, and neither of us gave ground. One fragment (297 in Brunschvicg's numbering) says, almost cryptically: "Veri juris. — We have it no more; if we had it, we should take conformity to the customs of a country as the rule of justice. It is here that, not finding justice, we have found force, etc." The very next fragment is the famous one: "Justice without might is helpless; might without justice is tyrannical... being unable to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just." And elsewhere in the same run of fragments, arguing against Montaigne, Pascal adds: "It would therefore be right to obey laws and customs, because they are laws; but we should know that there is neither truth nor justice to introduce into them, that we know nothing of these, and so must follow what is accepted."

My friend took these three together as proof that Pascal was, underneath the piety, a straightforward cynic: there is no justice beyond whatever force and custom happen to stabilize, and talk of a further, "true" justice is idle. I argued the wording resists that reading: "we have it no more" and "we know nothing of these" are claims about our epistemic situation, not claims that there is nothing to know, and that on Pascal's own view of reason as corrupted since the Fall, this is exactly the gap we should expect if justice were real but unknowable rather than illusory.

Write an essay adjudicating this dispute. Weigh the textual and philosophical evidence for each reading as carefully as you can, and defend a conclusion about which reading -- or what third option -- is most defensible, rather than treating it as a draw.
