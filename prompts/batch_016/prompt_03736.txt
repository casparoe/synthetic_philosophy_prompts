I'm an electrical engineer who has recently been asked to rewrite our facility's shock-hazard training materials, and doing so sent me back to a philosophy course I took years ago. Explaining why a ground-fault circuit interrupter (GFCI) is a fundamentally different safeguard from ordinary insulation made me realize the two map onto a distinction philosophers draw when analyzing dispositions like fragility: a GFCI protects you by cutting power to a wire within milliseconds of a fault, so the wire genuinely stops being live at the very instant it would otherwise shock you, whereas insulation leaves the wire exactly as live as before and merely blocks the shock from reaching anyone who touches it. I recall these are called 'finks' and 'masks' respectively, and that they're treated as counterexamples to defining a disposition purely in terms of what would happen if its trigger condition were met. Could you lay out, in real depth, what precisely distinguishes a finkish disposition from a masked one, why each is taken to refute the simple conditional analysis of dispositions, and whether David Lewis's well-known attempt to repair that analysis in response to finks actually survives the problem of masking as well?
