I am part of a small reading group that has been alternating between classic papers in value theory and material from the AI alignment literature, and a recent session paired Eliezer Yudkowsky's 2004 essay on coherent extrapolated volition (CEV) with two older papers on idealized-preference accounts of well-being that we had read months earlier. Nobody in the group had drawn the connection before, but once it was pointed out it seemed hard to unsee: CEV cashes out what a superintelligent AI should be built to want as, roughly, what humanity would converge on wanting if we knew more, thought faster, and had in some sense matured further together, with the initial dynamic meant to act on points where individual volitions show strong "coherence" and to hold back wherever they show significant "spread" or "muddle." That is structurally the same idealizing move that Brandt, Railton, and others proposed for individual well-being decades earlier, defining a person's good in terms of what their fully informed, fully rational counterpart would want them to want.

The two papers we read were David Sobel's "Full Information Accounts of Well-Being" and Connie Rosati's "Persons, Perspectives, and Full Information Accounts of the Good," and both argue that this idealizing move is far less innocent than it looks. Sobel's worry is that there may be no coherent way to construct a single, determinate "fully informed and rational" advisor in the first place, since the available ways of building one, such as having it live through every alternative option from the inside versus judging them from the outside on propositional information alone, tend to generate inconsistent or radically incomplete verdicts rather than one authoritative verdict. Rosati's worry is different: even granting a well-defined idealized counterpart, it is unclear why that counterpart's preferences should be authoritative for, or even meaningfully continuous with, the actual person's good, given how alien from the actual person the idealized version might become.

I would like to know what you take to be the most important implications of these two objections for CEV specifically, as distinct from their implications for the individual-level theories of well-being they originally targeted. Does moving from idealizing a single person to idealizing all of humanity at once, and building in machinery for tolerating spread rather than forcing convergence, give CEV real resources for escaping either worry, or does it just relocate the same problems to a higher, harder-to-inspect level, trading a mysterious individual ideal advisor for an even more mysterious collective one? And if the objections survive largely intact, is there a way of weakening or reformulating CEV that would blunt them without discarding what made CEV an attractive alignment target in the first place?
