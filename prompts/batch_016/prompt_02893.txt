The setup comes from this old legend where a guy runs into Death in the Damascus market, Death gives him some kind of startled look, he panics and figures Death's coming for him, so he rides all night to Aleppo to escape. Except Death's already there waiting, and says (basically, i'm paraphrasing) that the look wasn't a threat, she was just surprised to see him in Damascus earlier since she already had an appointment to meet him in Aleppo that same night. The decision-theory version turns this into an actual choice problem: Death is like a near-perfect predictor of which city you'll pick, you have to choose stay-in-Damascus or flee-to-Aleppo (fleeing costs a bit extra, money/exhaustion/whatever), and you die if you end up wherever Death predicted you'd go.

What got us actually arguing is that apparently this isn't just "causal decision theory gives a weird answer" like in Newcomb's problem — my friend claims causal decision theory basically breaks here, because whichever city you settle on, the second you've settled on it the logic flips and makes the other option look better, so you just flip-flop forever and never land on a stable ("ratifiable," i think was the word he used) choice at all. Meanwhile evidential decision theory apparently just calmly says "stay in Damascus, don't bother paying to flee, it doesnt change your odds either way." We could not agree on whether that flip-flopping thing actually sinks causal decision theory as a whole or whether its totally fine/even correct for a theory to admit there's no stable answer in a case like this.
