One argument that keeps coming up is the "coherence argument": the idea that any sufficiently advanced, goal-directed agent that doesn't want to be exploitable (i.e., "money-pumped") will end up behaving as though it is maximizing some expected-utility function, on pain of violating one of the four von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms — completeness, transitivity, continuity, and independence. I brought this up recently with a friend from grad school who actually studied philosophy, and we ended up disagreeing about something more basic than the argument itself: whether the four axioms are equally hard to reject. He was fairly relaxed about giving up completeness — he'd seen it floated as a possible route to building AI systems that don't resist being shut down, since their preferences over certain outcomes would simply stay undefined — but treated any violation of transitivity as an obvious, decisive mark of irrationality. Neither of us could settle whether that asymmetry is actually well-argued or just a matter of philosophical habit.

I'd like your help thinking this through properly. Based on that, rank the four axioms from the one that is hardest to justify giving up to the one that is easiest to justify giving up, for an agent whose main concern is not being money-pumped.
