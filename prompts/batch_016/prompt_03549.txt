I spent thirty-five years as a seismologist, much of it on probabilistic seismic hazard assessment, which meant a good deal of my working life was devoted to assigning single numbers to things I only half understood, forcing thin and incomplete data into point probabilities because a hazard curve has to say something in the end. Since retiring I have been filling some of my time reading backward through the history of probability theory, partly to see whether anyone had already put into words the unease I used to feel about that practice. This led me, a few months ago, to Keynes's 1921 Treatise on Probability, which I confess I had previously known only as a footnote to his economics. I was surprised to find that he illustrates his notion of the "weight of argument" — the idea that the completeness of the evidence behind a probability judgment matters over and above the probability itself — with a thought experiment about drawing balls from urns of imperfectly known composition, and that he explicitly denies that probabilities always form a single, fully comparable ordering.

Reading further, I learned that Ellsberg's own doctoral dissertation — submitted at Harvard in 1962 and published as a book only decades later, as Risk, Ambiguity and Decision (2001) — explicitly recognizes Keynes's notion of "weight" as akin to what Ellsberg was calling ambiguity, and that some commentators now treat Keynes's urn example as a precursor of the Ellsberg paradox in its own right. Yet Keynes died in 1946, a full fifteen years before Ellsberg's famous 1961 paper on the Savage axioms, so he could have known neither Savage's formal framework nor the whole subsequent industry of axiomatic "ambiguity-averse" decision theory that grew out of it: Gilboa and Schmeidler's maxmin expected utility, Schmeidler's Choquet expected utility theory, and the later "smooth ambiguity" model of Klibanoff, Marinacci and Mukerji.

I have no formal training in philosophy or decision theory, so what I would like from you is a reconstruction, argued as carefully as you can manage from the actual content of the Treatise rather than from Keynes's general reputation for being "skeptical of probability": how do you think he would have reacted, first, to Ellsberg's specific experiment and its violation of Savage's Sure-Thing Principle, and second, to the formal machinery later built to accommodate findings of that kind? Would Keynes have read this literature as a vindication of his claim that probability need not be numerically precise or fully comparable, or would he have objected that these models, by locating ambiguity aversion in preferences over bets, are still performing exactly the sort of subjective, behaviorist reduction of probability that his own logical theory — probability as a relation between evidence and conclusion, rather than a disposition to bet — was meant to resist? I would also be curious whether, in your assessment, "weight of argument" can be reconstructed within any one of these later formal models, or whether it is after something none of them were ever designed to capture.
