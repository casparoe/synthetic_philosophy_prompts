A few of us on my team have an ongoing lunchtime debate about Nick Bostrom's simulation argument, and last week it took an unexpected detour into philosophy of language. One colleague, who did a philosophy minor before switching to software engineering, claimed that Hilary Putnam had already dealt a serious blow to the whole simulation hypothesis decades before Bostrom, in his 1981 book Reason, Truth and History. Apparently Putnam's "brains in a vat" argument doesn't try to rule out the scenario empirically at all; instead, it uses a causal theory of reference to argue that the sentence "I am a brain in a vat" is self-refuting, so that no envatted brain could ever truly think or say it.

I looked into the argument a bit afterward and got stuck on two things. First, I don't see how a claim about what our words refer to — something about how a brain's word "vat," spoken in what philosophers apparently call "vat-English," only hooks onto features of the simulating computer rather than onto real vats — is supposed to settle the actual metaphysical question of whether we're being simulated, rather than just being a clever point about language that leaves the metaphysics untouched. Second, my colleague mentioned that the argument only works if you've been in the vat for your entire existence, and that it doesn't apply to a brain that lived a normal life and was only recently envatted, but she couldn't explain why that distinction matters, or how it would even map onto a scenario like Bostrom's, where we'd have no way of knowing whether a simulation (if we're in one) started at the Big Bang or five minutes ago.

Could you describe the most important implications of Putnam's semantic argument for how seriously I should take the modern simulation hypothesis? I'd especially like you to address whether the argument gives us any real grounds for believing we're not simulated, as opposed to merely showing that we couldn't coherently assert that we are; how the "always envatted" versus "recently envatted" distinction is supposed to work, and whether it has any real analogue in simulation-theory discussions; and where, in your view, the argument is most exposed to criticism.
