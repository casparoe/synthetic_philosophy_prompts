Simone Weil's 1943 essay "On the Abolition of All Political Parties" argues that parties should be eliminated from political life altogether rather than merely reformed. Her case rests on three claims she treats as plain empirical facts about how any party operates, regardless of its ideology: a party is a mechanism for manufacturing collective passion in order to mobilize supporters; a party exerts constant pressure on its own members to fall in line with its platform instead of exercising independent judgment; and a party's deepest, most basic aim, prior to any specific programme, is simply its own unlimited growth. On Weil's view, these three features make every party totalitarian in embryo, whatever its stated values, because they subordinate the individual pursuit of truth and justice to the party's collective self-interest. Tellingly, though, her proposed remedy is not a ban on political organizing as such: she wants any group that crystallizes into a formal, dues-and-membership-rolls organization legally suppressed, while she is willing to tolerate looser, informal circles of debate gathered around journals and similar publications, so long as they stop short of anything like official membership or electoral endorsements.

I've been assigned this essay for a political theory seminar, and the professor wants us to come prepared to discuss whether Weil's framework says anything useful about phenomena she could not have anticipated in 1943. The case I can't quite resolve is the modern online political "side": a loose, leaderless cluster of social media accounts, with no dues, no membership card, and often no identifiable leadership, that nonetheless visibly manufactures intense collective passion, enforces conformity through pile-ons and shaming, and, thanks to engagement-maximizing recommendation algorithms, grows itself more or less automatically, without anyone in particular intending or directing that growth.

Working only from the specific mechanisms Weil actually identifies, rather than from generic talk of polarization, reconstruct as carefully as you can how she would most plausibly classify this phenomenon. Does it belong on the tolerated side of her distinction, as an informal, non-crystallized circle of debate, or does it in fact reproduce everything she found totalitarian about parties while dispensing with the formal apparatus she thought was doing the damage? And does her own argument, in particular the idea that a party pursues its own growth as a goal, covertly presuppose an organized, intending agent in a way that a leaderless, algorithm-driven tribe cannot satisfy, such that her framework would need real revision, and not merely extension, to reach a verdict here? Indicate clearly where you think her argument continues to hold and where you think it runs out.
