For a paper I'm writing for my school's philosophy club, I've been comparing epistemicism and supervaluationism as rival responses to the Sorites paradox. I understand the basic divide: epistemicists hold that there is a sharp, if unknowable, cutoff for a vague predicate like "bald," while supervaluationists deny that borderline claims are true or false at all. What I can't quite reconcile is that supervaluationists still insist a sentence like "Bob is bald or Bob is not bald" is true in a borderline case, even though they deny that either disjunct is individually true. Doesn't that violate the basic principle that a true disjunction needs at least one true disjunct? Could you explain how supervaluationists justify holding onto this and other classical logical laws while abandoning bivalence, and how that move differs in spirit from how epistemicists keep classical logic fully intact?
