So I'm writing a piece about the endless originalism-vs-living-constitution fights in American law, and my editor wants some kind of punchy "what would a historical figure think of this" hook, which sounded a little gimmicky to me until I went down a rabbit hole on Frederick Douglass and realized he actually had a surprisingly detailed, idiosyncratic theory of constitutional interpretation of his own. Apparently he spent about a decade as a committed Garrisonian, agreeing that the Constitution was basically a slaveholders' bargain (Garrison's own line was that it was "a covenant with death and an agreement with hell"), and then in 1851, under the influence of Gerrit Smith and Lysander Spooner, he flipped completely and started arguing that, read correctly, the Constitution was actually an antislavery document. He gave the fullest version of the argument in an 1860 speech in Glasgow, and his actual method is pretty specific: he insisted that "the paper itself, and only the paper itself, with its own plainly written purposes, is the Constitution" -- meaning the framers private intentions and whatever horse-trading happened in the convention room don't count for anything unless it shows up plainly in the text. Combine that with the fact that the document never once uses the word "slave" (it hides behind stuff like "persons held to service or labour"), and you get his signature move: any genuine ambiguity has to be resolved on the side of liberty, because an unjust reading shouldn't win by default. He also leans hard on the Preamble's declared purposes -- justice, the general welfare -- as the lens for reading everything else. And yet in other spots he talks about the Constitution's meaning as something that should keep pace with the progress of civilization, which honestly sounds a lot more like living-constitutionalism, so im not sure the guy is as tidy a case study as my editor wants.

Anyway, here's my actual question, and feel free to get into the weeds -- I already know what originalism and living constitutionalism are in the textbook sense. Given that specific, kind of idiosyncratic mix -- hardcore textual literalism, real hostility toward "original intent" evidence from outside the four corners of the document, a moral thumb on the scale for liberty, but also some openness to the document's meaning developing over time -- what do you think Douglass would make of constitutional argument today? Would he look at Scalia/Thomas/Gorsuch-style "original public meaning" originalism and see his own method reflected back at him, or would he think something essential is missing from it? And specifically, how do you think he'd compare something like Gorsuch's opinion in Bostock, which is textualist to the bone but lands on a very progressive result, to Dobbs, which claims the same originalist mantle but leans on "history and tradition" to reach a conservative one -- are those two applications of a single method in his eyes, or would he say only one of them is actually doing what he was doing?
