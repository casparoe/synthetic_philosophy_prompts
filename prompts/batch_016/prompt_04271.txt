for my philosophy of education seminar we just read Whitehead's 1922 lecture "The Rhythm of Education" (it got folded into Aims of Education later) and theres a tension in it i can't untangle.

Early on he sets up his big three stages like this:

"I think that Hegel was right when he analysed progress into three stages, which he called Thesis, Antithesis, and Synthesis; though for the purpose of the application of his idea to educational theory I do not think that the names he gave are very happily suggestive. In relation to intellectual progress I would term them, the stage of romance, the stage of precision, and the stage of generalization."

Then he spends most of the lecture describing kids moving through romance -> precision -> generalization in pretty definite chunks (romance roughly ages 8-12, a hard pivot to precision from 12-15, etc). But right at the end he adds this almost as an afterthought:

"My second caution is to ask you not to exaggerate into sharpness the distinction between the three stages of a cycle... romance, precision, generalization, are all present throughout. But there is an alternation of dominance, and it is this alternation which constitutes the cycles."

I can't figure out how to hold both of these in my head at once. If it's a real Hegelian dialectic, that implies discrete, sequential moments where the second stage actually negates the first. But "alternation of dominance" among three things "present throughout" sounds like three continuously overlapping tendencies, not a sequence at all. And separately, in what sense is precision even an "antithesis" to romance? Negation is supposed to cancel or oppose the thesis, but precision seems more like it just narrows romance down rather than opposing it.

Is Whitehead being sloppy here, or is there a reading where the Hegel comparison and the closing caveat actually fit together? Have a short paper due on this so I want to actually understand the structure, not just gesture at it.
