Descartes' argument for the real distinction between mind and body partly rests on an indivisibility claim: the body is by nature divisible into parts, but introspection reveals the mind to be simple and without parts. He reused this very premise in his correspondence to argue that the soul's seat in the brain must be the pineal gland — the one midline structure that isn't duplicated left and right the way the hemispheres, eyes, and ears are — reasoning that an indivisible soul requires an undivided point of attachment to the body.

Split-brain surgery raises a complication he never had occasion to consider. Suppose Descartes were briefed on the full subsequent history: Sperry and Gazzaniga's classic finding that callosotomy patients can display two dissociated streams of perception and intention, and then Pinto et al.'s (2017) reopening of the question, reporting that some complete split-brain patients can in fact act on information from either visual half-field with either hand — evidence they read as undivided consciousness despite divided perception, which Volz and Gazzaniga have instead attributed to cross-cueing between the hemispheres rather than genuine unity.

Write the response you think Descartes would actually give, reasoning strictly from his own premises rather than from contemporary philosophy of mind — the aim is to pass an ideological Turing test for Cartesian dualism, not to strawman it. Does his pineal gland hypothesis already anticipate and defuse the split-brain challenge, since callosotomy severs the corpus callosum but leaves the unpaired pineal gland untouched? Or would the classic dissociation findings, taken at face value, force him to give up the soul's indivisibility?
