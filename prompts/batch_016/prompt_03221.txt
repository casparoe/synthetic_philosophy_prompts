Ok weird question but it's been stuck in my head since last week and I can't shake it. At work we're building a little dedup tool for our CRM (small startup, nothing fancy, just a Postgres table of customers), and we hit a row pair that matched on literally every single field we store: first name, last name, DOB, home address, phone, email, even middle initial. My first instinct was just merge them and close the ticket. But one of our data scientists pushed back in the Slack thread and said something like: technically we can't be sure it's one person, because it's at least logically possible there are two seperate humans - twins, or a father and son sharing a name and a house - who happen to match on every column our schema is capable of recording. That's the part that sent me spiraling past the actual bug. Forget our messy real data for a sec - even in some idealized case where you had total certainty that two things agreed on literally every property there is, with zero exceptions, would that logically force them to be one and the same thing? Or could two things, at least in principle, be complete duplicates in every respect and still genuinely be two things instead of one?

I brought this up with my housemate (humanities PhD, so she loves this stuff), and she got really animated and said it reminded her of some famous debate from one of her seminars, but she was a couple drinks in and couldn't reconstruct the actual argument. I already fixed the real bug btw, we just added a manual review queue, so no need to help with that part. What I'm actually curious about is the conceptual question sitting underneath it: is my data scientist onto something genuinely substantive, or is he just dressing up a boring "our schema doesn't capture enough columns" problem as something deep? And if it's a real puzzle and not just an artifact of sloppy data, what's the strongest argument on each side?
