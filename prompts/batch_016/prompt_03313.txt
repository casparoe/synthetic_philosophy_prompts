I'm a poli sci PhD student working on medieval and early modern resistance theory, and I've been stuck for like a week on two Aquinas passages that are supposedly consistent but read to me like they're pulling in opposite directions. My advisors on sabbatical this term so I can't just corner her about it, and everything I've found in the secondary lit just asserts the two texts are compatible without actually walking through why, which isn't super helpful when I'm the one who'll have to defend this in front of my committee eventually.

Passage one is from his Commentary on the Sentences (Book II, dist. 44, q. 2, a. 2), where he works through a scenario borrowed from Cicero: a ruler who seized power "by violence and ruse," whose subjects never consented to him and have "no recourse open to a superior who might pronounce judgment upon the usurper." Aquinas's verdict: "he that kills the tyrant for the liberation of the country is praised and rewarded." That reads like a pretty clean green light for private citizens to kill a tyrant, given the right circumstances.

Then in De Regimine Principum (Book I, ch. 6), written later on, he says something that sounds like close to the opposite about tyranny in general: that moving against tyrants "is an action to be undertaken not through the private presumption of a few, but rather by public authority," and that private individuals who take it on themselves to kill even a genuinely tyrannical ruler create a situation that "would be dangerous for the multitude as well as for their rulers." There's no carve-out mentioned anywhere for the "no recourse to a superior" scenario. The move I keep seeing gestured at, but never actually argued for, is that he must mean two different kinds of tyrant here – a usurper with no legitimate claim to rule vs. someone who came to power legitimately but governs badly – such that only the first can licitly be killed by a private person. Problem is, I can't point to where either passage actually says this, so it feels like I'd be doing Aquinas's work for him rather than reading it off the page. Is there real textual warrant for that distinction, is this just an earlier-vs-later shift in his thinking across his career, or is there some other principle reconciling these that I'm missing? I'd genuinely rather have someone walk through the texts with me than just be told "scholars usually resolve it by saying X," since that move is exactly what I'm suspicious of.
