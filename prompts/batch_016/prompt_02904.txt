So I'm working on a longer piece about how "falsifiability" gets thrown around as a rhetorical weapon in public fights over evolution, and a philosopher of biology I interviewed last week mentioned, almost as an aside, that Karl Popper's actual position on Darwinism is way messier than how he usually gets cited by either side. I went and did some digging afterward and now I'm kind of stuck on the details.

As I understand it, at some point in the mid-70s (I think its in Unended Quest?) Popper argued that "survival of the fittest" basically collapses into "survival of the survivors" -- so natural selection, taken as a strict law, is almost tautological and therefore not really falsifiable -- and on that basis he called Darwinism a "metaphysical research programme" rather than a proper testable scientific theory. Creationists have been mining that exact line for decades to claim Popper thought evolution wasn't science. But then a couple years later he gave a lecture -- "Natural Selection and the Emergence of Mind," I believe it was called -- where he says flat out that he's changed his mind about the theory's testability, like an actual recantation.

What I can't quite untangle, and where I'd love the in-the-weeds version rather than the soundbite, is three things. First, what was actually doing the philosophical work in the original tautology complaint -- is it just a cheap point about "fittest" being defined circularly in terms of who survives, or is there something deeper about the logical structure of selection as a theory that makes it genuinely hard to falsify? Second, when Popper recants, is that a real reversal, or is he more quietly reformulating the theory (building in independently specifiable fitness criteria, say) so it escapes the tautology charge without him ever admitting the original worry was confused rather than substantive? And third -- this is the part that really gets me -- Popper leaned incredibly hard on natural selection as the model for his whole theory of how knowledge grows, with conjectures as variation and refutation as selection, "survival of the fittest theories" and all that. If he was worried the biological version was too tautological or untestable to count as solid science, why did he build so much of his epistemology on that exact analogy? Is there a real tension there, or does "metaphysical research programme" mean something more specific and less dismissive in Popper's own framework than it sounds like to modern ears?
