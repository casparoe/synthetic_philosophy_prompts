Ok so for my calc class we have to do this whole "mathematician as a person, not just their math" project, and I picked Gödel because, obviously, incompleteness theorem, cool guy. But I went way down a rabbit hole and found out that in 1961 he wrote his mom this series of letters explaining why he believed they'd see each other again after death — he even uses this specific German word, "Wiedersehen," instead of some big religious term. And it's genuinely not a religious argument, its a logic one. His reasoning is basically: the world is rational and orderly (thats the whole assumption behind science working at all), and if the world really is rational all the way through, it can't just produce a person with this huge amount of potential for growth and learning and relationships, and then let them die having barely used any of it — he compares it to pouring a house's foundation and then abandoning the whole project to rot. That would mean the universe is pointless, which breaks the "rational" premise, so there has to be some further existence where all that unused potential actually gets to happen. The part that messes with my head is that the argument needs our lives to be unfair and cut short, since thats literally his evidence — a complete, satisfying life wouldn't give him anything to point to.

I brought this up with my friend (she's very "the brain is just meat, no soul" about everything) and she said Gödel obviously just really didn't want to die and dressed it up in logic to feel better, which felt like too easy a dismissal given we're talking about maybe the most rigorous thinker of the century. I want actual back and forth, where the skeptic attacks specific parts of the argument (like "the world is rational" or "unfulfilled potential = waste") instead of just going "no evidence," and Gödel has to really respond to those specific attacks instead of just repeating himself.
