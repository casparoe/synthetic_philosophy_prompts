As a journalist covering science and ideas, I'm interested in a pattern that recurs across political and cultural commentary: when a critic turns out to be guilty of the very fault they condemned in someone else, we brand them a 'hypocrite' and treat their original accusation as discredited -- as though hypocrisy doesn't just make someone a worse person, but actually disqualifies them from leveling the charge at all. Philosophers who write on the 'standing to blame' take this question seriously, distinguishing sharply between whether an accusation is true and whether its author has the standing -- the moral authority -- to make it. Write an essay that weighs the strongest arguments on both sides of whether a critic's hypocrisy can genuinely undermine their standing to blame, as distinct from being merely a separate flaw in their own character. Engage in particular with the hardest case for your position -- someone who once shared the fault they now condemn but has since sincerely reformed -- and close with a clear, defended verdict on if and when hypocrisy defeats standing, rather than a hedge that merely says 'it's complicated.'
