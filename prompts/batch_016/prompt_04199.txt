I spent just over thirty years as an experimental particle physicist before retiring, and I've been using the extra time to finally read some of the philosophy of science I never got around to during my working life. I started with Popper's The Logic of Scientific Discovery and then moved on to Lakatos's essay on the methodology of scientific research programmes, which a former colleague recommended on the grounds that it fits the actual practice of physics far better than naive falsificationism does: none of us ever dropped a theory the moment a single anomaly turned up, and the real question was always whether a whole programme was still generating fresh, testable insight or just being patched up to survive another day. This reading has landed me squarely in the middle of the demarcation problem, by way of a dispute I kept encountering in the popular science press: whether string theory, and the landscape of possible vacua that gives rise to the string-based multiverse, still deserves to be called science, given how far its distinctive claims sit from anything we could hope to test experimentally. I even sought out the Ellis and Silk piece in Nature arguing that treating such theories as exempt from experimental verification undermines the integrity of physics, and Richard Dawid's String Theory and the Scientific Method, which makes the case for the "non-empirical" arguments physicists actually use to justify their confidence in string theory regardless.

What I can't settle for myself is whether my unease is just old-fashioned Popperian purism, or something that survives even on Lakatos's own, more forgiving terms. If I have him right, part of what makes a research programme "progressive" rather than "degenerating" is that it keeps throwing off novel predictions that are later confirmed -- and by that standard, string theory's track record over several decades looks rather thin, whatever its mathematical elegance. That makes me suspect a committed Lakatosian who wants to defend string theory's scientific credentials is in a genuinely tougher spot than someone who simply wants to dismiss Popper as too crude for real science.

Could you write me a dialogue between two philosophers of science who trained together but have since ended up on opposite sides of this question -- one a strict Popperian falsificationist, the other someone sympathetic to Lakatos's picture of scientific progress and to the kind of non-empirical arguments Dawid offers on string theory's behalf? Imagine them running into each other at a conference dinner after a keynote on the subject, and have them argue it through over the course of the meal. I'd like them to be evenly matched, genuinely responding to whatever the other has just said rather than to a weaker version of it, and in particular I'd like the Lakatosian to confront the novel-predictions problem head-on rather than simply retreating to the claim that Popper was too strict. If, by the end, they can at least agree on what would have to be true of string theory for it to count as good science by each of their own standards -- even without agreeing on whether that condition is actually met -- so much the better.
