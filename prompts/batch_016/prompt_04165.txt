I am developing a graduate seminar on the epistemology of markets, and next week's session pairs Aumann's agreement theorem with the no-trade theorems it later inspired in financial economics, particularly the result due to Milgrom and Stokey. While drafting my lecture notes, I found myself glossing the "common knowledge" condition that does the real work in these proofs along the following lines: two risk-averse Bayesians who share a common prior cannot rationally continue to see a trading opportunity in one another once they have fully disclosed their respective private signals to each other, since at that point each would simply be conditioning on the same pooled body of evidence and would have to arrive at the same valuation. On this reading, the theorems formalize something fairly unsurprising: that full mutual disclosure dissolves any purely informational motive for disagreement or trade.

I mentioned this gloss to a colleague in the economics department who works on games of incomplete information, and she was adamant that I had the mechanism backwards — that this is exactly why these results are usually presented as surprising rather than banal. On her account, the unraveling occurs even when neither party learns anything about the other's underlying signal or reasoning, provided only that their posteriors, or their standing willingness to trade at some price, become common knowledge between them. This suggests that "common knowledge," in the relevant sense, need not involve any exchange of evidence at all, but I have not been able to reconstruct, to my own satisfaction, why common knowledge of a bare opinion or a bare willingness to trade — entirely apart from whatever reasoning or evidence lies behind it — should be enough to force agreement or eliminate the incentive to trade. Could you help me see exactly where my original gloss goes wrong, and reconstruct what is actually doing the work in these theorems?
