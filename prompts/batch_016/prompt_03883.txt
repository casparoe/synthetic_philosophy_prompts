I've recently been working through the Sleeping Beauty problem in the philosophy of probability. In the classic version, a subject is put to sleep and, depending on the toss of a fair coin, is woken either once or twice, with her memory of any earlier awakening erased before each subsequent one; on every awakening she is asked what credence she should place in the coin having landed heads. What I find most interesting is not the basic setup but how stubbornly the disagreement between "halfers" and "thirders" persists even once both sides agree on every fact about the scenario, since they simply weigh the same agreed-upon facts differently. I'd like to see that persistence dramatized rather than explained all over again.

Please write a short story, somewhere around 800 to 1200 words, set in a near-future laboratory that studies self-locating belief through whole-brain emulation. A volunteer's mind is scanned, and the flip of a fair coin she never gets to observe determines whether the lab will then run a single emulation of her or ten thousand emulations in parallel. Each emulation is isolated from the others, carries identical memories up to the moment of the scan, and wakes with no way of telling, from the inside, how many of her currently exist. Every emulation, immediately upon waking, is asked to state her credence that she belongs to the many-copies branch rather than being the sole instance. Write the scene from inside the perspective of a single waking emulation as she reasons her way toward an answer. Give genuine, unresolved weight both to the consideration that the coin itself was fair, which seems to fix her credence at one-half, and to the consideration that almost every copy who ever wakes in this experiment does so in the many-copies branch, which seems to pull her credence close to one. Let the story end on the credence she states, without having the narration settle which consideration should have won.
