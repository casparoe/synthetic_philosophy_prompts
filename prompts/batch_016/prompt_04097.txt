For my philosophy of religion seminar, we've just finished discussing David Lewis's short paper "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" (1997). Lewis's central observation is that our practice looks inconsistent: we deny that a devoted friend's serving someone else's prison or death sentence ever gives any reason to let the actual offender off (a "debt of punishment," unlike a monetary debt, has no creditor to be repaid - on any standard account of punishment's point, it is specifically the offender who must suffer), yet we don't object when a friend pays someone else's fine, even a serious one. Lewis argues that this shared condition of being, in his words, "of two minds" about penal substitution - not only among Christians who explain the Atonement this way, but among all of us - gives such Christians a kind of tu quoque against their critics, and counts as weak evidence that penal substitution might make sense somehow, even though no one can yet say how.

Write a dialogue between two philosophers of religion working through this argument together. One should try to show that the fines case admits of a principled explanation that leaves the underlying disanalogy between debt and desert intact, so that the tu quoque fails and penal substitution remains an incoherent theory of the Atonement; the other should argue that no such principled line can be drawn, so that Lewis's charge of inconsistency - and the conceptual room it opens for penal substitution - survives. Have them respond to each other's actual arguments rather than talking past one another, and don't let either concede more than the dialectic really forces.
