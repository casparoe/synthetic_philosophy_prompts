So we did the classic dollar auction demo in my behavioral econ seminar last week and the fallout is still going on among my friends. For anyone who doesn't know it: the prof auctions off a $20 bill to the whole room, bids go up in $1 increments, and whoever bids highest wins the $20 — except BOTH the highest and second-highest bidder actually have to pay whatever they last bid. Apparently some Yale guy invented this as a party trick back in the 70s, and there's a famous version some Harvard professor runs every year on his MBAs where his record is something like $200+ paid for a single $20 bill, which still kinda blows my mind. In our class it wasn't that bad, but two of my friends, Theo and Nadia, ended up being the last two standing, and it escalated until Nadia dropped out at $27 and Theo "won" the bill at $28 — so he was down $8 overall and she was down $27 for literally nothing.

At dinner afterwards they got into it about whether they'd each been irrational, and honestly they're both making decent points so I can't referee it myself. (1) Nadia's opening claim is that the whole thing was just the sunk cost fallacy / escalation of commitment in action — you keep raising your bid because of how much you've already put in, exactly the irrational pattern from the reading. (2) Theo says that mischaracterizes what was going through his head: at the point where his last bid was $26, he wasnt thinking about that $26 at all, he was comparing two live options going forward — drop out and be down $26 for sure with nothing to show, or bid $28 and in the worst case be down only $8 — and given where he already stood, bidding actually was the better option, so it's not sunk cost reasoning, it's forward-looking. (3) Nadia's comeback is that the exact same comparison is available to whoever's in second place at any price at all, with no natural stopping point, and that a "rule" which always tells you to bid again, and which if you both follow it leaves you both way worse off than if neither of you had bid past $20 in the first place, can't be what we mean by rational — he's just redescribing the trap, not defending himself from it. (4) Theo's last move is to half-concede this: fine, maybe once you're already several rounds into it there's no bid that beats bidding again, but then the actual "mistake," if there was one, has to be located earlier, in the decision to counter-bid at all once the price passed $20 — not in any of his individual bids afterward, each of which, given his position at the time, really was his least-bad option.
