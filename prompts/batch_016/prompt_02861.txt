I've spent years following effective-altruist and rationalist writing on "cluelessness" as an objection to consequentialism, and a disagreement about it broke out between two people at my local EA reading group after we'd covered Hilary Greaves's paper on the subject. I wasn't part of the argument, but I'd like an outside verdict on it.

(1) Caleb opened by arguing that cluelessness bites harder against near-term giving than against existential-risk work. Saving a life through a charity like AMF predictably shifts population size, and population effects are Greaves's paradigm case of "complex" cluelessness, since more people is ambiguous in sign. Extinction avoidance doesn't have that problem, he said, because almost any plausible view of value ranks a large future over none, so the sign of its direct effect is comparatively secure.

(2) Naomi answered that the asymmetry doesn't survive contact with Andreas Mogensen's "maximal cluelessness" argument, which says the same inscrutability that makes AMF's population effects unrankable also infects the indirect effects of x-risk work -- e.g., which lab a safety intervention advantages in a capabilities race, or how it reshapes state incentives. And even setting Mogensen aside, she added, "a large future beats none" isn't the consensus Caleb is treating it as: on suffering-focused or other population-ethical views, more lives isn't straightforwardly good, so extinction-avoidance's own sign is contestable too.

(3) Caleb replied that this proves too much: if cluelessness runs that deep, it doesn't just erase his asymmetry, it erases anyone's basis for preferring one cause over another -- Mogensen's own conclusion is that you needn't even prefer AMF to Make-A-Wish -- so Naomi can't use the argument to favor x-risk field-building over AMF either, since her own preferred cause is undercut by the identical move.

(4) Naomi conceded that cluelessness at the limit doesn't single out any specific favored cause, but said the lesson isn't "so go back to ranking by expected value" -- it's "so stop ranking by fine-grained expected value at all" and adopt some more permissive decision rule instead. Under such a rule, she said she'd still lean toward cheap, reversible, option-preserving bets, like funding AI safety field-building, over a large one-off transfer to a single object-level intervention.

(5) Caleb's rejoinder was that "cheap" and "reversible" are doing unearned work: cheap by what metric, if not some notion of expected cost, and reversible in what sense, given that redirecting marginal donations away from a proven intervention has its own hard-to-reverse opportunity costs? If she's really taking her own permissive rule seriously, it licenses her preference for field-building no more than it licenses his original extinction-avoidance intuition -- so she's landed on a preferred resting point, not a principled one.

Please go through moves (1)-(5) and say who's right about what -- don't just hand down an overall winner. Flag any point where someone trades on an ambiguity, quietly re-imports the assumption they were supposed to be arguing against, or draws a conclusion their own cited source doesn't support. Then say what you think is the strongest consideration neither of them raised.
