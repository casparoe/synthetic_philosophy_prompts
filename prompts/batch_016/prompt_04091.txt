My school recently started requiring everyone to lock their phones away for the whole school day, and at philosophy club this turned into a surprisingly technical argument that never really got resolved. We'd just read an excerpt of Clark and Chalmers's paper on the extended mind, plus a page or two from one of the standard replies to it, when two members, Devon and Naomi, ended up arguing about whether the new policy is philosophically defensible. I'm reporting their exchange as best I can reconstruct it, and I honestly don't think either of them was simply wrong.

(1) Devon argued that a phone most students check constantly for contacts, directions, and reminders meets Clark and Chalmers's own conditions for when something outside the skull counts as part of a cognitive process: it's reliably present, easily consulted, and automatically trusted once consulted. So confiscating it for seven hours isn't just taking a tool away — it's closer to switching off part of a student's memory.

(2) Naomi answered that Devon was confusing mere reliance with actual composition — what the reply we'd read calls the "coupling-constitution fallacy": something's being dependent on a process doesn't make it part of that process, any more than a sandwich is part of digestion just because digestion depends on it.

(3) Devon replied that the analogy fails, since cognitive states, unlike digestive ones, are normally individuated by the functional role they play rather than by material makeup — so if retrieving a fact counts as memory when it happens on neurons, there's no principled reason to deny it that status when it happens on a phone and plays the identical role.

(4) Naomi's response was that the roles aren't actually identical once you look closely: biological memory is colored by mood, fades along a forgetting curve, and gets distorted through reconstruction, while a saved contact entry never does any of that. So the "sameness of function" Devon needs is only rough, and it breaks down once function is individuated finely enough.

I'd also like to know whether there's a standard move in this literature showing that Devon and Naomi are both missing something.
