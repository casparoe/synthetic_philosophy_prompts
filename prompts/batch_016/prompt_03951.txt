I'm taking a seminar on decision theory this term, and we have spent the last few weeks on the money-pump argument for the transitivity of preference. The setup: an agent with cyclic preferences (A ≻ B ≻ C ≻ A) starts out holding A. She is then offered a sequence of trades — A for C, then C for B, then B for A minus a small fee — and at each step her preferences recommend accepting, until she ends up back at A, but strictly poorer than when she started. The standard rejoinder discussed in class is that this only indicts naive, myopic trading: an agent who knows the whole sequence of offers in advance and reasons by backward induction can foresee where the cycle is heading and simply refuse to trade further once continuing stops being worthwhile, thereby escaping exploitation while holding onto her cyclic preferences.

Please argue against this rejoinder by defending the claim that foresight and flawless backward-induction reasoning cannot, in general, save a cyclic-preference agent from being money-pumped: for some suitably redesigned sequence of offers, even such an agent can still be exploited. Make the case as concrete as possible rather than purely abstract — an explicit scheme of offers, together with an account of why backward induction would recommend walking straight into it, would be more convincing than a general argument alone. Please also address the natural worry that backward induction may not be a trustworthy guide to action at choice-nodes that, by its own prescriptions, a rational agent should never actually reach. I want a direct, well-defended verdict, not a survey of the considerations on both sides.
