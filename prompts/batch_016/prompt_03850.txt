Last friday a bunch of us from the department went for drinks after a job talk, and two of my PhD students, Marcus and Priya, ended up hashing out the fine-tuning argument for like half an hour while the rest of us just watched -- I've taught this stuff in my phil of religion seminar for years and even I couldn't tell you who won. Marcus opened with the standard argument: a bunch of physical constants (cosmological constant, strength of the strong force, etc) have to sit in an absurdly narrow range for any life-permitting universe, and that's much better explained by a designer, or by there being tons of universes with different constants, than by one universe where the dial just happened to land right by chance. He pre-empted the obvious reply with Leslie's firing squad case: fifty expert marksman all somehow miss you, and saying "well I had to survive to notice that, so there's nothing to explain" is a terrible response, you'd still suspect a fix. Priya ran that objection anyway -- the anthropic one -- arguing that any observers, in any universe, only ever find constants compatible with their own existence, so once you factor in that you had to exist to even ask the question, there's nothing surprising left over. Marcus threw the firing squad back at her: you also had to survive to ask why you survived, and that doesn't make the bribed-marksman explanation of your survival any less obviously better than the lucky-miss explanation, so her selection effect isn't doing the work she wants. At that point Priya switched tack -- fine, she said, it needs explaining, but going from "our universe is fine-tuned" to "therefore there are probably tons of other universes" is a textbook inverse gambler's fallacy: it's like inferring, from someone rolling double sixes at a craps table, that they must have been playing all night, when actually each roll is independent and the one roll tells you nothing about how many others happened. Same with universes, she said -- if each one's constants get set independently, ours being fine-tuned is no evidence at all that others exist, so Marcus can't lean on the multiverse horn of his argument.

Nobody at the table could settle it and I've been going back and forth ever since. Could you go through the exchange move by move -- Marcus's opening argument, Priya's anthropic objection, Marcus's firing-squad rejoinder, and Priya's inverse-gambler's-fallacy point -- and say who has the better of it at each stage? I'm especially interested in whether Priya's last move actually works: is inferring a multiverse from fine-tuning really the same structure as the dice case, or is there a relevant disanalogy? And if she's right that it is the same fallacy, does that complaint boomerang back onto the design horn of Marcus's original "designer or multiverse" disjunction too, or does design escape it for some principled reason?
