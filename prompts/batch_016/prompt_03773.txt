After thirty-five years as a research biologist working mainly on the evolution of feathers and flight, I retired three years ago and have been filling some of the free time by finally reading philosophy properly, through a small reading group organized by my university's continuing-education programme. We recently spent two sessions on the treatment of animal sacrifice in Maimonides' Guide for the Perplexed, and it provoked me into drafting a short piece of my own, reproduced below, for the next meeting. Before I inflict it on the group I would like an outside opinion, particularly on whether I have represented Maimonides fairly and on whether the central comparison I borrow from my old field does real philosophical work or merely decorates the argument.

Maimonides argues, in Guide III:32, that God never wanted or needed animal sacrifice as such, but permitted and redirected it because the Israelites, like every nation around them, had never known worship in any other form; an abrupt demand that they worship through prayer and contemplation alone would have been, in his own analogy, like weaning an infant onto solid food in a single day rather than gradually. In III:46 he tries to make this concrete: he derives the specifics of the sacrificial code from what he took to be the actual practice of the surrounding Sabian star-worshippers, reconstructed largely from a text called the Nabatean Agriculture. Herd animals, for instance, were eligible for sacrifice precisely because the Egyptians and others revered them as deities, so that offering them up demonstrated their ordinariness. Given my background in biology, I could not help reading all this as an early statement of what we would now call exaptation: a structure retained from an earlier context and repurposed rather than discarded, because starting over is neither available nor adaptive, in the way feathers evolved for insulation and only later were co-opted for flight. But the disanalogy struck me as at least as important as the analogy: exaptation is blind, an incidental by-product of selection acting on whatever variation happens to be lying around, whereas Maimonides' God does the repurposing with the pedagogical endpoint fully in view, which makes the process resemble a scaffolded curriculum far more than natural selection.

The piece then raises three difficulties. The first is Nahmanides', pressed directly against this passage in his commentary on Leviticus 1:9: Cain, Abel, Noah, and Abraham were all sacrificing long before any specifically Israelite encounter with idolatry existed for God to be weaning anyone away from, and in any case reducing the cult to anti-idolatry pedagogy trivializes the Torah's own language of a pleasing offering, as though the institution existed only to humor the ignorant. Maimonides' likeliest reply, that none of those earlier sacrifices were commanded, seemed to me to rescue the letter of the theory at some cost to its spirit, though I am not sure I am being fair to him. The second difficulty is closer to home: Maimonides' own law code has the messianic Temple restoring the sacrificial order down to its particulars (Hilchot Melachim 11:1), which sits awkwardly with the Guide's picture of sacrifice as a stage of religious development we are meant to be outgrowing. The third is more historiographical than theological: the Nabatean Agriculture is itself a text of contested date and authorship, so Maimonides may be anchoring his account of the Torah's accommodation to a particular historical milieu on evidence no firmer than the text it is meant to illuminate.

I would like genuine pressure-testing rather than reassurance. Is this exposition accurate to Maimonides, or have I simplified either his position or Nahmanides' response past the point of fairness? Does the exaptation comparison illuminate something a philosopher would not already have a sharper term for, or is the disanalogy about foresight serious enough that I should drop the comparison rather than merely flag its limits? And of the three difficulties, which would you judge most damaging to Maimonides' broader project of finding reasons for the commandments, and is there a way of reconciling them that I have overlooked? Tell me plainly where the argument is weakest, not just where it is adequate.
