Please describe the most important implications of consequentialist cluelessness for how I should think about a decision I make every year.

I've spent a long time reading the EA Forum and LessWrong, and one debate that keeps nagging at me is the "cluelessness" objection to consequentialism. I understand the basic shape of it: James Lenman's original worry that we can almost never trace out the full downstream consequences of an act, and Hilary Greaves's reply that this only really bites for what she calls "complex" cluelessness, where we have some asymmetric reason to expect an effect to skew in a particular direction but no way to quantify it, since "simple" cluelessness cases can be handled by treating unforeseeable effects as washing out in expectation. What I can't quite get past is a further wrinkle from Andreas Mogensen's Global Priorities Institute work on "maximal cluelessness": the suggestion that this complex cluelessness problem bites hardest exactly where longtermists most need it not to, namely in assessing the far-future effects that are supposed to dominate the expected value of interventions like AI safety research.

This isn't a purely academic worry for me. I set aside roughly the same sum every year to split between a GiveWell-recommended global health charity and a longtermist organization, on the rough theory that the bounded, comparatively well-understood good of the former should be weighed against the vastly larger but radically uncertain good of the latter. Given the tension above, I'm no longer sure that framing is even coherent: if complex cluelessness afflicts the far-future effects of AI safety work at least as much as it afflicts the more distant ripple effects of bednet distribution, it isn't obvious that either side of the ledger comes out ahead, or even that there's a well-defined expected value to compare in the first place. Please lay out the most important implications of this for how someone in my position should actually go about dividing a giving budget between neartermist and longtermist causes. I'm especially interested in whether taking cluelessness seriously gives any principled reason to favor one horn over the other, or whether it instead counsels some quite different response, such as leaning on robustness heuristics, worldview diversification, or abandoning strict expected-value comparisons for this sort of decision altogether.
