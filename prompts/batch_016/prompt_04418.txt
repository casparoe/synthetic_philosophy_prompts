I've been chewing on something for a few weeks now and my writing group is sick of hearing about it, so hoping you can actually help me settle it. I'm about two thirds through a novel with a first person narrator who lies to pretty much everyone around him but, because we're stuck inside his head the whole book, keeps accidentally tipping the reader off to the truth through what he doesn't say. I want that to work by having him "flout" maxims left and right (if I'm using that word right — I did one semester of a linguistics minor a hundred years ago, so I'm dangerous but not reliable on this stuff). Anyways, the thing that's been bugging me is this: I also ran into the idea, I think from Searle, that dialogue in a novel isn't actually a "conversation" in Grice's sense at all — that the characters exchanges are pretended speech acts, and the real, sincere communicative act is happening one level up, from me to the reader. If that's true, I can't tell whether Grice's whole setup (the cooperative principle, the maxims, that bit where implicature depends on the hearer recognizing the speaker's intention) is describing anything that actually happens between my characters, or whether it only ever applies at the author-reader level, with the in-scene "conversation" just being scaffolding for that.

So: what are the most important implications of this for how implicature actually works in written dialogue? Like, does it change anything about how deliberately I can use flouting a maxim vs. quietly violating one vs. having a character just opt out of answering, once you take seriously that the "hearer" who has to recognize my intentions is really the reader and not the other character standing in the room? I'm especially curious whether some of Grice's classic distinctions stop doing real work in fiction, or start collapsing into each other, once you're honest about where the actual speech act is happening.
