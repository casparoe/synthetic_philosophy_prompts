Retired physicist here (spent about 35 years doing condensed matter stuff), and since retiring I've fallen into reading the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy for fun, which honestly nobody warned me was a bottomless pit. Lately my little book club of fellow retired-scientist types has been chewing on the free will literature, Frankfurt cases specifically. The basic idea I get: in Frankfurt's original case, Black wants Jones to shoot Smith, and he's got some way of making sure it happens if Jones doesn't do it on his own, but since Jones goes ahead and decides to shoot Smith anyway, Black never actually has to intervene. Frankfurt's point is that Jones still seems responsible even though, thanks to Black lurking in the wings, Jones couldn't have done otherwise. So much for the "Principle of Alternative Possibilities," or so the argument goes.

Where I got stuck was a few rungs further down then I expected, in the "dilemma defense" that Kane and Widerker raise against this (I gather it's also called the prior-sign dilemma). As I understand it: for Black to know ahead of time that he won't need to step in, there has to be some "sign" that tips him off to what Jones is about to do. But then either that sign is itself causally sufficient for Jones's decision, in which case the whole example is secretly assuming determinism, which just begs the question against a libertarian who never granted that assumption to begin with, or the sign is merely a reliable-but-not-guaranteed indicator, in which case Jones could, strictly speaking, still have decided otherwise even after the sign showed up, so the alternative possibility was never actually eliminated. Its a neat little trap, if I've got it right, and it's apparently kept a lot of very smart people busy for the better part of three decades now.

So here's what I'd like from you: invent a brand-new Frankfurt-style case -- not a reskin of Jones and Black, please, I've seen enough of those -- that's specifically built to slip between those two horns. Walk me through the mechanics in enough detail that I can actually see how your intervener knows what's coming without either (a) relying on a sign that's causally sufficient for the decision, or (b) relying on one that's merely probabilistic. Then, in a short closing bit, tell me plainly whether you think your case actually escapes the dilemma, or whether -- like I gather happens with some of the fancier "buffer" cases people have proposed -- it just relocates the problem to an earlier point in the story.
