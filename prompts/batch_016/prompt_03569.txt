I've been a fairly close reader of the Alignment Forum since around the time the "Eliciting Latent Knowledge" report came out, and I recently went back through both the original document and the follow-up post on the ELK prize results — partly to see how much of that 2021–2022 builder–breaker back-and-forth has been overtaken by more recent empirical work on model honesty (linear probes, contrast-consistent search, and the like), and partly just to refresh my own sense of which proposed strategies actually held up best. One thing that struck me on the reread is that, by the report's own account, every strategy ARC considers is eventually met with a counterexample — none is declared an outright winner against the breaker. But the counterexamples clearly don't all land with the same force: some feel like they expose a genuinely fundamental obstruction to distinguishing a direct translator from a human simulator, while others read more like open engineering problems that a cleverer regularizer might still get around.

I'd like a second opinion on how these stack up against each other. Here are nine of the strategies discussed across the report and the prize results, each meant to push training toward a direct translator rather than a human simulator:

- Penalizing the reporter for complexity, on the reasoning that direct translation should be a simpler function to compute than simulating an entire human
- Penalizing the reporter for requiring extra computation on top of whatever the predictor itself already performs
- Penalizing the reporter for depending on "downstream" (late) nodes of the predictor's Bayes net rather than earlier ones
- Penalizing the reporter for staying coherent when the underlying predictor is perturbed or swapped out for a different one
- Penalizing inconsistencies in the reporter's answers across pairs of nearly identical inputs
- Having a human operator, rather than only a human observer, generate the training labels from inside the SmartVault
- Using imitative generalization ("learning the prior") so that humans adopt an improved world-model before any labeling happens
- Training an auxiliary model that must find the reporter's internal activations "useful," so the reporter is rewarded for encoding decision-relevant facts rather than a simulation of human expectations
- Requiring the reporter's latent space to be continuous, so it can't flip its answer at exactly the threshold of what a human observer could detect on camera
