I'm a novelist currently at work on a longer piece set among the Cambridge circle that founded the Society for Psychical Research, and Henry Sidgwick has become the character I keep circling back to rather than treating as background color. Having worked through the 1906 memoir his brother and widow assembled after his death, along with a good number of the old SPR Proceedings, I've become fascinated less by the figure familiar from ethics survey courses than by the man who spent something like three decades sitting with mediums, testing claimed telepathists, and compiling case files on apparitions, trying to settle a question that his own rigor as a philosopher seems to have made him constitutionally unable to decide by an act of will — even though his peace of mind quite plainly depended on the answer.

I'd like you to write a short story, roughly 1000–1500 words, built around a single self-contained episode drawn from this life. You might use the sittings of the mid-1870s that left him disillusioned, the unmasking of a celebrated pair of telepathy subjects who turned out to be working from a signal code, the years spent quietly gathering testimony for what the Society called its census of hallucinations, a sitting late in his career with a well-known American medium, or some other piece of the real record you judge more useful — I would rather you chose the scene than have me dictate it. What I would like the story to earn, without ever stating it as a moral, is why a man who had already concluded, in his own published work, that reason alone cannot close the gap between a person's private good and their duty to others without some further fact about how the universe is ultimately arranged, would treat this research not as a diversion from his real thinking but as its continuation.

One request on tone: please resist the gothic-pastiche register that stories about Victorian ghost-hunting tend to default to. I would rather have something closer to a quiet, exact character study — alive to the comedy of eminent Cambridge men soberly searching a séance room for hidden wires and trapdoors, but never losing sight of how much its narrator (whether Sidgwick himself, or someone watching him closely) actually had riding on the outcome — and willing to end in the same unresolved place where, by all accounts, he seems to have spent most of his working life.
