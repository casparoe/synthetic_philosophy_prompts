Random one, but I've been thinking about this a lot lately because I built myself a dumb little app that's supposed to block Twitter and YouTube after 9pm so I actually get side-project time in, and I still find ways around it constantly (incognito mode, "just checking one notification" that somehow turns into 40 minutes, etc). My housemate is doing a philosophy MA and told me this is literally one of the oldest problems in philosophy, akrasia, aka weakness of will, and that economists have basically their own version of it called hyperbolic discounting -- like, we don't discount future rewards smoothly, we discount them way more steeply the closer they get, which is supposedly why "I'll start tomorrow" always feels so believable tonight even though we know from experience it wont happen. She also mentioned economists split people into "naive" and "sophisticated" discounters -- naive ones dont see the next temptation coming, sophisticated ones do and try to lock themselves out in advance, like Ulysses tying himself to the mast.

That second category is what really got me, so instead of just having it explained to me I'd rather see it played out in fiction. Could you write me a short story, set in a near-future-ish tech world, about a founder or engineer character who builds some kind of serious commitment-device app or gadget to fight their own akrasia -- something way more binding than my little browser extension? I don't want it to just be "she builds a lockout gadget and it works, the end," I want the story to actually sit inside the puzzle: when the device kicks in and overrides what she wants in the moment, is that "her" being irrational, or is the in-the-moment her just as rational as the planning her, only running a different discount rate? Does past-her even have the right to bind future-her like that? No need to namedrop philosophers or economists in the story itself, I just want the ideas to be genuinely load-bearing instead of window dressing.
