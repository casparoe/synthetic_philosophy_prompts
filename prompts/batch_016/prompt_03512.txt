In my seminar on Buddhist philosophy, our instructor mentioned in passing that Nāgārjuna's catuṣkoṭi resembles the Jain theory of syādvāda, formalized in the sevenfold predication (saptabhaṅgī) — both are sometimes grouped together as "many-valued" departures from classical bivalent logic that emerged from classical Indian philosophy. I'd like to understand exactly where that comparison holds up and where it breaks down. Nāgārjuna deploys his tetralemma, in service of the doctrine of emptiness (śūnyatā), both to negate all four alternatives about a given topic (e.g., whether the Tathāgata exists after death) and, in at least one well-known verse, to affirm all four simultaneously. Is this doing something fundamentally different from the Jain sevenfold schema, or is it essentially a compressed version of the same idea — and which of the two strikes you as more internally coherent on its own terms?
