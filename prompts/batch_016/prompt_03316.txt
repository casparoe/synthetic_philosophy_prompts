I've been drafting a short note for my MPhil seminar on twentieth-century views on the meaning of life, for the week we cover Frank Ramsey before moving on to Nagel and Camus on cosmic absurdity. The set text is the piece Ramsey read to the Cambridge Apostles in 1925 under the title (sometimes rendered) "On There Being No Discussable Subject," published only after his death as the "Epilogue" to his Foundations of Mathematics. Every year my students seize on the same line — "I don't feel the least humble before the vastness of the heavens" — nod along, and move straight on to the following week's reading without asking whether the argument around it actually holds up. I've written the paragraph below to force the issue, and I'd value your comments on it before I hand it out.

Here is the paragraph. Ramsey's case against cosmic humility really involves two separate moves. The first is a straightforwardly evaluative point: size and the capacity for thought or love are independent properties, so the stars' mere bulk gives them no claim on our deference. He dresses this up in a painter's metaphor: his picture of the world is drawn in human perspective, crowded in the foreground with people, with the stars reduced to the size of small coins. He even extends the thought to time, reasoning that the eventual heat death of the universe has, discounted over so long an interval, a present value of almost nothing — a bit of financial arithmetic he would soon put to more serious use in his papers on saving and taxation. So far, so tidy. But Ramsey then adds a second, harder-to-place remark: he insists that the facts are in themselves neither cheerful nor depressing, so that a thrilled reaction to them is no more fitting than a despairing one — and in the same breath he pities the despairing reader nonetheless, and insists he has good reason to, since a thrill is both pleasanter and, generally, better for one's activities than despair. Read as rival claims about which attitude correctly corresponds to the facts, these two remarks flatly contradict one another. My suggestion is that the contradiction dissolves once you remember whose paragraph this is: the same Ramsey who, within a couple of years, would argue that calling a proposition true adds nothing to it beyond asserting it. On that view there is no robust notion of fact left over for an attitude to correspond to or fail to correspond to, so the reason he cites for preferring cheerfulness cannot be a correspondence claim smuggled back in — it has to be doing the purely practical, action-guiding work that his contemporaneous account of belief and preference would lead you to expect. So there is no inconsistency, just two different jobs — fitting the facts, and being useful — that Ramsey, for once, keeps admirably separate.

I'd be glad to have your comments on three things in particular: whether this is a fair reading of the passage, or whether I am helping myself to machinery — the truth-talk, the belief-talk — that the Epilogue itself gives no license for; whether my proposed reconciliation is doing real philosophical work, or is really just relabelling the puzzle rather than solving it; and whether some more standard apparatus, such as a fitting-attitudes or buck-passing account of value, would let Ramsey have both claims more cheaply than my appeal to his theory of truth does. And of course tell me if you think there was never a tension here to begin with, and that I've simply leaned too much weight on two sentences that were never built to bear it.
