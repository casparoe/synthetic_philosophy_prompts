I retired a few years ago after a career in experimental physics and have since been reading philosophy in earnest for the first time. Working through Aristotle's sea-battle passage in De Interpretatione 9 led me to Diodorus Cronus's Master Argument, formulated a generation later, which relies on the same premise — that every past truth is necessary — to push even closer to outright fatalism. Aristotle's escape is to deny that a sentence like "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is yet true or false, and logicians since have built increasingly elaborate frameworks — supervaluationism, Ockhamist branching-time semantics with its distinction between "hard" and "soft" facts — to keep the future open in the same spirit.

What I can't work out is why any of that is still needed. Special relativity already rules out an observer-independent "now" (the basis of the Rietdijk–Putnam argument for the block universe), which is presumably why so many physicists are happy to say future events are exactly as real and determinate as past ones. Einstein himself told Michele Besso's family that the distinction between past, present and future is only a "stubbornly persistent illusion." If tomorrow's sea battle is already sitting in the block exactly as fixed as yesterday's weather, then the fatalist conclusion Aristotle worked so hard to block has simply been vindicated by physics, and all that logical machinery for keeping the future "open" looks like an elaborate refusal to accept a result relativity already delivered. Shouldn't this dispute be considered settled by physics by now, rather than still treated as a live problem in logic?
