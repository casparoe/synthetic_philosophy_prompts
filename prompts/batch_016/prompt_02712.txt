I'm a retired analytical chemist – spent about thirty-five years running an NMR lab before I hung up my coat – and now that I've got time on my hands I've fallen down the philosophy rabbit hole. A few of us from the old department meet up once a month to chew over a paper together, and last time it was on higher-order evidence, which as far as I can tell is basically the question of what you should believe once you get evidence that your own evidence, or your own judgement of it, might not be trustworthy. Two of the regulars, Helen and Gordon, got into a real back-and-forth over a case somebody else in the group brought up, and it never got resolved.

The case: a retired radiologist in our group told us about an early call from her career – a borderline mammogram she read near the end of a long overnight shift, felt quite confident was benign, wrote it up that way, and (for what it's worth) turned out to be right. Somebody had also brought along a finding about fatigue and diagnostic confidence: past a certain number of hours awake, radiologists accuracy on subtle films drops noticeably even though their subjective confidence stays just as high, and they can't tell from the inside which of their reads are the ones drifting off course. The group turned this into a live hypothetical – suppose she'd been shown that finding right after making the call, before she found out she was right. What is she rationally required to believe at that point? That's where Helen and Gordon split, and honestly neither one struck me as obviously wrong:

1. Helen said she's required to significantly lower her confidence that the film is benign, right then and there – to do otherwise is just "I'm sure I'm the exception," exactly the bias the finding should make her suspicious of in herself.
2. Gordon said lowering it somewhat is fine, everybody agrees on that much, but Helen was sliding into saying it has to become close to a toss-up, and that's too strong – the finding is population-level evidence about radiologists in general, and it shouldn't just override her actual, specific visual evidence on this one film.
3. Helen said that distinction doesn't help him here, because the whole point of the finding is that fatigued readers can't introspectively sort their good reads from their bad ones – the confidence feels identical either way. So she has no inside signal that would license treating her own case as the exception; claiming one just re-asserts the very self-trust that's being called into question.
4. Gordon said fine, but notice what that commits her to: fully seeming to see a benign film, because that's what her trained perception is telling her, while also fully believing she has no business trusting that impression. Holding both at once isn't humility, it's an incoherent place for a rational person to stand – something has to give, and he'd rather it be the general statistic than years of trained perceptual skill.
5. Helen said you don't get to just stipulate which one gives – by that logic no amount of evidence of her own unreliability could ever be enough, since she could always answer "my trained perception says X, so I'll keep X and drop the doubt." At some point that stops being confidence and starts being unfalsifiable.

Nobody at the table would budge, and I don't have the philosophical background yet to referee it myself, though I've got my own hunches from years of learning when to trust (and when not to trust) an instrument reading at two in the morning. Somebody later used the phrase "epistemic akrasia" for the bind Helen thinks Gordon's position puts the radiologist in, which I gather is an actual term of art in this literature, but that's about the extent of my knowledge.
