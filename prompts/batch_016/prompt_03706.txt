I've been reading around in the philosophy of biology and keep running into a specific episode in Karl Popper's career that none of the summaries I've found seem to resolve satisfyingly. In his intellectual autobiography Unended Quest (1976) and in several earlier pieces, Popper argued that the theory of natural selection, taken on its own, is "not a testable scientific theory, but a metaphysical research programme": a fruitful framework for generating testable hypotheses, but not itself falsifiable, which on his view was the touchstone of genuine empirical science. His stated reason, repeated in various forms over more than a decade, was that talk of "the fittest" surviving is "almost tautological" once fitness is cashed out in terms of actual survival and reproductive success, so that the claim threatens to collapse into the bare observation that those who survive are those who survive.

Then, in a lecture delivered at Darwin College, Cambridge in 1977 and published the following year in the journal Dialectica, Popper said he had changed his mind about the testability of the theory and was glad to offer "a recantation." What makes the episode strange is what surrounds that word. In the sentence just before it, he reaffirms that he still regards natural selection as working, just as before, as a metaphysical research programme. And the claim he actually goes on to demonstrate is testable — and false — is not the modest, tautology-prone principle that fitter variants tend to spread, but, in his words, a "most daring and sweeping form" of the theory: that all organisms, all complex organs, and all behavior have evolved "as the result of natural selection." This bolder claim, he says, is "not only refutable but actually refuted," and he offers the peacock's tail (a product, on his reading, of sexual selection rather than survival advantage) and genetic drift as counterexamples.

Write an essay that reaches and defends a definite verdict on this episode. You should take a clear position on both of the following, and explain how they bear on each other: first, whether Popper's original tautology worry identifies a genuine problem for the scientific status of natural selection, or whether it rests on a confusion that dissolves once fitness is specified independently of actual survival (through measurable physiological, behavioral, or population-genetic properties fixed in advance, for instance); and second, whether the 1977/1978 statement amounts to a genuine, if narrow, change of mind, or whether Popper — by refuting a much bolder claim than the one that originally worried him — leaves his original target essentially untouched while claiming to have retracted it. A conditional verdict is fine (for example: "the tautology charge is dissolved by independent fitness measures, but Popper's lecture doesn't actually invoke any such measure and so isn't a real recantation of the original worry" — though you need not agree with this particular combination). What you should not do is conclude merely that both readings have something to be said for them.
