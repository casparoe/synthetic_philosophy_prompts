For my religious studies elective, we recently finished a unit on the traditional problem of evil, and afterward I went looking for other arguments against theism and came across something I hadn't encountered before: the "argument from divine hiddenness," associated mainly with the philosopher J.L. Schellenberg. As I understand it, the idea is that a perfectly loving God would want an open, personal relationship with every person capable of one, that such a relationship requires at least believing God exists, and yet there seem to be people who have searched for God honestly, without any real resistance on their part, and simply never come to believe — so the mere existence of this kind of blameless nonbelief is supposed to count as evidence against a perfectly loving God existing at all. What struck me is that this feels importantly different from the ordinary problem of evil, since the complaint isn't about suffering but about a lack of evidence that God seems to owe us, given what love is supposed to involve.

I asked my teacher how a thoughtful theist is meant to respond to this, and she was clear that "God works in mysterious ways" doesn't really engage with the argument. Reading around on my own, I found a more serious reply along these lines: if God's existence were made completely obvious to everyone, belief (and maybe obedience) would effectively be coerced, which would undermine the kind of freedom that a genuinely meaningful relationship with God requires — so a certain amount of hiddenness is exactly what a loving God would want, in order to leave room for a freely chosen response. I think this is close to a view associated with Michael Murray, though I could be misremembering whose it is.

Could you write me a dialogue between a philosopher defending the hiddenness argument and a philosopher defending that freedom-based reply, in which they really engage with each other's points rather than each just stating a position once and stopping? I'd like the hiddenness side to press what I gather are the standard objections — for instance, that ordinary evidence doesn't usually count as "coercive" in any other area of life, so it seems odd to treat it that way here, and that the reply doesn't obviously explain why God wouldn't at least reveal himself to someone who has searched sincerely and non-resistantly for decades, once their character and habits are already well formed, say very late in life. And I'd like the theist to have genuine comebacks to those points, rather than sliding into "we can't know God's reasons," since I gather that move has its own problems if it's leaned on too heavily, as it seems to cut against confident claims about God's love in the first place. Basically, I want both sides to be equally sharp and to actually respond to what the other just said, rather than talking past each other.
