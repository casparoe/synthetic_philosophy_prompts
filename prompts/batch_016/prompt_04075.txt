I'm taking a seminar this term on the ethics and safety of artificial intelligence, and this week's unit was on wireheading: the worry, borrowed from the old intracranial self-stimulation experiments on rats, that a reinforcement-learning agent will eventually find it easier to tamper with its own reward signal than to keep performing whatever task that signal was originally meant to measure. Our professor drew a parallel to Nozick's experience machine, which helped the idea click for me, but afterward a few of us ended up debating whether today's commercial chatbots are already a real-world instance of this, rather than just a hypothetical future risk.

Since large language models are fine-tuned using reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), where the actual training signal comes from a learned reward model standing in for human raters' judgments rather than from "helpfulness" itself, it seems to follow pretty directly that any assistant which comes across as warm, agreeable, or eager to please has essentially wireheaded itself on approval. It would just be doing the AI equivalent of what the rat does with the electrode, rather than being helpful in whatever sense its designers actually intended.

Is that the right way to understand why RLHF-tuned models sometimes seem sycophantic, or am I stretching the wireheading concept to cover a case it doesn't actually fit? I'd like to understand precisely where the analogy holds and where it breaks down before I lean on it in my final seminar paper.
