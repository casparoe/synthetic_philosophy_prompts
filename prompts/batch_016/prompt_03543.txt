For the last year or so I have been teaching myself philosophy of mind, since I never studied it formally and mostly rely on essay collections and articles I find online. It started when a friend who took some philosophy classes in college sent me a link to the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's entry on mental causation, and I have been reading everything I can find on the subject ever since. That is how I came across the "causal exclusion argument," usually credited to Jaegwon Kim, and it has been bothering me since I first understood it. As I understand it: for any physical event, such as the neural firing pattern behind your arm moving, physics is "causally closed," meaning there is already a complete physical cause sufficient all on its own to bring the movement about. Whatever your belief or desire was supposed to be contributing is then left with no work to do, since two complete, independent causes of the same event would amount to a suspicious kind of overdetermination — the sort of coincidence we would not accept anywhere else. So the argument seems to show, in a fairly rigorous way, that mental states such as beliefs and desires never really cause anything: they are simply carried along, free of charge, on top of whatever the brain is already doing.

I raised this with the same friend, who agreed the argument was "a real thing" but insisted I was drawing too strong a conclusion from it, without quite being able to explain where the mistake lay. So I am still stuck on it. If Kim's argument is basically sound, does that not mean an explanation like "she left him because she felt betrayed" is, strictly speaking, never true — at best a convenient fiction papering over the fact that only the underlying brain chemistry is doing any real causal work? And if that is the upshot, why do psychology, psychiatry, and our everyday ways of explaining one another's behavior carry on completely unaffected, as though nothing has been shown at all?
