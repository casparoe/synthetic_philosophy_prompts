I'm at work on a novel about a family of academic philosophers, and there's a scene I'd like help getting right before I draft it. An eminent, newly retired philosopher and his adult daughter, herself a philosopher, are talking late on the night of his retirement dinner — I've deliberately built the pair to echo the real Strawsons, though both characters are my own invention. He has spent his career defending moral responsibility along the lines of P. F. Strawson's reactive-attitudes argument: our practices of resentment, gratitude, and blame are rooted in unrevisable features of human psychology and don't stand or fall with the truth of determinism. She has come around to something like Galen Strawson's Basic Argument: to be truly responsible for an action you'd have to be responsible for the way you are, which would take either completing an infinite regress of self-forming choices or else conceding that you are, at bottom, a matter of luck — and this holds whether or not determinism is true.

Write their exchange. She should press him on whether describing why we hold reactive attitudes can do the work of justifying them; he should press her on what "truly" or "ultimately" responsible is supposed to mean, and whether her conclusion proves too much, too little, or exactly what she thinks. Neither should fold prematurely — each should be answering what the other just said, not a more convenient version of it. Mostly dialogue, minimal stage direction, roughly 500 words.
