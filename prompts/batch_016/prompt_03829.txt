So there's this pretty obscure decision-theory puzzle called the Pasadena game (Nover and Hajek, early 2000s). Flip a fair coin til it first lands heads, say on flip n: you win 2^n/n utils if n is odd, but have to pay 2^n/n utils if n is even -- they insist on utils rather than dollars specifically so nobody can dodge the puzzle with a diminishing-marginal-utility-of-money move. Add up the payoffs in the order the flips actually happen and you get 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ... which converges to ln2. Thing is, that series is only conditionally convergent, so by the Riemann rearrangement theorem you can reorder those exact same payoffs to sum to literally any number you like, or to +infinity, or -infinity. Hajek and Nover's own illustration: write each payoff on a card, then imagine you drop the deck by accident and stack the cards back up in a different order -- same game, totally different "expected value."

Write a dialogue between two decision theorists hashing this out, maybe while literally holding the reshuffled deck. One of them thinks this shows the game just doesn't have a well-defined expected value, period, and that's a genuine hole in decision theory. The other thinks there's some principled way to pin down what it's worth anyway -- e.g. by arguing the temporal order isn't actually arbitrary, since that's the order the uncertainty really resolves in, or via some fair-price-for-repeated-play move like Easwaran's -- and treats the reshuffled-cards business as a cute curiosity that doesn't threaten any real decision. Make them actually engage with each other's specific arguments instead of just restating their own side, and don't let either one turn into a pushover.
