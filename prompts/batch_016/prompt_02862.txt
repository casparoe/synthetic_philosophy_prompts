I'm taking a course on formal epistemology this term, and this week's unit on group belief and disagreement covered Aumann's 1976 "agreeing to disagree" result and its extension into the so-called no-trade theorems, especially Milgrom and Stokey's 1982 paper, with a nod to the later Sebenius and Geanakoplos result extending the impossibility to contingent bets more generally. I can follow the proofs step by step: common priors, an initial Pareto-optimal allocation, risk-averse traders, and common knowledge that a proposed trade is individually rational for everyone involved, together imply that the trade can't actually go through, because the mere fact that it's being offered already carries enough information to unwind whatever private edge motivated it in the first place. What I don't yet have is the intuition underneath the algebra; right now it feels like a card trick I can verify step-by-step but not really see through.

This came up over dinner with a couple of people from my cohort, and we ended up going back and forth about what the theorem is supposed to tell us about actual financial markets, which obviously do see enormous trading volume around informational events like earnings calls or elections. One friend argued that the theorem essentially proves that none of that volume can be driven by pure differences in information among otherwise rational, like-minded traders, and that it must instead come from noise traders, liquidity needs, differing priors, or risk-sharing motives. I wasn't fully convinced this was the right gloss, but I couldn't articulate exactly why, or which of the theorem's assumptions is really doing the work.

Could you help me build up an intuitive, mostly formalism-free picture of why no-trade theorems hold? In particular, I'd like to understand what it means, mechanically, for an offer to trade to function as a public signal, and why that signal ends up being self-defeating rather than merely informative. I'd also appreciate your take on my friend's stronger claim: does the theorem really force us to locate all real-world informational trading in a failure of common priors (or one of the other assumptions), or is that too quick?
