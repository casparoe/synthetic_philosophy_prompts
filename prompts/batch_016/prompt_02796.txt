In a reading group on patristic ethics, we've been working through Augustine's two treatises on lying, De Mendacio and Contra Mendacium, and a disagreement broke out about how well people actually understand his famous rigorism. Everyone remembers that Augustine holds that every lie is sinful, full stop, even one told to save an innocent life — but far fewer people recall that he also builds a fairly intricate hierarchy of gravity among lies, organized around a value-ordering rather than a simple "more harm equals more sinful" rule. Roughly, the gravity of a lie is set by what kind of good is at stake for the person deceived or the person protected (religious truth, a neighbor's standing, habitual vice, sociability, property, bodily life, or bodily integrity), and this produces at least one placement that most people in our group found genuinely surprising on first encounter.
