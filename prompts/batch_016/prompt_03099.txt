I'm plotting a novel about the founding of a fictional republic. In his closing speech, one of my framers—a shrewd constitutional lawyer—defends an unamendable "eternity clause" by invoking Ulysses binding himself to the mast: a constitution that future majorities can never revise, he argues, is simply the republic doing for itself what Ulysses did for himself, and no one thinks Ulysses wronged himself by being tied down. I'd like him to win this argument, but only if it actually holds up. Does the logic of a man binding his own future self really transfer to a founding generation binding every generation that comes after it, or is my lawyer's cleverness eliding something important?
