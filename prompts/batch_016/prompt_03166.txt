Write a dialogue between two delegates at a constitutional convention who disagree about whether the new republic's charter should permit political parties. One delegate, persuaded by Simone Weil's essay "On the Abolition of All Political Parties," argues they must be banned outright: on her analysis, every party is a machine for generating collective passion, a device for disciplining members into uniform opinion, and an organization whose real objective is its own unlimited growth, all at the expense of truth, justice, and the public interest. The other shares Weil's suspicion of faction but takes the Madisonian view of Federalist No. 10: since the causes of faction cannot be removed without destroying liberty, the only real remedy is to control its effects by multiplying interests and checking power within a large, well-constructed republic. The dialogue should work through whether Madison's remedy genuinely answers Weil's diagnosis or merely relocates the problem, and whether anything short of an organized party system could perform the coordinating and accountability functions parties now serve, with each delegate pressing the other's strongest point rather than restating their own position.
