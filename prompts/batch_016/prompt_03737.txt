For my Jewish philosophy elective, I've been comparing how Maimonides and Nahmanides explain the Torah's commandment of animal sacrifice. In the Guide for the Perplexed (III:32, echoed in III:46), Maimonides argues that sacrifice was instituted only as a concession to the idolatrous worship practices the Israelites had become accustomed to in Egypt, meant to gradually redirect that impulse toward God alone. Nahmanides, in his commentary on Leviticus 1:9, harshly rejects this account and insists that sacrifice carries a deeper, non-historical significance. Could you reconstruct the strongest version of each thinker's position, and then tell me which account you find more philosophically persuasive as an explanation for why God would command this practice, and why?
