So I've been down a real rabbit hole on the free will / moral responsibility thing for like two weeks now, ever since me and my roommate got into it about whether anyone actually "deserves" punishment or praise for anything, given that nobody picks their own genes or upbringing or brain wiring. That turned into a big Wikipedia and Stanford Encyclopedia binge, and eventually I landed on this thing called Galen Strawson's "Basic Argument," which from what I can tell says something like: to really be responsible for who you are, you'd have had to make yourself that way from nothing, but literally nothing can cause itself to exist, so nobody is ever "ultimately" morally responsible no matter how free a choice feels in the moment. The general shape of it makes sense to me but I keep loosing the thread on exactly why the regress is supposed to be totally inescapable.

Anyways, buried in one of those articles was this quote from Nietzsche saying basically the same thing almost a hundred years earlier, from Beyond Good and Evil (aphorism 21 I think). This is the bit they quoted:

"The CAUSA SUI is the best self-contradiction that has yet been conceived, it is a sort of logical violation and unnaturalness; but the extravagant pride of man has managed to entangle itself profoundly and frightfully with this very folly. The desire for "freedom of will" in the superlative, metaphysical sense, such as still holds sway, unfortunately, in the minds of the half-educated, the desire to bear the entire and ultimate responsibility for one's actions oneself, and to absolve God, the world, ancestors, chance, and society therefrom, involves nothing less than to be precisely this CAUSA SUI, and, with more than Munchausen daring, to pull oneself up into existence by the hair, out of the slough of nothingness."

I can sort of feel what he's getting at (people wanting credit and blame so bad they're basically demanding to be their own god or something), but the actual sentences kind of lose me line by line, especially stuff like "logical violation and unnaturalness," the "half-educated" dig, and that whole image of pulling yourself out of a swamp by your own hair like Munchausen. Could you break down what Nietzsche is actually saying here, more or less sentence by sentence? And then the bigger question I actually care about: is this literally the same argument as Strawson's causa sui regress, just angrier and older, or is a 19th century guy ranting about human "pride" doing something meaningfully different from what a modern academic paper is doing with the same latin phrase? I don't want to go around telling people "oh Nietzsche basically called this 100 years before Strawson" if that's actually kind of a sloppy thing to say.
