hey, I'm a phil PhD student (mostly working on phil of language, this whole thing is a rabbit hole I fell into instead of my actual dissertation chapter) and I want a gut check on an argument before I say it out loud to my advisor and regret it.

Background in case it's not familiar: theres a diary Frege kept for about a month in spring 1924, the year before he died, that didn't get published til the 90s. It shows him as a full-blown ethnonationalist -- anti-Weimar, anti-suffrage, wants Jews stripped of political rights and ideally expelled from Germany, dreams of some strongman "statesman" figure who'd override the whole party system. Dummett read fragments of it in the 50s going through the Nachlass and has said it genuinely rattled him, since he'd basically idolized Frege as the paradigm of a purely rational mind.

The standard move (I think this is more or less Dummett's own implicit stance, and it's definitely the majority view among people I've seen discuss it) is total separation: you can be a completely orthodox Fregean about sense and reference or the theory of the ancestral and also think Frege-the-man was a bigot -- zero tension, same way Carnap was his student in logic and more or less his political opposite. Fine, I basically buy this as a default.

But I've been reading a recent piece (guest post, apparently there's a whole book behind it now) that goes through Frege's actual political writings rather than just the diary, and it flags two spots where the logic and the politics seem to actually touch, and I can't decide if either amounts to anything.

(1) Frege apparently tries to think through "Aryan descent" using the machinery of the ancestral itself: descent as the transitive closure of the parent-of relation, straight out of the Begriffschrift. Feels to me like pure man-with-a-hammer stuff -- not philosophically interesting, he just reached for whatever tool was lying around.

(2) Separately, he worried at some length about how you'd pin down "Jewish" precisely enough for discriminatory laws to actually be enforceable given intermarriage -- a real-world instance of exactly the "concept without sharp boundaries" problem from Grundgesetze, where Frege insists a predicate that doesn't yield a definite true or false for every single object isn't a legitimate concept at all. This one nags at me more. It's not just tool-reuse -- the demand itself, that every boundary be sharp and every case decidable, feels like it could be continuous with the same impulse that can't tolerate a mixed-marriage case being genuinely, irreducibly in between, rather than two unrelated commitments that just happen to share a description.

Tentative (probably too-cute) thesis: (1) really is just contingent reuse and tells us nothing, but (2) might be evidence of one underlying temperament -- a horror of vagueness wherever it shows up -- instead of two independent things that just happen to wear the same label. Obvious objection: this is unfalsifiable, the kind of move that can retrofit a "connection" onto any two commitments of any philosopher if you squint hard enough, and bivalence is just a semantic requirement on formal languages with no actual entailment to how someone feels about intermarriage.

Things I'd love comments on: does the temperament thesis do any real philosophical work, or have I basically just reinvented "everything's connected" and dressed it up in Frege scholarship? Is there a principled way to tell "contingent reuse of a tool" apart from "shared temperament," or are (1) and (2) actually the same kind of thing and I'm kidding myself that they differ? And separately -- tell me if I'm mangling the sharp-boundaries material itself, it's been a while since I sat down properly with Grundgesetze. Also totally fine to just say this whole angle's a dead end.
