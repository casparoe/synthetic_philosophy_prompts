I'm plotting a novel narrated in the first person by a woman who underwent callosotomy surgery years earlier for intractable epilepsy, and I need one pivotal scene grounded in the real philosophical debate about the unity of consciousness rather than in the handful of anecdotes everyone already recycles — the chicken-claw-and-shovel confabulation experiment, the hand that buttons a shirt while the other unbuttons it, naming a word flashed to one visual field. What I want instead is a scene set in ordinary life, not a laboratory, in which her right hemisphere clearly wants or does something her narrating "I" has no access to, and her left-hemisphere "interpreter" (to use Gazzaniga's term) improvises a confident, false account of it, while leaving the reader — from details she herself never registers — able to reconstruct what actually happened.

Once you've given me the scene, tell me plainly whether a moment like that would be genuine evidence for something like Puccetti's view that she is, in effect, two separate subjects sharing one body, or whether it's just as well explained by Bayne's "switch model," on which there is only ever one stream of consciousness, hopping between hemispheres. And if the scene can't actually discriminate between those two pictures, say so, and tell me what kind of scene, if any, could.
