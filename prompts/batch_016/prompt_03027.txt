I've spent the past few months reporting on claims that certain AI systems can predict human decisions with unsettling accuracy, and the debate sent me down an unexpected rabbit hole into sixteenth-century theology. It turns out the Jesuit Luis de Molina wrestled with a structurally similar puzzle: how can God be all-knowing and human choices still be free? His answer, now known as Molinism, was that God possesses "middle knowledge" — knowledge, prior to any decision to create at all, of exactly what every possible free creature would freely do in every possible circumstance. God then actualizes one particular world simply by choosing which circumstances to bring about; nobody's will is ever overridden, because the truth of how each creature would freely respond is already fixed, quite apart from anything God decides. The view has had a long afterlife in analytic philosophy of religion, not least because critics have never agreed on what could make those counterfactuals true before any of the relevant creatures exist to choose anything — the so-called grounding objection.

I'd love to see this idea handled in fiction rather than in another explainer. Could you write me a short story, somewhere in the 800–1500 word range, built around a being — divine, alien, artificial, bureaucratic, in whatever guise you like — who has exactly this kind of knowledge: not foreknowledge of what will happen, but complete knowledge of what every person under its purview would freely do in any circumstance whatsoever, knowledge it then uses to steer events purely by deciding which circumstances to create. I'd like the story to take the underlying logic seriously rather than flattening it into a generic "the machine knows your choices before you make them" premise: ideally it sits with the question of whether a choice is still free when someone else already knew, with certainty and before you ever existed, exactly what you'd choose — and maybe even brushes up against the grounding objection itself, i.e., what could possibly make that certainty true in the first place. I'd rather the ending leave a reader unsettled than tie things up too neatly.
