I'm taking a philosophy of mind seminar this semester that spent its first few weeks on Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument and the prospects for non-reductive physicalism, and this week our professor had us read backward into the history of the mind-body problem to see where the underlying worry about causal closure actually comes from. Alongside excerpts from Descartes and Leibniz, we were assigned Part III, Proposition 2 of Spinoza's Ethics, together with its scholium, on the grounds that his parallelism doctrine is arguably the first fully worked-out "no downward causation" position in the Western canon. I followed Part II, Proposition 7 reasonably well when we covered it a few weeks back (the famous line that the order and connection of ideas is the same as the order and connection of things), but the demonstration of III.2 and the note attached to it have left me stuck, and a couple of classmates and I went around in circles about it in office hours without resolving much. I have a short response paper on this due Friday, so I'd genuinely appreciate some help. Here is the passage, from the Elwes translation used in our course reader:

"Proposition 2. Body cannot determine mind to think, neither can mind determine body to motion or rest or any state different from these, if such there be.

Proof — All modes of thinking have for their cause God, by virtue of his being a thinking thing, and not by virtue of his being displayed under any other attribute (Proposition 6 of Part 2). That, therefore, which determines the mind to thought is a mode of thought, and not a mode of extension; that is (Definition 1 of Part 2), it is not body. This was our first point. Again, the motion and rest of a body must arise from another body, which has also been determined to a state of motion or rest by a third body, and absolutely everything which takes place in a body must spring from God, in so far as he is regarded as affected by some mode of extension, and not by some mode of thought (Proposition 6 of Part 2); that is, it cannot spring from the mind, which is a mode of thought. This was our second point. Therefore body cannot determine mind, &c. Q.E.D.

Note — This is made more clear by what was said in the Note to Proposition 7 of Part 2, namely, that mind and body are one and the same thing, conceived first under the attribute of thought, secondly, under the attribute of extension. Thus it follows that the order or concatenation of things is identical, whether nature be conceived under the one attribute or the other; consequently the order of states of activity and passivity in our body is simultaneous in nature with the order of states of activity and passivity in the mind. The same conclusion is evident from the manner in which we proved Proposition 12 of Part 2.

Nevertheless, though such is the case, and though there be no further room for doubt, I can scarcely believe, until the fact is proved by experience, that men can be induced to consider the question calmly and fairly, so firmly are they convinced that it is merely at the bidding of the mind, that the body is set in motion or at rest, or performs a variety of actions depending solely on the mind's will or the exercise of thought. However, no one has hitherto laid down the limits to the powers of the body, that is, no one has as yet been taught by experience what the body can accomplish solely by the laws of nature, in so far as she is regarded as extension. No one hitherto has gained such an accurate knowledge of the bodily mechanism, that he can explain all its functions; nor need I call attention to the fact that many actions are observed in the lower animals, which far transcend human sagacity, and that somnambulists do many things in their sleep, which they would not venture to do when awake: these instances are enough to show, that the body can by the sole laws of its nature do many things which the mind wonders at."

Two things about the passage itself are tripping me up. First, I can't tell whether the demonstration of the proposition is doing any independent philosophical work, or whether it just builds the "no interaction" conclusion into its own setup: it seems to derive "body cannot determine mind" purely from the claim that modes of thought are caused by God qua thinking thing and modes of extension by God qua extended thing, which already assumes the very separation of causal orders that parallelism is supposed to establish. Is there an independent argument here that I'm missing, or is the proof basically definitional? Second, I can't work out how the remark that "no one has yet determined what the body can do" is meant to coexist with a proof that has, a few lines earlier, ruled out mind-to-body causation with complete certainty. Is Spinoza hedging on how strict the parallelism really is, or is that remark perfectly consistent with, maybe even required by, full-blown parallelism?

For the paper itself, I also want to place Spinoza's position on the map we've been using for Kim's argument, and this is the part I'd most like your take on. Does his view amount to an early form of epiphenomenalism about the mental, given that the mind is never among the real causes of bodily motion, or is it actually closer to what today's non-reductive physicalists want, where mental and physical causal orders are both preserved as fully real but simply run in lockstep? And either way, does his solution genuinely dissolve the exclusion worry, or does it just relocate the same problem one level up, into the relation between the attributes themselves?
