so this came up in my jurisprudence reading group and nobody could settle it. we'd just read the Hart/Fuller exchange about the grudge informer case -- the wife who denounced her husband to the Nazis in '44 for badmouthing Hitler (mostly bc she wanted him out of the picture, she was seeing someone else), then got prosecuted herself in '49 once the war was over, for unlawfully depriving him of his liberty. two people in the group, Marco and Yuki, went back and forth on which of Hart's or Fuller's proposed fixes is actually better, and it kind of spiraled:

(1) Marco said Hart's solution -- get the postwar legislature to pass a frankly retroactive statute criminalizing what she did, rather than have the court just declare the Nazi statute invalid -- is more honest, since it openly names the trade-off (we're overriding non-retroactivity for justice's sake) instead of pretending no legal value got sacrificed.

(2) Yuki said that's letting Hart off easy: the Nazi system had already trashed basically all of Fuller's eight desiderata (secret decrees, rules aimed at named people, courts as party tools, retroactivity whenever convenient), so refusing to dignify that one statute with the name "law" costs nothing extra in rule-of-law terms -- it never had the internal morality of law to lose. Hart's fix, by contrast, introduces a brand new derogation from legality into a system that's actually trying to be lawful again.

(3) Marco came back with: that argument proves too much. if courts can decide case-by-case that some duly enacted rule from a regime they dislike "wasn't really law," you've handed judges exactly the discretion the rule of law is meant to keep in check. a one-off retroactive statute, passed by an actual legislature, is at least a contained, openly-labeled exception -- not a standing license for after-the-fact moralizing.

(4) Yuki replied that Fuller's test isn't "any injustice voids the law," its a structural test (generality, promulgation, prospectivity, congruence between rule and enforcement, etc.), so its not the free-floating discretion Marco's describing. and anyway, Hart's fix still needs somebody, in a barely-reconstituted postwar German state, to decide this particular woman deserves special retroactive treatment -- which is its own act of discretion, just wearing a legislative hat instead of a judicial one.
