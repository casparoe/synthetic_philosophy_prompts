Ok so this has been living in my head for like two weeks now. I went down a rabbit hole that started with Eliezer's old "Causal Diagrams and Causal Models" post on LW (part of the Highly Advanced Epistemology 101 sequence) and ended with me actually reading through the SEP entries and some of the primary philosophy-of-causation literature. And I've become kind of convinced that the rationalist-sphere consensus -- that Pearl/Woodward-style interventionism (structural equations, do-calculus, "what would happen under an idealized intervention on this variable") just cleanly superseded Lewis's old similarity-of-possible-worlds counterfactual theory -- is way overstated. A bunch of the classic counterexamples that are supposed to be the nail in the coffin for Lewis turn out to still be awkward for the structural-equations crowd too, they just get patched with extra apparatus like normality/default rankings or Halpern-Pearl-style "witness" variables, which honestly feels like the same kind of epicycle Lewis needed with his own quasi-dependence patch for late preemption.

I got into a whole argument about this exact point with a friend from my irl rationalist-adjacent discussion group, and we couldn't agree on which specific cases actually show interventionism earning its keep vs. which ones just inherit the identical problem dressed up in new notation. So, structured-output request to settle it: can you sort the following 7 classic challenge-cases for theories of causation into S/A/B/C tiers, where S = interventionism has a clean, non-ad-hoc advantage over Lewis-style counterfactual dependence on that particular case, and C = interventionism doesn't really help, or the same problem just resurfaces in structural-equations clothing. The cases: late preemption (Billy and Suzy both throw rocks at a bottle, Suzy's gets there first); symmetric overdetermination (a firing squad where either shot alone would be fatal); trumping preemption (Schaffer's case of a major and sergeant simultaneously ordering soldiers to advance, who obeys the higher rank); switching (flipping a switch determines whether the left or right of two bulbs lights up once the power comes on -- Pearl's own example, apparently); double prevention (releasing a catch that had been preventing a catapult from launching a rock through a window); causation-by-omission, aka the "profligate omissions" problem (your neighbor promised to water your plant while you were away and didn't, and it died); and Ned Hall's "short circuit" case (a boulder that both threatens a hiker and, by being visible, causes them to duck out of its path -- structurally isomorphic to ordinary early preemption, which is what makes it so nasty).
