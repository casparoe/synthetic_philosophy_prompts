Over a long career in experimental solid-state physics, I made regular use of quantum mechanics without ever examining its foundations closely; in retirement, I have finally begun catching up on the philosophical literature. One thing I have always told students, following the standard textbook formulation of Bell's theorem, is that violating the Bell inequalities forces us to abandon either locality or realism, and that interpretations such as Copenhagen quantum mechanics preserve locality precisely by relinquishing realism, since they decline to assign definite values to observables prior to measurement. Given that Copenhagen has, on this reading, already conceded the "realism" horn of the dilemma, I am puzzled why so many philosophers of physics nonetheless insist that quantum mechanics itself — and not merely explicitly nonlocal rivals such as Bohmian mechanics — is committed to a genuine failure of locality. Could you help me see what I am missing?
