I've been part of a long-running decision-theory reading group that grew out of the old LessWrong sequences, and an argument from this week's session is still nagging at me. We were discussing why advanced AI systems should be expected to be dangerous by default, and the case that carried the room went roughly as follows: if an agent's preferences over outcomes are ever incomplete or intransitive, a sufficiently clever counterparty can walk it through a cycle of trades that each look like a strict improvement at the time, yet leave it worse off than when it started — the classic money-pump. Since no optimization process capable enough to matter would tolerate having resources pumped out of it indefinitely, it seems to follow that any system smart enough to meaningfully self-improve will inevitably iron its preferences into those of a coherent expected-utility maximizer pursuing some stable goal, which is exactly why we should expect such a system to hoard resources, resist being shut down, and generally behave like the sort of ruthless optimizer that AI safety people worry about.

Two people in the group pushed back, saying something about the argument smuggling in assumptions about how an agent is allowed to react when offered a series of exploitative trades, but neither could spell out what exactly that was supposed to undermine, and I'm not convinced they knew either. The money-pump step still strikes me as closer to arithmetic than to a substantive assumption, so I'm inclined to think the rest of the argument goes through more or less as stated. Given that, is it fair to conclude that observing a given AI system's preferences to be messy, incomplete, and inconsistent right now tells us essentially nothing about its long-run danger, since capability alone should be expected to smooth those inconsistencies into the fixed, resource-hungry goal-structure the argument predicts?
