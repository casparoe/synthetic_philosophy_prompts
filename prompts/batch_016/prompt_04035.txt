I run my high school's philosophy club, and this semester I'm leading a session on Nietzsche, which has had me reading Twilight of the Idols and Ecce Homo more closely than I expected to. One passage that's stuck with me is in "The Four Great Errors," where Nietzsche mocks Luigi Cornaro, the Renaissance Venetian famous for a treatise crediting his own longevity to a notoriously spartan diet. Nietzsche's complaint is that Cornaro reversed cause and effect: it wasn't the meagre diet that produced his long life, but an unusually slow metabolism that produced both the long life and the need to eat so little, so that anyone with a different constitution who copied the diet would be harmed rather than helped by it — Nietzsche claims the book had already "shortened more lives" than almost any other. He calls this "the intrinsic perversion of reason" and generalizes it into a complaint about every morality that promises flourishing to whoever follows its recipe.

What I can't get past is that Nietzsche seems to commit the identical error himself, in a book he started only about six weeks later that same year. In "Why I Am So Clever," he mentions that a single glass of wine or beer a day is, for him, "amply sufficient to turn life into a valley of tears," and then says he "cannot with sufficient earnestness advise all more spiritual natures" to give up alcohol entirely; he rules that coffee "makes one gloomy," specifies exactly how strong morning tea should be brewed, dismisses German and English cooking as ruinous to the intellect in favor of Piedmontese cuisine, and recommends specific places — Nice, Turin, Sils Maria — as suited to serious thought. That looks like exactly the Cornaro move: an intensely personal, even idiosyncratic physiology dressed up as a rule for a whole type of person. When I brought this up with my philosophy teacher, she said it's a genuinely contested question among people who write on Nietzsche, not something with an obvious answer, and suggested I present both sides at the session rather than settle it myself.

So rather than write that part of the session myself, I'd like to ask you for it: a dialogue between two Nietzsche scholars, equally sharp, who disagree about exactly this. One should argue that Nietzsche is caught in a real, embarrassing inconsistency — that he quietly exempts himself from a standard he uses to humiliate Cornaro. The other should argue that the charge doesn't actually stick, and should have to show why in a way that engages the cause-and-effect argument itself, rather than just insisting Nietzsche gets a pass because he's Nietzsche. I'd like them to go back and forth for a dozen or so exchanges, genuinely responding to whatever the other just said, and I'd like the dialogue to end with each side having landed at least one point the other can't fully answer, rather than either one simply winning.
