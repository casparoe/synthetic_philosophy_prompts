I'm part of my school's philosophy club, and for the past few weeks we've been working through a unit on possibility and necessity that eventually led us to David Chalmers' zombie argument against physicalism. As I understand it, the argument runs like this: since we can coherently conceive of a "zombie world" that is physically identical to our own in every last detail but contains no conscious experience whatsoever, such a world must be genuinely possible. And if a world with all the same physical facts but no consciousness is possible, then the physical facts alone can't be what fixes the mental facts, so physicalism must be false.

What's been nagging at me is how this fits with something else we read that same week: Kripke's claim that statements like "water is H2O" are necessarily true, even though this was only discovered a posteriori, through empirical investigation. The trouble is that conceiving of water turning out not to be H2O seems just as easy as conceiving of a zombie world -- people did exactly that for most of human history, since nobody had any idea what water was made of until relatively recently, and there doesn't seem to be anything contradictory about imagining that the clear liquid filling our oceans could have turned out to be some other compound. So if conceivability is a reliable enough guide to possibility to make Chalmers' argument work, it seems like it should also make "water is H2O" merely contingent rather than necessary. And if conceivability isn't reliable enough to do that, I don't see how it's reliable enough to get us from "zombies are conceivable" to "zombies are possible" either. Our teacher insists both claims can be true at once, but I haven't been able to figure out how. Could you help me untangle this?
