I'm a philosophy PhD student working on the meaning-in-life literature for my dissertation lit review, and my advisor pointed me toward some of the less-read stuff the logical positivists wrote on value theory, which is how I ended up reading Schlick's 1927 essay "On the Meaning of Life" (the Peter Heath translation, in the Klemke & Cahn anthology, if that matters for exact wording). It's a genuinely strange text for someone mostly known for hardnosed work in epistemology and philosophy of science — way more personal and sermon-like. His basic move is to locate meaning in "play" (activity pursued for its own sake) rather than "work" (activity pursued for some further end), which is fine and I get that part. What's tripping me up is the last stretch of the essay, where he tightens this into a claim specifically about *youth*.

At one point he writes: "The meaning of life is youth. Youth, in fact, is not just a time of growing, learning, ripening and incompleteness, but primarily a time of play, of doing for its own sake, and hence a true bearer of the meaning of life." That reads like hes talking about actual chronological youth — like childhood/adolescence really is the meaningful chapter of a life in a way adulthood structurally isn't. He even glosses "the enthusiasm of youth" a bit earlier as basically "what the Greeks called Eros... devotion to the deed, not the goal." But then only a couple pages later he seems to hedge: "the word 'youth' does not have the external meaning of a specific period of one's life, a particular span of years; it is a state, a way of leading one's life." That sounds like hes now saying any adult, at any age, can be "young" in the relevant sense, which is a pretty different (and much weaker) claim than the first one.

My office mate says I'm overreading a popular essay that was never meant to be this rigorous, but I can't tell if these two claims are actually compatible or if Schlick is just equivocating between a literal/biographical sense of "youth" and a metaphorical one for a playful disposition, without noticing the slide. If its the metaphorical reading he ultimately wants, I don't get why he'd build the whole thesis around the word "youth" (with all the baggage about actual childhood) instead of something age-neutral, or why he leans so hard on imagery of children throughout. If it's the literal reading, he seems stuck with some pretty strong claims about adulthood being inherently less meaningful than youth. Is there a charitable way to read these two passages together that I'm missing, or is this just a real soft spot in the essay?
