I'm a novelist, and something that happened in my writers' group a few months back has been stuck in my head, partly because of the drama and partly because I think there's an actual scene hiding in it somewhere. One of the guys in the group, I'll call him Callum, had blown two deadlines in a row on his novel, so he installed one of those hardcore blocking apps where you set a lockout period and there's genuinely no password reset, no uninstall, nothing -- it just runs until the timer's up. He set it for 35 days and blocked everything except his manuscript and email from his editor. About a week and a half in, his dad had a heart attack, and because his family only ever reaches him through Facebook messenger (long story, his mom refuses to text), nobody could get through to him for almost two days. He only found out because he caved and drove to an internet cafe out of boredom, not because the app let him.

Two other people in the group have been arguing about it ever since and I honestly can't tell who's got the better of it. (1) Owen says the whole plan was reckless on its face -- no amount of productivity is worth being unreachable for over a month, full stop. (2) Nadia said that's exactly the point of what she called a "Ulysses pact" -- Callum's day-to-day self would rationalize literally any excuse to get online for five minutes, so the moment you build in an override for "real emergencies," that becomes the loophole, and it gets used constantly, not just during actual emergencies. (3) Owen answered that you could keep the lock airtight for Callum specifically while still giving some other person, a friend, an emergency override code, so the guy in withdrawal never gets a vote on his own case. (4) Nadia said that just moves the problem over one seat: now the whole scheme rests on that friend holding firm against a very persuasive novelist begging for "just this once," and theres no real reason to think a friend is any more Ulysses-proof than Callum was. (5) Owen pointed out that this is true of actual constitutions too -- amendment thresholds get enforced by politicians who can absolutely be lobbied and worn down, and we don't usually conclude from that that constitutional constraints are pointless, just that a high bar beats no bar. (6) Nadia said the comparison doesn't hold up, because a legislature is a lot of people with competing interests, so no single one of them can just quietly cave, whereas Callum's "trusted friend" is one person with an actual friendship on the line, which is a lot closer to one override switch than to a legislature, and that's exactly the kind of concentrated emergency power constitutional theorists worry about.

Could you go through it move by move, 1 through 6, and say who actually has the better argument at each step, not just who wins overall? I'd also love to know if you think there's some design for this sort of self-binding that would dodge the whole dilemma, since Callum himself now says he's not sure he'd risk the airtight version again, but also doesn't think he'd have finished the book without something like it -- which feels like its pointing at a third option neither Owen nor Nadia quite landed on.
