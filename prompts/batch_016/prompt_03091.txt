For my IB Theory of Knowledge class, I've been looking into the claim, repeated in almost every biology textbook, that DNA is "the code of life." The more philosophy-of-biology material I read on the side, the less obvious this seemed. On one hand, the mapping from codons to amino acids looks genuinely arbitrary rather than dictated by chemistry — essentially Crick's "frozen accident" idea — and arbitrariness of that kind is usually treated as the hallmark separating real codes, like language or Morse code, from things that are merely causally correlated with an outcome. On the other hand, developmental systems theorists defend what they call the "parity thesis": maternal cytoplasm, epigenetic marks, and even reliable features of the environment are just as tightly correlated with, and causally implicated in, the resulting organism as DNA sequences are, so if that kind of correlation is enough to earn something the label "information," we'd have to call all of those things codes too — which seems to drain the word of any real content.

I haven't been able to decide whether there's a principled way to give DNA some privileged status anyway (maybe something about the code and the machinery that reads it having been shaped together by natural selection?), or whether "genetic code" is really just a leftover metaphor from the information-theory-obsessed early days of molecular biology. Could you write an essay that works through this debate, weighs the strongest versions of both sides, and actually commits to a real answer rather than settling for "it's a bit of both"?
