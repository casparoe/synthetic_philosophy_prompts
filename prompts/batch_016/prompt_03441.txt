I'm a novelist currently working on a near-future book in which teletransportation booths have become as mundane as elevators, and my protagonist — a claims adjuster I've been calling Novak in the drafts — steps into one during an ordinary evening commute. The scan and reconstruction on the receiving end go off without a hitch, so a version of him, with no sense that anything went wrong, walks out across town and goes home to his family. But the booth on this end fails to destroy the body that stepped into it, and it turns out the scanning process has damaged his heart badly enough that he has two or three days left. For that short stretch there are two of him, in direct phone contact, each carrying an identical set of memories up to the instant he pressed the button.

I've been rereading Derek Parfit's discussion of exactly this scenario in Reasons and Persons — he calls it the Branch-Line Case — and his argument that whether the dying original should regard his prospects as close to ordinary death or close to ordinary survival turns on whether personal identity, as opposed to mere Relation R, is what actually matters in survival. Before I draft the hospital scene, I would like help thinking through the most important implications of that argument: what should actually change in how Novak reasons about his own dying once he accepts Parfit's premise; how his wife and children, who can simply go visit the other Novak, ought to regard their remaining obligations to the man in the hospital bed; and whether Parfit's own discussion really supports the kind of clean equanimity I am tempted to give him, or whether that would flatten an argument he leaves more genuinely unsettled than that. I would rather the scene earn its philosophy than lean on the secondhand version of this thought experiment that everyone already associates with Star Trek transporters.
