I'm a novelist working on a book set partly in Copenhagen in the 1840s, centered on a fictional satirical weekly modeled loosely on Corsaren, whose editors destroy a minor composer's reputation for sport. For background texture I've been sitting in on a small Kierkegaard reading group through my local university's continuing-education program, and a few weeks ago we spent a session on "Two Ages: A Literary Review" (the section on modernity is often anthologized on its own as "The Present Age") — the book-length review of Thomasine Gyllembourg's novel that Kierkegaard used as an occasion to lay out his views on the present age, the public, leveling, and the press. Someone mentioned, almost in passing, that Kierkegaard published this on March 30, 1846, at a point when Corsaren's weekly caricatures of him — which he had effectively dared the paper into running three months earlier, after tangling with the critic P. L. Møller — had already been running for a couple of months and still had a few more to go. That offhand remark set off an argument between two of the group's regulars, Elena and Marcus, that nobody managed to settle, and it's stuck with me since.

(1) Elena's opening claim was that the timing gives the game away: a treatise describing "the public" as a cowardly, faceless, gossip-sustained phantom, written by a man who was at that very moment being drawn as a freak in the country's most-read satirical paper, looks less like a dispassionate diagnosis of modernity than an elaborate, dignified way of calling Corsaren's readers cowards without naming them. (2) Marcus answered that this is a textbook genetic fallacy: where a claim comes from has no bearing on whether it's true, and plenty of genuine insights start life as raw personal grievance — a fraud victim's understanding of fraud isn't worth less for having been paid for in humiliation. He added that the opposition between "the single individual" and the crowd runs through Kierkegaard's authorship well before Corsaren existed as a problem for him, so the idea can hardly be an after-the-fact rationalization of one specific persecution. (3) Elena replied that she wasn't committing a genetic fallacy so much as flagging a suspicious inference: the specific claims made about the public in this essay — anonymous, cowardly, incapable of action, sustained by newspaper gossip — are generalized from exactly one small paper with a personal vendetta against a man who had all but begged it to attack him, and the essay's structure conveniently exempts its own author as the heroic exception to the very leveling it describes, which is precisely the self-flattering moral a humiliated man would want to draw, whether or not it happens to be true. (4) Marcus's rejoinder was that Kierkegaard offers a mechanism, not just an assertion: the argument is that reflection and comparison, mediated by a press that manufactures "the public" out of people who share no direct or committed relation to one another, level individuals into an abstraction regardless of who is doing the leveling — a structural claim about the present age, occasioned by a novel spanning two historical periods, not a claim about Corsaren specifically. He granted that Kierkegaard's self-image as the exceptional individual is self-serving and worth criticizing on its own terms, but held that this is a separate question from whether "leveling" correctly names something real.
