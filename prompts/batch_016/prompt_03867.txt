I'm taking a history of political thought seminar and we just spent a whole class period on how Hobbes basically wanted political philosophy to work like geometry: start from a handful of clear definitions (of stuff like "liberty," "person," "commonwealth") and then just deduce everything else, so that once you accept the definitions your not supposed to have any wiggle room left to reject the conclusions. My professor mentioned that Hobbes got kinda obsessed with Euclid as an adult (apparently he'd draw lines and figures on his own thigh, and on the bedsheets, when he was lying in bed without paper handy) and that this obsession is basically why Leviathan is structured the way it is, with all those definitions up front before he even gets to the state of nature or the sovereign.

Given how serious Hobbes was about geometry, and that he clearly thought it was the one truly settled, dispute-proof science, doesn't that mean his argument for why we all owe total obedience to an absolute sovereign is basically as airtight as a Euclidean proof? Like, once you grant his opening definitions you shouldn't be able to coherently reject the conclusion any more than you could coherently reject that a triangle's angles add up to 180 degrees. Is that the right way to think about why Leviathan is supposed to be so compelling, or is it a mistake to treat his "geometrical method" as putting the sovereignty argument on the same footing as an actual math proof?
