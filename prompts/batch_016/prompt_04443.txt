A colleague of mine, Marcus, has spent weeks unable to choose between two job offers: a well-paid strategy role at a large firm in another city, and a lower-paid but more meaningful position at a small nonprofit near his family. Repeated pro-and-con lists haven't settled it. Overhearing him agonize about this at lunch, two other colleagues fell into an argument of their own about what his indecision actually shows.

(1) Dana said Marcus is simply reasoning badly: there must be some fact about which job is better for him, and once he weighs income, location, meaning, and career growth correctly, one option will come out ahead. His paralysis just means the calculation isn't finished.

(2) Farah disagreed: some choices are hard not because anyone has calculated badly, but because the options are, as she put it, "on a par" -- neither better, worse, nor exactly equal -- so no amount of further reflection could ever turn up a winner. That, she said, is exactly why he can deliberate forever without making progress.

(3) Dana objected that this doesn't add up: "better than," "worse than," and "equally good" exhaust the ways two things can compare, so calling the jobs none of these is either a contradiction or just a dressed-up way of saying Marcus doesn't know yet -- maybe his judgment is simply vague here, the way it's vague whether someone who is 5'9" counts as "tall," rather than there being some fourth relation at work.

(4) Farah replied that equality, unlike parity, is fragile to small improvements: sweeten the nonprofit salary slightly, and if the two jobs had really been equally good, the sweetened offer should now clearly win -- but it doesn't; the comparison stays exactly as unsettled as before. And unlike ordinary vagueness, which more precise information can in principle resolve, Marcus already has full, clear-eyed knowledge of his own preferences and of every relevant fact, yet no added precision breaks the tie -- which is exactly what parity, and not mere fuzziness, would predict.

Go through this move by move and say who has the better of the argument at each step, not just who wins overall, and note anything important that both Dana and Farah are missing.
