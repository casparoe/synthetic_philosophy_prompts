I have been writing about Galileo's hermeneutics of scripture, and there is a move he makes near the end of the Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina (1615) that I cannot quite reconcile with the rest of his argument. The context is the episode in Joshua 10:12–13, where Joshua commands, "Sun, stand thou still," and the text reports that "the sun stood still in the midst of heaven," so that the day was lengthened for his army's victory. What follows is my paraphrase of the relevant stretch of Galileo's argument, condensed for length:

Under the Ptolemaic arrangement, Galileo observes, the sun's own motion runs opposite to the great daily sweep that carries the heavens from east to west; so if the sun merely ceased its own motion, the day would be shortened, not lengthened, since it would be carried along all the more freely by that contrary sweep. Taken at face value, then, Joshua's words cannot be true under that system, which is why some commentators held that it was really the outermost sphere (the primum mobile), and not the sun, that was stayed. But suppose instead, as his own recent observations of sunspots suggest, that the sun turns upon itself, and that this turning is the very source of motion for all the planets that circle it, much as the beating of the heart sends motion through the limbs of an animal. On this supposition, the sun need only cease its own turning for every motion in the heavens to cease along with it, and the day would be lengthened exactly as the text says, with no strained reading required. The same supposition also resolves a second puzzle: the text has the sun standing still "in the midst of heaven," which fits awkwardly with either noon or evening on the common view, but fits precisely if the sun genuinely occupies the midst — that is, the center — of the world, as the Copernicans hold.

What I cannot work out is why Galileo troubles himself with this argument at all. Earlier in the letter, following Augustine, his official position is that scripture accommodates itself to popular understanding and was never meant to teach astronomy, so that its statements about the sun's motion should yield to demonstrated science whenever the two seem to conflict. Yet here he labors to show that, read quite literally, Joshua's words actually fit the Copernican system better than the Ptolemaic one. Does this sit uneasily with his official hermeneutic, or is there a way of reading both moves as part of a single, coherent strategy? What do you think this argument is really doing for Galileo, and for which readers is it intended?
