I'm taking a seminar on medieval and early modern political philosophy, and this week we moved from Aquinas's writings on tyranny to the later scholastics who invoked him, especially Juan de Mariana, who is notorious for defending the assassination of tyrants by private citizens. Our professor mentioned that Mariana saw himself as continuing an already-established Thomistic position rather than innovating, and this made more sense to me once I reread Aquinas's commentary on the Sentences (In II Sent., d. 44, q. 2, a. 2), where he says outright that a tyrant is a kind of public enemy to whom no obedience is owed, and that a man who frees his country by killing such a ruler deserves praise and reward. Given this, it looks to me like Aquinas had already settled the moral question in favor of private tyrannicide well before Mariana came along, and that Mariana was simply extending settled doctrine into a more dangerous political climate rather than breaking new ground.

What's confusing me is that when I read Aquinas's De Regimine Principum for this week's class, he seems to flatly reverse himself. He insists more than once that proceeding against a tyrant is a matter for public authority rather than the "private presumption" of individuals, and he spends several pages arguing that private citizens who attempt to kill tyrants usually accomplish nothing beyond getting themselves killed or goading the tyrant into greater cruelty. When I raised this contrast in section, my TA said the two texts are not really as opposed as they look, but that struck me as a way of avoiding a fairly obvious inconsistency: either a private citizen may licitly kill a tyrant or he may not, and Aquinas appears to have said both, in works separated by only about a decade.

For my response paper, I want to argue that the Sentences commentary captures Aquinas's real, considered view on the underlying question of permissibility, and that De Regimine Principum merely layers on prudential advice about when it is tactically wise to exercise a permission Aquinas had already granted. Does that seem like a defensible way to frame the tension between the two texts, and if so, how would you recommend I handle the very emphatic language Aquinas uses in De Regimine Principum against private action, so that my paper doesn't look like it's just ignoring the passages that cut against its own thesis?
