For an extra-credit essay in my school's philosophy elective, I've been reading around a unit on W. V. O. Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism," where he dissolves the line between analytic and synthetic statements by arguing that our whole web of beliefs, logic and mathematics included, answers to experience only as one interconnected whole rather than sentence by sentence. Curious whether he ever said anything comparable about morality, I learned that in 1978 Quine wrote what was apparently the only essay he ever devoted entirely to ethics, called "On the Nature of Moral Values." It opens strangely, with a scene of a dog sitting motionless near a turnip, which he uses to argue that behavior always mixes belief and desire together, and it later argues that something which starts out as a mere means — fishing, say, as a way of getting food — can turn into an end valued for its own sake, and that it is often genuinely hard to say where prudence ends and morality begins (he contrasts obvious cases, like concern for a neighbor's welfare, with a fondness for peanut brittle, then works through trickier in-between cases involving customs around food and dress).

What confused a few of us in class is where Quine ends up. Even though he denies there is a hard wall between fact and value, he still argues that ethics is methodologically worse off than science: scientific disagreements can eventually appeal to observation, even if only holistically and imperfectly, while moral disagreements can only be settled by appeal to moral standards people already share, so that, as he puts it, a coherence theory of truth is simply "the lot of ethics." A classmate then found out that Quine's own longtime colleague at Harvard, Morton White, thought this was a cop-out: in a book called "What Is and What Ought to Be Done," White argued that since Quine had already dissolved the analytic/synthetic wall by insisting that even logic answers to experience as part of one seamless web, consistency required dissolving the fact/value wall too, treating moral sentiments as a kind of empirical edge for ethics in exactly the way sense experience is an empirical edge for science.

I would like to actually understand who wins this dispute rather than just hearing that both sides have a point. Could you write an essay that first reconstructs Quine's case for ranking ethics beneath science methodologically, then reconstructs White's holism-based objection that this ranking is inconsistent with Quine's own principles, and then comes down clearly on one side, or on some precisely stated middle position (something like, "Quine is right that ethics lacks anything resembling science's checkpoint, but wrong to call this an outright infirmity rather than just a difference in kind"), rather than leaving the matter open?
