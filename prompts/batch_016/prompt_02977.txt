I'm taking a course on early modern philosophy of science this term, and we just spent two weeks on Hobbes: first De Corpore, then the secondary literature on his decades-long feud with John Wallis over Hobbes's repeated, and repeatedly mistaken, attempts to square the circle. What struck me most wasn't the faulty mathematics itself but the epistemology behind Hobbes's confidence. In the Six Lessons to the Professors of the Mathematiques, he argues that geometry and civil philosophy are the only fully demonstrable sciences, because in both cases we ourselves construct the objects we reason about — the figures in one case, the commonwealth in the other — whereas natural philosophy can only ever be conjectural, since we did not build the natural bodies whose hidden causes we're trying to infer. A few of us got into an argument after seminar about whether this "maker's knowledge" doctrine is really coherent, and someone pointed out that it's also presumably what let Hobbes shrug off decades of correction from a professional mathematician: if you're the author of the construction, on this view, nobody else has any special standing to tell you that you've made an error.

That argument is what got me wondering how Hobbes would have handled two things that came well after his death in 1679. First, the discovery by Lobachevsky, Bolyai, and Riemann that you can rigorously construct internally consistent geometries in which Euclid's parallel postulate simply fails. Second, Einstein's later use of exactly that kind of non-Euclidean geometry to argue that physical space itself is curved, not flat. Given that Hobbes identifies space with nothing more than "the phantasm of a thing existing without the mind" and insists that geometric figures are literally generated by the motion of bodies, I can't decide whether he should have found the existence of alternative geometries congenial or catastrophic. On one hand, it seems to fit his nominalism perfectly: if geometric certainty just comes from our being the authors of our own definitions, then a hyperbolic or elliptic geometry, honestly constructed, is exactly as demonstrable as Euclid's, and none of them needs to answer to some independent, mind-external space. On the other hand, Hobbes wanted natural philosophy to borrow its certainty from geometry precisely because he thought geometrical extension and the extension of real bodies were the same thing — and if it turns out to be an open, broadly empirical question which geometry actually describes the body you and I are made of, that continuity looks much harder to maintain.

Could you reconstruct, as specifically as you can, what you think Hobbes's actual response would have been, and defend it as a definite answer rather than an even-handed list of considerations on both sides? I'd especially like you to engage with the tension I've sketched above — between his constructivism about geometrical demonstration and his insistence that geometry and physics share a single subject matter — rather than settling for the general observation that Hobbes admired mathematical certainty.
