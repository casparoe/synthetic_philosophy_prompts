okay so for my econ class we're doing this whole unit on inflation and monetary policy (mostly because my uncle wont shut up about "the fed just printing money" at literally every family dinner) and my teacher gave us this excerpt from a David Hume essay called "Of Money" from like 1752 as a "see, people have been arguing about this forever" reading. i got through most of it ok but there's this one part where he seems to just flat out contradict himself and its bugging me. here's the passage:

"It is indeed evident, that money is nothing but the representation of labour and commodities, and serves only as a method of rating or estimating them. Where coin is in greater plenty; as a greater quantity of it is required to represent the same quantity of goods; it can have no effect, either good or bad, taking a nation within itself; any more than it would make an alteration on a merchant's books, if, instead of the Arabian method of notation, which requires few characters, he should make use of the Roman, which requires a great many... Accordingly we find, that, in every kingdom, into which money begins to flow in greater abundance than formerly, every thing takes a new face: labour and industry gain life; the merchant becomes more enterprising, the manufacturer more diligent and skilful, and even the farmer follows his plough with greater alacrity and attention... To account, then, for this phenomenon, we must consider, that though the high price of commodities be a necessary consequence of the encrease of gold and silver, yet it follows not immediately upon that encrease; but some time is required before the money circulates through the whole state, and makes its effect be felt on all ranks of people. At first, no alteration is perceived; by degrees the price rises, first of one commodity, then of another; till the whole at last reaches a just proportion with the new quantity of specie which is in the kingdom. In my opinion, it is only in this interval or intermediate situation, between the acquisition of money and rise of prices, that the encreasing quantity of gold and silver is favourable to industry."

so like, first he's comparing the amount of money in a country to writing a number in roman numerals vs arabic numerals — basically saying its just a labeling convention and having more or less of it CANNOT actually make a country richer or poorer. fine, ok. but then two sentences later he says when new gold and silver flows into a country, people genuinely work harder and get more prosperous for a while, and that this only happens in the "interval" before prices rise to catch up with it. isnt that just contradicting the first point? like either money is neutral and doesnt really do anything, or it actually causes more real production and effort, it feels like he wants to have it both ways here. can someone help me figure out what's actually going on — like what mechanism does Hume think is making people work harder specifically DURING that gap, and why would that effect necessarily disappear once prices adjust instead of just being permanent? also is this basically just an early version of the "money is neutral in the long run but not the short run" idea that modern economists argue about (kind of like the whole debate over whether printing money can actually boost the economy short term before it just becomes inflation), or is Hume actually saying something weirder or different from that? my teacher said economists still don't fully agree on how to read this passage which seems wild to me for something this old lol
