A friend and I spent several hours last night arguing about vagueness and never really reached agreement. She defended Timothy Williamson's epistemicism—the view that predicates like "bald" and "heap" have perfectly sharp extensions that we are constitutively unable to know—while I defended the supervaluationism of Kit Fine and Rosanna Keefe, on the grounds that it respects the intuition that there is no fact of the matter in borderline cases without committing us to unknowable, brute cutoffs. She then objected that supervaluationism merely relocates the problem one level up, since the boundary between the "definitely true," borderline, and "definitely false" regions is itself not sharp, and I had no good reply on the spot. Please construct the strongest argument you can that supervaluationism cannot give a coherent, non-question-begging treatment of this higher-order vagueness without either collapsing into something functionally equivalent to epistemicism or triggering a vicious regress of ever-higher-order precisifications.
