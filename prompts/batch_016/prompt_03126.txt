I cover science and ideas for a regional newspaper, and for the past few weeks I have been reporting a retrospective on how various local commentators called last month's unexpectedly close special election. Two of the people on my list have proven particularly difficult to write about fairly. One is a data reporter who built her own model from polling averages, fundraising totals, and turnout patterns in comparable districts; she published her assumptions in advance, updated the model weekly as new figures came in, and can explain in detail exactly why she arrived at her final call. The other is a longtime local radio host who uses no model at all - he says he simply "gets a feel" for a race from rally crowds and yard-sign counts, and when I pressed him on it, he could not translate that feeling into anything more specific than a hunch. As it happens, both of them named the winner correctly.

My editor wants to list the two of them side by side in our year-end accuracy rankings, on equal footing. Her argument is that a reader who wanted to know who was going to win was served exactly as well by the radio host as by the data reporter, so treating one as more credible than the other is just methodological snobbery dressed up as rigor: the outcome was identical, and outcomes are presumably what an accuracy ranking exists to measure. I find this difficult to rebut cleanly, since for this particular race she is correct that the practical payoff to a reader was the same either way.

And yet it still seems to matter to me that the data reporter's call was backed by something in a way the radio host's was not, and I do not think this is only because his hunches have also been confidently wrong in other years. Suppose, hypothetically, that his past calls turned out to have a track record just as strong as her model's, purely by coincidence rather than any real sensitivity to how these races break - would there still be a principled basis for writing about the two of them differently, or would that just be my own aesthetic preference for tidy methodology masquerading as a substantive distinction? I would like to understand what, if anything, is genuinely at stake in that instinct before my editor and I settle on how the piece treats them.
