I spent thirty-five years as a nuclear physicist before retiring, and I have been using part of my retirement to finally read the philosophy of religion I never had time for during my career. This is how I came back to the kalam cosmological argument, which I had only ever encountered in its bumper-sticker form: whatever begins to exist has a cause, the universe began to exist, therefore the universe has a cause. Wanting to check the physics side for myself rather than take it on faith, I read an essay Alexander Vilenkin wrote a few years back explaining his own Borde-Guth-Vilenkin theorem, the one showing that any universe which is, on average, expanding cannot be extended eternally into the past. He is refreshingly candid about what this rules out, working through both Ellis's "emergent universe" and the Steinhardt-Turok cyclic model and giving independent reasons why neither escapes the theorem, and he ends up granting that the universe in all likelihood really did begin to exist.

What stopped me was his very next move. Having granted a beginning, Vilenkin denies that it needed a cause at all: he describes a closed universe of zero net energy and charge tunneling into existence out of nothing, with quantum mechanics alone fixing the odds, and he compares this directly to radioactive decay. That comparison landed close to home, since nuclear instability was my own field for three decades, though on reflection I am no longer sure it is doing the innocent work he wants it to do. His own summary of the position is blunt: "Modern physics can describe the emergence of the universe as a physical process that does not require a cause." Digging further, I found that Craig has two separate replies to this which, taken together, are sharper than the popular debates let on. One distinguishes an indeterministic event happening to an already-existing thing, such as a decaying nucleus, from a thing's beginning to exist out of nothing, so that quantum indeterminism among events is no counterexample to a premise about the origin of substances. The other accuses Vilenkin of a specific inferential slip, on the grounds that "Vilenkin mistakenly assumes that anything not prohibited by the laws of nature is metaphysically possible."

I would like to see this precise disagreement argued out properly rather than summarized for me. Could you write a dialogue, with real back-and-forth over a dozen or more exchanges and neither side a pushover, between a philosopher defending the kalam causal premise along Craig's lines and a physicist who thinks Vilenkin's account of quantum origination genuinely defeats it? Please have both sides grant, purely for the sake of argument, that the universe did have an absolute temporal beginning, so that nothing turns on relitigating the theorem itself; the whole exchange should hinge on whether that beginning needed a cause. I would especially like the physicist to have a substantive answer to the events/things distinction and to the charge that "not forbidden by law" is being confused with "needs no explanation," rather than simply repeating the tunneling story, and I would like the philosopher to be pressed on whether positing an uncaused, timeless, personal cause for this one unrepeatable event is any less ad hoc than the move Craig accuses Vilenkin of making. Neither one needs to come out the clear winner.
