In Guide of the Perplexed III:32, Maimonides argues that God commanded animal sacrifice not because it has value in itself, but as an accommodation to the Israelites' deep habituation to sacrificial worship among the surrounding idolatrous nations: unable to abolish the practice outright, God redirected it toward Himself, much as an infant is weaned from its mother's milk gradually rather than all at once. On this view, animal sacrifice is the lowest rung of a ladder that rises through prayer to the pure intellectual worship of God, which Maimonides takes to be the Law's ultimate aim. Nahmanides rejects this account. Commenting on Genesis 4:4 and 8:20, he objects that it cannot explain why Abel and Noah, who lived before any idolatrous sacrificial cult existed, nonetheless offered sacrifices that pleased God; commenting on Leviticus 1:9, he further argues that treating sacrifice as a mere psychological concession sits badly with the Torah's own description of it as a "pleasing odor" to the Lord, and he proposes instead that the sacrificial animal vicariously enacts the fate that the sinner himself deserves. The dispute is sharpened by the fact that Maimonides' own legal code, the Mishneh Torah, affirms that the messianic king will rebuild the Temple and restore the sacrificial service as a permanent institution, rather than as a stage to be outgrown.

Write an essay assessing which account of the reasons for animal sacrifice is more defensible. Weigh the strongest considerations on each side, address the tension within Maimonides' own corpus, and defend a definite conclusion.
