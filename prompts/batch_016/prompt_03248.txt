I'm putting together a graduate seminar on theories of legal interpretation, and before we get to the familiar territory of Dworkin, Scalia, and the modern originalism debates, I want to open with a less obvious case study: Frederick Douglass's 1860 Glasgow address, "The Constitution of the United States: Is It Pro-Slavery or Anti-Slavery?" written against Garrison's view that the Constitution was irredeemably pro-slavery. Two passages from it are giving me trouble, and I'd like your help thinking them through before I put them in front of students.

First, arguing that only the ratified text can count as the Constitution's meaning, Douglass writes:

"It should also be borne in mind that the intentions of those who framed the Constitution, be they good or bad, for slavery or against slavery, are so respected so far, and so far only, as we find those intentions plainly stated in the Constitution. It would be the wildest of absurdities, and lead to endless confusion and mischiefs, if, instead of looking to the written paper itself, for its meaning, it were attempted to make us search it out, in the secret motives, and dishonest intentions, of some of the men who took part in writing it. It was what they said that was adopted by the people, not what they were ashamed or afraid to say, and really omitted to say. Bear in mind, also, and the fact is an important one, that the framers of the Constitution sat with doors closed, and that this was done purposely, that nothing but the result of their labours should be seen, and that that result should be judged of by the people free from any of the bias shown in the debates."

But a little later, arguing that no ambiguous clause should ever be read as sanctioning slavery, he writes:

"There must be something more than history—something more than tradition. The Supreme Court of the United States lays down this rule, and it meets the case exactly—'Where rights are infringed—where the fundamental principles of the law are overthrown—where the general system of the law is departed from, the legislative intention must be expressed with irresistible clearness.' The same court says that the language of the law must be construed strictly in favour of justice and liberty. Again, there is another rule of law. It is—Where a law is susceptible of two meanings, the one making it accomplish an innocent purpose, and the other making it accomplish a wicked purpose, we must in all cases adopt that which makes it accomplish an innocent purpose."

What I can't work out is whether these two moves are really compatible. The first sounds like a fairly austere formalism: meaning is fixed by the public text as adopted, and whatever the framers privately intended or argued behind closed doors is simply beside the point. The second, though, imports a substantive moral tiebreaker that isn't obviously licensed by the text alone. Can Douglass hold both consistently, or is the second principle doing all the real interpretive work while the first mainly lends an appearance of neutrality? And does the combination resemble any recognizable position in later interpretive theory, or should we just treat it as sui generis?
