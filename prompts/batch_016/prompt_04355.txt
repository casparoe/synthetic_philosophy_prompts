Now retired from a career in geology, I caught the tail end of the plate-tectonics revolution, old enough to remember colleagues who defended geosynclinal theory well after the evidence had turned against it -- an experience that is largely what drew me to Kuhn. Working through his later essays for the first time this year, though, I've run into a wrinkle in the secondary literature that I can't resolve on my own. In his post-"Structure" writings, Kuhn restricts incommensurability to "local" clusters of interdefined kind terms, governed by a taxonomic apparatus -- structured lexicons obeying a "no-overlap principle" -- meant to leave most of a theory's vocabulary translatable across a revolution, with failure confined to a handful of crucial terms. This looks tailor-made to blunt objections like the one Davidson raised in "On the Very Idea of a Conceptual Scheme," where he argued that wholesale untranslatability is unintelligible, since we could never even recognize an alien scheme as a scheme in the first place. Could you explain concretely how Kuhn's taxonomic machinery is meant to escape that Davidsonian charge, and then give me your own assessment of whether this "localized" incommensurability is still strong enough to do the job Kuhn originally wanted it to do -- namely, explaining why successive paradigms can be genuinely, rationally incomparable rather than merely difficult to compare?
