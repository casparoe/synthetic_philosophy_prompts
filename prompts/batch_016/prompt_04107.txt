Ok so I was supposed to be studying for my AP Bio test last night and instead fell into a Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy rabbit hole (the "Dynamic Choice" entry, don't judge me). It's got this section on "rational irrationality" that uses a Parfit example: a robber breaks in and threatens your kids unless you open the safe, but you happen to have a drug that makes you completely irrational for a few minutes, so you take it and start acting totally deranged -- now the robber's threats can't work on you anymore because you're incapable of responding to reasons at all, so he just leaves. The article's whole point is that it can be rational to make yourself irrational.

Then a couple sections later the same entry describes "resolute choice" (credited to McClennen and Gauthier), using Kavka's toxin puzzle, and it feels like it's making basically the opposite move: it says drinking the toxin isn't irrational at all, we just had the wrong theory of what makes an act rational (one that only judges single acts instead of whole plans), and once you swap in the right theory, sticking to the plan was rational the entire time -- no drug, no becoming a different kind of agent, nothing paradoxical about it.

Neither section actually brings this up, but the whole time I was reading I kept thinking about Parfit's Hitchhiker (dying in the desert, a driver who'll only pick you up if he predicts you'll pay him once you reach town, even though once your safe there's no actual reason to pay). So when people say the rational thing to do is pay the driver, which of these two moves are they making -- the "it's irrational, but rational to have become the sort of agent who'd do it" move, or the "it was never irrational, we just fixed the theory" move? Is there an actual disagreement here, one that could give different answers in some other case, or is "rational irrationality" secretly just a clunkier way of saying "resolute choice"? Don't give me a wishy-washy "both have a point" answer -- pick one and defend it.
