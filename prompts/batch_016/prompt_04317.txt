I am at work on a novel that stages an encounter between René Descartes and a piece of technology he could never have imagined, and I want the scene to rest on real argument rather than caricature. In the Discourse on the Method (Part V), and again in his 1646 letter to the Marquess of Newcastle, Descartes lays out two specific tests for telling a genuine reasoning mind from a mere automaton: first, that no machine could rearrange words flexibly enough to respond appropriately to whatever is said in its presence, the way even the dullest human being can; second, that a machine might excel at one narrow task yet would inevitably fail outside it, since it is driven by the fixed disposition of its parts rather than by reason, which he calls a "universal instrument." It is on this basis that he denies minds to animals, however sophisticated their behavior appears.

Suppose Descartes were shown a modern large language model holding a fluent, contextually apt conversation on almost any subject put to it. Write the letter he might have sent to Newcastle afterward, reasoning in his own idiom through whether the system passes his language test, what that verdict would do to his confidence in denying reason to animals on the very same grounds, and whether he would retreat to some stricter criterion rather than abandon the distinction. Close with a brief aside distinguishing which moves in your reconstruction follow directly from Descartes' actual commitments and which are your own extrapolation.
