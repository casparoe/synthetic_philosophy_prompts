I'm reporting a piece on "cliodynamics," the movement associated with scientists like Peter Turchin that uses statistical modeling to argue the rise and fall of societies follows discoverable general laws. For the piece, I want to imagine how Leopold von Ranke, the founder of modern source-based historiography, would have reacted to that ambition. Ranke insisted that each epoch be reconstructed "as it actually was" on its own terms, resisting Hegel's grand philosophical schemas and declaring that "every epoch is immediate to God" rather than a mere stepping-stone toward some later, more advanced age. He died in 1886, decades before Carl Hempel proposed what became known as the covering-law model of historical explanation—the idea that genuine historical understanding works by subsuming events under general laws plus initial conditions, precisely what cliodynamics now claims to do with data instead of metaphysics. Drawing on what we actually know of Ranke's method and his objections to speculative history, how do you think he would have judged the covering-law model and its data-driven descendants: a vindication of his own empiricism, or Hegelianism smuggled back in through statistics?
