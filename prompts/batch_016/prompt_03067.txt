I'm drafting a novel built around the estrangement between Dana Voss, a semi-retired macro fund manager, and Priya Anand, the protégée she trained who now runs a smaller, quant-driven fund of her own. Two years before the novel opens, Priya took the other side of Dana's largest position — a leveraged bet that a currency peg would survive a speculative attack — and won, and Dana has never quite let go of the question this raised for her: if Priya truly believed Dana was still the rational trader she had trained, why would she ever have agreed to take that trade at all?

Dana's private answer has hardened into something close to an obsession with Aumann's agreement theorem and its trading-theoretic descendant, the Milgrom–Stokey "no-trade theorem" — sometimes nicknamed, after Groucho Marx's telegram resigning from a club willing to have him as a member, the "Groucho Marx theorem." The result, as she understands it, is that among risk-averse Bayesians who share a common prior, the mere common knowledge that a counterparty still wants to trade with you at a given price should already be enough to update your beliefs and unwind the trade before it happens — part of why epistemologists working on peer disagreement treat it as one of the strongest arguments against the very possibility of two people who respect each other's rationality knowingly disagreeing. Taken seriously, this seems to rule out any purely speculative trade between two people who regard each other as rational, and yet real markets are saturated with exactly that kind of trade.

A version of this reasoning is going to surface almost verbatim in a late confrontation scene between Dana and Priya, so I would like it to be right rather than hand-wavy. Could you write an essay, pitched at an intelligent lay reader, that takes the no-trade result seriously as both game theory and epistemology, and then works through what the sheer volume of real-world speculative trading actually shows: that traders like Dana and Priya never really shared a common prior, that most of that volume is not truly speculative once hedging, liquidity, and rebalancing motives are separated out, that market participants are simply not reasoning the way ideally rational Bayesian agents would, or that the common-knowledge assumption the theorem needs essentially never holds outside toy models — and what, if anything, is left of the idea that prices aggregate private information into a collective rational judgment once all of this is taken into account. Please don't just set the options side by side: weigh them against each other and close on an actual verdict, specific enough that I could compress it into a line or two of dialogue for Dana.
