For a novel in which Émilie du Châtelet appears as a supporting character, I'd like a few of her genuine convictions about happiness to surface in her dialogue rather than as potted paraphrase. I've been working through her *Discours sur le bonheur* — written privately in the 1740s and published only after her death — in the original French, and the following stretch, in my own rough rendering into English, has me stuck on more than just vocabulary:

"Wisdom must always keep the counters in hand, for whoever says wise says happy — at least in my dictionary. One must have passions in order to be happy, but one must make them serve our happiness, and there are some to which all entry into our soul must be refused. I do not mean those passions that are vices, such as hatred, vengeance, or anger. Ambition, for instance, is a passion against which, I believe, one must guard one's soul if one wishes to be happy — not because it affords no enjoyments, for I believe this passion can furnish them, nor because ambition is never satisfied, for that, surely, is a great good, but because, of all the passions, it is the one that places our happiness most at the mercy of others. Now, the less our happiness depends on others, the easier it is for us to be happy. Let us not fear cutting too much away on this score; enough dependence will remain regardless. For this reason of independence, the love of study is, of all the passions, the one that contributes most to our happiness. Within the love of study lies enclosed a passion from which no elevated soul is ever entirely free: the passion for glory. Indeed, for half of humankind this is the only way of acquiring it at all — and it is precisely that half whose upbringing strips them of the means to it, and makes even the taste for it impossible.

It is certain that the love of study is far less necessary to the happiness of men than to that of women. Men have an infinite number of resources that women lack entirely. They have many other ways of attaining glory, and the ambition to make one's talents useful to one's country and to serve one's fellow citizens — whether through skill in the art of war, or through talent for government or negotiation — is unquestionably far superior to what one might hope to attain through study. But women are excluded by their condition from every kind of glory; and when, by chance, one is born among them with a sufficiently elevated soul, study alone remains to console her for all the exclusions and all the dependencies to which her condition condemns her."

Three things puzzle me. First, I can't tell whether the opening image of wisdom keeping its counters in hand is a throwaway flourish or is meant to do real work in the argument that follows. Second, she disqualifies ambition as a safe route to happiness on the grounds that it places us at the mercy of others, yet in almost the same breath she credits the love of study with harboring an inextinguishable passion for glory — and glory, surely, is nothing if not something other people confer on you. Is she contradicting herself, or is there a distinction here I'm missing? Third, I can't decide whether her closing turn to women's situation is meant as a kind of consolation prize — study standing in for the "real" glory available to men — or as a sharper claim in its own right. I'd like to actually understand her reasoning here before I put any version of it in a character's mouth.
