I've spent years reading around the rationalist blogosphere (LessWrong, Slate Star Codex/ACX, the old Overcoming Bias archives), and a few people I met through that scene have an ongoing reading group where we've lately been tracing the prehistory of the community's honesty debates. The lineage we'd assumed was more or less: Kant's notorious insistence that you may not lie even to a murderer asking after your friend's whereabouts, then Sidgwick's worries about "esoteric morality" and whether the truth about ethics should be told to everyone, and eventually things like Yudkowsky's "Meta-Honesty: Firming Up Honesty Around Its Edge-Cases," which tries to rescue something like honesty-as-policy once you admit that some lies (to murderers, to Nazis at the door) seem obviously permissible. Someone in the group pointed out that Kant was nowhere near the first person to defend an exceptionless "never lie" rule — Augustine got there roughly 1,400 years earlier, in a treatise called De Mendacio ("On Lying"), and worked out a far more elaborate casuistry than Kant ever attempted, including an explicit ranking of lies from most to least sinful. I tracked down the relevant sections in the 1887 Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers translation by H. Browne, and while I can follow Augustine's headline conclusion, the actual hierarchy he builds has me stuck, because the ordering doesn't track the harm-based intuitions I'd naturally bring to it.

Here is the material. First, on why he treats certain harms as uniquely corrupting in a way others aren't:

"The whole stress, then, of this question comes to this; whether it be true universally that no sin of another, committed upon you, is to be imputed to you, if, being able to avoid it by a lighter sin of your own, you do it not; or whether there be an exception of all bodily defilement. No man says that a person is defiled by being murdered, or cast into prison, or bound in chains, or scourged, or afflicted with other tortures and pains, or proscribed and made to suffer most grievous losses even to utter nakedness, or stripped of honors, and subjected to great disgrace by reproaches of whatsoever kind; whatever of all these a man may have unjustly suffered, no man is so senseless as to say that he is thereby defiled. But if he have filth poured all over him, or poured into his mouth, or crammed into him, or if he be carnally used like a woman; then almost all men regard him with a feeling of horror, and they call him defiled and unclean."

Then the taxonomy itself, in descending order of gravity:

"For first to be eschewed is that capital lie and far to be fled from, which is done in doctrine of religion; to which lie a man ought by no consideration to be induced. The second, that he should hurt some man unjustly: which is such that it profits no man and hurts some man. The third, which so profits one as to hurt another, but not in corporal defilement. The fourth, that which is done through only lust of lying and deceiving, which is an unmixed lie. The fifth, what is done with desire of pleasing by agreeableness in talk. All these being utterly eschewed and rejected, there follows a sixth sort which at once hurts nobody and helps somebody; as when, if a person's money is to be unjustly taken from him, one who knows where the money is, should say that he does not know, by whomsoever the question be put. The seventh, which hurts none and profits some: except if a judge interrogate: as when, not wishing to betray a man who is sought for to be put to death, one should lie; not only a just and innocent, but also a culprit; because it belongs to Christian discipline neither to despair of any man's amendment, nor to bar the way of repentance against any. Of which two sorts, which are wont to be attended with great controversy, we have sufficiently treated... The eighth sort of lie is that which hurts no man, and does good in the preserving somebody from corporal defilement, at least that defilement which we have mentioned above."

And his own summary of how the ranking is meant to work: "In these eight kinds, however, a man sins less when he tells a lie, in proportion as he emerges to the eighth: more, in proportion as he diverges to the first." (Augustine, De Mendacio, chs. 15, 25, and 42; trans. H. Browne, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, First Series, vol. 3, ed. Philip Schaff, 1887.)

What I can't reconstruct is the principle that makes this a coherent hierarchy rather than an arbitrary list. The part that trips me up most is the seventh-versus-eighth comparison: a lie told to keep even a guilty man from being executed comes out as more sinful than a lie told to keep someone from being raped. On pretty much any ordinary weighing of harms, preserving a life against death should count for at least as much as preventing a sexual assault, so why does Augustine's ranking come out the other way? I suspect it has something to do with the distinction he draws elsewhere between temporal and eternal goods, plus this specific notion of "defilement" as a category separate from ordinary bodily harm — but I can't get the pieces to click into a fully consistent story. Could you help me reconstruct the underlying logic that generates this hierarchy, and explain how it's meant to support Augustine's larger claim that lying has to be treated as an exceptionless rule rather than something weighed case by case against competing goods?
