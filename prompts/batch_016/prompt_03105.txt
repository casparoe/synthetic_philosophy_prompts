I convene a small faculty reading group working through Frege this term, and shortly after we finished "Der Gedanke," someone brought up his diary — a notebook from five weeks in the spring of 1924, found by Dummett in the Nachlass in 1954, flagged briefly (without quotation) in the preface to his 1973 book, and not published in full until 1994 in German, edited by Gottfried Gabriel and Wolfgang Kienzler, and 1996 in English. It records fierce nationalism, contempt for parliamentary government, and antisemitism extending to the view that Jews should be stripped of political rights or expelled outright. Two of the regulars, Daniel and Renata, have argued about what to make of this ever since, and since I keep finding real merit on both sides, I'd like a third opinion.

(1) Daniel's opening position was that the diary is simply irrelevant to Frege's philosophy of logic and language, and that treating it as relevant is a version of the genetic fallacy: whether Hume's Principle can salvage the logicist project despite Basic Law V's inconsistency, whether the context principle is well motivated, whether sense and reference are rightly distinguished — none of it, he said, is touched by what its author thought about parliamentary government or Jewish political rights. (2) Renata agreed that no specific argument in the Grundgesetze is thereby invalidated, but said this concedes less than Daniel thinks. Historians of philosophy routinely use a thinker's temperament to explain which premises seemed self-evident and which alternatives never got a hearing, and she floated — as a hypothesis, not a proof — that the impatience with any psychologistic softening of objectivity behind the third-realm picture in "Der Gedanke" looks of a piece with the diary's contempt for compromise and its hope for strong, unchallengeable leadership. She added that Frege himself asked his adopted son and literary executor, Alfred, to preserve his political writings alongside the logical and mathematical ones, trusting posterity to judge them more kindly than his contemporaries had — so the line Daniel draws between "real philosophy" and "mere diary entries" wasn't obviously one Frege himself drew. (3) Daniel replied that the "same sensibility" move is unfalsifiable, since one can always narrate some connection after the fact between a thinker's metaphysics and politics, and that the diary itself resists being smoothed into one rigid outlook, since it also records Frege's opposition to a nationalist mass rally being organized in Halle that same spring — hardly the mark of a man whose rigidity ran straight through from logic to politics. (4) Renata's rejoinder separated two claims she thought Daniel kept conflating: "the diary shows the logic is unsound," which she had already conceded was false, and "the diary is relevant to how the discipline reads and canonizes Frege," which the unfalsifiability worry doesn't touch, since at most it counsels caution rather than silence. She noted that excluding the diary from the officially edited Nachlass — a choice Alfred made and later editors sustained, against Frege's own expressed wishes, until Dummett's 1973 remarks eventually forced the issue — was already a judgment about relevance, not the neutral default Daniel was assuming. (5) Daniel's last word was a provenance worry: what survives isn't even Frege's original diary but a transcript Alfred produced well over a decade later, in the late 1930s, so whatever one thinks about the relevance question in principle, this particular piece of evidence may be too mediated to bear the interpretive weight either side has been putting on it.

They left it unresolved, and neither has persuaded the other. Could you go through the exchange move by move and adjudicate it?
