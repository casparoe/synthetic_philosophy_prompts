For an independent project in my school's philosophy elective, I've been working through some of Gottlob Frege's writing on logic and the philosophy of language — mainly the Begriffsschrift and the sense/reference distinction — and while reading around him for background, I went down a bit of a rabbit hole into his biography. It turns out that in the last year of his life, Frege kept a private diary, unpublished for about seventy years after his death, full of virulently antisemitic, anti-parliamentary, and nationalist opinions, including open sympathy for the völkisch far right in Weimar Germany. My philosophy teacher had only ever mentioned Frege as the inventor of modern predicate logic, so this genuinely surprised me. It apparently surprised Michael Dummett, too — the philosopher most responsible for how Frege is read in English today — who had already come to revere Frege as a model of pure rationality by the time he stumbled on the diary while going through Frege's unpublished papers as a young academic.

What I keep getting stuck on, though, is a further complication I found in an online debate about this: some scholars argue that Frege didn't just happen to hold repugnant political views alongside a separate, untouched body of logical work — they claim he actually put pieces of his own technical apparatus to use in his political writing, for instance his formal treatment of chains of descent (the "ancestral" of a relation, in logicians' terms) when arguing about who counts as being of "Aryan descent," or his general worries about vague predicates when thinking through how to draft enforceable antisemitic laws. Other commentators think this reads far too much into a few coincidental overlaps in vocabulary, and that Frege's logic and semantics can, and should, be judged completely independently of his politics — unlike Heidegger, where a lot of people think his ideas about authenticity and historical destiny are actually continuous with his Nazism, rather than just sitting next to it.

I'd like you to write me an essay that takes an actual position on this question, rather than settling for "it's complicated." Specifically: does the existence of Frege's political diary and writings give us genuine philosophical reason to reassess his logical and semantic work itself, or is this a case where the arguments truly can be evaluated on their own merits, independent of who made them and why? Please take the strongest version of both sides seriously, address the comparison with Heidegger directly, and finish with a clear, defended verdict — including the general principle about the relationship between a philosopher's biography and their arguments that you think this case actually supports.
