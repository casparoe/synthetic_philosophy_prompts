Random context: I'm a backend dev, zero formal philosophy training, and my wife and I have a kid on the way in a few months, so I've fallen into this weird rabbit hole of reading old parenting/education books for fun instead of doing my actual sprint work. Somehow this landed me on archive.org reading John Locke's "Some Thoughts Concerning Education" from 1693 (yes, that Locke). Most of it I can skim fine even with the weird capitalization and apostrophes everywhere, but there's a passage from sections 65-66 that I've re-read like five times and I still don't feel like I've got it. Here it is, spelling kept as-is:

"I have seen Parents so heap Rules on their Children, that it was impossible for the poor little Ones to remember a tenth Part of them, much less to observe them. However, they were either by Words or Blows corrected for the Breach of those multiply'd and often very impertinent Precepts. Whence it naturally follow'd that the Children minded not what was said to them, when it was evident to them that no Attention they were capable of was sufficient to preserve them from Transgression, and the Rebukes which follow'd it.

Let therefore your Rules to your Son be as few as possible, and rather fewer than more than seem absolutely necessary. For if you burden him with many Rules, one of these two Things must necessarily follow; that either he must be very often punish'd, which will be of ill Consequence, by making Punishment too frequent and familiar; or else you must let the Transgressions of some of your Rules go unpunish'd, whereby they will of course grow contemptible, and your Authority become cheap to him. Make but few Laws, but see they be well observ'd when once made. Few Years require but few Laws, and as his Age increases, when one Rule is by Practice well establish'd, you may add another.

But pray remember, Children are not to be taught by Rules which will be always slipping out of their Memories. What you think necessary for them to do, settle in them by an indispensable Practice, as often as the Occasion returns; and if it be possible, make Occasions. This will beget Habits in them, which being once establish'd, operate of themselves easily and naturally, without the Assistance of the Memory."

I think I get the surface-level point, sort of - drill it in as a habit instead of reciting rules at them, kinda like how a decent typist isnt consciously recalling "index finger to F key," their hands just do it. But two things are genuinely tripping me up and I'd like actual help, not a spark-notes paraphrase. First: that last line about habits operating "without the Assistance of the Memory" - is Locke saying a habit is stored/retrieved in some totally different way than a fact or a rule is, almost like a separate category of mental furniture? He's the guy whose whole "Essay Concerning Human Understanding" is built on ideas getting into the mind and combining, so I'm curious whether this habit stuff fits cleanly into that picture or is quietly doing something else. Second, and this is the one that's really bugging me: I'm pretty sure I've seen Locke described elsewhere (blog posts, some parenting-history article, cant remember) as basically "the philosopher who said to reason with your kids like small rational people instead of just drilling rules into them" - and this passage reads like the opposite of that, i.e. don't even bother routing things through their memory/understanding, just condition the habit directly. Is that an actual internal tension in his view, or is there a more careful way both pieces of advice are meant to fit together? Go as deep into the weeds as you need to, I'll look up any terms I don't know.
