Quick bit of context: I'm taking a philosophy of mathematics seminar this semester (cross-listed with the math department), and this week's session was on "intuitive" arguments against the continuum hypothesis — the kind of argument people reach for once they've accepted that Godel and Cohen closed off the option of just proving CH true or false from ZFC. My professor put two of these side by side and I honestly can't stop turning them over. First there's Godel's own move in "What is Cantor's Continuum Problem?", where instead of offering a proof he just lists off consequences of CH that he calls implausible or "highly unexpected" — like the fact that CH lets you decompose the whole plane into countably many curves via a clever use of choice — and basically argues that the sheer accumulated weirdness of these consequences is evidence CH is false. Then theres Freiling's "throwing darts at the real line" argument from the 80s, which is a totally different kind of animal: one clean symmetry/probability thought experiment about darts landing on the real line that, if you accept the setup, commits you to an axiom thats actually provably equivalent to not-CH.

I brought this up with my study group and we couldn't agree on how to even compare the two fairly, since they don't feel like the same species of argument at all — one's basically an aggregate "look how ugly all this is" argument, the other dresses itself up as almost a formal derivation from one self-evident premise. Could you write something that puts the two properly side by side for me? I want to know what kind of intuition or evidence each one is actually smuggling in underneath the surface, and I vaguely remember someone (Maddy, maybe?) objecting that a version of Freiling's argument proves too much — that it also seems to tell against the axiom of choice/well-ordering the reals regardless of what you think about CH — so I'd be curious if anything analogous can be said against Godel's approach. Most importantly, don't just leave it at "both are kind of interesting": tell me which of the two, if either, actually gives us stronger grounds for doubting CH, and defend that verdict.
