I spent most of my working life as an experimental physicist, and it is only in retirement that I have found time to read philosophy properly rather than in stolen evenings. A few former colleagues and I now meet monthly to work through a text together, and this month's choice was Leibniz — the Discourse on Metaphysics together with the more readable stretches of the Theodicy. What struck me, perhaps predictably for someone with my background, is how much Leibniz's picture of God "choosing" the best of all possible worlds resembles an optimization problem. God surveys the totality of possible worlds, each a maximal collection of mutually compossible individuals, and selects the one that is, in Leibniz's own phrase, "simplest in hypotheses and richest in phenomena" — rather as a physicist might seek the solution that extremizes an action integral. Perhaps it is not surprising that a co-inventor of the calculus should think this way. In any case, that resemblance is what makes an old objection to Leibniz bite for me harder than the more familiar Voltaire-and-earthquakes version of the problem of evil ever did. If the "goodness" of a possible world admits no upper bound, or admits one that is never actually attained, then talk of a single best possible world may be no better posed than talk of "the largest real number less than one." For any candidate world, however good, a better one might always be conceivable, leaving at most an unattained supremum, and perhaps not even that.

From what I have read since, Leibniz seems to have anticipated some version of this worry and to have answered it by invoking his principle of sufficient reason: if there were no uniquely best world, he suggests, God would have had no sufficient reason to actualize any one world rather than another, and yet a world has in fact been actualized, so a best world must exist after all. I find this move suspiciously quick, though I cannot immediately locate the flaw in it. I also gather, from a discussion that came up in our group, that some contemporary philosophers of religion have tried to rescue the existence of a best world in a quite different way — for instance, by having God actualize a whole plurality of sufficiently good worlds (a "theistic multiverse," as I believe Klaas Kraay has called it) rather than a single unsurpassable one — and that this maneuver has in turn been challenged on set-theoretic grounds.

Against that background, I would like you to write an essay assessing whether the "no-best-possible-world" problem is fatal to Leibniz's claim that the actual world is the best of all possible worlds. Please engage seriously with both Leibniz's own sufficient-reason rebuttal and at least one further strategy, contemporary or otherwise, for either rescuing a best world or dissolving the need for one, and weigh these against the plain alternative that "best possible world" simply fails to pick out anything determinate. Don't retreat into pure exposition: end with a clear, specific verdict on whether Leibniz's inference from "a perfect God actualized this world" to "this world is the best possible one" survives the objection, and state plainly what you take to be the single strongest consideration behind your verdict.
