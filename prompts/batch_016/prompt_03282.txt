In retirement I have finally found time to read philosophy properly, and I confess I gravitated toward the early logical positivists partly because Moritz Schlick himself trained as a physicist, which made me feel on somewhat familiar ground. This led me to Carnap's intellectual autobiography, where he remarks, almost in passing, that nearly everyone in the Vienna Circle thought of himself as a socialist and cared deeply about social and political renewal. Digging further, I found that the Circle's 1929 manifesto, co-authored by Carnap, Hahn, and Neurath, explicitly ties the "scientific world-conception" to movements for economic reform, popular education, and the rational reorganization of communal life, and that Neurath had actually headed a central planning office for the short-lived Bavarian Soviet Republic a decade earlier, in 1919. What puzzles me is that this is the very same circle that insisted, as a matter of principle, that value and normative statements have no cognitive content whatsoever: they are neither true nor false, and no amount of logic or observation could ever show that one social arrangement is better than another. When I mentioned this apparent oddity to an acquaintance with a philosophy background, she told me that historians of the Circle are themselves divided about it: some maintain there is a genuine, almost doctrinal link between the anti-metaphysical logic of science and its practitioners' socialism, while others insist the connection was purely biographical, a matter of reform-minded people who happened to gravitate toward positivism, with no logical relation between the two commitments at all.

I would like an essay from you on the philosophical question underneath this historical puzzle, rather than on the historiography as such: can a strict non-cognitivism about value, the view that normative claims are never candidates for truth or falsity, be held consistently alongside a wholehearted personal commitment to a specific, substantive political program such as rational economic planning? Or does taking non-cognitivism seriously undercut any principled basis for that further commitment, so that the Circle's politics could only ever be an unearned add-on to its official philosophy, bolted on rather than growing out of it? Please weigh the strongest considerations you can find on each side, but don't leave the matter unresolved: tell me where you ultimately come down and why.
