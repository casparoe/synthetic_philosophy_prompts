I'm taking an upper-level seminar this term that sits at the intersection of metaphysics and philosophy of language, and this week's unit paired two readings that, according to our instructor, are best understood as rival attempts to solve the same underlying puzzle in two very different vocabularies: the early chapters of Bradley's Appearance and Reality, where he develops his regress argument against relations, and Frege's "Function and Concept" together with "On Concept and Object." Bradley's argument is the one I feel I understand reasonably well: if some relation R is needed to unite two terms a and b, and R is conceived as an entity in its own right, distinct from a and b, then it seems we need a further relation to tie R to a and b, and another to tie that relation to its relata, and so on without end — a regress that Bradley takes as evidence that independent relations cannot do the unifying work we ordinarily assume they do. Frege's response to what looks like a version of the same predicament is to claim that concepts are essentially "unsaturated" or incomplete in a way that objects are not, so that a concept and an object simply combine without needing any further relation to glue them together — though this move is notorious for generating the "concept horse" problem, since trying to refer to a concept as such (as in "the concept horse") seems to yield an expression that names an object rather than a concept, so that the sentence "the concept horse is not a concept" comes out sounding simultaneously true and absurd.

After seminar, several of us could not agree on how seriously to take our instructor's framing: is Frege's unsaturatedness actually a solution to Bradley's problem, or does it merely relocate the very same difficulty, perhaps into the "fitting together" of the unsaturated concept and the saturated object, which starts to sound suspiciously like the sort of relation Bradley's regress was meant to rule out? I would like your help thinking this through properly. First, please spell out as precisely as you can what problem, if any, the two views genuinely share, given that Bradley is worried about relations holding between terms of the same basic kind while Frege is dealing with the fundamentally asymmetric structure of a function applied to an argument; it is not obvious to me that "the unity of the proposition" means quite the same thing in both contexts. Second, I would like your own assessment of which position offers the more stable account of how a proposition, or the fact it states, constitutes a genuine unity rather than a mere list of parts — Bradley's skepticism about the reality of independent relations, or Frege's unsaturated concepts together with the concept-horse cost they incur. Please do not simply summarize both positions; I am after an actual verdict, together with the reasoning behind it.
