My sister and her husband are about to begin a third round of IVF after two failed cycles. The clinic puts her odds this time at roughly one in six, and she has decided simply to believe the cycle will work, telling the family she would rather not hear the number again. Over dinner last weekend, my mother and my sister's closest friend, a fertility nurse, argued about this at length, and I still cannot decide who had the better of it.

(1) The nurse said this is not reasonable: the odds are the odds, and believing otherwise will not change them, only set my sister up for a harder fall if the cycle fails. No one is entitled to believe something simply because they want it true rather than because the evidence supports it.

(2) My mother replied that the belief is not mere comfort: some research suggests women who enter a cycle less anxious have somewhat better outcomes, so believing it will work could modestly help bring about its own success, much as confidence helps someone make a difficult jump.

(3) The nurse answered that this proves too much: by that logic, any patient facing bad odds could be told to believe their way past them, including someone who stops a needed medication because her anxiety about being sick is supposedly what is really making things worse. Steeling yourself to get through a hard cycle, she added, is not the same as believing, against the clinic's own number, that it will succeed -- my sister could manage the first without the second.

(4) My mother said the cases are not alike: my sister, unlike that hypothetical patient, is not choosing between believing and not believing, since she has to go through the cycle regardless and cannot simply suspend judgment as one might wait for better evidence elsewhere. She also doubted that the nurse's distinction holds up in practice, since for most people the resolve to continue is carried by the belief that this attempt might actually work, not by some cooler attitude standing behind it.
