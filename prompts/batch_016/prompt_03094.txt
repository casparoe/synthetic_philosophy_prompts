I've been teaching a graduate seminar on the philosophy of perception this term, and the unit on cognitive penetration always ends the same way. We work through Fodor's case for the informational encapsulation of perceptual input systems, Macpherson's proposal that background beliefs about an object's typical color might reach experience indirectly via associative imagery, and Siegel's argument that whether penetration helps or hurts your epistemic standing depends on its etiology — her point being that a radiologist's trained eye can genuinely find more in an X-ray than a novice's does. Then we read Firestone and Scholl's broadside against the whole research program, and the class more or less concludes that the debate is unresolvable in principle.

Their objection is that every striking case has a deflationary rival on offer. Take Levin and Banaji's finding that faces matched for luminance get rated as darker when categorized as Black rather than White, a case Firestone and Scholl went after with follow-up experiments of their own: is that a change in phenomenal lightness, or just a shift in how subjects use a rating scale? Or take the memory-color effect, where a banana silhouette has to be tinged slightly blue before it looks truly gray to observers — is that residue sitting in visual experience itself, or contamination from memory and imagery arriving after the perceptual work is already done? Once attention, memory, imagery, concept application, and response bias are all granted as independent sources of exactly these kinds of behavioral signatures, it starts to look like there's no fact of the matter left over about where perception ends and cognition begins, only a verbal dispute about how to use the words.

I don't want a survey of who holds which position; my students can already reconstruct that much on their own. What I'd like from you is an account of what would actually have to be true of a case for it to count as genuine top-down penetration rather than one of these look-alikes — whether that turns on timing, neural locus, some version of Siegel's phenomenal-contrast method, or something else entirely — and why you think getting this right matters for the further claim that does most of the work motivating people's interest in the topic: that a penetrated experience might justify the very belief that produced it only in a way that is viciously circular, and so shouldn't really count as justification at all.
