Hey, I'm putting together the opening bit for a seminar session on medieval resistance theory and want to try a provocation about Aquinas on you before I commit to it in front of students — can you tell me if the argument actually holds up, and whether it's interesting enough to be worth the class time?

Most students show up already thinking "Aquinas was fine with tyrannicide." That reputation rests almost entirely on one line, from his commentary on the Sentences (Book II, d.44, q.2, a.2), in the reply to the objection that invokes Cicero's defense of Caesar's assassins. Thomas's answer there is that someone who kills a ruler who siezed power "by violence and ruse," where the people never consented and there's no superior around to judge the guy, is "praised and rewarded" for freeing the country. Notice how narrow that box actually is: naked usurpation, zero consent, zero possible appeal. It's not a general license, but it's the one sentence everybody quotes.

Now put that next to De Regimine Principum (On Kingship) I.6, written maybe a decade later, where Thomas is way more skittish about the whole idea. He tells the story of the old woman in Syracuse who prays that Dionysius outlives her, because each tyrant who replaces the last one turns out worse, and he says outright that its dangerous for private individuals to go around killing rulers on their own initiative, tyrants included. The remedies he actually recommends all track who has standing to remove a bad ruler — the community, if they're the ones who installed him, or a superior authority, if there is one — with prayer as the fallback if neither is available. Nowhere does a private citizen just get to act. The Summa (II-II q.42 a.2 ad 3) has the same structure: overthrowing a tyrant isn't "sedition" — except Thomas immediately adds a hedge that this only holds if the resulting mess doesn't end up costing the community more than the tyranny did.

My claim: there's no real "young permissive Thomas, old cautious Thomas" arc here (I've seen it taught that way) — it's one restrictive view throughout, and the Sentences line is a narrow edge case about bare usurpers, not a template for tyranny in general. Two spots where I feel shaky: (1) am I harmonizing the Sentences passage too charitably instead of admitting a real change of mind, and (2) the usurper-vs-tyrant-by-misrule distinction I'm leaning on — is that actually Thomas's own, or am I backreading it from Bartolus and the later civilians? Push on both, and tell me honestly if this is old news to anyone who actually works on this stuff.
