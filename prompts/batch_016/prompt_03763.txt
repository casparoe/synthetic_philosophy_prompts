Since retiring from a career in evolutionary biology, I have finally had the leisure to read philosophy of science in some depth, and Karl Popper's relationship with Darwinism has turned out to be far more tangled than the popular caricature suggests. In his earlier work, Popper argued that natural selection is "almost tautological" and downgraded it from a testable theory to a "metaphysical research programme." Yet in the inaugural Darwin Lecture at Darwin College, Cambridge, on 8 November 1977 — published the following year in Dialectica as "Natural Selection and the Emergence of Mind" — he announced that he had "changed [his] mind about the testability and logical status of the theory of natural selection," while in the same passage insisting, "I still believe that natural selection works this way as a research programme." Several historians and philosophers of science have argued that this "recantation" conceded remarkably little, and that Popper's original misgivings survived essentially intact beneath a change of tone.

Write an essay on this controversy. Assess whether Popper's 1978 statement amounted to a genuine, substantive change of position on the scientific status of natural selection, or whether it is better read as a rhetorical retreat that left the underlying tautology and falsifiability worries unresolved. Weigh the textual evidence and the philosophical arguments on both sides, and close with a definite verdict of your own rather than a diplomatic non-answer.
