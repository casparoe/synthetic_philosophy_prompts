After a forty-year career as an experimental condensed-matter physicist, I retired a few years ago and have since, somewhat to my own surprise, spent more evenings with analytic metaphysics than with my garden. A retired colleague and I have taken to meeting monthly to work through the philosophy-of-science literature, and we have been stuck for weeks now on the dispute between David Lewis's Humean best-system account of laws and David Armstrong's rival view that laws are relations of necessitation holding between universals. Having spent a career treating conservation laws and symmetry principles as load-bearing explanatory structures rather than as convenient shorthand for observed patterns, I confess to strong, if perhaps naive, anti-Humean sympathies, and I would like to have them properly tested rather than simply confirmed.

Could you compare the two positions in some depth, beyond the "mere regularity versus genuine necessity" gloss one finds in introductory treatments? I would especially like you to engage with what strikes me as each theory's most serious internal wound. On the Armstrong side, there is Lewis's own "inference problem" objection (I gather from "New Work for a Theory of Universals"), his complaint that a brute, contingent, second-order relation N(F,G) between universals cannot intelligibly force it to be true that all Fs are Gs, so that simply naming the relation "necessitation" does no explanatory work on its own. On the Lewis side, there is what he himself called the "Big Bad Bug": the way undermining futures threaten to make his best-system account of objective chance inconsistent with the Principal Principle. Which of these two difficulties do you take to be more damaging to the view it afflicts, and does your answer push someone with realist instincts about the modal force of laws toward Armstrong, or is his cure worse than the Humean's disease?

As something of a bonus, I have also recently been reading Alexander Bird's Nature's Metaphysics, where dispositional essentialism is offered as a third way on which laws come out metaphysically necessary because the relevant properties have their nomic roles essentially. I understand E. J. Lowe pressed an individuation-regress, or circularity, charge against this sort of view, which Bird tried to deflect with a "structuralist" move. Does dispositional essentialism actually buy its way out of both the inference problem and the Big Bad Bug, or does it just trade them in for a regress problem of its own? I would appreciate your own considered, justified verdict on which of the three positions best survives its besetting difficulty, rather than an even-handed survey of considerations on all sides.
