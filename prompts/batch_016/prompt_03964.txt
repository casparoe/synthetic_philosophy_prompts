I work as a software engineer and have no formal training in philosophy, but over the past year I have been working through Nietzsche on my own, mostly during train commutes. I recently reached Ecce Homo, and the chapter "Why I Am So Clever" caught me off guard: rather than the sweeping pronouncements about the Übermensch or the death of God I had been expecting, Nietzsche spends page after page on his diet, on which climates and altitudes suit a thinker, on the precise amounts of tea and coffee one should permit oneself, and on why a philosopher should avoid sitting still for too long. Coming from someone who spends a fair amount of free time building small tools to track his own habits, it read almost like a nineteenth-century self-optimization log.

What I cannot quite settle for myself is how this sits alongside the Third Essay of On the Genealogy of Morals, "What Is the Meaning of Ascetic Ideals?", where Nietzsche goes after the ascetic priest precisely for treating the body as something to be starved, monitored, and denied, diagnosing the whole apparatus as ressentiment turned inward, a "will to nothingness" dressed up as virtue. I mentioned this to a friend who studied philosophy, and she said Nietzsche has a ready answer available to him: the priest disciplines the body out of hatred of life, whereas Nietzsche's own regimen is an expression of strength and of what he elsewhere calls "great health," so the two projects point in opposite directions even though they look similar from the outside. That answer strikes me as a little too convenient, but I do not have the background to press on it myself.

I would like you to make the strongest case you can for the less comfortable position: that Nietzsche's program of dietary, climatic, and recreational self-regulation in Ecce Homo is not a genuine alternative to the ascetic ideal he attacks in the Genealogy, but is instead a variant of the very same disciplinary logic, merely redirected toward a different end. Please engage directly with the "direction of the will" defense my friend offered rather than setting it aside, and explain concretely why the structural similarities between the ascetic priest's regimen and Nietzsche's own outweigh the difference in aim that he claims for himself.
