I'm putting together a lecture on the hole argument for a philosophy of physics seminar I'm teaching this term, and there's a piece of the standard story I can gesture at but not actually explain with any precision -- which is an awkward place to be a couple days out from facing a room of sharp grad students. Here's what's bugging me: everyone treats the old Leibniz-Clarke shift business (God could've plopped the whole material universe five feet east, or built it mirror-reversed with East and West swapped, and nothing qualitative would differ) as basically a verificationist/PII worry about redundant, indiscernible possibilities. The hole argument, by contrast, is supposed to be strictly worse, because it doesn't just leave the substantivalist with redundant possibilities, it leaves them with genuine indeterminism -- the same laws and the same data turn out compatible with different developments. I can recite that this is the recieved view, but I cant cleanly explain WHY restricting the diffeomorphism to be identity everywhere outside some bounded hole (rather than a rigid transformation applied to the whole universe at once, the way Leibniz's shift does) is the specific move that upgrades "merely indiscernible worlds" into "same past, different future." Could you walk me through the actual gears of that, at a fairly intuitive level -- not just "diffeomorphisms are the gauge symmetries of GR," but why the locality of the transformation specifically is doing the work?

Bonus question if you've got room for it, since it comes up every year in discussion section and I never give a fully satisfying answer: the standard escape route for a substantivalist is to go "sophisticated" and deny that spacetime points have primitive trans-world identity, so the hole-transformed model isn't really a distinct possibility, just the same possibility redescribed. But once you've dropped haecceities like that, in what sense are you still a substantivalist rather than someone who's quietly conceded the relationist's picture, on which points just are individuated by their qualitative/metrical role? Some critics push exactly this worry and I've never seen a response to it that felt like more than "well, we still insist the manifold exists independently of matter" -- which just relocates the mystery rather than resolving it. Curious what you think the best substantivalist comeback actually is.
