I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind currently drafting a qualifying paper on deflationary approaches to phenomenal consciousness, and a recent reading-group session on this material left me more confused than when I started, since two positions I'd always treated as basically interchangeable turned out to provoke very different reactions from people in the room. Keith Frankish's illusionism holds that phenomenal consciousness, as classically conceived (intrinsic, ineffable, directly and non-inferentially known qualitative character), does not actually exist, and that the real explanatory burden is to account for why it nonetheless seems to us that it does — what Frankish calls "the illusion problem," offered as a replacement for the traditional hard problem. Michael Tye's representationalism, by contrast, is standardly classified as a realist, reductive theory: on his PANIC account, phenomenal character just is a certain kind of representational content (poised, abstract, nonconceptual, intentional content), so qualia are real and perfectly physical, merely mischaracterized by opponents as irreducible, non-representational properties.

What I can't work out on my own is whether these are genuinely two different kinds of theory or the same underlying picture wearing different rhetorical outfits. Illusionists still owe an account of why introspection represents our experiences as having rich, ineffable qualitative properties even though, on their view, no such properties are ever instantiated — and discharging that burden seems to require positing some introspectively represented content doing more or less the job Tye assigns to phenomenal character itself. So the disagreement might just be about whether that content is veridical, rather than about the representational structure of experience as such.

Could you compare the two positions in some depth and adjudicate this? I'd find it useful if you framed part of your answer around David Chalmers' "meta-problem of consciousness" — the problem of explaining why we judge consciousness to be puzzling at all — since both camps arguably owe an answer to it whether or not they use his terminology. Concretely: is illusionism a genuinely distinct explanatory strategy from representationalism, or does it collapse into representationalism-plus-an-error-theory? And, taking a side, which of the two views does more real explanatory work toward resolving the meta-problem without simply relabeling or relocating the hard problem? Please defend your answer rather than just laying out both sides neutrally.
