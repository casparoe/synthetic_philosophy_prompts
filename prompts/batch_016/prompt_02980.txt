I recently came across the private argument Kurt Gödel made to his mother, Marianne, for why he was convinced an afterlife exists: since the world is rational and meaningful, he thought it made no sense for a being with as much room for personal and intellectual growth as a human to be annihilated long before that potential -- especially our capacity to keep learning from our own mistakes -- was ever more than barely tapped, so some further, non-earthly existence must complete it. He also held, partly on the strength of his own incompleteness theorems, that such a mind could never simply be identical to a finite mechanical system, whether a brain's neurons or a computer program.

Holding both convictions together, how do you think Gödel would have judged today's proposals for "mind uploading" -- scanning a brain and running the resulting model on a computer so that a person's learning could continue indefinitely after biological death? Would this count, on his own terms, as a genuine continuation of a person's rational essence, or merely a mechanical impersonation of one? Please reconstruct the reasoning he would most plausibly have used, not just your verdict.
