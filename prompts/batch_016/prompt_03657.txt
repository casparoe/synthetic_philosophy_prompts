I'm a backend engineer, and one question that comes up constantly in my work is what it actually means for one system to "implement" another — whether a VM running bytecode is really executing a program or just simulating it, and whether that distinction carries any real weight. A friend doing a philosophy PhD told me this has a notorious pathological case in philosophy of mind. Putnam, and later Searle, argued that almost any physical system with a large enough state space — a rock, or in Searle's preferred image, a waterfall — can be paired up after the fact with the state-transition diagram of essentially any finite automaton, including one that supposedly "runs" a mind. If that's right, saying a physical process implements a given computation is close to content-free, and computationalism (the view that having the right computational organization is what makes something a mind) ends up trivially satisfied by everything, which amounts to explaining nothing.

Digging further, I found two rescues for computationalism that seem to pull in quite different directions. David Chalmers, responding directly to Putnam, argues that a genuine implementation has to preserve the automaton's whole causal and counterfactual profile: the system must behave correctly not just on the input it actually got, but on every input it could have gotten, which he formalizes by moving from bare finite-state automata to richer "combinatorial-state automata." Scott Aaronson, writing considerably later, barely engages with that move — he name-checks Chalmers's response among a few others, then argues that a more direct fix is complexity-theoretic: a physical process should count as implementing a computation only if there is no efficient way to use it as an oracle that beats solving the problem from scratch, essentially the same move complexity theorists make when they rule out "trivial" reductions in defining NP-completeness.

I'd like you to actually compare these two proposals rather than summarize them in parallel. Do they agree about what, precisely, is wrong with Putnam and Searle's construction, or are they targeting two different failure modes that happen to share a symptom? Is either one secretly a special case of the other — for instance, would a mapping forced to respect counterfactual structure across exponentially many possible inputs thereby also be forced into Aaronson's kind of computational expense, making his criterion redundant, or is it easy to satisfy one without the other? If you can, sketch a toy system where the two criteria actually diverge, one calling it a genuine implementation and the other not, since that would settle whether this is a real disagreement or just a difference in emphasis. Finally, tell me which proposal does more to actually rescue computationalism from the waterfall argument, and why; if you think neither fully succeeds, say what a successful criterion would still need to supply.
