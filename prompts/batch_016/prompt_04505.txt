I spent my career as a field ethologist, watching corvids solve puzzles and octopuses navigate mazes; only in retirement have I turned to philosophy, which has sent me back to Descartes with newly skeptical eyes. I recently learned that his claim that animals are mere unthinking automata did not go unchallenged in his own lifetime: the Cambridge Platonist Henry More pressed him on it directly, in a real exchange of letters in 1648–49, and Descartes replied at length in his own defense.

Please dramatize that dispute as a face-to-face dialogue between the two men, each arguing with the specific reasoning he actually used, not a modern paraphrase of it. Descartes should rely on his own criteria for detecting thought rather than simply asserting that animals feel nothing, while More should press him with the sort of concrete natural-historical evidence and theological misgivings he actually raised. Neither should get the last word too easily: each must answer the other's strongest point, and the whole exchange should stay within the intellectual horizon of the 1640s rather than smuggling in later biology.
