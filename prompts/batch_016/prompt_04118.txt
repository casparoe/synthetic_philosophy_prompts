I'm a PhD student in metaphysics currently TAing a graduate seminar on modal logic and the semantics of de re modality. Every year students arrive able to recite the standard contrast between David Lewis's counterpart theory and the more orthodox view that one and the same individual can literally exist in more than one possible world — "worlds are concrete and individuals are world-bound" versus "just rigidly designate the individual and stipulate what happens to them" — without ever having felt any real argumentative pressure on either side. For this week's section I'd rather hand out a dialogue than another secondary-literature summary.

Could you write me a dialogue of roughly 900-1100 words between "C," a committed counterpart theorist, and "T," a defender of genuine transworld identity, who run into each other after a colloquium talk? I'd like it to open with T pressing Kripke's "Humphrey objection" (footnote 13 of Naming and Necessity) in its full strength: not just the complaint that counterpart theory's truth conditions are about a mere stand-in rather than Humphrey himself, but the sharper worry that Humphrey couldn't care less whether some other individual, however similar, wins an election in some other world. I'd like C to give the genuine Lewisian responses to this, including the point that any semantics for de re modal claims has to say something about cross-world predication, so that T's own view owes its own account of what makes some individual at another world "the same" as Humphrey, on pain of facing exactly the same "change of subject" charge.

From there, I'd like C to turn the tables using Chisholm's Paradox: the sorites-style argument that chains together many small qualitative differences across a sequence of worlds to force the conclusion that Adam could, absurdly, turn out to be identical to Noah. T should get to explain why positing non-negotiable individual essences, rather than tracking identity through degrees of resemblance, gives the transworld-identity view real resources for blocking that chain partway through — even if it can't say in advance exactly where the chain breaks. C should then press on how costly and ad hoc that essentialism looks next to simply letting the counterpart relation be non-transitive, the way ordinary comparative resemblance already is, while conceding that this cheap fix has a price of its own: it allows principles along the lines of "possibly possibly p, therefore possibly p" to fail even when the underlying space of worlds is perfectly well-behaved. Please have both characters genuinely engage with what the other just said rather than delivering prepared lectures side by side, and don't feel obliged to hand either of them a clean win at the end — I don't think this particular debate has one.
