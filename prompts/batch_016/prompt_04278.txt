I am taking a graduate seminar on philosophy of mathematics this term, and for my final paper I want to write about Skolem's Paradox and its extension into Putnam's model-theoretic argument against realism. Before I try to turn this into a full paper, I have written up the core of the argument as I currently understand it, and I would like feedback on it — both on whether I have the technical details right and on whether the objection I raise at the end is any good.

Here is the sketch. The Löwenheim–Skolem theorem guarantees that ZFC, if it has any model at all, has a countable one; call it M. Yet ZFC proves that uncountable sets exist, so M must contain some element that M "thinks" is uncountable. This looks contradictory until we notice that when M satisfies "x is uncountable," this only means that no bijection between x and the natural numbers belongs to M's own domain — not that no such bijection exists at all. Since M itself is countable, there is, from the standpoint of the background universe of sets, a genuine bijection between the naturals and (M's version of) that "uncountable" set; M simply does not contain it among its elements. Uncountability turns out not to be an absolute property of a set but one that can fail to be tracked correctly by a given model — which is more or less the moral Skolem himself drew when he concluded that axiomatizing set theory only yields a relativity of set-theoretic notions, rather than pinning down one determinate universe of sets.

Putnam tries to generalize this. In "Models and Reality," he argues that no matter how many theoretical and observational constraints we pile onto our total theory, we can never rule out unintended interpretations of it: as one demonstration, he shows that ZF + V=L has a countable model containing any countable set of real numbers we care to specify, and treats this as evidence that the truth-value of "V=L" is no more fixed by our theorizing than the "true" size of a Skolem model's version of the reals is fixed from inside that model. Here is where I get uneasy: Skolem's Paradox is defused precisely because we can retreat to a background theory (or to the "real" universe of sets) in which the missing bijection does exist, so that uncountability keeps an absolute sense even where a particular model fails to register it. But Putnam's whole aim is to deny that there is any comparably absolute, non-relative fact about reference to retreat to. So it looks as though he wants the reassurance of the Skolem structure — a merely relative failure set against an absolute backdrop — while rejecting the very backdrop that made the original failure feel harmless rather than skeptical.

Could you look this over and tell me: (1) whether I have the mathematics of Skolem's Paradox and its standard resolution right, especially the bit about transitive models, which I have simplified away; (2) whether my summary of the V=L argument is a fair one-paragraph gloss of what Putnam is actually doing, or whether I have skipped a step that matters; and (3) whether the tension I describe in the third paragraph is a real problem for Putnam, a well-known objection that goes by some other name and that I should be citing instead, or a confusion on my part that a more careful statement of his argument would dissolve?
