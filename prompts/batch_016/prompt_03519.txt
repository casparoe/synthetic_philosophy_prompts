Ok so this has been living in my head for weeks now. I grew up Catholic (not practicing anymore but apparently still religious-topic-brained) and the whole "penal substitution" idea -- that Jesus took the punishment that was supposed to be ours -- always felt like a logic trick to me, even back when I still believed it. Like, obviously you can't just swap who gets punished for something. If my cousin robs a store, I can't volunteer to do his prison time instead and have that somehow count as him being punished, thats just not how guilt works. So imagine my surprise when I fell down a philosophy forum rabbit hole and found out David Lewis -- the modal realism/possible-worlds guy, a philosopher who as far as I know wasn't even religious, an atheist actually -- wrote an actual paper taking penal substitution seriously and trying to work out if it could be made logically coherent.

From what I pieced together (never read the actual paper, just people quoting bits of it), his whole starting point is this weird asymmetry: basically nobody thinks it's ok for a friend to serve your prison sentence for you, but almost everybody's totally fine with a friend paying off your parking ticket or even a big fine, even though a fine is technically a punishment too. And apparently, instead of treating that as us just being sloppy or inconsistent, he thought the inconsistency itself was a kind of clue -- like maybe both the "substitution is never ok" side and the "sometimes its fine" side are onto something real, and that's worth taking seriously rather than explaining away. I also vaguely remember something about him bringing in a legal comparison, like how a company can sometimes be held liable for stuff an employee did -- but honestly I might be misremembering that part or mixing it up with someone else responding to him, so don't take that bit as gospel (no pun intended).

Anyway, could you write me a dialogue between two people hashing this out? One of them should be running the standard hard-nosed objection (guilt isn't a debt, it doesn't transfer the way money does, hence the prison-sentence-swap point) and the other should be giving an honest, well-informed version of Lewis's actual case, using that fine-vs-prison asymmetry as the centerpiece rather than just falling back on generic "God's ways are mysterious" apologetics. I really want them to engage with each other's strongest point instead of talking past each other -- the skeptic needs a real answer for why we let fines slide, and the Lewis-defender needs a real answer for why prison time still feels so different if the underlying logic is supposedly the same. It doesn't need a tidy resolution at the end, I'm mostly just curious to watch the actual argument play out.
