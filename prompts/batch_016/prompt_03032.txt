I'm a PhD student in philosophy working mainly in metaethics and philosophy of language, and I've been drafting a dissertation chapter on where the Frege–Geach problem actually stands after several decades of proposed fixes. Most treatments I encounter in seminars stop at the textbook moral-modus-ponens case and declare victory for whichever theorist's solution happens to be on the syllabus that week. But the more I dig into the primary literature, the more the interesting action seems to happen one level down. There's Unwin's point that negation poses a difficulty distinct from the general embedding problem: accepting the negation of a claim like "lying is wrong" is not the same as merely refusing to accept it, and it's not obvious that Blackburn's or Gibbard's apparatus can actually mark that distinction. And there's Dreier's "creeping minimalism" charge, which suggests that any expressivist sophisticated enough to handle these embedding worries has helped themselves to so much minimalist talk about truth and representation that it becomes unclear what, if anything, their view still denies that a cognitivist would accept. This came up in an argument with a friend in my cohort who thinks the whole dialectic is basically settled in expressivism's favor at this point, and I wasn't convinced.

Could you write me a dialogue (a couple of pages is fine) between a sophisticated contemporary expressivist and an equally sharp cognitivist critic that works through this later stage of the debate, rather than re-litigating the textbook modus ponens case? I'd like the expressivist to offer a specific, real proposal for handling negation — something in the spirit of Gibbard's hyperplans or Schroeder's "being for" account, or some hybrid of the two — and to actually apply it to a concrete embedded sentence. I'd like the critic to press the negation problem first, then pivot to the creeping-minimalism worry once the first objection has been provisionally answered, forcing the expressivist to say something substantive about what really separates their view from a sufficiently minimalist cognitivism. Please have both sides genuinely respond to each other's specific moves rather than talking past one another — neither should be a pushover — and don't feel obliged to wrap things up neatly at the end; a sharpened disagreement would be a more honest outcome than a tidy resolution, given where the literature actually stands.
