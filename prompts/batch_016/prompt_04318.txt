So a couple years ago I picked painting back up after basically a decade-long gap since art school, and to force myself to take it more seriously I signed up for a non-credit adult-ed seminar on aesthetics at the community college near me. The first assigned reading was Tolstoy's "What is Art?", which I'd only ever heard summarized in one lazy sentence ("art = sharing feelings, and it should be moral"). Actually reading the thing kind of got under my skin, partly because by his own three-part test he ends up throwing out basically all of his own fiction, except two early short stories, as "bad art" -- and now i keep side-eyeing my own paintings wondering if they'd survive the same test.

Anyway, during the discussion section two people in the class -- I'll call them Deb and Marco -- got into a whole thing about whether the famous wordless opening of Pixar's "Up" (marriage, miscarriage, and widowhood in about four minutes, reliably wrecks whoever watches it) would count as good art, bad art, or not-art-at-all -- "counterfeit," in Tolstoy's word -- by his own standards. It went roughly like this. (1) Deb said it's actually close to Tolstoy's ideal: it's wordless, so it's understandable to literally anyone regardless of language, culture, or education (his "clearness" condition, and the whole universal-vs-upper-class-art thing), and it demonstrably infects a huge, totally mixed audience with real grief and tenderness rather than just a trained arty crowd -- the same combo he praises in Bible stories and folk legends. (2) Marco said clearness and infection aren't the whole test -- sincerity is, and Tolstoy says flat out that's the most important of the three, and it's almost always missing from anything produced by professionals for pay rather than out of inner need. A four-minute sequence storyboarded, test-screened, and reworked by a rotating team of animators for a nine-figure movie is exactly the "manufactured to a ready-made recipe" thing he spends whole chapters ranting about, not sincere expression. (3) Deb came back that Tolstoy also loved plenty of art with no single traceable author -- folk songs, hymns, communal peasant crafts -- so sincerity can't mean "one identifiable person's private inner feeling"; it has to cash out as an effect, i.e. whether it reliably makes people feel something real versus leaving them cold or just diverted, and by that test the scene clearly passes. (4) Marco said that's a real disanalogy though: folk art comes out of a shared lived experience the makers actually had, while a corporate storyboard process runs backward from "audience reaction," via testing and notes -- the opposite direction from sincerity as Tolstoy defines it -- and if "reliably produces an effect" were sufficient on its own, manipulative ad jingles and insurance-commercial tearjerkers would count as sincere too, which seems to prove way too much.

Nobody at the table settled it, and honestly the more I think it over the less sure I am either, especially since it's made me weirdly paranoid about my own painting lately.
