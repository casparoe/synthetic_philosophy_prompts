I've been a longtime lurker on LessWrong and the Alignment Forum, and one debate that resurfaces every few months there is whether it even makes sense to say a large language model "believes" anything, as opposed to just being a very sophisticated compressor of statistical regularities in human text. Usually the discussion gestures vaguely at Dennett's intentional stance or Searle's Chinese Room, but it occurred to me recently that Donald Davidson's account of radical interpretation, which I first ran into in a philosophy of language course years ago, is a much more rigorous framework for asking exactly this question, and I've never seen anyone actually run it on a modern model rather than on Davidson's usual case of an isolated human speaker.

For context: on Davidson's picture, the radical interpreter has no prior access to a speaker's language, beliefs, or desires, and must solve simultaneously for a Tarski-style truth theory (functioning as a theory of meaning) and a theory of belief, using only the pattern of sentences the speaker holds true under various observed circumstances. Because this evidence alone can't disentangle "what they believe" from "what their words mean," Davidson resolves the problem with the Principle of Charity: interpret the speaker as mostly right about the world and mostly rational by the interpreter's own lights. He insists this is not a convenient heuristic but partly constitutive of belief and meaning as such. In his later work he tightens the picture further with the idea of triangulation: genuine thought and objectivity, on his view, arise only from at least two creatures jointly responsive to shared causes in a common environment, each thereby able to locate the "same" object as the other. He also accepted a version of the indeterminacy of interpretation, though he insisted it was benign, comparing it to the "indeterminacy" involved in reporting the same temperature in Celsius or Fahrenheit.

Davidson died in 2003, well before anything like GPT-4 or Claude existed, so he never had occasion to apply this apparatus outside the case of embodied speakers sharing a perceptual world. I'd like your best reconstruction of how he would have approached a modern LLM, working strictly from the actual commitments described above rather than a generic appeal to "charity." Does the absence of any direct perceptual triangulation between the model and a shared environment mean radical interpretation simply cannot get started for it, even though its training corpus is itself the fossilized output of millions of humans who were triangulating with each other and the world all along? Or is there a way, on Davidson's own terms, for the model's relation to that corpus to do the work triangulation ordinarily does? And separately, is the indeterminacy at stake here still the "benign," Celsius/Fahrenheit-style indeterminacy Davidson was willing to live with, or does removing embodiment change its character into something he would have found more threatening?
