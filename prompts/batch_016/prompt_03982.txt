I am putting together a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and this week's session pairs I. J. Good's 1967 paper, "On the Principle of Total Evidence," with some applied readings on disclosure policy. As I understand the theorem Good proves there, for a Bayesian agent facing a fixed decision problem, the expected utility of consulting a piece of cost-free evidence before acting can never be lower than the expected utility of acting without it: free information, properly used, is never harmful in expectation. This looks to me like a strikingly clean, decision-theoretic vindication of transparency norms quite generally. Mandatory disclosure requirements in finance, pre-registration of clinical trials, and "sunshine" rules for regulatory rule-making all look like straightforward instances of exactly the free-information-before-a-decision structure that Good's proof covers, so it would seem to follow that any resistance to such requirements must ultimately rest on something other than the effects of information as such — protecting incumbents, say, or paternalism about people's capacity to cope with bad news — rather than any genuine decision-theoretic cost of the disclosure itself.

What has been nagging at me is that actual policy does not behave as though this were settled. The 2008 Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act bars American health insurers and employers from requiring or using an applicant's genetic test results, and the British insurance industry has operated for more than two decades under a voluntary arrangement with government — originally a moratorium, since 2018 the Code on Genetic Testing and Insurance — under which insurers do not ask most applicants to disclose existing predictive genetic test results. In neither case is the restriction presented as a regrettable compromise against an otherwise desirable free flow of information; keeping the information out of the underwriting decision is treated as the whole point of the policy. Before I present Good's theorem to my students as grounding a general presumption toward maximal disclosure, I would like a precise answer: is there a genuine decision-theoretic rationale for restrictions like these, and if so, exactly which premise of Good's argument fails to carry over from the case of a single, lone decision-maker to a setting like insurance underwriting? I would rather have the precise mechanism than a gesture toward "incentives are complicated."
