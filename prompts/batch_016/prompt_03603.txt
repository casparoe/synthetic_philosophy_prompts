I'm writing a research paper for my U.S. History class on Frederick Douglass's break with William Lloyd Garrison over how to read the Constitution, and I've gotten stuck on a passage from Douglass's autobiography, *Life and Times of Frederick Douglass*, where he explains why he abandoned the Garrisonian view that the Constitution was a pro-slavery document. Here is the passage:

"But for the responsibility of conducting a public journal, and the necessity imposed upon me of meeting opposite views from abolitionists outside of New England, I should in all probability have remained firm in my disunion views. My new circumstances compelled me to re-think the whole subject, and study with some care not only the just and proper rules of legal interpretation, but the origin, design, nature, rights, powers, and duties of civil governments, and also the relations which human beings sustain to it. By such a course of thought and reading I was conducted to the conclusion that the Constitution of the United States—inaugurated 'to form a more perfect union, establish justice, insure domestic tranquility, provide for the common defense, promote the general welfare, and secure the blessings of liberty'—could not well have been designed at the same time to maintain and perpetuate a system of rapine and murder like slavery, especially as not one word can be found in the Constitution to authorize such a belief. Then, again, if the declared purposes of an instrument are to govern the meaning of all its parts and details, as they clearly should, the Constitution of our country is our warrant for the abolition of slavery in every State of the Union."

What I can't quite untangle is what kind of argument Douglass is actually making here. In the space of a few sentences, he seems to reach for at least three different grounds at once: that the word "slavery" appears nowhere in the text itself, that the document could not have been "designed" to protect something so at odds with its own stated ends, and that the preamble's "declared purposes" should govern how every other clause gets read. Are these genuinely distinct lines of argument doing different logical work, or is Douglass really just restating one argument in three guises? And if someone granted him all of this but still insisted, the way Chief Justice Taney effectively did in Dred Scott, that the framers' actual intentions and the historical bargains behind clauses like the fugitive slave clause are what should settle the Constitution's meaning, does anything in this passage give Douglass a way to block that response, or would he need a premise he hasn't actually supplied here?
