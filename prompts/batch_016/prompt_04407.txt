I have no academic background in philosophy, just years of reading on my own, and something about Plato has been nagging at me. In Book Three of the Republic, Socrates insists that a guardian, of all people, should be the very last person to get drunk and lose track of himself, since a healthy soul depends on reason keeping a permanent grip on the appetites. Yet early in the Laws, the Athenian Stranger spends a long stretch defending supervised drinking parties, run by a sober overseer he calls the "symposiarch." His argument is that getting drunk together tests and toughens self-control, in the same way a soldier's courage is tested by facing real danger rather than by being shielded from it. One passage seems to treat virtue as reason's grip on the soul never slipping; the other treats virtue as something that only becomes real once it has been exercised under exactly that kind of slippage. Please argue that the position on drunkenness Plato defends in the Laws cannot actually be reconciled with the moral psychology he lays out in the Republic.
