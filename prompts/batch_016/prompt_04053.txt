I write about the history of science and technology for a general-interest magazine, and I'm currently drafting a retrospective on Alan Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" to mark the seventy-fifth anniversary of its publication. Most such retrospectives dwell on the imitation game itself and skip past a section I find far more interesting: about two-thirds of the way through, right after laying out the test, Turing turns to what he calls the argument from extrasensory perception, and he does not treat it as a throwaway objection. He tells the reader that the statistical evidence for telepathy is, in his own words, "overwhelming," worries that a human contestant with genuine psychic ability could give the game away — for instance by correctly reporting the suit of a card the interrogator is secretly holding — and proposes, apparently in earnest, that the test be conducted inside a "telepathy-proof room." Historians usually read this as a product of its milieu: Cambridge, where Turing studied and later held a fellowship, had a long and quite serious entanglement with psychical research, with philosophers like C. D. Broad treating mediums and card-guessing experiments as data worth taking seriously well into the 1960s. An editor of mine wants the section cut from my draft as an embarrassing footnote; I keep arguing it's the moment the paper is most honest about what is actually at stake in calling something a "thinking" thing.

For a sidebar, I'd like to commission short fiction instead of more exposition, and I'm hoping you'll write it. Could you produce a short story, somewhere around 800 to 1,200 words, built around Turing's telepathy-proof room? I'm not looking for another machines-turn-against-their-creators plot; I'd rather the story sit inside the specific problem Turing was wrestling with — namely, what happens to a test built entirely on indistinguishable behavior, and to the concept of "thinking" it was meant to operationalize, if minds really can exchange information through a channel that owes nothing to computation. You could set it in Turing's own Cambridge, in a present-day lab quietly re-running a version of the imitation game and hitting the same snag, or somewhere else you find more compelling — I just want the psychical-research element handled with real specificity, the way Turing handled it, rather than used as mere atmosphere.
