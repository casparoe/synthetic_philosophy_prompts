Over the past two years, the engineering team I work on has been tracking a persistent intermittent failure in one of our production services. The failure has a distinctive signature: it occurs roughly once a week, always during periods of peak traffic, always on the same subset of servers, and always within a few minutes of a nightly batch job finishing. We have logged this pattern across so many incidents that no one on the team doubts it any longer; at this point it is simply an established fact about how the system behaves. Last month, a newly hired engineer proposed a detailed hypothesis involving a timing conflict over a shared resource between the batch job and the service, and he showed that his hypothesis would produce exactly this failure pattern, down to the timing and the affected servers. The rest of the team was impressed and began treating that fit as strong evidence that he had identified the real cause.

This is where I got stuck. Nothing new was actually observed after he proposed his hypothesis; he constructed it entirely to match a pattern we already knew in exhaustive detail. So has that pattern really tested his hypothesis at all, or has he simply reverse-engineered an explanation that was guaranteed to fit, given that he already had the answer key going in? At the same time, it seems wrong to say his hypothesis deserves no credit whatsoever - an explanation that neatly ties together every strange detail we had noticed over two years does not seem worthless merely because we already knew those details before he arrived. I mentioned this puzzle to a friend who is finishing a PhD in philosophy of science, half-expecting her to tell me I was overanalyzing an ordinary engineering meeting. Instead, she told me, with obvious delight, that I had stumbled onto a genuine and long-running tension in how philosophers think about the relationship between evidence and theory, though she refused to just explain it to me and insisted I work through it myself.

I would like to actually get to the bottom of this rather than rely on intuition. Is there a principled basis for saying whether, and to what extent, a hypothesis should be credited for explaining facts that were already fully established before it was ever proposed, or does genuine evidential support require a hypothesis to anticipate something not already known? And if a fact we were already certain of cannot, strictly speaking, do any evidential work once a hypothesis comes along to explain it, how should I make sense of cases in the history of science where explaining a long-standing, already-documented anomaly is often described as having provided some of the strongest evidence for a theory?
