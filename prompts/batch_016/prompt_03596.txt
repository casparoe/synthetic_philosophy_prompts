I'm working on a feature about the deep prehistory of an idea that keeps resurfacing in debates over central-bank stimulus and inflation: that pumping new money into an economy can give real activity a genuine, if temporary, boost, even though most economists agree that in the long run "money is neutral" and merely resets the price level. In the course of researching it, an economic historian I interviewed for the piece remarked, almost as an aside, that this whole puzzle is already laid out with striking clarity in David Hume's 1752 essay "Of Money" — so I went back to the primary text myself rather than relying on the secondhand version I half-remembered from a college economics course.

Hume opens the essay with what reads like a clean statement of monetary neutrality: considered within a single country, he writes, the "greater or less plenty of money is of no consequence; since the prices of commodities are always proportioned to the plenty of money." That much I can follow. But several pages later, while explaining why countries that had recently come into new supplies of gold and silver seemed to experience a genuine boom in industry rather than just higher prices, he writes the following (I'm quoting from the Liberty Fund online edition, which keeps Hume's original spelling):

"To account, then, for this phenomenon, we must consider, that though the high price of commodities be a necessary consequence of the encrease of gold and silver, yet it follows not immediately upon that encrease; but some time is required before the money circulates through the whole state, and makes its effect be felt on all ranks of people. At first, no alteration is perceived; by degrees the price rises, first of one commodity, then of another; till the whole at last reaches a just proportion with the new quantity of specie which is in the kingdom. In my opinion, it is only in this interval or intermediate situation, between the acquisition of money and rise of prices, that the encreasing quantity of gold and silver is favourable to industry."

I can't quite tell whether this second passage is consistent with the first, or whether Hume has quietly reached for a second, not-obviously-compatible theory to explain a real-world pattern that his official neutrality claim can't otherwise accommodate. Could you help me reconstruct, step by step, the actual mechanism he seems to have in mind — why a lag between new money arriving and prices rising should translate into more output and employment, rather than simply into a delay? And in your understanding, do historians of economic thought generally treat this "interval" argument as a serious, structurally important anticipation of later ideas about the short-run non-neutrality of money, or is it more of a loose, half-developed aside that later readers have made more of than Hume intended?
