Last night I had a long argument with a friend who is midway through a critical-care residency, and neither of us really conceded anything. It started when she mentioned a family on her unit that won't consent to withdrawing support from a relative who has already been declared dead by neurological criteria. I brought up Jahi McMath — the California teenager who was declared brain dead in 2013 after complications from a tonsillectomy, was then moved by her family to New Jersey (the only state with a full statutory religious exemption to its brain-death law), and was only pronounced dead a second time, of liver failure, in 2018. My friend's view is that this is basically a case of a grieving family refusing to accept settled medicine and law: on the whole-brain standard, Jahi died in 2013, full stop, and everything that happened afterward was just a body being run by machinery. I argued that this is too quick once you look closely at what the whole-brain standard is supposed to mean, and why we're entitled to treat it as death rather than as a catastrophic injury.

In particular, I brought up Alan Shewmon's 1998 meta-analysis of roughly 175 cases of patients who, despite meeting every clinical criterion for total and irreversible loss of brain function, went on — while ventilated — to heal wounds, fight infections, grow, and in some cases sexually mature or gestate a pregnancy, occasionally for years and in at least one well-documented case for more than two decades. This seems to threaten the classic "integrationist" rationale for equating whole-brain death with death: the idea that the brain is what welds the body's organs into a single organism, so that losing all brain function should leave you with a mere disconnected heap of organs rather than an organism that could still, in principle, be alive. The President's Council on Bioethics tried to repair this rationale in its 2008 white paper by relocating the relevant criterion from bodily "integration" to the organism's "fundamental work" — its drive to engage with the environment, expressed through consciousness and through the impulse to breathe — so that Shewmon's cases could be reclassified as isolated, non-organismic reflexes rather than genuine counterexamples. My friend finds this a reasonable clarification of what we meant by "integration" all along; I find it suspiciously convenient, and I'm more drawn to the harder-nosed position of people like Truog and Miller, who argue we should just abandon the "dead donor rule" and justify organ procurement through consent rather than through a contested claim about exactly when death occurs.

We never resolved this, so I'd like you to. Please write an essay that lays out the strongest form of the case for the whole-brain criterion as tracking a real fact about when a human organism ceases to exist, and the strongest form of the case that it is at best a defensible legal convention (or at worst a fiction) shaped by the needs of transplant medicine — taking Shewmon's evidence and the Council's proposed repair seriously on both sides — and then reach and defend an actual verdict, rather than resting on "reasonable people disagree." A conditional verdict is fine (for instance, one that distinguishes what's defensible as legal or clinical policy from what's true as a matter of biology or metaphysics), but if you draw a distinction like that, say so explicitly and defend it.
