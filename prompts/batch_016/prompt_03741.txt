I'm a software engineer, and I got interested in the St. Petersburg paradox a few weeks ago after writing a quick simulation to confirm for myself that the game's expected payout really does diverge (it does, just very slowly). I already knew the classic textbook resolution from a probability course years ago: Daniel Bernoulli's idea that rational agents maximize expected utility rather than expected money, so that a concave utility function like log(wealth) tames the infinite expectation into something finite. That felt satisfying until I read further into the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's entry on the paradox and learned that decision theorists generally regard this fix as incomplete on its own, because of an argument going back to the mathematician Karl Menger. As best I can tell, the move is to stop talking about dollars altogether and define a new St. Petersburg-style gamble directly in units of utility -- a 1/2 chance of a prize worth 2 utils, a 1/4 chance of one worth 4 utils, and so on -- and this reproduces exactly the same divergence for any utility function that is unbounded above, no matter how sharply its marginal value diminishes. The only fix that seems to survive this move is insisting that a rational agent's utility function must be bounded above, which is apparently also what falls out of the continuity (Archimedean) axiom in von Neumann–Morgenstern-style expected utility theory. I gather there's an even stranger variant of this whole debate called the Pasadena game, but I'd like to set that aside and just get the Menger/boundedness story straight first.

Could you walk me through this with enough technical precision that I could reconstruct the argument myself, rather than giving me the standard one-paragraph gloss? Specifically: (1) why is unboundedness itself -- rather than the presence or absence of diminishing returns -- what determines whether the paradox can be regenerated for a given utility function; and (2) is there a good answer to the complaint that insisting on bounded utility doesn't really resolve the underlying weirdness so much as relocate it, since it commits you to some fixed ceiling on how good things could ever be for you, such that no sequence of additional good outcomes could ever add up to more? I've seen that objection gestured at but never spelled out carefully, and I don't yet have the background to assess it on my own.
