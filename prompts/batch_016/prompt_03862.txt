I spent the better part of four decades as an analytical chemist, and in retirement I have been filling my mornings with philosophy, working my way rather haphazardly backward from Hume and the classical empiricists into contemporary epistemology and the philosophy of probability. Old professional habits die hard, though: I still sit on the scientific advisory board of a small family foundation that funds water-purification projects in low-resource regions, and something that happened at our most recent funding meeting has stayed with me rather longer than I expected. I have come to suspect it points to a genuine philosophical puzzle rather than a simple lapse of judgment on the board's part.

We had a single grant to award this cycle, and the choice came down to two proposals. The first was a conventional chlorination-based treatment that has by now been through more than forty field trials across a dozen countries; the reported reductions in pathogen load cluster tightly between 40 and 50 percent, averaging almost exactly 45 percent. The second was a newer silver-embedded ceramic filter, tested so far only in two small pilot villages with strikingly different results: one showed roughly a 20 percent reduction, the other close to 70 percent. Split the difference and the average comes out to about 45 percent as well, but the honest truth is that no one on the board could say with any confidence where the real figure would land in a new village, or even how tightly it would cluster if we ran the pilot fifty more times. When the vote came, the board funded the chlorination proposal almost unanimously, myself included, even though several of us admitted afterward, over coffee, that the ceramic filter's expected benefit looked at least as good on paper. Our newest member, trained as an economist, was rather pointed about this: he argued that we had let a vague unease about "not really knowing the odds" override a plain expected-value comparison, and that a board reasoning correctly should have been indifferent between the two proposals, if not slightly more favorable toward the filter, given its higher upside.

This has unsettled me rather more than I expected, given that I spent a working lifetime trying to train this sort of unquantified instinct out of myself in the laboratory. I cannot quite locate the flaw in the young man's reasoning, yet I remain unconvinced that he is simply right. There seems to me a real difference between a well-worn 45 percent and a 45 percent stitched together from two wildly divergent pilot studies, even if I struggle to say what work that difference is doing once the arithmetic comes out the same. So I would genuinely value your judgment: was the board being irrational, as my colleague insists, or is there a defensible principle behind our reluctance that a straightforward expected-value calculation simply misses?
