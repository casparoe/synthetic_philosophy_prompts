I am writing a novel featuring a scholar who has devoted her career to Jeremy Bentham's unpublished manuscripts on sexual morality -- writings in which he defended consensual homosexual acts between men solely on the grounds that they caused no real harm, attributed the severity of the laws against them to mere "antipathy" and "asceticism" rather than genuine utility, and treated the underlying inclination as a matter of individual taste and degree, long before the modern concept of a fixed sexual identity existed. For a scene in which she is pressed to speak plainly at a public lecture, I would like help reconstructing, strictly from Bentham's own premises rather than any modernized gloss on him, how he would likely have judged today's dominant case for same-sex marriage and anti-discrimination protections -- a case built not on any calculus of pleasure and pain but on sexual orientation as a stable identity meriting equal dignity. Would his own framework lead him to welcome that rationale, consider it beside the point, or actively resist it, and why?
