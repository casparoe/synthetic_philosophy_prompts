In metaontology, Eli Hirsch has argued that the dispute between mereological universalists (who hold that any objects whatsoever compose something further) and mereological nihilists (who deny that composition ever occurs, so that only simples exist) is merely verbal: by a principle of charity, each side should be interpreted as using "exists" with a different but equally legitimate meaning, so that both parties come out speaking truly in their own idiolect. Theodore Sider has resisted this deflationary diagnosis, arguing that one candidate meaning of "exists" is objectively distinguished because it alone carves reality at its ontological joints, which suffices to make the dispute substantive rather than merely terminological.

As a philosophy PhD student drafting a seminar paper on this exchange, I want to pressure-test my own view before committing to it in writing. Write an essay adjudicating the disagreement. Rather than resting on general remarks about quantifier variance, apply Chalmers' subscript-gambit test for identifying verbal disputes directly to the universalism/nihilism case, and use the outcome to weigh Hirsch's charity-based argument against Sider's joint-carving response. Close by defending a specific verdict -- merely verbal, substantive, or genuinely indeterminate between the two -- rather than ending non-committally.
