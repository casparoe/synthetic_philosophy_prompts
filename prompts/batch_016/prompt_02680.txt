I'm about two-thirds into a novel written in first-person present tense (a bit like what Suzanne Collins does in The Hunger Games, except literary fiction instead of YA), and I hit a weird snag with a scene last week. My narrator is in denial about her marriage falling apart, and I wanted a line where she basically says "He's leaving me, but I don't believe it" — I wanted exactly that flavor of denial, saying the true thing out loud while refusing to own it. But on the page it didn't read as poignant and self-deceived, it just read as broken, like a sentence that doesn't parse psychologically, even though nothing in it is actually false or contradictory (it's perfectly possible for him to be leaving while she fails to believe it). Out of curiosity I tried the same content in past tense ("he was leaving me, but I didnt believe it") and in third person ("he's leaving her, but she doesn't believe it"), and both of those are totally fine, no weirdness at all.

I mentioned this to a friend who does philosophy and she said this is basically Moore's paradox, and that the fact that it only misfires in the first-person-present-tense combo is its own whole puzzle people have written about, not just a quirk of my prose. Could you walk me through the actual mechanics of why that specific combination breaks? Every explanation I can find online just stops at "well, it's not a contradiction, it's just absurd to assert," which doesn't tell me why swapping the pronoun or the tense fixes it so cleanly everytime. Is there something substantive to say about what a belief report is actually doing — functionally, pragmatically, whatever the right register is — in the present-tense first-person case that it isn't doing in the other two?
