Random context for this one: my grandma died a couple months ago, and it sent me and my partner down a whole rabbit hole about death, to the point that we actually just signed up with a cryonics org (yes, I know how that sounds). Ever since, I've been reading about how serious, rigorous thinkers dealt with the idea of surviving death, as opposed to just the standard religious stuff, and I got kind of fixated on Gödel of all people. Turns out that in a series of four letters to his mother in 1961, he laid out this really careful, rationalist case for an afterlife - the whole exchange actually starts because his mother asks if he believes in a "Wiedersehen" (roughly "seeing each other again") rather than "the afterlife," and he runs with that framing. The gist as I understand it: the world displays a deep rational order, which he thinks is basically what natural science itself presupposes, and if the world really is rational and meaningful all the way through, then a human life that ends in the middle of a bunch of unfulfilled potential can't be the whole story - otherwise the world wouldn't actually be rational after all.

There's more in the weeds too: Gödel apparently had this 14-point outline of his own worldview (via Hao Wang's book on him) where "the world is rational" sits right next to claims about there being other worlds and higher rational beings, he took Leibniz's monads seriously as a picture of what a person actually is, and he was quietly deep into parapsychology and reincarnation reports on the side - even though Wang himself, when he pushed back on Gödel directly about all this, came away with the impression that Gödel knew his own answers weren't landing. Seperately, in his actual math-logic work (the Gibbs lecture), Gödel's the source of the "disjunction" argument that either the human mind outstrips every possible finite formal system, or there are absolutely undecidable problems for us - which seems to me to reflect a broader discomfort on his part with the idea that a mind could just be a mechanism.

So here's my actual question, because none of the pop-sci writeups I've found touch this: taking Gödel's actual premises seriously, rather than the "famous logician believed in ghosts" meme version, what do you think he would've made of things like cryonic preservation, whole-brain emulation / mind uploading, or simulation-argument style "afterlives," the kind of stuff that gets floated in rationalist and EA circles today as secular routes to surviving death? Would his rationalism plus his suspicion of pure mechanism actually make him skeptical that a computational copy could really be "you" continuing on - in real tension with the tech-optimist crowd I've been talking to lately - or is his view, followed through carefully, actually more compatible with that kind of continuation than I'd have guessed? Dont just give me a one-line take - I'd like you to actually walk through which of his commitments point in which direction, and be upfront about where the whole thing just runs out because he never had to think about any of this stuff.
