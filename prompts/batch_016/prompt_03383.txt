Ok so this is a bit of a rabbit hole but bear with me. I've been reading around on nudge theory / libertarian paternalism lately (the Thaler-Sunstein "choice architecture" stuff), and some comment thread in the rationalist-sphere - cant remember if it was ACX or an old LW post - sent me back to actually reread Mill's On Liberty properly instead of just knowing the "harm to others" one-liner like everybody does. And I got completely stuck on this bit from chapter 5, where he's walking through practical applications of the harm principle and suddenly carves out this exception around contracts:

"In this and most other civilised countries, for example, an engagement by which a person should sell himself, or allow himself to be sold, as a slave, would be null and void; neither enforced by law nor by opinion. The ground for thus limiting his power of voluntarily disposing of his own lot in life, is apparent, and is very clearly seen in this extreme case. The reason for not interfering, unless for the sake of others, with a person's voluntary acts, is consideration for his liberty. His voluntary choice is evidence that what he so chooses is desirable, or at the least endurable, to him, and his good is on the whole best provided for by allowing him to take his own means of pursuing it. But by selling himself for a slave, he abdicates his liberty; he foregoes any future use of it, beyond that single act. He therefore defeats, in his own case, the very purpose which is the justification of allowing him to dispose of himself. He is no longer free; but is thenceforth in a position which has no longer the presumption in its favour, that would be afforded by his voluntarily remaining in it. The principle of freedom cannot require that he should be free not to be free. It is not freedom, to be allowed to alienate his freedom. These reasons, the force of which is so conspicuous in this peculiar case, are evidently of far wider application."

What I can't get past is how this ISN'T just paternalism smuggled in through the back door. Up to that point the entire essay is basically "a person's own good, physical or moral, is never sufficient reason to override their choices, full stop" - and then here Mill overrides someone's fully voluntary, presumably informed, choice, for their own good, and gets out of it by redefining "freedom" so that permanently giving it away doesn't count as an exercise of it. But that exact move - "we're not restricting your autonomy, we're protecting your future autonomy" - seems to me like basically the same rhetorical trick modern soft-paternalists use to justify sticky defaults, cooling-off periods, bans on waiving certain rights, etc. And Mill even says himself that the reasoning is "of far wider application," which he then never really cashes out or bounds.

So, two things I'd love actual help with rather than a summary of the passage: (1) is there a non-question-begging way to draw the line between "stopping someone from destroying their own future freedom" (apparently ok) and "stopping someone from making a bad choice for their own good" (explicitly not ok, per Mill's whole thesis) - or does the slavery case only work because it's total and irreversible, so the logic quietly stops applying to literally anything less extreme? And (2) is this passage a real, coherent extension of the harm principle, or is it the one spot in the book where Mill's anti-paternalism hits a wall and he's kind of fudging it?
