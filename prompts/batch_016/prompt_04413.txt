A close friend of mine, Priya, and I have been arguing for the better part of a week over an arrangement she proposed, and I'd like a more dispassionate take than either of us can offer. Priya has been a committed vegetarian for years because she considers factory farming one of the great moral failures of our time, but she flies constantly for work and has never paid much attention to her carbon footprint. I'm more or less her mirror image: I bike everywhere and avoid flying when I can because I think of climate change as the defining moral issue of our generation, but I eat meat most days without much guilt. Half-jokingly at first, Priya proposed a deal: for every week I went fully vegetarian, she would buy carbon offsets equivalent to one of her short-haul flights; for every week she cut her meat intake down to almost nothing, I would put fifty dollars toward a climate charity in her name. Her reasoning was that each of us finds it relatively cheap to do the thing the other one cares about, so instead of separately trying — and mostly failing — to become better all-around people, we should just pay each other to be a little better according to the other's values.

The idea appealed to me at first, but the more I thought about it the less sure I became, and saying so turned it into a real argument. I said the whole scheme felt less like ethics and more like buying indulgences: if it only works because neither of us has to actually change our minds about what matters, it's hard to see how it counts as moral improvement rather than a clever way of paying to feel better about ourselves. Priya replied that the animals and the atmosphere don't care about our motives, only about outcomes, and that by my logic, someone who pays other people to carry out good deeds rather than doing them personally would also be "laundering" their obligations — which struck her as absurd. I said there's a real difference between paying toward a goal you already share and paying someone to act against what they themselves consider trivial, funded by what they themselves consider important — that the second sounds more like a bribe than a donation, even when the outcome looks identical from outside. She countered that the distinction was doing a lot of work I hadn't actually earned, since ordinary cooperation is full of people doing things they wouldn't otherwise bother with only because someone else made it worth their while, and nobody calls that bribery. When I pushed further and asked where, if anywhere, this logic would stop — surely not any two people who each think the other's priority is comparatively unimportant can strike this kind of bargain about literally anything — neither of us could articulate a principled line, and that's roughly where the conversation died.

I'd like to know who had the better of this exchange, ideally move by move rather than just declaring an overall winner, and whether there's a principled way of telling a legitimate arrangement like ours apart from a bribe or a piece of moral self-deception. If the underlying logic is sound, I'm also curious exactly where it stops being sound, since it presumably can't license any two people with opposing priorities to strike this kind of bargain about anything whatsoever. And, purely practically, based on your analysis: should Priya and I actually go through with it?
