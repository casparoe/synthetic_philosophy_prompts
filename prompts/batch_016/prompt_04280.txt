In my high school's intro-to-philosophy elective, we just spent a unit on Augustine's absolutism about lying, and one part of it hasn't left me alone. Our teacher walked us through the eight-fold ranking of lies he lays out in "On Lying" (De Mendacio), ordered from worst to least bad: (1) lying about religious doctrine, (2) lying that helps no one and hurts someone, (3) lying that helps one person by hurting another, (4) lying purely for the pleasure of deceiving, (5) lying to be agreeable in conversation, (6) lying to protect someone's property, (7) lying to keep a person, guilty or innocent, from being executed, and (8) lying to protect someone's chastity, which he ranks as the least bad of all — even less bad than lying to save a life. In the same text, when Augustine considers whether you may lie to protect someone hiding from people who want to kill him, his actual proposed solution isn't to lie or to simply stay silent, but to say, in effect, "I know where he is, but I will never tell you," and then to accept whatever follows, up to and including your own death or his, rather than assert a falsehood. The wrongness, on his view, is located entirely in the intention to assert what you take to be false; the purpose behind a lie changes how bad it is, never whether it's wrong at all.

What I keep measuring against this is something much closer to home. My grandmother has dementia and lives in a memory care facility, and the staff there practice what's called "therapeutic fibbing": when she asks where her late husband is, they tell her he's "at work," rather than that he died, because otherwise she re-lives the grief of his death from scratch, sometimes several times in one afternoon. Nearly every caregiving resource I've come across defends some version of this. It's obviously not a scenario Augustine considered, and it sits awkwardly next to his own examples, which are all one-off, acute confrontations with someone who has no right to the truth in the first place — whereas this is a standing, institutionally endorsed practice of deceiving someone who loves the person she's asking about and has every right to ask.

Reasoning only from Augustine's own actual commitments, and not just "he'd disapprove of lying" in general, I'd like help thinking through three things: where, if anywhere, therapeutic fibbing fits into his eight-fold hierarchy, or whether it falls outside it entirely; whether his own preferred alternative to lying — truthful refusal rather than silence or falsehood — could actually do any work here, given that it seems to assume an interlocutor capable of accepting a refusal and moving on, which a person with dementia often cannot do; and whether there's something about this case that isn't just a hard application of Augustine's view but an actual pressure point against it — one that should give even a fully committed Augustinian pause.
