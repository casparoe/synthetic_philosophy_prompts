At Thanksgiving, my uncle, a retired Navy pilot, described a radar-and-visual encounter, years ago, with an object that maneuvered and accelerated well beyond the limits of any known aircraft, and he cited the recent congressional hearings, where pilots and intelligence officials testified under oath, as corroboration that this is genuine non-human technology rather than mundane misidentification. I do not want to dismiss him outright, but I am unsure how much such testimony should move my credence, and it struck me that Hume's essay on miracles was built for exactly this problem: weighing testimony to an event that would violate well-established laws of nature against the ordinary chance that witnesses are mistaken, exaggerating, or lying. Drawing on his actual argument — his criteria for credible witnesses, his maxim that a wise man proportions belief to the evidence, and the tension he creates for himself with the Indian prince who justly refuses to believe true reports of ice — how would Hume evaluate modern testimony about unidentified aerial phenomena? Please reconstruct his likely reasoning in some detail, rather than simply guessing whether he would end up a believer or a skeptic.
