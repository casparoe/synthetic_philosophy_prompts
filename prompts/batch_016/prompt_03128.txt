ok so I had this whole argument with my friend last night about whether acting is just a fancy form of lying, and he kept playing the "we're all performing selves in everyday life anyway" card (some Goffman thing he read). I countered with Rousseau, who apparently hated actors for way more specific reasons than "theater = bad," and dug up this bit from his Letter to d'Alembert on the Theatre (in translation, obviously):

"What is the talent of the actor? The art of counterfeiting himself, of putting on a character not his own, of appearing to be other than what he is, of growing passionate in cold blood, of saying what he does not think as naturally as if he really thought it, and, in short, of forgetting his own place by dint of taking another's. What is the profession of the actor? A trade by which he puts himself on display for money, submits to the disgrace and affronts that others purchase the right to inflict on him, and publicly puts his own person up for sale. I entreat every sincere man to say whether he does not feel, deep in his own being, that there is something servile and base in this trafficking of himself."

Reading it back now I honestly cant tell if he's making one argument or two seperate ones. Like, the first half is about psychologically losing yourself by faking passions on command, but then the "puts his own person up for sale" bit sounds like a totally different complaint, almost economic/social rather than psychological. Are these actually the same worry for Rousseau once you factor in his whole amour-propre thing (needing others' good opinion), or is he just stacking unrelated objections because he really wants Geneva to not get a theatre? Trying to go back to my friend with something better than "idk man, some Frenchman agrees with you."
