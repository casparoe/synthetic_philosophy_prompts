I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but Skolem's Paradox is a piece of model theory I ran across in some old logic notes that has stuck with me ever since. The setup, as I understand it: if ZFC is consistent, the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem guarantees it has a countable model M; yet ZFC proves Cantor's theorem, so M must contain some object, call it m, that M itself judges to be uncountable, since M contains no bijection between m and ω — even though M's entire domain is countable when viewed from outside. Everyone agrees this is not a formal contradiction, merely two different readings — model-relative versus absolute — of the same formula. What's disputed is whether this shows something deeper: that "uncountable" never picks out an absolute, model-independent property of a set at all, so that the axioms of set theory are best understood as merely implicitly defining whatever structures happen to satisfy them, rather than as describing one fixed, antecedently given universe of sets.

Please argue for that stronger, "Skolemite" conclusion rather than the more comfortable view that, once the mathematics has been sorted out, the paradox carries no further philosophical import. In particular, address why retreating to a second-order axiomatization — under which models of ZFC compute power sets and cardinalities correctly — doesn't simply presuppose the very absoluteness that's supposed to be in question.
