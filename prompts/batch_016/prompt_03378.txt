Six months ago I joined the board of trustees of a small rewilding charity that manages roughly 300 hectares of former sheep pasture in the Scottish Borders, and a decision we are currently wrestling with has pulled me much further into moral philosophy than I ever expected a volunteer trustee role to require. Our flagship proposal is to reintroduce Eurasian lynx as a means of naturally controlling a roe deer population that is currently kept in check mainly by winter starvation and occasional ranger culls. At our last meeting, one trustee objected on "wild animal suffering" grounds, arguing, with reference to writers like Oscar Horta and Yew-Kwang Ng, that reintroducing a predator does not remove suffering from the system so much as trade one form of it for another — slow starvation for death by predation — and that if we take welfare seriously we should be looking at interventions that rely on neither. Another trustee, more sympathetic to Holmes Rolston-style environmental holism, called this a category error: on her view, predation is a constitutive part of a functioning ecosystem rather than a problem to be engineered away, and treating it as the latter smuggles in an anti-nature bias dressed up as compassion.

I spent a good part of the winter break reading around the topic — Palmer's discussion of the "laissez-faire intuition," McMahan's old New York Times essay on the ethics of predation, Johannsen's book on wild animal ethics, and some of the more radical proposals from people like David Pearce and Brian Tomasik involving gene drives and the genetic "reprogramming" of predators — and I now understand the shape of the disagreement much better. What I still lack is a settled view on how the concrete policy options actually on our table stack up against one another, both philosophically and practically. I would like your help thinking this through for a memo I am preparing ahead of our next board meeting, in the hope that a structured ranking will make our eventual discussion more tractable than it was last time.

Could you rank the following seven options into four tiers: "recommend," "cautiously support," "reject on practical grounds," and "reject on principle"? By "recommend" I mean ethically sound and reasonably tractable now; "cautiously support" means ethically sound in principle but burdened by serious tractability or risk caveats worth flagging to the board; the two "reject" tiers should track whether your main objection is about feasibility and unintended ecological consequences versus the underlying ethical case for the intervention itself. The options are: (1) reintroducing lynx as a predator to control deer numbers; (2) immunocontraception to prevent deer overpopulation and subsequent starvation die-offs; (3) periodic lethal culling of deer by trust rangers; (4) supplementary winter feeding to reduce starvation deaths; (5) a vaccination programme against painful endemic wildlife diseases; (6) funding longer-term research into gene-drive-based population control; and (7) a strict non-intervention policy, on the grounds that the trust has no mandate to manage wild animal welfare in the first place.
