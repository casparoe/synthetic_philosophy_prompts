Last month I found a stranger's wallet at a coffee shop containing just under $1,000 in cash, an ID, and a business card. I tracked down the owner and returned everything, cash included. When I told the story at dinner, I mentioned, almost as an aside, that the business card was from a small firm in my own line of work, and that while deciding what to do I'd been dimly aware I might cross paths with this person again someday. That detail set off an argument between two friends, Owen and Maya, about whether I actually deserve any credit for returning the wallet.

(1) Owen said credit is obviously due: I handed back close to $1,000 I could have kept with essentially no risk of being caught, and a passing thought about small-world professional reputation doesn't undo that.

(2) Maya said that passing thought is exactly the problem. If it was actually necessary to tip me toward returning the wallet -- if I would not have done so absent any chance the owner might later recognize my name -- then what explains the act isn't honesty but self-interest wearing honesty's clothes, and that doesn't deserve moral credit, even though returning the wallet was still the right thing to do.

(3) Owen replied that this test can't be run on anyone. Nobody gets to rerun their own decision with the incentives surgically removed, motives always come tangled together, and if praise required proving self-interest played no role, nobody would ever qualify, since there's always some self-interested angle available after the fact.

(4) Maya answered that she wasn't demanding proof of zero self-interest, only that the honest motive be doing the real work, and that certainty was never on offer -- but the fact that the professional connection was the detail I volunteered first, unprompted, is itself evidence about what was actually live in my thinking, so it's fair to weigh it even without a controlled experiment.
