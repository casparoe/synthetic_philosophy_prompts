I'm a retired experimental physicist (spent about thirty years doing spectroscopy before I hung up my lab coat), and now that I've got time on my hands I've been getting into philosophy, mostly at the mercy of my granddaughter, who's doing a philosophy MA and keeps handing me books "for my own good." Over the holidays she gave me Kripke's short book on Wittgenstein and rule-following, and I actually read the whole plus/quus argument pretty carefully — the part where the sceptic points out that all my past additions are compatible with my having meant "quus" instead of "plus," quus being defined to match addition everywhere except that it returns 5 whenever the numbers involved are 57 or bigger, so that 68+57 should "really" be 5, not 125.

Here's what's bugging me. This just looks to me like ordinary underdetermination of a model by finite data, which is something every working scientist runs into constantly and has good formal tools for. If I hand you ten data points, there is technically an infinite family of curves that pass through all of them exactly, but nobody looses sleep over that, because we have Occams razor — or, done properly, something like an AIC/BIC penalty for extra model complexity, or a simplicity prior if you want to be Bayesian about it. Quus is a monstrously gerrymandered, high-complexity function compared to plain addition, so any halfway sane prior favours plus by an enormous margin. I noticed Kripke does gesture at a "simplicity" response for a couple of pages and then more or less shrugs it off, which honestly reads to me like someone who hasn't had to do actual model selection on messy data for a living. When I asked my granddaughter about this she just grinned and told me to "sit with it," which was not exactly helpful. So: am I missing something genuinely deep here, or does modern statistics basically dissolve Kripke's whole paradox, and it's just that philosophers haven't fully absorbed how model selection actually works?
