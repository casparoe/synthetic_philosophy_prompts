I'm a philosophy PhD student writing a dissertation chapter on Parfit's case for temporal neutrality, and I've been reading into the climate-economics literature to see how his arguments bear on actual policy debates. Ramsey, and later Parfit, argued that discounting future well-being merely because it lies further away in time — "pure time preference" — has no defensible ethical rationale and amounts to little more than a failure of imagination or a bias toward the near at hand. I had assumed this was essentially what was at stake in the well-known dispute between the Stern Review and Nordhaus over climate policy: Stern took the Ramsey-style argument seriously and adopted a near-zero rate of pure time preference, while Nordhaus's insistence on a much higher discount rate (commonly cited as somewhere around 4%, versus Stern's roughly 1.4%) looked to me like exactly the kind of unprincipled favoritism toward the present generation that Ramsey and Parfit had already shown to be untenable.

Two things are complicating this picture, though. First, Stern's own discount rate on consumption is not actually zero but that same 1.4% a year, even though the pure-time-preference term in his calculation is set to almost nothing — so some of that 1.4% must be coming from somewhere else in the model entirely. Second, when I look at how economists on either side actually argue about the remaining gap between Stern's and Nordhaus's numbers, much of the disagreement seems to turn on a separate parameter called eta (the elasticity of marginal utility of consumption) and on differing assumptions about future economic growth, rather than on any explicit claim about how much future generations' welfare matters as such. Before I build a section of my chapter around this case, I'd like to understand whether the philosophical argument against pure time preference really settles very much about what the social discount rate should be, or whether I've been running together a distinction that is doing most of the real work in this debate.
