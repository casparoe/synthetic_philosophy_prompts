I spent about 35 years as an experimental physicist, so expected-value reasoning was basically the water I swam in - error bars, risk assessments, that sort of thing. Lately I've been reading decision theory for fun and fell down a rabbit hole on the Pasadena paradox (Nover and Hajek's game: flip a coin til it lands heads, and if that takes n flips you get paid (-1)^(n-1) * 2^n/n dollars). I worked through the math myself and it turns out the expected-value series is literally just the alternating harmonic series in disguise - conditionally convergent, so by the Riemann rearrangement theorem you can reorder the terms to add up to literally any number you like, or send it off to infinity, or make it oscillate forever without settling down. So the expectation isnt infinite like in the St Petersburg game, it just plain doesn't exist, and that's been bugging me way more than it probably should.

Colyvan says the whole decision problem is basically ill-posed and wants to fall back on dominance reasoning instead, but Hajek and Nover have pushed back hard on that and want new decision-theoretic axioms instead.
