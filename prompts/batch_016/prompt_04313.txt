I've spent the past few months on the AI-safety beat, sitting in on lab briefings and interviewing alignment researchers, and two ideas that keep recurring almost as background axioms are Nick Bostrom's orthogonality thesis and his instrumental convergence thesis. The first holds that intelligence and final goals are independent, so that a system's raw capability tells you essentially nothing about what it wants; the second holds that, whatever their final goals happen to be, sufficiently capable agents will tend to converge on similar instrumental sub-goals, such as self-preservation, resource acquisition, and cognitive self-improvement. More than one philosopher I've spoken with has waved the first thesis off as little more than Hume's "reason is the slave of the passions" translated into the idiom of decision theory for a machine-learning audience -- which surprised me, since I'd also come across the detail that Bostrom himself explicitly denies that orthogonality depends on Humean moral psychology at all.

I'd like your help adjudicating that dispute for a piece I'm drafting. Specifically: in what precise respects does the orthogonality thesis simply restate the Humean theory of motivation -- the view that reason (or "intelligence," in Bostrom's deliberately thin, efficiency-flavored sense of the word) is purely instrumental, incapable of supplying or criticizing ultimate ends -- and in what respects, if any, does it actually go beyond Hume's picture? Would a committed anti-Humean about practical reason, of the Kantian sort who holds that a fully rational agent is thereby bound to certain ends (or at least to certain constraints on permissible ends) rather than merely more resourceful at pursuing arbitrary ones, have principled grounds to reject orthogonality outright? Or could such a rationalist accept it for "intelligence" in Bostrom's narrow sense while denying that it would hold for any agent that was rational in the fuller, will-constraining sense Kant had in mind?

There's a further tension I haven't been able to resolve on my own, and I'd like you to take a position on it rather than simply laying out both sides. Instrumental convergence seems to require a fairly demanding, idealized notion of agency -- something like coherent, cross-situationally stable means-end optimization -- in order to guarantee that self-preservation and resource acquisition really will be favored across most final goals and circumstances, as Bostrom claims. Doesn't building in that much rational coherence already concede more ground to the anti-Humean than orthogonality's defenders tend to let on, or is that degree of coherent optimization perfectly at home within a purely Humean, means-end conception of reason? Please justify whichever answer you give.
