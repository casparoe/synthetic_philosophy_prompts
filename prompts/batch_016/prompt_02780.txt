I've been reading LessWrong and its neighboring blogs since roughly the Overcoming Bias days, and one thing that has always structured my thinking about epistemology is the sequences-era conviction that idealized Bayesian conditionalization is basically what correct reasoning looks like. So it was a little destabilizing to work through LessWrong's own wiki entry on the "problem of old evidence" and realize how much trouble the framework has with one of its own favorite examples: the perihelion precession of Mercury was already known with certainty decades before Einstein arrived at General Relativity, so at the moment GR was proposed, P(precession) was already 1 in everyone's credence function, and strict conditionalization therefore implies the precession could not have raised GR's probability at all — even though everyone, including Einstein, treated it as a genuine and important confirmation of the theory. The wiki page walks through Marcus Hutter's algorithmic-information-theoretic fix (a "cheating" hypothesis that just bakes the known orbit into its source code pays a complexity penalty that exactly cancels whatever credit it gets for "predicting" it, so a Solomonoff-style simplicity prior is supposed to save the day), but it also flags, via Paul Christiano's related "paradox of ignorance," a worry that even the standard Bayesian trick of pretending every hypothesis was around from the very beginning starts to break down once the old evidence is effectively baked into the prior itself rather than delivered as a discrete update.

Curious how this connects to the pre-existing philosophy-of-science literature on the same problem, I went and read around Clark Glymour's original 1980 statement of it and the decades of proposed fixes that followed, and it turns out academic epistemology has been fighting essentially the same fight in parallel, with less crossover than I would have expected. As best I can reconstruct them: the Garber–Jeffrey–Niiniluoto approach gives up on logically omniscient agents and treats a scientist's discovery that "GR entails the precession" as itself new, contingent information that can be genuinely conditionalized on; Howson's older counterfactual strategy instead asks what your credence in GR would have been had the precession counterfactually been subtracted out of your background knowledge, a move Howson himself later disavowed, eventually concluding that subjectivist Bayesianism simply cannot handle old evidence and should be judged a failure as an account of confirmation on those grounds; Will Fleisher's 2023 fragmentation-theoretic proposal instead locates the relevant "learning" inside a not-yet-integrated fragment of the scientist's overall credal state, borrowing an "ur-prior" concept rather than positing any counterfactual; and John Earman, surveying all of the above in Bayes or Bust?, essentially concludes that none of it fully works and that the problem marks a genuine, probably permanent scar on personalist Bayesian confirmation theory.
