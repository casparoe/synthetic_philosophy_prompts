I'm a novelist working on a scene I keep putting off, because I don't yet trust myself to write it honestly rather than merely gesture at it. The character is a retired philosophy don, trained at Oxford in the early sixties, right when J. R. Lucas's paper on minds, machines, and Gödel was making the rounds of the senior common rooms and settled into him as a kind of intellectual bedrock. At his niece's wedding he gets cornered by her new boyfriend, a self-assured machine-learning engineer, and after a couple of glasses of wine the old man starts sketching the Gödelian argument on the back of a seating chart: that any formal system strong enough to do arithmetic contains a true sentence it cannot prove, that he can see this particular sentence is true precisely because he stands outside the system in a way the machine cannot, and that this is enough to show no machine could ever fully capture him. I don't want him to win the scene just by dazzling a layperson with jargon she can't push back on, and I don't want her comeback to be a throwaway line either; I want both of them arguing close to the top of their respective games, each landing real points on the other.

To write that honestly I need to actually follow the dialectic rather than wave at it, which is where I keep getting stuck. Could you first lay out the precise logical shape of the Lucas–Penrose argument: what the Gödel sentence for a given formal system F actually says, and exactly what further premise the argument needs in order to move from "F cannot prove G(F)" to "a human mind can nonetheless see that G(F) is true"? Then give me the strongest form of the classical reply, associated with Putnam, that this premise covertly requires the human to know that F is consistent, and that Gödel's own second theorem seems to forbid exactly that kind of self-knowledge from inside F.

Finally, I'd like to understand what changes when Penrose, in Shadows of the Mind, tries to rebuild the argument around a system that is not merely consistent but knowably sound, folding in the assumption "I am F" to get a further system F′ and deriving a contradiction from there. Is that a genuinely sturdier argument, or does it just relocate the same vulnerable premise — that we can know something like this about our own reasoning — one level up, as I gather Chalmers and others have contended? I know there are other objections in the literature too, about idealization and about whether anyone could actually locate their own Gödel sentence in practice, but for this scene I specifically need the consistency-versus-soundness exchange to be right, since that's the crux of the argument I've given my two characters. If there is a genuine, still-live move available to the old don at that point, I'd like him to make it; if the engineer is quietly the one who's right, I would rather know that now than have some logician friend tell me so after the book is out.
