Had a long, kind of ridiculous argument with my friend Mara last night about whether species are actually "real" — like, joints nature has independent of us — or just bins we sort organisms into because it's convenient. Shes a microbiologist and has strong feelings about how bacteria break every species definition anyone's ever proposed. Anyway we ended up pulling up the actual text of On the Origin of Species on my phone around midnight because she remembered Darwin saying something like this, and we found the passage, but we've been arguing about how far it actually goes ever since.

Here's the bit (1859 first edition, end of chapter 2):

"Hence I believe a well-marked variety may be justly called an incipient species; but whether this belief be justifiable must be judged of by the general weight of the several facts and views given throughout this work. ... From these remarks it will be seen that I look at the term species, as one arbitrarily given for the sake of convenience to a set of individuals closely resembling each other, and that it does not essentially differ from the term variety, which is given to less distinct and more fluctuating forms. The term variety, again, in comparison with mere individual differences, is also applied arbitrarily, and for mere convenience sake."

(That "..." is me trimming one paragraph out, not Darwin being coy.)

And then near the end of the book he basically doubles down:

"In short, we shall have to treat species in the same manner as those naturalists treat genera, who admit that genera are merely artificial combinations made for convenience. This may not be a cheering prospect; but we shall at least be freed from the vain search for the undiscovered and undiscoverable essence of the term species."

Mara's reading is that this is full eliminativism — there's no such thing as a species, only organisms and lineages, and biologists still hunting for the one correct species concept are chasing something Darwin already told them doesn't exist. My reading is that he can't mean it that strongly, because right before the "arbitrarily given" line he's perfectly happy calling a variety "incipient," which only makes sense if there's something real it's incipient towards — so what's arbitrary is where we draw the line (species vs. subspecies vs. variety) on top of a perfectly objective continuum of divergence, not whether that continuum itself is real. She thinks I'm just inventing a distinction Darwin never makes so I don't have to accept the plain sense of a claim he states twice.

Could you help settle what Darwin is and isn't actually committed to here — is my "realist about the continuum, conventionalist about the labels" reading textually defensible, or is Mara right that it's a dodge? And however that shakes out, does his move here basically anticipate the later distinction between species-as-category and species-as-taxon that shows up in the modern species problem literature, or is it closer to a genuinely eliminativist position like some biologists/philosophers actually hold today?
