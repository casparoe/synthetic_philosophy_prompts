I'm drafting a near-future novel in which humanity has developed a computational substrate capable of sustaining an uploaded mind indefinitely, and the plot turns on a global referendum over whether to activate "perpetual" consciousness for everyone who has already been uploaded. The complication driving the debate is that the substrate is imperfect: alongside the endless flourishing it would make possible, it also, as an unavoidable side effect, causes a small but continuous fraction of uploaded minds to fail in a way that produces something like unending torment — a defect that, once the system is activated, will persist for as long as it runs, which is to say, forever. My protagonist, an engineer campaigning for activation, resolves the ethical objection with a speech I'm rather pleased with: she argues that once both the resulting joy and the resulting suffering are infinite in extent, there is no longer any meaningful sense in which one outweighs the other, since, unlike finite quantities, two infinities cannot be netted against each other to see which side wins. On her view, the ordinary worry about creating additional suffering is calibrated for finite lives, and it simply stops tracking anything real once the stakes become infinite — so the vote, to her mind, should be an easy yes.

I would like her opponents in this debate to have a substantive rebuttal rather than a merely sentimental one, but I am struggling to write it, since her point about infinite quantities not subtracting cleanly seems, as far as the arithmetic goes, basically correct to me. Is there an actual philosophical answer to this kind of argument, or would any sufficiently rigorous opponent more or less have to concede that once both joy and suffering become infinite, our ordinary reasons for caution genuinely do stop applying?
