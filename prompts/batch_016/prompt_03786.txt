I'm a retired experimental physicist - spent about thirty-five years building detectors for nuclear and particle physics experiments, so my working life was basically scintillators, photomultiplier tubes, and miles of wire - and since retiring I've been working through philosophy mostly on my own, with occasional help from an old colleague who's daughter is doing a philosophy PhD and keeps us supplied with jargon. Last week we got to the Stanford Encyclopedia entry on dispositions, and I nearly spat out my coffee at the "electro-fink" example: a dead wire hooked up to a gadget that senses an incoming touch and juices the wire live just in time, so the simple test "it's live iff it would conduct electricity if touched" comes apart from whether the wire actually has the disposition. There's a companion case too - poison with an antidote on hand, or a "masked" disposition as I gather Johnston called it - where the disposition to kill hangs around the whole time, the stimulus happens, but something downstream quietly blocks the outcome without the poison ever loosing its lethality.

Here's what's been bugging me on my walks since. Both of those classic counterexamples seem to trade on there being some separate physical fact sitting underneath the disposition - free electrons in the wire, molecules in the stomach - that a sufficiently clever saboteur can get at and fiddle with right around the moment of the trigger. But what happens once you go down to dispositions that physics treats as genuinely fundamental and, on the ordinary indeterministic reading of quantum mechanics, not grounded in any further hidden state at all: a given nucleus's propensity to decay within the next second, say, or an electron's disposition to repel another charge in virtue of being negative. (I know full well that hidden-variable pictures like Bohmian mechanics would just deny the premise here - let's set those aside for now.) There doesn't seem to be any earlier fact for a fink-device to detect and react to in time, and no separate categorical basis sitting under the particle for it to swap out while nobody's looking.

So here's what I'd like from you: build the strongest case you can that finkish counterexamples of Martin's sort cannot, even in principle, be constructed for maximally fundamental, irreducibly stochastic dispositions like these - while masking- and antidote-style counterexamples still can be, since you can always imagine something further down the line (a grounded conductor soaking up the field before it reaches the second charge, say) blocking the manifestation without ever touching the particle's actual disposition. Really commit to the argument instead of hedging every other sentence; I can supply my own objections later. And once you've made the case, I'd love your take on what the resulting asymmetry - fink-proof but not mask-proof - would mean for the old fight between the simple conditional analysis of dispositions and Lewis's "reformed" version: does immunity to finks vindicate the simple analysis down at the fundamental level even though most people seem to agree it's hopeless for things like fragility or solubility?
