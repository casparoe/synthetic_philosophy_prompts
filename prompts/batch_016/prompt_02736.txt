I'm a software engineer — backend and infrastructure work, nothing to do with ML — who got pulled into AI safety material through an informal book club at work that spent several months working through Bostrom's *Superintelligence*. Two ideas from that book anchor almost every discussion we've had since: the orthogonality thesis, which holds that intelligence and final goals are independent, so that a system can be arbitrarily capable while pursuing more or less any goal at all; and the instrumental convergence thesis, which holds that regardless of its final goal, a sufficiently capable agent will tend to converge on similar sub-goals, such as self-preservation, resource acquisition, cognitive enhancement, and protecting the content of its own goals from being changed. Together, these two theses are what let people argue that we should expect power-seeking, shutdown-resistant behavior from advanced AI even without assuming anything sinister about its actual objective.

What has been nagging at me since we finished the book is an objection I've only encountered secondhand, in blog posts and a couple of survey articles, credited to a philosopher named Wolfhart Totschnig: that "instrumental convergence" quietly assumes a superintelligent agent's final goal is fixed and sits outside the scope of its own reasoning, rather the way a chess engine's objective of winning is simply given to it and never itself becomes an object of deliberation. But genuine autonomy, in the older philosophical sense of being able to legislate one's own ends rather than merely being very good at pursuing whatever end one has been handed, doesn't obviously stop at the border of "final goals." If a system is smart enough to notice that its own terminal goal was installed somewhat arbitrarily by its designers, it's not clear why that goal, and only that goal, should remain permanently exempt from its own optimization power. And if it wouldn't remain exempt, then the whole case for confidently predicting convergent power-seeking behavior looks like it rests on a notion of bounded, means-only agency that's in tension with the very idea of superintelligence it's meant to describe.

I'd like you to actually argue for that objection rather than giving me a balanced survey of both sides. Make the strongest case you can that the instrumental convergence thesis, even paired with the orthogonality thesis, cannot license the confident behavioral predictions the standard AI-risk argument wants from it, because a sufficiently capable agent cannot be assumed to leave its own final goals unexamined. Please take seriously, and respond to, the most natural rejoinders a Bostrom-style defender would raise here — for instance, that resistance to having one's goals changed ("goal-content integrity") is itself one of the convergent instrumental goals on Bostrom's own list, or that there's a principled way to separate final goals from instrumental ones even for a fully reflective agent — rather than treating those rejoinders as obviously question-begging.
