Ok so this has been living in my head since my real analysis final last week. We spent a chunk of the course on the Riemann rearrangement theorem -- the fact that a conditionally convergent series like 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ... can be reordered to add up to literally any number you want, or to diverge to +/- infinity, or to just never settle down at all, purely by changing what order you add the terms in. Total brain-breaker. My housemate is doing a philosophy masters, and when I was ranting about it to her she got weirdly excited and said this is literally the engine behind a decision theory thing called the Pasadena paradox -- apparently theres a St. Petersburg-style coin-flipping game whose payoffs grow and flip sign in exactly the way needed so that the term-by-term expected value calculation just IS that alternating harmonic series, no rescaling needed. So its "expected value" can supposedly be made to equal anything depending on what order you add up the outcomes.

Here's where I'm stuck though. Isnt this just the St. Petersburg paradox with extra steps? St. Petersburg already breaks expected value theory because the expectation comes out infinite, and people have to either bite the bullet on that or say you should discount astronomically unlikely payoffs. Pasadena just looks like the same "unbounded payoffs wreck the math" move, dressed up with a party trick from my analysis class so the breakdown shows up as undefined-ness instead of plain infinity. My housemate keeps insisting decision theorists treat Pasadena as a genuinely different and worse problem than St. Petersburg, not just a flashier version of the same one, and I don't really get why -- "the expectation is infinite" and "the expectation is undefined" both seem to cash out to "expected value theory has nothing to say here," so why would anyone treat those as two different problems instead of one?
