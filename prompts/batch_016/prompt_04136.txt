I am putting together the capstone session for a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of modality, and I want to push the discussion well past the standard textbook contrast between David Lewis's counterpart theory and the more traditional doctrine of transworld identity associated with Kripke and Plantinga. Most introductory treatments are content to note that Lewis denies that one and the same individual could ever be a literal inhabitant of more than one possible world, replacing strict cross-world identity with a similarity-based counterpart relation, and leave matters there, as though the dispute were simply a matter of taste. One of the sharper students in the seminar has been pressing me on whether either side actually gets the better of the exchange once the slogans are set aside, and I would like to bring a more considered answer to our next meeting than the one I currently have.

Could you compare the two views by engaging directly with the strongest objection each has raised against the other, rather than simply restating the positions? On one side is Kripke's well-known objection built around Hubert Humphrey: when we say that Humphrey might have won the 1968 election, we seem to be making a claim about what could have happened to Humphrey himself, and it is hard to see how a mere counterpart, however similar, winning in some other world could secure the truth of that claim. On the other side, Lewis presses what he calls the problem of accidental intrinsics against any view on which an individual is literally a shared constituent of several worlds. If Humphrey sits in one world he inhabits and stands in another, and sitting and standing are paradigm intrinsic properties, the transworld-identity theorist seems to owe an account of how one and the same thing can simply have both properties simpliciter — short of either relativizing intrinsic properties to worlds in a rather ad hoc manner, or retreating to a non-overlapping, stipulative or haecceity-based picture of the sort Kripke and Plantinga favor. I would also like you to weigh the companion difficulty on Lewis's side of the ledger: the vagueness and multiplicity of the counterpart relation, which notoriously forced him to abandon his own postulate that nothing is ever its own worldmate's counterpart, and which can leave a claim like "Humphrey could have won" without any determinate truth value whenever a candidate has several equally good, and mutually disagreeing, counterparts.

Given all this, which side, in your judgment, pays the higher philosophical price? And is the disagreement between counterpart theory and transworld identity a substantive metaphysical dispute, or might a sufficiently careful transworld-identity theorist, one who avoids the overlap picture, end up holding a position that is extensionally indistinguishable from counterpart theory under another name? Please defend your verdict rather than simply laying out both sides.
