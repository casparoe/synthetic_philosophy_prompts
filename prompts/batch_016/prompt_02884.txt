I'm a PhD student working on medieval Jewish philosophy, and two people in my Maimonides reading group, Yael and Marcus, had an argument last week that the rest of us couldn't settle. I'd like an outside read on it.

(1) Yael opened by pointing to Guide III:32, where Maimonides argues it was "impossible to go suddenly from one extreme to the other," and concluded that God retained animal sacrifice only because the Israelites, fresh out of a culture built entirely around sacrificial worship, could not have been expected to jump straight to worship through prayer and contemplation alone. On her reading, the whole point of the institution was to manage a transitional state of mind, so once that state is gone, the practice has done its job and has no remaining rationale.

(2) Marcus objected that this can't be Maimonides' full view, since in the Mishneh Torah (Hilkhot Melakhim, ch. 11) he rules flatly that the messianic king will rebuild the Temple and restore the sacrificial service in full, rather than letting it lapse. If sacrifice were only ever a concession to a mindset the Jewish people are supposed to have long since outgrown, he asked, why would Maimonides' own law code reinstate it instead of retiring it?

(3) Yael replied that there's no real inconsistency. She noted that Maimonides himself, in the Mishneh Torah (Hilkhot Me'ilah 8:8), treats the sacrificial laws as chukim — statutes whose grounds aren't fully transparent to reason — and argued that a commandment doesn't need its originating rationale to stay intact in order to remain binding, any more than a promise stops being a promise once your reason for making it no longer applies. Once God legislates a practice, she said, it stays law regardless of what happened to the problem it was designed to solve.

(4) Marcus pushed back that this dodges the harder question: why would a lawgiver who foresaw the entire future build in, by name, a permanent fix for a problem he already knew would be solved? He also invoked Nachmanides' well-known objection to Maimonides (on Leviticus 1:9): Cain, Abel, and Noah all sacrificed long before there was any idolatry for anyone to be weaned off of, so weaning people off idolatry cannot have been the whole story even for the earliest sacrifices.

(5) Yael answered that Maimonides only owes an explanation of the commandment given to Israel at Sinai, not of Genesis-era sacrifice, so Nachmanides' objection may be aimed at a stronger claim than the one she's defending. She added that treating the Mishneh Torah ruling as a kind of noble lie Maimonides didn't really believe is a far costlier interpretive move than simply living with some tension.

Neither of them budged, and both seemed to me to be onto something real. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each step and why? I'd also be curious whether you think both of them are missing something, and whether the tension between the Guide and the Mishneh Torah here is genuinely resolvable or not.
