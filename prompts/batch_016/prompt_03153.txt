I've been preparing a response for a graduate seminar on the philosophical foundations of convention, where we've been asked to connect Hume's discussion of the origin of justice to modern epistemic game theory, and I've gotten stuck on the following passage from Book III, Part II, Section II of the *Treatise*:

"This convention is not of the nature of a promise; for even promises themselves, as we shall see afterwards, arise from human conventions. It is only a general sense of common interest; which sense all the members of the society express to one another, and which induces them to regulate their conduct by certain rules. I observe, that it will be for my interest to leave another in the possession of his goods, provided he will act in the same manner with regard to me. He is sensible of a like interest in the regulation of his conduct. When this common sense of interest is mutually expressed, and is known to both, it produces a suitable resolution and behaviour. And this may properly enough be called a convention or agreement betwixt us, though without the interposition of a promise; since the actions of each of us have a reference to those of the other, and are performed upon the supposition that something is to be performed on the other part. Two men who pull the oars of a boat, do it by an agreement or convention, though they have never given promises to each other. Nor is the rule concerning the stability of possession the less derived from human conventions, that it arises gradually, and acquires force by a slow progression, and by our repeated experience of the inconveniences of transgressing it. On the contrary, this experience assures us still more, that the sense of interest has become common to all our fellows, and gives us a confidence of the future regularity of their conduct; and 'tis only on the expectation of this that our moderation and abstinence are founded."

What I can't pin down is exactly what epistemic state Hume thinks he's entitled to when he says the parties' shared interest is "mutually expressed, and is known to both." Taken one way, this is just first-order mutual knowledge: I know your interest, you know mine, full stop. But the cases he reaches for — the rowers, and especially the "confidence of the future regularity" that underwrites stable possession — seem to need each party to trust not merely that the other currently has the relevant interest, but that this is appreciated by the other as something both of them appreciate, and so on upward, which is much closer to the full common-knowledge hierarchy that later treatments of convention (Lewis onward) rely on to explain why a coordination solution becomes self-enforcing. Yet Hume is explicit that no single act of mutual acknowledgment settles the matter — the convention "arises gradually" through "repeated experience," which is a strikingly different generative story from the public announcements or shared observations that standard treatments invoke to explain how common knowledge is supposed to get established in the first place.

So: does Hume's gradual, experience-based mechanism actually deliver common knowledge of the parties' dispositions in the strict sense epistemic game theorists have in mind when they invoke common knowledge of rationality to justify equilibrium play, or does it only ever generate some high but finite order of mutual knowledge that falls short of the limit? And if it's the latter, is "convention" in Hume's sense better reconstructed as something like a correlated equilibrium or an evolutionarily stable regularity that never actually required common knowledge to begin with — making the appeal to common knowledge in later, more formal accounts an add-on rather than something Hume's own picture needs? I'd be curious how you would reconstruct the epistemic structure he seems to be assuming, and whether this looks to you like a genuine gap in his argument or just a difference in vocabulary between his framework and its formal descendants.
