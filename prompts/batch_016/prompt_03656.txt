This past summer, my family and I went hiking on a trail we do most years, and something happened that I haven't stopped thinking about since. There's a steep section we climbed in the cool morning air when everyone was fresh, then descended again that evening, completely worn out. On the way down, that same stretch looked distinctly steeper to me than it had that morning, even though it was obviously the identical slope. When I mentioned this, my dad said, "that's just because you were exhausted," as though that settled things, but the more I think about it, the less sure I am what his explanation actually claims. At least two different things could have happened. Either I saw the same slope both times and only judged or felt it to be steeper on the way down, since I knew descending it while tired would be worse. Or being exhausted actually changed my visual experience itself, so the hill genuinely looked steeper to me before I'd judged anything about it at all. My dad's explanation doesn't obviously pick out one of these over the other, since my exhaustion is doing the causal work either way, and in both cases the effect is ultimately produced by my own brain rather than by anything changing out on the trail itself.

Curious, I looked into it afterward and was surprised to find that psychologists have actually studied almost exactly this question: whether hills look steeper to people who are fatigued, unfit, or carrying a heavy pack. What surprised me more is that researchers apparently disagree about whether this reflects a genuine change in some inner state at all, as opposed to people simply reporting whatever answer they sense is expected of them, with neither their experience nor their honest judgment actually changing. One way people have apparently tried to pull these apart is by comparing a person's verbal or visual estimate of a slope to some more physical, hands-on measure, like tilting an unseen board to match it by feel, on the idea that a purely physical response should be less contaminated by conscious expectations than a spoken estimate would be. My AP Psychology class touched on the general idea that expectations and internal states can shape perception, but we never got into whether that means perception itself is altered, rather than just the layer of judgment sitting on top of it, or whether that's even a coherent line to draw in the first place.

So here's what I'd like help untangling: is there a principled way to distinguish a genuine change in what's perceived, from an unchanged perception paired with a changed judgment, from no real change in either beyond what gets reported out loud? Is that a real, fact-of-the-matter distinction, or does it dissolve the more carefully you try to pin it down? And if there is a fact of the matter, and exhaustion (or, presumably, fear, hunger, or some other bias) can really reach into perception itself rather than just coloring the judgment layered on top of it, what would that mean for how much we should trust our senses more generally?
