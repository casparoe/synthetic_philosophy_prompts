I did thirty-five years in condensed matter physics before I retired, and these days I've fallen into a bit of a philosophy of mind rabbit hole — there's a little reading group at my local library, mostly fellow retirees, and it's honestly some of the most fun I've had with my brain in years. We just spent two sessions on predictive processing and Karl Friston's free energy principle, and something about it has been nagging at me since. When somebody says "free energy" to me, my first association is Helmholtz and Gibbs, not perception, so watching that exact vocabulary get exported into a theory of mind, and then into a theory of literally any self-organizing system right down to individual cells, put me on guard immediately. Funny enough, Helmholtz is also the fellow that 19th-century vision science credits with the idea that perception is a kind of unconscious inference, which is the historical ancestor predictive processing people always cite — so the borrowed vocabulary and the borrowed insight both trace back to the same man. Made me even more suspicious, not less.

So I read past the popular framing (Clark's book, Hohwy's book) into some of the back and forth online, and it turns out plenty of people, including folks who work inside that research community, are willing to grant that the free energy principle, taken as the grand claim, is basically an unfalsifiable tautology: if you persist as a bounded system at all, you can be redescribed after the fact as minimizing variational free energy, no matter what you actually do. The standard test case is the "dark room" problem: minimizing surprise should send an organism into a quiet dark box and keep it there, which plainly isn't what animals do, and the standard patch is that exploring reduces your *expected* free energy over the long run, since unresolved uncertainty gets counted as aversive too. That patch feels suspiciously convenient to me, like the theory just grew a new dial to turn whenever the data misbehaved. Same with the other defense people reach for, that it's fine for the principle to be untestable because it's "just a framework," the way the principle of least action is in physics. That comparison bugs me, because once you actually pin down a Lagrangian for a real system it forces very specific, checkable predictions, whereas here the "generative model" always seems to get fitted to whatever the organism happened to do anyway.

So here's what I'd like from you: write me an essay on whether the free energy principle, taken as that grand claim about all self-organizing systems, is a genuine explanatory scientific/philosophical theory or an unfalsifiable tautology dressed up in borrowed thermodynamic language. Feel free to treat plain predictive processing (the narrower, more testable hypothesis about prediction-error minimization in the cortex) as a seperate question from the grand principle, if you think that distinction actually does real work here. Weigh the strongest arguments on both sides, including the two objections above, and then actually commit to a verdict rather then punting to "reasonable people disagree" — I get enough of that from the reading group already.
