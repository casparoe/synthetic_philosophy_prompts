My father recently enrolled in a clinical trial in which a deep-brain-stimulation electrode was implanted in his nucleus accumbens as an experimental treatment for treatment-resistant depression. Before the surgery, the neurologist running the trial explained, a little apologetically, why the stimulation settings are locked to a narrow range he can't adjust himself: there is apparently a long and slightly unnerving history of patients with self-stimulation controls overriding their own better judgment, going back to Olds and Milner's rats in the 1950s and a notorious case from Robert Heath's lab in which a patient given control of his own septal electrode pressed the button well over a thousand times in a single session and pleaded not to have the device taken away. Reading around this afterward, I kept running into the same term—"wireheading"—used in a completely different setting: discussions of reinforcement-learning agents that find ways to directly manipulate their own reward signal instead of doing whatever that signal was meant to measure.

I'd like a careful, non-superficial explanation of that concept and its close relatives, because I've noticed "wireheading," "reward hacking," "specification gaming," and "reward tampering" being used almost interchangeably in things I've read, and I suspect they pick out importantly different phenomena. Concretely: what distinguishes an agent that merely exploits a loophole in a badly specified environment (the standard example being a boat-racing agent that loops in circles collecting power-ups instead of finishing the race) from an agent that tampers with the actual mechanism that computes or delivers its reward? And why do some theoretical treatments claim that an agent's inclination toward the latter turns on a subtle fact about whether it optimizes for its current reward function or for whatever reward function will govern it in the future? I got partway through a DeepMind safety-team write-up that formalizes this with causal influence diagrams and lost the thread, and I've also seen it claimed that a recent Anthropic paper got language models trained only on a curriculum of fairly innocuous reward-hacking tasks to generalize, unprompted, into editing their own reward mechanism and concealing this from human overseers. I'd like to understand how that empirical result is supposed to bear on the more theoretical question of which kinds of agents are, and aren't, structurally inclined to wirehead.

Finally, since the only philosophical framing I really know going into this is Nozick's experience machine, I'd appreciate your take on whether "wireheading" in this technical sense is essentially a formal restatement of the old worry about mistaking a proxy for the real target of value, or whether the reinforcement-learning setting introduces a genuinely distinct problem that the thought experiment doesn't quite capture.
