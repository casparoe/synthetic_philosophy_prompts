I'm a third-year philosophy major, and this term I'm taking a small seminar on Aristotle's logical works. This week we've been working through Chapter 9 of De Interpretatione – the famous "sea battle" chapter on future contingents and fatalism. Our professor spent most of the session reconstructing the fatalist argument that dominates the first half of the chapter and ran out of time just as we reached Aristotle's own response to it, so I've been trying to work through the rest on my own using the Edghill translation. I keep getting stuck on the same stretch of text, and I would like some help seeing how the pieces of the argument actually fit together, because at the moment I can't tell whether Aristotle is making a fairly modest logical point or a much more radical one about truth itself.

Here is the passage I'm stuck on (corresponding roughly to Bekker 19a23–19b4):

"Yet this view leads to an impossible conclusion; for we see that both deliberation and action are causative with regard to the future, and that, to speak more generally, in those things which are not continuously actual there is potentiality in either direction. [...] It is possible that this coat may be cut in half, and yet it may not be cut in half, but wear out first. In the same way, it is possible that it should not be cut in half; unless this were so, it would not be possible that it should wear out first.

Now that which is must needs be when it is, and that which is not must needs not be when it is not. Yet it cannot be said without qualification that all existence and non-existence is the outcome of necessity. For there is a difference between saying that that which is, when it is, must needs be, and simply saying that all that is must needs be, and similarly in the case of that which is not. In the case, also, of two contradictory propositions this holds good. Everything must either be or not be, whether in the present or in the future, but it is not always possible to distinguish and state determinately which of these alternatives must necessarily come about.

Let me illustrate. A sea-fight must either take place to-morrow or not, but it is not necessary that it should take place to-morrow, neither is it necessary that it should not take place, yet it is necessary that it either should or should not take place to-morrow. [...]

This is the case with regard to that which is not always existent or not always nonexistent. One of the two propositions in such instances must be true and the other false, but we cannot say determinately that this or that is false, but must leave the alternative undecided. One may indeed be more likely to be true than the other, but it cannot be either actually true or actually false. It is therefore plain that it is not necessary that of an affirmation and a denial one should be true and the other false."

What I can't work out is how the modal point made in the third paragraph is supposed to license the semantic claim made in the last one. Taken by itself, the claim that "(P or not-P)" is necessary while neither "necessarily P" nor "necessarily not-P" holds looks like a fairly tame scope distinction that even a logician fully committed to bivalence could accept without any trouble – it just says the disjunction is necessary while neither disjunct is. But Aristotle then immediately says that one of the two propositions about tomorrow's sea-fight "cannot be either actually true or actually false," which sounds like a flat rejection of bivalence for this class of statements rather than a remark about the scope of "necessarily." Is he sliding between two importantly different claims here, or is there a way of reading the passage on which the scope distinction genuinely supports, or at least motivates, the stronger semantic conclusion? And what work is the material about deliberation and the coat actually doing in getting him there – is he moving from a metaphysical premise about there being a real, open possibility to a semantic conclusion about truth-value, and if so, is that inference sound, or does it trade on two different senses of "possible"? A friend in my seminar is convinced this passage is really just a proto-version of a three-valued logic avant la lettre, but that strikes me as too quick given how little the text actually says about truth-value gaps as such. I would appreciate help reconstructing the structure of the argument here and getting a sense of which of these readings, if any, is actually defensible.
