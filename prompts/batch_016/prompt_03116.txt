I'm putting together a session on Bayesian epistemology for my formal epistemology seminar and I keep circling back to van Fraassen's Reflection Principle, from that 1984 "Belief and the Will" paper. The formal statement is simple enough — your current credence in A, conditional on the supposition that your credence in A at some later time will be r, should itself equal r (roughly, Cr_t(A | Cr_t'(A)=r) = r for t < t') — and I can walk students through why violating it is supposed to expose you to a diachronic Dutch book, the same basic move usually credited to David Lewis (via Teller's 1973 paper) for ordinary conditionalization. What I can't quite do is make it feel like more than bookkeeping for idealized agents who never really change their minds.

Here's the thing that's actually bugging me, and its not really a seminar question. My sister is about to go on one of those long silent Vipassana retreats (Goenka-style, several weeks), and she's told me flat out that she expects to come back with a much lower credence in there being a persisting self, or in libertarian free will — not because she'll encounter new arguments, but because that's apparently just what the practice reliably does to people, herself included, based on a shorter retreat she did years back. If she already knows her credence in "no persisting self" at t2 will be, say, 0.8, doesnt naive Reflection commit her — and by extension me, insofar as I trust her prediction — to already having credence 0.8 now, before any of the actual causal process that's supposed to produce the shift has even happened? That seems like a bizarre way to revise your metaphysics on the installment plan.

So, professor to whatever-you-are: what are the most important implications of the Reflection Principle for cases like this, where someone can confidently forecast their own future credence but the anticipated change isn't being driven by anything you'd normally call evidence or argument? Is there some principled reason the principle shouldn't bite here, or am I just smuggling in the conclusion I want because the alternative feels absurd? I'd like the actual substance, not just "well, obviously it only applies to ideal agents" — I want to know why that move is or isn't legitimate, and what's left of the principle once you make it.
