I'm a retired geophysicist who's gotten weirdly hooked on philosophy since I stopped working a couple years ago (my wife thinks its a stranger hobby than the fossil collecting, but here we are). Lately I've been reading about A.J. Ayer, the old logical positivist, and I got completely sidetracked by the story of his 1988 near-death experience: he choked on some smoked salmon while recovering from pneumonia in hospital, his heart stopped for about four minutes, and he came back describing a blinding, painful red light that he said was in charge of running the universe, with a couple of lesser beings overseeing space itself. What kills me is that this is a guy who'd spent fifty years insisting any talk of souls or an afterlife was strictly meaningless, unverifiable nonsense, and even after that episode all he'd allow in print was that it had "slightly weakened" his conviction that death would be the end of him -- before more or less walking that back in a follow-up piece a few weeks later, saying he'd only meant it loosened his attitude, not the belief itself.

Earlier in his career he'd also worked out a whole account of personal identity, in an essay I think is called "The Concept of a Person," where what makes someone the same person over time basically comes down to bodily continuity -- no shared body, no continuous "you," which already rules out disembodied souls before you even get to the verification stuff.

So here's what I keep chewing on, and I'd like you to really reason it through from how Ayer actually argued rather than give me a generic "positivists don't believe in ghosts" answer: what do you think he'd make of the "griefbot" or "deadbot" apps that are around now, where a company trains a chatbot on someone's old texts, emails and voice recordings after they die, so relatives can keep "talking" to them? Would his verificationism plus that bodily-continuity theory make this an even easier case to dismiss than an afterlife -- pure category error, nothing there to even argue about -- or does the fact that these bots produce something you can actually sit down and interact with (unlike a soul) create a genuinely new wrinkle that his old framework wasn't built to handle? And given how fast he tried to walk back even a "slightly" after his own brush with death, do you reckon he'd hold the line here too, or is there a version of this a rigorous empiricist should actually take seriously?
