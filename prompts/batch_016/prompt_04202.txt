I've spent a long time following the rationalist blogosphere's long-running argument over Pascal's Mugging: Yudkowsky's original 2007 post, Robin Hanson's comment proposing what came to be called the "leverage penalty" (discount the prior probability of any hypothesis in proportion to the number of people you would uniquely be positioned to affect, on top of the usual complexity penalty), and Yudkowsky's 2013 "Pascal's Muggle" follow-up, in which he concedes that a leverage penalty commits you to denying that you are pivotal no matter how much evidence you are shown, since it will always be more likely that the "evidence" is some kind of trick or hallucination than that a hypothesis with prior probability around 1/3^^^3 has turned out to be true.

Please argue for the following position, which I realize is somewhat contrarian within these discussions: that the leverage penalty is neither what actually stops most people from paying the mugger, nor a good reason for an ideal Bayesian reasoner to refuse him, and that all of the real work is instead being done by whether one's utility function is bounded (or at least sufficiently sublinear) in the number of people affected. Your argument should explain why a leverage-penalized agent whose utility is nonetheless unbounded and linear in population size ought, if it is properly calibrated, still end up paying a sufficiently insistent mugger, and it should confront the standard cost of the bounded-utility alternative: that it commits you to treating sufficiently large additional goods, such as more flourishing lives, as making essentially no further difference at all.
