Okay so I have zero background in philosophy or computer science, this is just something I've been chewing on after falling down a YouTube/blog rabbit hole about AI alignment a few months back. There's this idea from Eliezer Yudkowsky called "coherent extrapolated volition" (CEV) - the basic pitch is that instead of coding a superintelligent AI with whatever values some team of programmers happens to think is right, you have it figure out what humanity would actually want if we knew way more than we do now, thought things through more clearly, and "were more the people we wished we were, had grown up farther together." Basically our cleaned up, idealized wishes instead of our messy current ones, and the AI acts on that instead of what we say we want right now. I guess its kind of like those "idealized preference" theories of wellbeing that philosophers apparently have too (what's good for you is whatever you'd want if you were fully rational and informed), except CEV tries to pull off the same trick for all of humanity at once, not just one person.

Heres what's been bugging me for weeks though: the word "coherent" is carrying a LOT of weight in that phrase. Its not just saying "extrapolate everybody's volition separately," its assuming that if you push the idealization far enough, all these separate extrapolated wishes will actually converge, or at least be reconcilable into one combined output. But why think that's true rather than the opposite - that the more consistent, informed and reflective people get, the MORE their genuinely different starting temperaments and values show up, so idealizing everyone makes the disagreements sharper instead of dissolving them? And if that's how it goes, then whatever process actually turns a pile of separately-extrapolated volitions into one "coherent" answer isn't itself derived from the extrapolation at all - its an extra choice (majority rules? weighted blend? some people's volition just gets overridden?) getting smuggled in from outside, kind of like those weird paradoxes people run into with voting and ranking systems.

So here's what I want: can you build the strongest possible version of that argument - that the "coherence" step in CEV can't actually be derived from extrapolating individuals, and therefore it quietly reintroduces the exact "whose values count" problem CEV was supposed to get around in the first place? I'm not after a summary of the usual objections you'd find on a wiki somewhere, I want an actual worked-through argument, and ideally a concrete example of two or more idealized people whose extrapolated wishes just wouldn't converge no matter how carefully you tried to make the process "coherent."
