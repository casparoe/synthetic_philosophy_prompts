I spent about thirty-five years doing experimental condensed matter physics before I retired, and now that I've got time on my hands I've fallen into reading philosophy of science, which my wife finds hilarious ("shouldn't you be taking up woodworking or something at your age"). Lately I've been stuck on the Humean vs anti-Humean fight about what a "law of nature" actually is. The Humean side (Lewis originally, and I gather Loewer is the guy carrying the torch these days) says a law is just whatever comes out as an axiom or theorem of the best deductive systemization of the entire history of the world -- the single best trade-off of simplicity and strength, read off after the fact from the complete four-dimensional pattern of events. The anti-Humean side, which I've mainly been getting from Maudlin, says no: laws are ontologically primitive and unanalyzable, and they actually govern the pattern rather than merely summarizing it -- on this view the total state of the universe is more like a product of the laws churning away on some initial state, not the other way around.

Here's the specific bit that's been keeping me up at night (well, up past 9pm anyway, one of the perks of retirement). Maudlin argues that Humeans can't really claim their laws explain anything, because on their view the whole mosaic of events has to already be complete and fixed before you can even extract the "best system" from it -- so the laws can't be doing explanatory work on the very facts that made them laws to begin with. That's viciously circular, not an explanation, and it matches my gut instinct as an old experimentalist that Newton's second law explains why the apple accelerates, full stop, rather than being some shorthand tallied up after the fact. But apparently Loewer's comeback is that there are two different senses of "explain" smuggled in there (something like a "metaphysical" one and a "scientific" one), so the circularity is only apparent, not real -- and I honestly can't tell if that's a real distinction or just a clever bit of footwork. Meanwhile it seems to me the primitivist doesn't obviously get off easier either, since just declaring laws to be brute, fundamentally-governing facts sounds suspiciously like Lewis's old complaint against Armstrong's necessitation relation between universals -- that calling something "necessitation" doesn't actually explain why instances comply, it just hangs a fancy label on the exact same mystery.

So here's my request: could you write me a dialogue between a committed Humean (defending something like the Lewis/Loewer best-system view) and a committed anti-Humean (defending something like Maudlin's primitivism about governing laws), where they go at each other specifically on this question -- whether either of their laws can really explain the regularities they're supposed to be laws of, or whether both sides are just re-describing the same mystery in fancier language. I want genuine back and forth, where each side has to directly answer the other's strongest objection instead of talking past them -- no strawmen, they should be equally sharp -- and its totally fine, maybe even preferable, if it ends in a real stalemate rather than a tidy resolution, since from what I can tell, that's honestly where the actual debate stands right now.
