I'm a philosophy PhD student currently drafting a dissertation chapter on formal epistemology, and for the past few weeks I've been living inside I. J. Good's short 1967 note "On the Principle of Total Evidence" — the source of what people now sometimes call Good's theorem: the proof that a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer should never expect, in advance, to be worse off for consulting a piece of free evidence before acting, however badly that particular piece of evidence happens to turn out. It has become a kind of folk axiom among decision theorists (look before you leap, because looking is always weakly rational), and it came up recently over drinks with a few people from my cohort, when someone joked that the theorem is basically a mandate to always open your biopsy results.

What's actually been nagging at me, though, is a colder companion result. Jack Hirshleifer's 1971 paper on the economics of information argues that once you put more than one such agent into the same world, the comfort mostly evaporates: information that is individually rational for each person to go looking for can be collectively ruinous, because it can dissolve the shared ignorance that mutually beneficial arrangements — insurance, certain kinds of trust, risk-pooling of any sort — depended on in the first place. Nobody in Hirshleifer's scenario is behaving irrationally, and Good's theorem holds perfectly well for each of them taken individually; the arrangement comes apart anyway. I keep noticing, too, that this is the same I. J. Good who, a couple of years earlier, had written the essay that more or less invented the modern idea of an intelligence explosion — the same mind arguing both that looking is always locally sound and that a process which never stops looking might eventually remake everything around it.

I'd like you to try writing a short story (something like 1000–1500 words) built around the Hirshleifer side of this rather than the reassuring Good side. Center it on some small group bound together by an arrangement that depends on nobody being able to foresee a particular future outcome — a marriage, a fishing crew splitting an uncertain catch, a village's harvest-insurance scheme, a syndicate of some kind, or any setting you find more generative than mine — into which one character introduces a way of finding that outcome out ahead of time. Every character reasoning the decision through should have a perfectly sound, individually rational case for wanting to look; what I'd like the story to earn, scene by scene, is how the looking unravels the arrangement anyway, without anyone behaving irrationally, maliciously, or even all that unusually. I'd rather you steer away from making this explicitly about AI or superintelligence, since I've already encountered a great many stories along those lines — I'd rather see whether the mechanism holds up when it's dramatized at ordinary human scale, with an ending that follows from the setup rather than moralizing over it.
