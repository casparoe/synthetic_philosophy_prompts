Okay, this is going to sound random, but I've been on a real Spinoza kick these past few weeks, mostly becuase I keep doom-scrolling about democratic backsliding and a friend mentioned that Spinoza was basically the first major modern philosopher to argue FOR democracy as the best, most "natural" regime, way before that was a normal thing for a philosopher to say. I read through the relevant part of the Theological-Political Treatise (ch. 16, where he calls democracy the most natural form of government because it stays closest to the freedom everyone already has by nature) and got genuinely kind of moved by it, so I kept going into the unfinished Political Treatise to get his fuller, more systematic take, since apparently he was mid-way through writing the actual democracy chapter when he died in 1677.

And then I hit chapter 11 and... yikes. Section 3 defines his target democracy as one where everyone who's independent and "of respectable life" gets to vote and hold office, then immediately clarifies that "independent" is doing a lot of quiet work, since it's specifically there to exclude "women and slaves... and also children and wards." Section 4 is him actually arguing for the exclusion of women in particular — and then the manuscript just stops forever, because this is literally the last paragraph Spinoza ever wrote before he died. Here's the whole thing (using the old 1883 Gosset/Elwes translation, since that's the one I could actually find for free):

"But, perhaps, someone will ask, whether women are under men's authority by nature or institution? For if it has been by mere institution, then we had no reason compelling us to exclude women from government. But if we consult experience itself, we shall find that the origin of it is in their weakness. For there has never been a case of men and women reigning together, but wherever on the earth men are found, there we see that men rule, and women are ruled, and that on this plan, both sexes live in harmony. But on the other hand, the Amazons, who are reported to have held rule of old, did not suffer men to stop in their country, but reared only their female children, killing the males to whom they gave birth. But if by nature women were equal to men, and were equally distinguished by force of character and ability, in which human power and therefore human right chiefly consist; surely among nations so many and different some would be found, where both sexes rule alike, and others, where men are ruled by women, and so brought up, that they can make less use of their abilities. And since this is nowhere the case, one may assert with perfect propriety, that women have not by nature equal right with men: but that they necessarily give way to men, and that thus it cannot happen, that both sexes should rule alike, much less that men should be ruled by women. But if we further reflect upon human passions, how men, in fact, generally love women merely from the passion of lust, and esteem their cleverness and wisdom in proportion to the excellence of their beauty, and also how very ill-disposed men are to suffer the women they love to show any sort of favour to others, and other facts of this kind, we shall easily see that men and women cannot rule alike without great hurt to peace. But of this enough."

My first reaction was honestly just to wince, but I'd rather actually understand the argument than file it under "product of his time." Two things I can't work out. First: the main argument — no society has ever had women ruling on equal terms with men, therefore women must lack equal natural power/ability — reads to me as basically inductive, an argument from the absence of observed counterexamples, which seems like a strange move coming from someone whose whole signature method (at least in the Ethics) is deducing things necessarily from definitions and axioms rather than reasoning from "well, history hasn't shown us X." Is this really just a bare empirical induction, or is his necessitarianism doing something sneakier underneath it — like, given that for Spinoza whatever happens follows necessarily from the total causal order, is he treating "what has in fact always occurred" and "what nature dictates" as basically interchangeable in a way an ordinary empiricist wouldn't be entitled to? Second: since Spinoza more or less everywhere else identifies natural right with power (potentia), does this argument just fall directly out of that identification, so that excluding women is a structural consequence of his whole "right = power" framework — or is it really more of a seperate, weakly-supported empirical add-on that a committed Spinozist could drop without touching the underlying metaphysics at all? And where does the closing bit about jealousy and "the passion of lust" fit in — a second, independent argument, or just supporting colour for the first one? I'd love an actual close reading of how the inference is supposed to work here, not just a comment on how badly it's aged.
