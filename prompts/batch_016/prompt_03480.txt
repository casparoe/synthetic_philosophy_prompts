I'm a PhD student in history and philosophy of science, writing a chapter on early computing's entanglement with psychical research. In "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950), Turing calls the Argument from Extra-Sensory Perception "quite a strong one": he finds the statistical evidence for telepathy "overwhelming," illustrates the danger with a telepathic guesser scoring 130 out of 400 on a card-suit-guessing task against a chance expectation of 100 (in the vein of the card-guessing trials that J. B. Rhine's Duke laboratory had made famous), and concludes that the imitation game would need to be run in a "telepathy-proof room." Rhine's results were never independently replicated and are now considered thoroughly discredited, but parapsychology had one more act to play: Daryl Bem's 2011 "Feeling the Future" studies used entirely conventional, peer-reviewed significance-testing methods to report similarly "overwhelming" evidence for precognition, and the failed replications that followed became a landmark exhibit in the case against practices like optional stopping and flexible data analysis.

Given that trajectory — and given that Turing himself had independently pioneered rigorous sequential-testing methods at Bletchley Park — what verdict do you think he would reach on ESP today, and would he still see any need for a telepathy-proof room? Defend a specific answer rather than a hedge, grounded in what we actually know of his intellectual character.
