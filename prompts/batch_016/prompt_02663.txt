Quick one from the trenches of final-paper grading. In my Kant seminar I always teach the "contradiction in conception" test the same way: take the false-promising maxim, universalize it, and you get a flat-out logical contradiction, no different in kind from asserting "this circle is square" — the maxim literally can't be conceived as a universal law, end of story, thats the whole test. A sharp student cornered me in office hours and said this can't be right, since there's nothing strictly self-contradictory about picturing a grim world where nobody's promises get believed anymore — that world is awful and self-undermining in some sense, sure, but it's not incoherent the way a square circle is incoherent.

I told her she was basically describing a distinction without a difference — sure, people argue about whether to call it a "logical" contradiction or a "practical" one or a "teleological" one or whatever, but I said all these rival readings in the secondary lit are just relabelings of the same basic intuition and cash out in the same verdicts on the same maxims anyway, so it's not worth loosing sleep over. She wasn't having it, and now that I've mulled it over some more between stacks of ungraded papers, neither am I. Did I actually shortchange her, or is she just being fussy about a merely verbal dispute among Kant scholars that doesn't actually change which maxims pass or fail the test?
