A friend and I spent most of a long dinner this week arguing about my cousin Mateo, and I still haven't managed to talk myself out of the position I ended up defending, which I held mostly on instinct. Mateo has become the most dependable volunteer at the community fridge two blocks from his apartment: he checks on it most days, keeps track of which families rely on it and what they tend to need, clears out anything that's spoiled, and is warm and unhurried with everyone who stops by. The complicating detail, which he's quite open about with family, is that he keeps doing all this mainly because his ex-girlfriend also volunteers there and he hasn't given up on winning her back; ask him directly and he'll admit that the food insecurity of the people he's helping isn't really what's on his mind from week to week. My friend's position was that none of this is morally admirable, however reliably good the results are, because Mateo isn't doing any of it for the reasons that actually make it good. I wanted to resist that conclusion but couldn't get past intuition to anything more precise.

Thinking it over afterward, I realized we were basically just rehearsing Kant's old view (the shopkeeper example from the Groundwork) that an act has genuine moral worth only if it's done from the motive of duty, rather than from self-interest or even ordinary sympathy. But I also remembered that this is exactly the assumption that later philosophers working on "moral worth" have tried to dismantle, without giving up on the thought that motives matter. Julia Markovits, for instance, defends what she calls the Coincident Reasons Thesis, on which an act is morally worthy just in case the reasons for which it is performed coincide with the reasons that actually make it right or good, with no reference to duty as such required. Nomy Arpaly, whose account Markovits explicitly engages with, instead locates moral worth in the agent's being appropriately responsive to whatever features of a situation make an act right, whether or not the agent ever represents those features to herself in so many words — and even, as in her well-known discussion of Huckleberry Finn, when the agent's own explicit judgment runs the other way.

What I would actually like from you is a precise account of how Markovits's and Arpaly's views differ from one another, and not merely from Kant, since it's clear to me they aren't just two labels for the same underlying thought. Then, applying each view in turn, could you work out what each would say about Mateo — does he come out with no moral worth, partial moral worth, or full moral worth on the Coincident Reasons Thesis, and would Arpaly's responsiveness account actually hand down a different verdict? Finally, I would be curious which of the two accounts strikes you as doing the better job of tracking what we actually care about when we grant or withhold moral credit from someone — and why.
