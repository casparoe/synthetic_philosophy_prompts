I'm about a third of the way into a novel with a subplot set at Oxford in 1956, around the actual dust-up over the university giving Harry Truman an honorary degree and Elizabeth Anscombe's pamphlet trying to block it (she lost the vote, which is basically the shape of the scene I want to write). To make sure I had the philosophy straight before writing the argument into a dinner party scene, I tried the bare case out on my actual writing group last week, and two of them, Nadia and Callum, ran with it for a solid twenty minutes while everyone else just picked at the cheese board.

(1) Nadia took Anscombe's line: Truman chose to obliterate two cities specifically to terrorize Japan into surrendering, thats using the deaths of innocents as a means to an end, and no amount of downstream good -- a shorter war, fewer total casualties from an invasion -- changes what kind of act that was; you don't get to count the cost once you've already decided to kill people that way. (2) Callum said the intending/foreseeing line is carrying more weight then it can hold here, since both cities had genuine military value -- Hiroshima had an army headquarters, Nagasaki had major shipyards and arms works -- so the civilian dead could just as honestly be described as a foreseen but unintended side effect of hitting legitimate targets, no different in kind from plenty of other raids near cities that nobody was calling murder. (3) Nadia answered that you can tell which one it was from the strategy itself: the plan was "flatten a city to shock them into quitting," not "degrade this factory, and unfortunately people live nearby," and choosing a whole city as the aim point is itself evidence of what was intended. (4) Callum's comeback was that you can't settle what somebody intended just by redescribing the strategy in whichever words make your preferred verdict fall out -- and when a demonstration over an empty area got proposed and rejected, the actual reasons on record were fear of a dud and only having a couple of bombs ready at that point, not any confessed preference for dead civilians, so even that piece of evidence doesn't clearly favor Nadia's reading. (5) Nadia's last word was that the same discussions also weighed keeping the "shock value" of hitting a live city without warning, which she said is just the maximize-the-terror reasoning wearing a lab coat.

Neither of them budged, and I went home honestly unsure who'd gotten the better of which exchange -- which is a problem since I actually have to write the scene now. Could you go through it move by move and say who's got the better of it at each numbered point, rather than just declaring an overall winner? And if you think there's some consideration neither of them raised that would actually move the disagreement forward, I'd love to hear it too, since it might be worth putting in a character's mouth.
