I'm putting together a small collection of philosophically-minded short fiction for my seminar on decision theory, and I'd like a contribution built around the Pasadena paradox (Nover and Hájek, 2004). Briefly: in the Pasadena game, a fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads; if heads first appears on toss n, the payoff is (-1)^(n-1) · 2^n / n. Weighting each payoff by its probability of 2^(-n) yields the term (-1)^(n-1)/n, so the game's expected value is the alternating harmonic series — which sums to ln 2 in its natural order, but only conditionally. Since it doesn't converge absolutely, the Riemann rearrangement theorem guarantees that reordering these very same terms, without altering a single one of them, can make the total equal any real number, or diverge to positive or negative infinity. Unlike the St. Petersburg game, then, the Pasadena game's expectation isn't simply infinite — it is strictly undefined, even though every individual payoff and probability is perfectly well-defined.

Please write a short story of roughly 800 to 1,500 words that dramatizes this predicament rather than merely describing it. I'd especially like the form of the piece, and not just its content, to carry some of the philosophical weight: find some way for the story's own structure or telling to enact the paradox's central sting — that reordering identical elements, none of which is itself altered, can change the apparent sum of things entirely. If possible, avoid the well-worn "a stranger offers a suspicious wager" set piece. The story need not resolve the paradox or draw an explicit moral; I am more interested in whether it can make a reader feel something like the vertigo that decision theorists report on encountering this example.
