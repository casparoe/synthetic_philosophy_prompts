I went down a real rabbit hole last week that I can't quite shake. It started innocently enough: I was reading about the Two Generals Problem and why distributed systems can never fully reach "common knowledge" over an unreliable network (that whole infinite regress of acks — I know you got my message, but do you know that I know you got it, and so on forever). A footnote in one of the papers mentioned that the epistemic logic behind all this usually just assumes "positive introspection" as a simplifying idealization — basically, if an agent knows P, it automatically also knows that it knows P — and then casually noted that philosophers actually argue about whether that's even true for humans. That sent me down an internet philosophy wormhole into Timothy Williamson's stuff on "luminosity" and the so-called KK principle. As far as I can tell (pls correct me, I have zero philosophy training and might be mangling this), Williamson's argument is that almost nothing about our own minds is "luminous" — meaning a state such that whenever your in it, you're automatically in a position to know you're in it — because of these margin-for-error arguments: roughly, if knowing "I still feel cold" only required being reliable right up to the exact threshold where you stop feeling cold, you'd also end up "knowing" it in some cases where you're actually wrong, so there has to be a buffer zone where you're in the state but can't yet know it. Since the same reasoning generalizes to knowledge itself, it ends up implying the KK principle fails — you can know something without knowing that you know it.

This has been messing with me because it feels uncomfortably close to something that happens to me constantly at work. I'll be debugging something and hit this rock-solid feeling of "okay, NOW I actually understand why this is happening," ship the fix, and then two days later realize I didn't understand it at all — and the unsettling part is that the confident-but-wrong version felt, from the inside, exactly like the confident-and-correct version usually feels. We also have this ritual after incidents where someone writes up "the root cause" in the postmortem doc like it's now settled, verified knowledge, and everyone just nods along. Could you walk me through the most important implications of the KK principle failing, in Williamson's sense, for how much weight I should actually put on that inner "I've got it" feeling — both when I'm the one debugging, and when a team collectively decides it "knows" why something broke? I'd love actual substantive implications here, not just "be more humble," since that feels like too easy an answer given how specific and technical the actual argument is.
