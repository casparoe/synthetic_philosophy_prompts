Over the past year or so I've gotten into reading old political and religious texts on my own, mostly downloading translations of classic works whenever I get curious about where some idea "everybody knows" actually comes from originally. Right now I'm working through Thomas Aquinas's short treatise on kingship, "De Regno" (also published as part of the larger "De Regimine Principum"), because I kept seeing him cited as the source of the Catholic tradition's position on when it's permissible to resist or even kill a bad ruler. When I got to the actual passage where he deals with this question, though, it left me more puzzled than informed, and a friend of mine who took a couple of philosophy classes in college wasn't able to untangle it for me either. Here is the passage, from Book I, Chapter 7, in the standard Phelan/Eschmann translation (paragraph numbers included, as they appear in most editions I've found online):

"[45] If the excess of tyranny is unbearable, some have been of the opinion that it would be an act of virtue for strong men to slay the tyrant and to expose themselves to the danger of death in order to set the multitude free. An example of this occurs even in the Old Testament, for a certain Aioth slew Eglon, King of Moab, who was oppressing the people of God under harsh slavery, thrusting a dagger into his thigh; and he was made a judge of the people [Judges 3:14 ff]. [46] But this opinion is not in accord with apostolic teaching. For Peter admonishes us to be reverently subject to our masters, not only to the good and gentle but also the froward [1 Pet 2:18-19]: 'For if one who suffers unjustly bear his trouble for conscience' sake, this is grace.' Wherefore, when many emperors of the Romans tyrannically persecuted the faith of Christ, a great number both of the nobility and the common people were converted to the faith and were praised for patiently bearing death for Christ. They did not resist although they were armed, and this is plainly manifested in the case of the holy Theban legion. Aioth, then, must be considered rather as having slain a foe than assassinated a ruler, however tyrannical, of the people. Hence in the Old Testament we also read that they who killed Joas, the king of Juda, who had fallen away from the worship of God, were slain and their children spared according to the precept of the law (2 Sam 14:5-6). [47] Should private persons attempt on their own private presumption to kill the rulers, even though tyrants, this would be dangerous for the multitude as well as for their rulers. This is because the wicked usually expose themselves to dangers of this kind more than the good, for the rule of a king, no less than that of a tyrant, is burdensome to them since, according to the words of Solomon [Prov 20:26]: 'A wise king scatters the wicked.' Consequently, by presumption of this kind, danger to the people from the loss of a good king would be more probable than relief through the removal of a tyrant. [48] Furthermore, it seems that to proceed against the cruelty of tyrants is an action to be undertaken, not through the private presumption of a few, but rather by public authority. [49] If to provide itself with a king belongs to the right of a given multitude, it is not unjust that the king be deposed or have his power restricted by that same multitude if, becoming a tyrant, he abuses the royal power. It must not be thought that such a multitude is acting unfaithfully in deposing the tyrant, even though it had previously subjected itself to him in perpetuity, because he himself has deserved that the covenant with his subjects should not be kept, since, in ruling the multitude, he did not act faithfully as the office of a king demands. Thus did the Romans, who had accepted Tarquin the Proud as their king, cast him out from the kingship on account of his tyranny and the tyranny of his sons; and they set up in their place a lesser power, namely, the consular power. Similarly Domitian, who had succeeded those most moderate emperors, Vespasian, his father, and Titus, his brother, was slain by the Roman senate when he exercised tyranny, and all his wicked deeds were justly, and profitably declared null and void by a decree of the senate."

What I can't work out is how paragraphs 46 and 48-49 fit together. In 46, Aquinas seems to rule out killing a tyrant on the grounds that Christians are supposed to patiently endure even cruel rulers, and he deals with the obvious counterexample of Aioth (Ehud) by simply declaring that it doesn't count as "assassinating a ruler" — which looks to me less like an argument than a relabeling of the one case he can't easily dismiss. Then, two paragraphs later, he says the real problem with killing tyrants is that it is done on "private presumption," which seems to imply that the very same act might be acceptable if it carried the backing of "public authority" instead. But his own illustration of that in paragraph 49 — Domitian being "slain by the Roman senate" — was, as far as I understand actual Roman history, a palace conspiracy that the senate only blessed after the fact, not a considered decision to try or execute him through some formal process. So is "public authority" doing any real work here beyond just meaning "a sufficiently large or official-sounding group of people," and if not, doesn't that quietly re-permit the very thing paragraph 46 seemed to rule out? I'd really appreciate help thinking through whether there's a consistent position here that I'm simply missing, or whether this is a genuine tension in Aquinas's own reasoning.
