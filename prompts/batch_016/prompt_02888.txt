I write about the ideas and formal theories that quietly shape policy debates, and I'm currently working on a piece about the perennially stalled "loss and damage" climate negotiations between historically high-emitting wealthy nations and smaller, low-emitting, climate-vulnerable countries. While researching the game theory behind bargaining for the piece, I noticed that nearly every popular explainer reaching for bargaining theory defaults to the Nash bargaining solution, but a game theorist I interviewed insisted that the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution is a more honest lens for a negotiation this lopsided, precisely because of a technical property that Nash's solution lacks. I have a rough grasp of both solution concepts from an undergraduate course years ago, but I would like a sharper, non-superficial understanding before I put anything about this in print.

Could you lay out the most important implications of using the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution, rather than the Nash solution, as a normative benchmark for a negotiation like this one? I am especially interested in what actually follows, substantively, from Kalai-Smorodinsky's monotonicity property and its rejection of Nash's independence of irrelevant alternatives: concretely, under what circumstances would a smaller, more vulnerable country be predicted to fare better or worse under Kalai-Smorodinsky than under Nash, and what does each solution implicitly assume about how "ideal" or maximal claims should shape a fair compromise between parties with very unequal bargaining power? I would also value your candid view on whether either concept is doing real normative work here, or whether invoking either one in coverage of an actual climate negotiation risks lending a spurious air of mathematical rigor to what is, in practice, a raw power struggle.
