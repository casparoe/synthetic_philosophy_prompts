I write about science and ideas for a regional newspaper, but a dispute in my own apartment building has left me stuck on a puzzle that feels closer to philosophy than journalism. A slow leak in a shared utility closet went unaddressed for weeks and eventually flooded the basement storage units, ruining a good portion of my own belongings along with those of several neighbors. It turns out the building superintendent had been told about the leak well before it burst and simply never got around to dealing with it. At the co-op board meeting, nearly everyone treated his inaction as the obvious reason the flood happened, and there is now a proposal to dock his pay to help cover the damages. One board member, a retired engineer, pointed out that the same reasoning used against the superintendent, namely, that if he had only acted, none of this would have happened, applies just as well to every tenant who used that closet and never mentioned the damp patch on the floor, or never wondered why the pipe behind it always felt warm. Nobody thinks those tenants owe the building anything, yet their inaction seems to bear exactly the same relationship to the flood as the superintendent's.

Since several of the ruined boxes were mine, I would like to work out what I actually think before the next meeting rather than simply defer to the room's consensus, and the puzzle strikes me as bigger than this one building. Is there a principled basis for treating the superintendent's failure to act as more responsible for the flood than everyone else's failure to act, or are we really just deciding in advance whose job it was to intervene and then describing that decision as though it were a discovery about what caused the damage? I would be grateful for help thinking this through, since versions of the same question seem to lurk behind a lot of the stories I cover, from workplace accidents to public-health failures, whenever people argue about whether someone's inaction actually caused harm or merely failed to prevent it.
