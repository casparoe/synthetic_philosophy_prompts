At work last month, our team spent nearly an hour arguing about whether a forty-line change to a shared utility function counted as a "large pull request" that required a second reviewer under our style guide. The conversation kept circling back to the same unresolved question: exactly where the line between "large" and "not large" was supposed to sit, and why none of us could defend a specific cutoff with any confidence. Out of curiosity, I read around the topic afterward and ended up at the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's entry on the Sorites paradox, which treats this kind of case as the occasion for a real dispute between two camps: epistemicists, following Timothy Williamson, who insist there is a genuine, fact-of-the-matter cutoff between "large" and "not large" that competent speakers are simply unable to know; and supervaluationists, in the tradition of Kit Fine and Rosanna Keefe, who hold instead that "large" comes out true on some acceptable ways of making the term precise and false on others, so that borderline cases are neither true nor false, even though classical principles like excluded middle survive intact. I can follow the basic shape of each position, but I don't yet have a good feel for how a genuine defender of either one would hold up under the sharpest objections to it.

Could you write me a fairly substantial dialogue between a committed epistemicist and a committed supervaluationist as they work through a single running example? I'd rather you invent a fresh sorites series, or build on the pull-request case above, than reuse the standard heap-of-sand or bald-man examples, since I want to see the reasoning actually applied rather than recited from a textbook. Each character should press the other on real pressure points rather than simply restating their own view: the epistemicist should push on whether higher-order vagueness -- the fact that "definitely large" seems just as vague as "large" itself -- relocates the sharp-boundary problem for the supervaluationist rather than dissolving it, while the supervaluationist should press back on whether an unknowable, exact cutoff is really less strange than admitting there is no fact of the matter, and should ask the epistemicist to explain why competent speakers could be ignorant of such a cutoff in a way that seems unlike ordinary ignorance elsewhere in language. Neither side should fold or be easy to refute; I would like to come away still genuinely unsure who has the better of the argument.
