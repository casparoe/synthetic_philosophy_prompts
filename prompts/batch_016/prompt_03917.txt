One crucial premise in "coherence arguments" for the claim that sufficiently advanced artificial agents will act as expected-utility maximizers is the completeness axiom: for any two options, a rational agent must strictly prefer one to the other or be indifferent between them. This premise is sometimes defended with a money-pump argument. Suppose an agent has a genuine preferential gap between A and B: it doesn't prefer A to B, doesn't prefer B to A, and isn't indifferent between them either. Since neither option is thereby ruled out, suppose the agent, offered a choice between A and B at Node 1, picks B. At Node 2 it is offered a seemingly costless trade: B for A-, a slightly inferior version of A. Since the gap is robust to a small enough reduction in A's value, the agent isn't barred from taking this trade either; suppose it does. It ends up with A-, even though choosing A outright at Node 1 was available all along and would have left it strictly better off — a dominated outcome.

The obvious escape is for the agent to adopt, in advance, a resolute policy: refuse, at Node 2, anything it strictly disprefers to an option — here, A — that it earlier passed up. Critics object that this makes the Node-2 choice depend on an alternative no longer on the table: nothing actually available at that node is strictly preferred to A- (B isn't), so refusing the trade has no node-local justification. This, critics say, is an illegitimate form of path-dependence.

Please argue that the critics are wrong: that an agent may rationally let a foreclosed alternative constrain its later choices in just this way, so that this money-pump argument fails to establish completeness as a requirement on rational agents, artificial or otherwise.
