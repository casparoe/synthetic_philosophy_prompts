I'm taking a small philosophy elective at my school this term, and for our unit on Victorian ethics we've spent the last couple of weeks working through parts of Sidgwick's Methods of Ethics, including the famous concluding chapter where he admits that egoism and utilitarianism can't be fully reconciled by reason alone, so that what he calls the "Cosmos of Duty" threatens to collapse into a "Chaos" unless some further premise, like a moral government of the universe, turns out to be true. In a short biographical sketch our teacher handed out for the same unit, I learned that Sidgwick spent nearly two decades as a leading figure of the Society for Psychical Research, helping found it and serving as its president more than once, personally testing mediums and reported cases of telepathy. This apparently wasn't an embarrassing hobby unrelated to his real philosophy: he more or less admitted that he had been drawn to Kant's move of simply postulating an afterlife to guarantee that virtue and happiness ultimately coincide, but that he wanted actual empirical evidence for that postulate instead of just assuming it, and psychical research was where he went looking for it.

For my term paper I want to push this connection a step further than "he did psychical research because of his ethics," which I gather is already a fairly standard thing to say about him, and I'd like your feedback before I commit to the idea. Elsewhere in the Methods, in the part he calls "Philosophical Intuitionism," Sidgwick lays out conditions a belief has to meet before it counts as genuinely self-evident, and one of them is roughly that persistent, unresolved disagreement among careful, competent people about a proposition is itself a reason to lower your confidence in it, not to dig in further. My argument is that his own psychical research should have tripped this wire quite early, since the investigations were dogged for years by exactly that kind of disagreement: outright fraud exposed in some cases, continuing skepticism from scientists outside the Society, and shifting, uncertain verdicts even within his own research circle from case to case, and yet he kept at the search for the rest of his life anyway. So my claim is that there's a real tension, maybe even something like a performative contradiction, between the careful epistemologist who wrote that chapter and the man who spent decades chasing mediums. Could you tell me: whether this is a fair reading of Sidgwick or something I'm forcing onto him; whether there's an obvious way he, or a good defender of his, could dissolve the tension that I'm not seeing; and, honestly, whether this angle is original enough to carry a full essay, rather than being a fairly obvious point that anyone who has read a decent biographical summary of him would already have made.
