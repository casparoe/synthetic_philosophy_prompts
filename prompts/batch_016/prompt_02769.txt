So I'm a few years into a math PhD (commutative algebra-ish) and I've been sitting in on a history-of-analytic-philosophy reading group for fun, currently on Russell's Introduction to Mathematical Philosophy (1919) — which means this is a solid 16 years before Zorn's actual paper existed. In the chapter on what he calls the "multiplicative axiom" (his term for one version of the axiom of choice), he gives this example:

"This is illustrated by the millionaire who bought a pair of socks whenever he bought a pair of boots, and never at any other time, and who had such a passion for buying both that at last he had ℵ₀ pairs of boots and ℵ₀ pairs of socks. The problem is: How many boots had he, and how many socks? One would naturally suppose that he had twice as many boots and twice as many socks as he had pairs of each, and that therefore he had ℵ₀ of each, since that number is not increased by doubling. [...] In our case it can be done with the boots, but not with the socks, except by some very artificial device. The reason for the difference is this: Among boots we can distinguish right and left, and therefore we can make a selection of one out of each pair, namely, we can choose all the right boots or all the left boots; but with socks no such principle of selection suggests itself, and we cannot be sure, unless we assume the multiplicative axiom, that there is any class consisting of one sock out of each pair. Hence the problem."

Two things about this are bugging me. First: I use Zorn's lemma all the time (maximal ideals, bases, algebraic closures) always in the "every chain in a poset has an upper bound, so there's a maximal element" packaging, and I can't for the life of me see a poset or a chain anywhere in the sock story — what would even be the maximal element, a really good sock? Can you actually walk through how his version of the problem and the Zorn's-lemma version are the same underlying worry? Second, and this is the one I find more interesting: Russell talks about whether the axiom is true as a totally open question, like its an empirical matter we simply don't know the answer to yet — but nobody I know in my department looses any sleep over invoking Zorn's lemma before breakfast. Did something actually get settled between 1919 and now, or did everyone just quietly agree to stop worrying, and if it's the latter, should that bother me more than it apparently bothers anyone else?
