Been down a rabbit hole for a health-policy story I'm reporting and wanted to sanity-check my reaction with someone outside the beat. I was interviewing a health economist about how NICE (the UK body that decides what the NHS will and won't pay for) makes its calls, and she walked me through the QALY system — every treatment gets scored on how many "quality-adjusted life years" it buys per pound spent, and NICE has for years used a rough cutoff around £20-30k per QALY to decide what clears the bar. Fine so far. But then she gave me an example that's been bugging me since: a one-off gene therapy for a rare, fatal childhood disease costs well over a million pounds per kid and doesnt clear the threshold, so it goes unfunded, while a cheap, marginal improvement to a common migraine drug DOES clear it, because you multiply a tiny quality-of-life bump across a few hundred thousand patients and the total QALYs comes out higher then what you'd get from saving a much smaller number of kids from an early, painful death.

When I pushed back on this she said my discomfort is a textbook case of something economists actually have a name for, "the rule of rescue" — the documented pull we feel toward an identifiable, dying person in front of us over diffuse statistical suffering — and that once you can name a gut reaction like that, you're basically supposed to discount it as a known glitch rather than treat it as insight. That logic got to me a bit, not gonna lie, but it still feels like there's something more principled under my discomfort than just a cognitive bug — like maybe small harms or benefits, spread thin enough across enough people, just aren't commensurable with one severe, concentrated harm to a few, no matter how big you make the multiplier. Then again maybe that's just sentimentality wearing a philosophy costume. Is there an actual argument for my side, or is my source right and I should just trust the math and move on?
