Years ago I read Godel Escher Bach like every other CS major and loved it, and a while back I went down a rabbit hole into more Hofstadter and found this other essay collection of his, Metamagical Themas, where he lays out the idea of "superrationality." The bit that hooked me is apparently based on a real experiment he ran in the 80s: he sent a telegram to twenty of his friends and colleagues inviting them into a one-shot, multiplayer prisoner's dilemma with actual money on the line, and his whole argument was that truly rational people (the "superrational" ones) should cooperate, because if everyone's reasoning from the same premises, everyone lands on the same answer, so you might as well pick whichever answer is best assuming everyone picks it too -- which means cooperating instead of defecting.

I brought this up with an old coworker whos way deeper into the LessWrong/rationalist stuff than I am, and we got into a whole back-and-forth about whether this is an actually sound rational argument or just a nice-sounding way to smuggle in an assumption that begs the question. His take is that its legit, and basically the same move underlying one-boxing on Newcomb's problem. My gut says standard game theory has it right, that defecting still dominates in a genuinely one-shot game against a stranger, and "we think alike so we'll choose alike" is sneaking something past us.

Could you write me an essay that actually settles this instead of just listing pros and cons on each side? Steelman both positions properly -- I think it turns on the causal-vs-evidential decision theory split somehow, and also on whether the other player has to be a literal copy of you versus just "similarly rational," though I'm not sure how much that second thing even matters -- and then commit to a real verdict on when, if ever, superrational cooperation is genuinely rational rather than just psychologically comforting. Bonus points if you can tie it back to Newcomb's problem properly, since I can tell they're related but couldn't explain how myself.
