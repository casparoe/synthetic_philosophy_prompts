I'm about two hundred pages into a novel that follows a merchant family through the slow collapse of an invented trading city-state, and I've hit one of those procrastination spirals where I start reading philosophy instead of actually drafting chapter 14. Specifically I went down a hole on the old covering-law vs. narrative-explanation debate in philosophy of history -- Hempel's 1942 essay, then some stuff responding to Danto -- because I realized I had no idea what I'm actually doing, structurally, when I write a line like "that winters grain shortage was the first sign of the collapse." My housemate, who's ABD in history, just laughed and said welcome to the semester-long seminar she had on exactly this.

Here's the bit that's stuck with me. Hempel's fallback position, when critics point out that historians almost never actually cite a law, is that what they're giving is an "explanation sketch" -- an incomplete deductive-nomological argument, with placeholders for laws and conditions that could in principle be filled in later, even if no one bothers to. But then there's Danto's whole thing about narrative sentences: descriptions like "the first sign of the collapse" or "the opening skirmish of the war" are only true of an event in light of something that hadn't happened yet when the event occurred. Danto's Ideal Chronicler, sitting there with a perfect real-time record of literally everything, still couldn't write sentences like that down at the time. And it seems like you can't have a law covering an event under a description that didn't even apply to it yet, so it's not obvious there's any law, sketched or not, just waiting to be filled in.

So: can you make the strongest case that this actually sinks Hempel's "explanation sketch" move for a big chunk of ordinary historical (and novelistic) explanation -- not just that we're missing the relevant laws, but that no amount of filling in could ever turn something like "the grain shortage was the first sign of the collapse" into a covering-law explanation? And could you actually engage with the obvious Hempelian comeback -- that the narrative-sentence business is just about which label gets attached to an event after the fact, and that underneath the label there's still some perfectly law-explicable event (a bad harvest causing hoarding causing whatever) doing the real explanatory work -- rather than just asserting that the narrativists win? Apparently this whole area gets grouped in with Dray and Mink under "narrativism" more broadly, but I'm mainly stuck on the Hempel/Danto piece specifically.
