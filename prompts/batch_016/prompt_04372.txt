I'm about 60 pages into a novel set in Turin in the autumn of 1888 — the last "sane" months before Nietzsche's collapse — and I've got a secondary character, a failed philologist, who's been copying long stretches of Ecce Homo into his own notebook and arguing with it in the margins. I want his marginalia to sound like he's actually wrestled with the text rather than just going "wow this guy was nuts," so I've been re-reading the "Why I Am So Clever" chapter myself (in the old 1911 Ludovici translation, since thats plausibly the edition my character would have had his hands on) trying to work out what I even think Nietzsche is doing in it. Theres one stretch I keep getting stuck on and can't seem to argue my way through.

Here's the bit, more or less as it appears in that translation:

"I am much more interested in another question,—a question upon which the 'salvation of humanity' depends to a far greater degree than it does upon any piece of theological curiosity: I refer to nutrition... But as to German cookery in general—what has it not got on its conscience! Soup before the meal... meat boiled to shreds, vegetables cooked with fat and flour... And, if you add thereto the absolutely bestial post-prandial drinking habits of the ancients, and not alone of the ancient Germans, you will understand where German intellect took its origin—that is to say, in sadly disordered intestines.... German intellect is indigestion; it can assimilate nothing... All prejudices take their origin in the intestines. A sedentary life, as I have already said elsewhere, is the real sin against the Holy Spirit."

My problem is I can't tell how load-bearing this is meant to be. Is Nietzsche seriously proposing that German idealism — Kant, Hegel, the whole tradition — is, like, causally and specifically a byproduct of bad digestion and stodgy cooking, in a way thats supposed to actually count against it? Or is the extremity and the jokiness the whole point — is he dramatizing his "philosophy as symptom" idea (the stuff from Beyond Good and Evil and the Genealogy about moral systems being symptoms of the physiological state of whoever dreamed them up) by picking the most undignified possible cause? And either way, how does that sit next to the fact that a few sections later in that same chapter he's asking how one becomes what one is, which is literally the book's subtitle and sounds a lot more like self-authorship than like being at the mercy of your gut and the weather? I went back and forth about this with a friend who thinks I'm overreading a man who was, let's remember, about four months from a total breakdown, but I'd like a more careful take before I decide how seriously my guy in the notebook should be taking it.
