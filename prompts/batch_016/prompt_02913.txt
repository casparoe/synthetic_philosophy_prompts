I'm about a third of the way into a novel where the conceit is that David Hume gets yanked out of 1770s Edinburgh and dropped into present day London (don't ask about the mechanics, time travel logic is always the most boring part of these books to actually explain). To get his voice right I've been reading through his economic essays instead of just relying on the greatest hits everyone remembers from undergrad (the is-ought gap, the billiard balls). Turns out he was way more sophisticated on money and trade than I expected — the price-specie-flow stuff in "Of the Balance of Trade," the line from "Of Money" about how currency is "none of the wheels of trade" but "the oil which renders the motion of the wheels more smooth and easy," and that genuinely alarming bit from "Of Public Credit" where he says flat out that either the nation must destroy public credit or public credit will destroy the nation. That last one is basically begging to be a scene where my Hume opens a newspaper to the national debt figures and just goes quiet for a page.

Here's where I need help. I want him to have a real, specific reaction to a few things that obviously didn't exist in his lifetime, not just generic "gadzooks, the future" filler dialogue: fiat currency with zero specie backing anywhere in the system, central banks conjuring money via quantitative easing, decades-long trade deficits (US-China being the obvious one) that never self-correct the way his mechanism says they should, and crypto as this weirdly stateless not-quite-commodity money. My instinct is he'd read all of this as total vindication of his fears about paper credit outrunning real wealth — but I'd rather you push back on that if it's too easy. Are there places where his own framework, built around the assumption that currency is ultimately anchored to gold or silver, just doesn't extend cleanly to a world of floating fiat and would leave him genuinely confused or torn rather than smugly right?
