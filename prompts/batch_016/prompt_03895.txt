I am a PhD student in the history of political thought, currently drafting a dissertation chapter on Bentham's unpublished writings on sexual morality, especially the "Offences Against One's Self" essay (c. 1785) and the related material now collected in the Bentham Project's volume Of Sexual Irregularities. Bentham's case for leniency toward what he calls the "irregularities of the venereal appetite" rests on two conditions jointly obtaining: the conduct is consensual and hidden from public view, and the only identifiable harm is the antipathy felt by observers, which he refuses to treat as a legitimate ground for punishment (he likens licensing disgust in this way to licensing the burning of Moors or Jews out of religious antipathy) — noting, further, that such antipathy already exacts its own extra-legal punishment on the offender.

I would like help thinking through a case that inverts both conditions at once, one Bentham plainly never anticipated: non-consensual, AI-generated sexual "deepfakes" of real people — images made and circulated precisely so that others will see them, targeting someone who never consented and who plausibly suffers genuine reputational and psychological harm even though no physical act occurs. Reasoning strictly from Bentham's own principles rather than from present-day consensus, how do you think he would have classified this practice? Would his framework condemn it cleanly, or does the case expose some instability in categories built to handle a rather different problem?
