Ok, this is a bit of a rabbit hole but I keep circling back to it instead of actually doing my comps reading. The intro-level story everyone gets taught is that Kant's ethics has no room for supererogation—you've either got a duty or you don't, full stop, so "saints and heroes" type behavior just doesn't have anywhere to live in his system. But then I actually read Thomas Hill's old Kant-Studien paper where he leans hard on the fact that imperfect duties (beneficence, developing your talents, etc) leave what Kant calls a "latitude" (latitudo) for how, how much, and on what occasions you fulfill them—so on Hill's reading, once you've done "enough" acts of beneficence to count as a compliant Kantian agent, anything further is genuinely above and beyond, even though beneficence as such is obligatory. Then I found out Marcia Baron wrote a whole response basically arguing this doesnt work—that a wide duty is just as fully binding as a narrow one, and the latitude is only ever about which occasions you pick to discharge it on, never about whether your allowed to stop short of "more." So nothing actually ends up being extra on her view, it's all just flexible compliance dressed up to look like heroism.

I get the shape of the disagreement but can't tell if there's an actual fact of the matter here, or if it's just a semantic dispute about what "required" means once requirements are this fuzzy. Could you compare the ordinary notion of supererogation against how latitude works for Kantian imperfect duties, and actually stake out a position on whether Hill's move secures a real supererogation category for Kantians, or whether Baron-style skepticism wins? It'd help if you worked through a concrete case, like a doctor who does a couple of unpaid clinic shifts a month on top of her normal job—walk through how a Hill-ish Kantian and a Baron-ish Kantian would each describe what she's doing.
