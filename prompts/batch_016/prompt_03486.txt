As best I can reconstruct it: (1) Marta opened with the claim that Voltaire's basically right, because if Leibniz genuinely thinks a perfectly good, all-knowing, all-powerful God surveyed every possible world and picked this one as the best, then logically every specific horror in it -- the earthquake, the Inquisition burning people afterward to try to stop another one, all of it -- has to be part of what makes it the best one, and dressing that up in Latin doesn't make it any less obscene then what Pangloss says out loud. (2) Owen pushed back that this isn't quite fair to the actual text: Leibniz splits evil into metaphysical (just being finite and imperfect, which anything short of God has to be), physical (suffering), and moral (sin), and Pangloss's habit of tracing each specific disaster to a tidy, one-to-one "necessary cause" is a much cruder, more mechanical claim than anything Leibniz actually argues. (3) Marta's comeback was that this is a distinction without a difference: if God chose this exact world because, all things considered, its optimal, then a world identical to ours minus the earthquake would've been strictly better while changing nothing else -- so either Leibniz has to deny that's even coherent, in which case he's stuck with exactly the "necessary" earthquake Owen's trying to spare him from, or he's got no real answer. (4) Owen replied that denying "changing nothing else" is coherent is exactly Leibniz's move -- his compossibility doctrine means the world is one giant interlocking package, every substance mirroring the whole from its own angle, so you can't subtract one earthquake and hold everything else fixed, because "everything else" was never separable from it to begin with. He admitted this doesn't prove Leibniz is right, only that Marta's "distinction without a difference" line moves faster than it should.
