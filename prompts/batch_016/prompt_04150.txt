So my book club just had a whole blow-up over this: half the group loved this new novel everyone's been raving about, and it turned out afterward that at least two of them hadn't actually finished it, they were just going on the strength of what the rest of us said plus a couple of reviews. Got me thinking about something I only vaguely remember from a philosophy elective in college, this idea that aesthetic judgements might be different from ordinary factual beliefs in a way that makes "testimony" not really work for them. Like, I can justifiably believe it's raining in Lisbon right now because my sister texted me, no need to be standing there myself. But can I justifiably believe a painting is beautiful, or a novel is genuinely good, just because someone who's taste I trust told me so, without ever actually reading/seeing it myself? Apparently theres a whole literature on this (something called the "acquaintance principle," and people talk about being an "optimist" or a "pessimist" about aesthetic testimony), and I think there's even a parallel debate about whether you can just be told what's morally right and thereby know it.

I'm actually toying with putting a version of this fight into my current novel. I've got two side characters, an old-guard theater critic and a younger one who reviews half the season off livestreams and other critics' takes, and I want their disagreement to feel like a real, live philosophical argument, not a strawman where the young one is obviously just being lazy. Could you write me a dialogue (maybe 2 pages worth) between the two of them, ideally at a bar after a press night, where the older one defends something like the acquaintance principle (you can't really know or be justified in an aesthetic verdict unless you've personally experienced the work) and the younger one pushes back with the strongest version of the optimist case? I want them actually responding to each other's best points instead of past each other, so if the acquaintance-principle character brings up why aesthetic testimony seems different from testimony about, say, whether a bridge is structurally sound, I want the other one to have a genuine comeback, and vice versa all the way through. It's fine if neither fully wins, but I'd like it to be clear by the end exactly where the two of them disagree and why it isn't just a semantic quibble.
