I'm working on a piece about the history of arguments for women's education, and I keep coming back to Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz's 1691 "Respuesta a Sor Filotea de la Cruz" — her reply to a bishop who, writing to her under a nun's pseudonym, had urged her to give up secular study for purely religious matters. Most popular accounts of the letter stop at the childhood anecdotes: learning to read at three, wanting to dress as a boy to attend university. I'm more interested in the scholastic argument she mounts later on, and one passage has me stuck. Here it is, in my own rough translation from the Spanish:

"All this calls for more learning than is possessed by some who, being mere grammarians — or, at most, armed with a handful of terms picked up from logic primers — try to interpret Scripture and cling to that 'Mulieres in Ecclesiis taceant' [let women be silent in the churches], without knowing how it is to be understood. And elsewhere it is written, 'Mulier in silentio discat' [let the woman learn in silence] — a passage more in women's favor than against them, since it commands that they learn, and while they are learning it is obviously necessary that they keep silent. And it is likewise written, 'Audi Israel, et tace' [hear, Israel, and be silent], addressed to the whole assembly of men and women together, all of whom are commanded to keep silent, because whoever is listening and learning has every reason to attend and hold their tongue. If that does not satisfy, I should like these interpreters and expositors of Saint Paul to explain to me how they understand that passage, 'Mulieres in Ecclesia taceant.' For they must take it either materially, of pulpits and lecterns, or formally, of the whole body of the faithful, which is the Church. If they take it in the first sense — which is, to my mind, its true sense, since women are in fact not permitted to read publicly in church or to preach — then why do they reproach women who study in private? And if they take it in the second sense, and want the Apostle's prohibition to hold without restriction, such that women be forbidden to write or study even in secret, then how is it we see the Church permit a Gertrude, a Teresa, a Bridget, the nun of Ágreda, and many others, to write?"

Could you help me actually work through this, rather than just summarize it? Specifically: (1) reconstruct the dilemma she poses about the "material" versus "formal" sense of "let women keep silence" — is it a genuinely valid dilemma, or is she quietly assuming what she needs to prove? (2) What argumentative work are the named women — Gertrude, Teresa, Bridget, the nun of Ágreda — actually doing, as opposed to serving as mere decoration? (3) Read closely, does this passage add up to a general claim about women's right to education, or something narrower and more strategic — and if the latter, what exactly is she conceding?
