Last month I was called for jury duty for the first time, and during deliberations on a case that came down almost entirely to one witness's account, I noticed how much weight I was instinctively prepared to give a stranger's word even without any corroborating evidence. That experience has stayed with me, and it has given some personal urgency to a debate we have just reached in an epistemology seminar I am taking this term: the dispute between "reductionists" about testimony, like Hume, who hold that our trust in what people tell us has to be grounded in our own accumulated experience of testimony proving reliable, and "anti-reductionists," like Thomas Reid, who hold that our disposition to believe testimony is basic and non-inferential, on a par with perception or memory.

To get a better handle on the anti-reductionist side, I went back to the source and read the relevant section of Reid's 1764 An Inquiry into the Human Mind on the Principles of Common Sense (the chapter "Of Seeing," Section 24, "Of the Analogy between Perception and the Credit We Give to Human Testimony"). I got stuck on this part:

"The wise and beneficent Author of nature, who intended that we should be social creatures, and that we should receive the greatest and most important part of our knowledge by the information of others, hath, for these purposes implanted in our natures two principles that tally with each other.

The first of these principles is, a propensity to speak truth, and to use the signs of language, so as to convey our real sentiments. This principle has a powerful operation, even in the greatest liars; for, where they lie once, they speak truth a hundred times. Truth is always uppermost, and is the natural issue of the mind. It requires no art or training, no inducement or temptation, but only that we yield to a natural impulse. Lying, on the contrary, is doing violence to our nature; and is never practised, even by the worst men, without some temptation.

Another original principle implanted in us by the Supreme Being, is a disposition to confide in the veracity of others, and to believe what they tell us. This is the counter part to the former; and as that may be called the principle of veracity, we shall, for want of a more proper name, call this the principle of credulity. It is unlimited in children, until they meet with instances of deceit and falsehood: and it retains a very considerable degree of strength through life."

What I cannot quite work out is how this opening move is supposed to be compatible with Reid's larger anti-reductionist project. If our trust in testimony is meant to be immediate and non-inferential — something we are entitled to without first establishing that speakers are generally reliable — why does Reid spend real argumentative effort establishing that veracity and credulity were arranged by "the wise and beneficent Author of nature" to "tally with each other"? That looks a lot like exactly the kind of background assurance of reliability a reductionist would say we need before testimony can be trusted, just relocated from an empirical track record onto a providential design plan. When I raised this with a couple of people from the seminar, one suggested that Reid is doing two different things at once: giving a first-personal account of why an ordinary hearer's belief is justified, which is supposed to be non-inferential, and separately offering a third-personal, theoretical explanation of why the faculty producing those beliefs is reliable, which is free to appeal to design without that appeal becoming part of the hearer's own reasoning. I was not fully convinced, mostly because Reid seems to treat the tallying of the two principles as doing real work in the argument itself rather than as an aside. Could you help me work through whether that two-levels reading actually holds up against the text, or whether there is a better way of understanding what this passage is contributing to Reid's case against reductionism about testimony?
