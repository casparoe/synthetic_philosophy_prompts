In my graduate seminar on the metaphysics of consciousness, we have just reached the session on the combination problem, and a disagreement broke out between two students that I would like an outside, considered opinion on before we reconvene. One student argued that most of the going strategies for handling the combination problem are not really solutions at all: each either quietly gives up something panpsychism was supposed to preserve, or simply relocates the original mystery one level over and declares victory. The other thought this was too quick, insisting that at least a couple of the proposals genuinely explain what they set out to explain. I would like your independent judgment on who has the better of this.

For background, on Chalmers's now fairly standard breakdown, the combination problem decomposes into a subject-combination problem (how micro-subjects could add up to a single macro-subject), a quality-combination problem (including the "palette" worry that there is too little qualitative variety among microqualities to ground the enormous diversity of macroqualities), and a structure-combination problem (the mismatch between, say, the fine-grained structure of neural micro-experience and the apparently unified structure of a macro-experience like a visual field). With that division in mind, please rank the following six proposals, each addressed primarily (though not exclusively) to the subject-combination problem:

(a) Goff's phenomenal bonding relation: a primitive relation among micro-subjects, posited to ground a distinct macro-subject without identifying that macro-subject with, or reducing it to, its constituents;
(b) priority cosmopsychism (Nagasawa and Wager; Shani): explanatory priority runs from a single cosmic subject down to individual subjects, which are somehow "decombined" from cosmic experience rather than built up out of micro-subjects;
(c) Coleman's panqualityism: there are no micro-subjects at all, only unsensed micro-qualities, and macro-subjectivity must instead be explained by some further mechanism that does not involve summing prior subjects;
(d) Seager's combinatorial infusion: constituent micro-experiential states do not persist as separable parts of the resulting macro-state, but are superseded by a genuinely new, fused state;
(e) identity panpsychism: macro-experience simply is identical to (some privileged pattern within) micro-experience, rather than composed out of it, at the cost of rendering the non-identical remainder of micro-experience causally otiose;
(f) non-constitutive ("emergent") panpsychism: micro-experience causes, but does not constitute or ground, macro-experience.

Please write two or three sentences on each proposal explaining whether it (i) solves the subject-combination problem, by giving a positive account of how combination actually occurs; (ii) dissolves it, by showing that the demand for such an account rests on a mistaken assumption; (iii) relocates it, by trading it for a structurally similar problem elsewhere in the system; or (iv) retreats from it, by giving up a commitment — universal subjecthood, or the rejection of brute emergence — that made panpsychism attractive as an alternative to physicalism and dualism in the first place. Then summarize your verdict as a JSON object with exactly those four keys, each mapping to a list of the letters (a)–(f) ordered from strongest to weakest instance of that category, formatted as follows:

```json
{
  "solves": [],
  "dissolves": ["a"],
  "relocates": ["b", "d"],
  "retreats": ["c", "e", "f"]
}
```

This is only an illustration of the format, not a hint about the distribution I have in mind.
