I write a column on the history of ideas, and I've spent the past few weeks re-reading David Hume's economic essays for a piece on how eighteenth-century arguments keep resurfacing, almost word for word, in today's trade debates. The idea took hold after I heard a trade official describe a persistent bilateral deficit as money the country was simply 'losing' to a rival — a framing that struck me as exactly the mercantilist instinct Hume spends "Of the Balance of Trade" dismantling through what is now called the price-specie-flow mechanism. He mocks the same instinct even more directly in "Of the Jealousy of Trade," where he insists that a trading partner's growing wealth should be welcomed rather than feared.

For the piece, I would like to imagine Hume dropped into a present-day debate over tariffs and trade deficits, and to work out what he would actually say by following the logic of his own arguments rather than simply invoking his reputation as an early free-trader. Specifically: his self-correcting story assumes a metallic currency whose supply automatically tracks the trade balance, so does that story survive more or less intact once gold flows are replaced by floating exchange rates and central-bank-managed fiat money, or does it need real revision? And how might he account for a case like the United States, where reserve-currency status seems to let large, persistent deficits go uncorrected for decades, in apparent tension with the automatic adjustment his mechanism predicts? Would he treat that as a genuine problem for his own argument, or does he have resources elsewhere in his essays — on money, or on public credit — for absorbing it? I am less interested in a general verdict on whether Hume would have liked or loathed tariffs than in seeing the actual mechanics of his economic thought put under some real pressure.
