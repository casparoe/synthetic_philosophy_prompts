I'm building a handout for a graduate seminar on interwar Cambridge philosophy (Moore, Russell, Wittgenstein, Keynes, Ramsey), and this week's session is on Ramsey — specifically that odd little "Epilogue" to The Foundations of Mathematics, which started life as a talk called "On There Being No Discussable Subject" that he gave to the Apostles in 1925, and which Braithwaite fished out of his papers and retitled after Ramsey died. Everyone who's spent time online has run into the "I don't feel the least humble before the vastness of the heavens" bit, it get's quoted approvingly all over the place as a nice bit of anti-cosmic cheerfulness, but I don't think many people actually stop and ask whether the argument in it holds up, and I want to have a go at that with my students. I mentioned this to a colleague over lunch and she thought I was overreading a throwaway bit of undergraduate-society showmanship, so now I'm second-guessing myself, which is where you come in.

Here are the notes I've drafted for the handout, still rough:

"Ramsey's refusal to be humbled by cosmic scale isn't just a temperamental tic set against Russell-style cosmic pessimism, it's continuous with his general philosophical method. His deflationary view of truth (saying it's true that p adds nothing to just asserting p) and his subjectivist account of probability as coherent degree of belief, developed partly against Keynes's older, logical and mind-independent notion of probability, both refuse to let some fact "out there," independent of human practice and attitude, do the justificatory work on their own. The meaning-of-life bit runs the same move: cosmic scale can't touch him because significance was never a further fact about the universe that a bigger telescope could confirm or disconfirm, no amount of size or duration is relevant to a question that was never really about size or duration. What's stranger, and what I actually want to spend the most time on with the class, is that Ramsey doesn't stop at plain relativism about all this (he's explicit that his own sunny attitude doesn't correspond to any fact any better than the depressed person's gloom does), but goes on to insist he still has more reason for his outlook than they have for theirs, on frankly prudential grounds: his mood is more pleasant and better suited to getting on with living. That looks like a normative criterion smuggled in to do work that his own official refusal to ground anything in the facts should rule out."

So: does that last move actually work, or is Ramsey just helping himself to a notion of "having reason" that his own view can't support? Is the throughline I'm drawing to his deflationism and subjectivism about probability real, or am I forcing unity onto pieces written for pretty different occassions (a talk to undergrad secret-society weirdos vs. papers on logic and economics)? And is my colleague right that I'm just overreading a joke? Feel free to be blunt, I'd much rather you tear into this now than have a smart 24-year-old do it to my face in week 7.
