So, I'm a retired immunologist (about 35 years at the bench, mostly vaccine trials) and lately, in retirement, I've fallen down a bit of a decision-theory rabbit hole. It started with Newcomb's problem, which my nephew showed me thinking I'd just dismiss it, and it's ended with me reading a bunch of old LessWrong posts about Wei Dai's "updateless decision theory" (UDT) and Vladimir Nesov's "counterfactual mugging" thought experiment. I got excited enough about it that I wrote a little essay connecting it too something from my own field, pre-registration of clinical trials, but I don't really have anyone around to check my reasoning against, so I was hoping you'd take a look.

Here's roughly what I wrote: "In counterfactual mugging, Omega flips a fair coin. On tails, it asks you for $100. On heads, it would have handed you $10,000 instead - but only if, on tails, it had predicted you'd hand over the $100. Standing there on the tails-branch actually being asked for the money, ordinary causal (and even evidential) reasoning says keep it: the coin has already landed, nothing you do now reaches into the heads-branch that didn't happen. But averaged over both branches before the coin was ever tossed, the policy of paying up nets $4,950 in expectation against $0 for refusing. An updateless agent picks that whole policy in advance and just executes it later without re-deriving the decision from what it's since learned - it doesn't update on finding out which branch it's in. What struck me is that this is basically what a well-run pre-registered trial is supposed to do: you lock in your primary endpoint and stopping rule before seeing any data, and then when the data comes in messy and some other endpoint would hand you a beautiful p-value, you're meant to ignore that and stick to the registered plan, because the whole point is committing to the rule ex ante rather then doing whatever looks best ex post."

What I can't settle is whether that's genuinely the same phenomenon as UDT or just a fancier name for ordinary precommitment - nobody thinks trial statisticians literally forget they've seen the data and rederive everything from a prior, they're just following a rule a journal will check them against later. So is "updatelessness" doing any real work in the analogy beyond what a garden variety binding commitment (Ulysses and the mast, say) already gives you, or is there something more genuinely decision-theoretic going on that survives that objection? I'd also happily take correction if I've mangled the counterfactual mugging setup itself, I'm working off memory of blog posts rather than the original source, and I know there's "timeless" and "functional" decision theory floating around too, which I've never quite managed to keep straight from UDT.
