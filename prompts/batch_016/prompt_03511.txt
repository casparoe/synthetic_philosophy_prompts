For a couple of years now I've kept up a hobby of amateur probabilistic forecasting with a small group of friends -- nothing official, just a shared spreadsheet where everyone puts numbers on things and we see who calibrates best over time. Somewhere in my reading around this hobby I came across Aumann's result that two honest Bayesians who start from the same beliefs can't end up in a position where it's fully out in the open that they assign different probabilities to the same event -- informally, "rational people can't agree to disagree." It's stuck with me ever since, especially after watching two people in the group, Miles and Renata, argue themselves in circles over it.

For months the two of them have each kept a running forecast on whether a mutual friend's startup will still be operating in two years, and they've swapped all their reasoning back and forth many times over -- his burn rate, her read on the founder's stubbornness, all of it -- yet their numbers refuse to converge. Last week it turned into an argument about what that even shows:

1. Miles said their failure to converge, given that each now knows exactly how the other is reasoning, has to mean one of them is making some kind of mistake -- that's the whole point of the "can't agree to disagree" result, and denying it is just refusing to follow the logic through.

2. Renata said the result only bites if the two of them share a common prior to begin with, and nothing requires that two people who lived completely different lives before this startup ever came up must have started from the same place. Rejecting a common prior isn't a dodge, she said; assuming one has to be there is the move that actually needs defending.

3. Miles replied that if "we just started from different priors" is always available as a stopping point, then the whole idea of rationality constraining disagreement becomes empty, since anyone could invoke it to justify never moving an inch toward anyone else, forever.

4. Renata answered that this is only a real worry if priors are allowed to differ for no reason at all; hers and Miles's differ because of specific, nameable facts about their earlier lives, and the question that actually matters is whether those particular origins are defensible -- not whether appealing to differing priors is legitimate in general.

I couldn't settle it myself, and I don't have the background to work through the logic on my own. Could you go through the four moves one at a time and say who has the stronger case at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner? Then, stepping back: does Miles and Renata's long-running, fully-informed disagreement actually amount to evidence that one of them is reasoning badly, or is it compatible with both of them being rational?
