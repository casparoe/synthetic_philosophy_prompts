About six weeks ago I received a terminal cancer diagnosis, and rather than simply distracting myself from it, I have been trying to reason my way toward some equanimity by returning to the ancient sources on death. Epicurus's argument in the Letter to Menoeceus strikes me as close to unanswerable: badness has to be badness for someone, and since I will not exist once I am dead, there will be no one left for my death to be bad for. Lucretius's related point — that this is no different from the eons of non-existence before my birth, which never struck me as remotely tragic — seems only to reinforce the same conclusion. I gather that the standard philosophical response, most associated with Thomas Nagel's "deprivation account," is to say that death can still be bad for me in a comparative sense, since it takes away goods I would otherwise have had, even though there is no experience of loss involved. To me, though, this looks like a semantic dodge: relabeling "bad" so that it means something purely comparative, rather than actually engaging with the premise that there has to be an existing subject for anything to be bad for. Is there a substantive reply here that I am missing, or does the view that death is bad for the one who dies persist in the literature mainly because its defenders cannot bring themselves to accept the alternative?
