So this argument happened in our group chat (a bunch of us who know each other from ACX meetups) after someone brought up the classic "could you go back and kill your own grandfather" thing, and it derailed the whole night. I can't tell who actually won and it's bugging me, so I want an outside read.

Alex started it: (1) says the grandfather paradox shows time travel to the past is just flatly logically impossible, not merely hard or unlikely -- if you could go back and prevent your own conception you'd get an honest-to-god contradiction, you'd exist and not exist, end of story. Maya pushed back: (2) that's not actually where most philosophers who've written on this land -- the standard move is that it's not that some force stops you, it's that it always just so happens, for completely mundane reasons, that you fail. Gun jams, you trip, you lose your nerve. No contradiction ever actually occurs, so nothing here rules time travel out. Alex wasn't having it: (3) says that's not a solution, it's a relabeling -- it requires reality to serve up some incredibly convenient coincidence every single time without exception, and calling that "not technically a contradiction" just dodges how weird it is. Also, doesn't it wreck free will? If the guy's genuinely free in that moment, why does the gun always jam -- shouldn't he succeed sometimes? Maya's response: (4) free will's fine, actually -- he can try anything he wants, nothing about the setup constrains his choices, he just never happens to succeed, kind of like how your free to attempt 20 coin flips in a row and go for all heads even though it won't happen. Also, "can" is just ambiguous here: relative to his own skill and the immediate situation he can kill his grandfather, relative to the complete history of the world (which includes his grandfather surviving) he can't, and both are true at once, no contradiction, just equivocation. Alex's comeback: (5) fine, clever, but if the "complete facts" sense of "can" is the one that actually governs what's really possible, then nothing that doesn't in fact happen was ever really possible in that sense either -- which just relocates the weirdness from logic into an infinite pile of suspicious coincidences, it doesn't get rid of it.

At that point everyone just moved on to arguing about something else. Could you go through this move by move -- (1) through (5) -- and say who's got the better of each individual exchange, not just who wins overall, plus flag anything substantive you think both of them are missing? Totally optional side thing, only answer if it's not a stretch: does (4)/(5) remind you at all of the Newcomb's-problem-flavored "your choice is correlated with stuff outside your causal control" reasoning that gets thrown around a lot in this community, or is that just a surface-level resemblance?
