Counterfactual theories of causation standardly allow absences to be causes: the gardener's failing to water my plant caused it to die, since it would have survived had he watered it. But this looks wildly overinclusive -- my plant would equally have survived had the Queen watered it, or a passing stranger, or virtually anyone else who in fact did nothing, yet we don't want to count all of these omissions as causes of the plant's death (this is sometimes called the problem of 'profligate omissions'). Could you explain, first, why this problem is so much more severe for omissions than for ordinary causation by production, where transference or process theories have an independent, non-counterfactual way of picking out causes (an actual transfer of some conserved quantity along a continuous process) that simply isn't available once nothing happens? And second, of the two main strategies for restricting which omissions count as genuine causes -- (a) building some notion of statistical or normative 'normality' into the causal analysis itself, so that only 'deviant' omissions count, or (b) staying fully egalitarian about which omissions are causes and relegating the gardener/Queen distinction to pragmatics -- which strikes you as more defensible, and what's the strongest objection facing whichever one you pick?
