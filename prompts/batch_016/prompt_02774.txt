I'm drafting a novel that reworks the old fable of the servant who flees to Samarra only to keep his appointment with Death there, and I want a pivotal scene, in which a character faces an equivalent dilemma, to be philosophically airtight. Decision theorists Allan Gibbard and William Harper turned this fable into a technical puzzle called 'Death in Damascus': a man meets Death, a reliable predictor of his movements, in Damascus, and must decide whether to stay or flee to Aleppo — Death will already be waiting in whichever city he chooses. What makes the case notorious is that it inverts the usual pattern: in Newcomb's problem, causal decision theory gives a stable, self-confirming recommendation, but here it is the causal theorist, not the evidentialist, who can never settle on an answer without wanting to switch cities. Write an essay that explains why the case produces this instability, weighs the main responses on offer — treating both cities as equally permissible, retreating to a randomizing strategy, or abandoning act-by-act evaluation for something like functional or updateless decision theory — and commits to a verdict, defended against the strongest objections, on what a rational person in the man's position should actually do.
