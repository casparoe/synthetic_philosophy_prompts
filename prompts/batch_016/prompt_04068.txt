I've been working through Caspar Oesterheld's writing on evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL, originally called "multiverse-wide superrationality") for a decision theory reading group, and I'd like to scrutinize one part of the case for it. As I understand the argument, it starts from the near-twin prisoner's dilemma: two agents whose decision procedures are similar enough that, under evidential decision theory (EDT), each agent's choosing to cooperate is strong evidence that the other cooperates too, even though neither can causally affect the other (the cleanest version has two agents running identical source code). ECL then adds the empirical premise that the universe or multiverse is extremely large, so causally isolated but relevantly similar agents of this kind are common; adopting a broadly cooperative, values-compromising disposition here and now is therefore evidentially tied to enormous gains realized elsewhere by agents relevantly like us, even though we can never interact with them.

One objection I have not been able to get past is modeled on the "tickle defense" that EDT's own defenders use against the smoking-lesion problem. There, the thought is that Susan's antecedent urge (the "tickle") to smoke already fixes whatever evidence is available about the lesion that supposedly causes both the urge and the cancer, so her subsequent choice to smoke adds nothing further; conditioning on the tickle screens off the correlation, and sophisticated EDT ends up recommending that she smoke after all. The same move looks available against ECL: whatever antecedent psychological facts make an agent the kind of reasoner who finds multiverse-wide cooperation compelling should be exactly what is correlated with similar facts about distant agents, so conditioning on those facts ought to screen off any further evidential import carried by the subsequent, actual choice to cooperate, leaving no residual practical reason for action that isn't already "priced in" beforehand.

Please construct the strongest version of this screening-off argument against ECL and actually defend its conclusion: that generalizing the tickle defense this way shows ECL gives agents no genuine additional practical reason to cooperate beyond what their antecedent dispositions already supply. Engage seriously with the most natural reply available to ECL's defenders -- that the correlation ECL trades on is a direct, logical correlation between decision procedures (as in the identical-source-code case), rather than a correlation mediated by an independent common cause the way the lesion is -- and explain why, despite that structural difference, the screening-off objection still succeeds. I want an actual rebuttal of that reply, not just an acknowledgment that it exists.
