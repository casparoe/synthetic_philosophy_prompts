Divine hiddenness stopped being an abstract puzzle for me this past year. While my father was dying, I prayed more sincerely than I ever had before for some unmistakable sign that God was there, and got what I can only describe as silence. Trying to make sense of that afterward, I started reading around the contemporary philosophical debate on hiddenness, including Schellenberg's argument that a perfectly loving God would ensure that no one who is capable of a relationship with him, and not resistant to having one, ends up simply not believing that God exists — which sounded uncomfortably close to my own situation. That reading eventually led me back to Pascal, who was clearly wrestling with much the same problem three centuries earlier.

I keep returning to one passage from a long fragment in the Pensées (Trotter's translation), part of an imagined address from God to humanity:

"If He had willed to overcome the obstinacy of the most hardened, He could have done so by revealing Himself so manifestly to them that they could not have doubted of the truth of His essence... It was not then right that He should appear in a manner manifestly divine, and completely capable of convincing all men; but it was also not right that He should come in so hidden a manner that He could not be known by those who should sincerely seek Him... willing to appear openly to those who seek Him with all their heart, and to be hidden from those who flee from Him with all their heart, He so regulates the knowledge of Himself that He has given signs of Himself, visible to those who seek Him, and not to those who seek Him not. There is enough light for those who only desire to see, and enough obscurity for those who have a contrary disposition."

The basic thought feels intuitive on a first read, but I'm having trouble pinning down its actual logical structure, and I'd like help reconstructing it properly. Is Pascal arguing that full revelation would be undesirable for some independent reason (something like a freedom- or merit-preserving theodicy), with this specific "calibration" of light and obscurity simply following from that? Or is he making the stronger, much less obviously defensible claim that the present mix of light and obscurity is itself precisely tuned to sort sincere seekers from everyone else? And when he distinguishes people by whether they seek God "with all their heart" or flee him "with all their heart," is he implicitly assuming exactly what Schellenberg's argument needs to deny — that apparent nonbelief is always, at bottom, a form of resistance? I'd appreciate help unpacking the argument on its own terms, and figuring out whether Pascal actually has the resources here to answer the modern hiddenness argument, or is just begging the question against it.
