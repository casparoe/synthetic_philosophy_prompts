This term I'm taking a seminar on classical Indian philosophy, and we've just spent two weeks on Nyāya epistemology and its theory of the pramāṇas, the recognized means of valid knowledge. The pramāṇa I keep getting stuck on is upamāna, usually translated "comparison" or "analogy." The stock illustration our professor gave is apparently the one everybody uses: someone who has never seen a gavaya (a kind of wild ox) is told by a forest-dweller that "a gavaya resembles a cow," and later, on actually encountering the animal and recognizing it as a gavaya on the strength of that remembered resemblance, is supposed to acquire a distinctive bit of knowledge — knowledge of the relation between the word "gavaya" and the class of things it names — that the Nyāya Sūtra insists cannot be reduced to perception, inference, or testimony on their own.

What makes this more interesting than a bit of exotic terminology is that it was clearly a live controversy rather than an uncontroversial fourth item on a list. A couple of us got into an argument in discussion section about this: the Vaiśeṣikas, and later the Buddhist epistemologists Dignāga and Dharmakīrti, denied upamāna any independent status, insisting that whatever work it seems to do can be folded into anumāna (inference) instead. Dignāga and Dharmakīrti in particular held that pratyakṣa and anumāna between them exhaust the genuine sources of knowledge, so that any further candidate is either invalid or a disguised case of one of the two.

Could you help me think this through properly rather than just reporting both positions? I'd like you to compare the Naiyāyika case for treating upamāna as a fourth, irreducible pramāṇa with the rival strategy of absorbing it into inference, focusing especially on whether the gavaya case really involves a cognitive achievement that an inference from resemblance couldn't equally deliver, or whether the Naiyāyikas are simply insisting on a separate faculty for something a sufficiently flexible account of anumāna would already cover. Please don't leave the two sides in neat symmetry — tell me which position you find more defensible overall, and justify the verdict.
