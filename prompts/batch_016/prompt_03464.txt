I spent the better part of four decades leading an experimental particle physics group before retiring, and for the last couple of years I have finally had the leisure to read philosophy properly rather than in stolen half hours between experiments. A retired colleague from the chemistry department and I have been working through Hume's first Enquiry together, and Section X, "Of Miracles," is the part we keep returning to. Part I struck me as something I recognized immediately: Hume's maxim that no testimony can establish a miracle unless its falsehood would be even more miraculous than the fact itself reads like an ancestor of the significance thresholds we relied on before ever announcing a discovery. Nobody claims a new particle on a two-sigma bump, and Hume seems to be making the analogous point about testimony weighed against the improbability of the event it purports to establish. I follow that part of the essay without much strain.

What has genuinely stumped me is a passage later in Part II, where Hume leaves off generalities and turns to an actual historical case: the reported cures at the tomb of the Jansenist deacon known as the Abbé Paris (a family name, unconnected to the city) in early eighteenth-century France. He writes: "There surely never was a greater number of miracles ascribed to one person, than those, which were lately said to have been wrought in France upon the tomb of Abbé Paris, the famous Jansenist, with whose sanctity the people were so long deluded. The curing of the sick, giving hearing to the deaf, and sight to the blind, were every where talked of as the usual effects of that holy sepulchre. But what is more extraordinary; many of the miracles were immediately proved upon the spot, before judges of unquestioned integrity, attested by witnesses of credit and distinction, in a learned age, and on the most eminent theatre that is now in the world. Nor is this all: A relation of them was published and dispersed every where; nor were the Jesuits, though a learned body, supported by the civil magistrate, and determined enemies to those opinions, in whose favour the miracles were said to have been wrought, ever able distinctly to refute or detect them. Where shall we find such a number of circumstances, agreeing to the corroboration of one fact? And what have we to oppose to such a cloud of witnesses, but the absolute impossibility or miraculous nature of the events, which they relate? And this surely, in the eyes of all reasonable people, will alone be regarded as a sufficient refutation." He then appends a long footnote piling on further particulars in the same spirit: attestation before the ecclesiastical court under the eye of Cardinal Noailles, twenty-two Paris curés petitioning an unsympathetic archbishop for a formal inquiry, the Paris lieutenant of police invested with full powers expressly to expose the fraud and coming up empty-handed, and a prominent physician who, sent to examine one of the alleged beneficiaries, could only report that the speed of her recovery was inexplicable on natural grounds.

My difficulty is this. Measured against Hume's own standard from Part I, this looks like about as strong a testimonial dossier as one could hope to assemble, certainly stronger than the evidence he is elsewhere quite content to rely on for perfectly ordinary matters of fact. Yet he dispatches the whole case in a single clause, appealing to the antecedent "impossibility or miraculous nature" of what is reported, which strikes a mind trained on the virtues of blind analysis and controls as uncomfortably close to smuggling the conclusion in as a premise. Is this a genuine weak joint in Hume's argument, the kind of thing that has drawn criticism in the secondary literature on Hume and testimony, or is there a more charitable reading of the passage on which Hume is not really trying to weigh the evidence here at all, but rather doing something else dialectically, such as daring his more credulous opponents to apply their own professed standard consistently? I would be grateful for a close reading of what work this passage is doing, and how well it can be squared with the methodology Hume lays out earlier in the same essay.
