Karl Popper initially argued that the principle of natural selection is "almost tautological," and that Darwinism, so understood, is not a testable scientific theory but a "metaphysical research programme": since fitness is standardly defined in terms of differential survival and reproduction, the claim that the fittest survive threatens to collapse into the claim that those who survive survive. Popper later published a widely discussed recantation, conceding that natural selection could after all be formulated as a testable, non-tautological theory. Independently of this reversal, philosophers of biology have argued that the propensity interpretation of fitness — which treats fitness as a probabilistic disposition to survive and reproduce, specifiable without reference to an organism's actual reproductive success — dissolves the tautology problem on its own.

Argue that Popper's original charge was substantially correct, and that neither his recantation nor the propensity interpretation succeeds in showing that the principle of natural selection itself, as distinct from particular, independently testable evolutionary hypotheses, is a genuinely falsifiable empirical claim.
