Hey -- I could use an outside opinion on something that's been dragging on in a committee I got roped into representing the philosophy department on. Our university rolled out an "early alert" algorithm last year that scores incoming students on their risk of failing out, so the advising office -- which only has capacity to give intensive support to maybe the riskiest 15% of each cohort -- knows who to prioritize. Before this, advisors just went with gut feel about who seemed shaky, which by all accounts was worse, so nobody on the committee wants to just scrap the thing entirely.

Here's the argument that's been going in circles for weeks now. The data office insists the tool is fine, because among students it flags as high-risk, roughly the same fraction actually end up struggling regardless of whether they're first-gen or not -- so, they say, a "high risk" flag means the same thing no matter who you are. But a couple of advisors did their own digging and found that among students who end up doing totally fine without any extra help, first-gen students are about twice as likely to have been flagged (and pulled into mandatory advising they apparently didn't need) as continuing-gen students are. Half the committee now thinks this proves the tool is quietly stacked against first-gen kids and needs to be rebuilt from scratch; the other half thinks the advisors are misreading their own numbers, since the data office's stats look totally clean. A couple of people keep asking the data team to just adjust the model so both complaints disapear, and the data team keeps saying, kind of vaguely, that it's "not that simple."

I don't have the technical background to referee the statistics myself, and it's a little embarrassing given that I spend half my ethics seminar gesturing at exactly this kind of dilemma in the abstract. Could you help me think through whether these two claims can actually both be true at once, whether that means somebody on the committee is just wrong, and -- if you were the philosopher in the room instead of me -- what you'd actually push us to do, given that hiring more advisors isn't on the table?
