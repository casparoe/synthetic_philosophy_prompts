While researching Kurt Gödel for a project in my math class, I came across something none of the textbooks mentioned: in a series of letters written to his mother in 1961, Gödel laid out a careful, explicitly non-mystical argument for why he believed people survive death. His reasoning was that the world is rationally ordered, that human beings are given far more capacity for growth and understanding than any single lifetime lets them use, and that reason itself therefore requires a further existence in which this unused potential is completed. He connected this to his rejection of materialism and his lifelong Platonism about mathematical truth, and in later years he also worked out a formal proof of God's existence that he showed only to a handful of trusted colleagues. Yet this is the same man who, by the end of the 1970s, had become convinced that unknown people were trying to poison him, would eat only food his wife tasted first, and ultimately starved himself to death once she was hospitalized and could no longer perform that role.

I haven't been able to decide whether these two facts about Gödel are actually connected. Please write an essay addressing whether his later, clearly pathological paranoia gives us genuine epistemic grounds to distrust the rationalist argument for an afterlife that he built years earlier, or whether holding his biography against the argument would just be a version of the genetic fallacy. Weigh the strongest considerations on each side, but don't hedge: close the essay by clearly committing to and defending a single answer.
