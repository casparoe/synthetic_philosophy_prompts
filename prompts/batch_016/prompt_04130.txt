I'm drafting a dissertation chapter on how Renaissance astrologers policed the boundaries of their own discipline, and I keep getting stuck on Kepler's peculiar reform project. In the Tertius Interveniens and De Fundamentis Astrologiae Certioribus, he dismisses the zodiacal signs, the twelve houses, and the sign-based planetary rulerships as baseless human convention, and treats the configuration at a person's birth as impressing a lasting psychological "excitation" rather than fixing a determinate fate. Yet he insists that the aspects — angular configurations between planets — act on a geometric faculty of the soul, present even in the earth itself, that resonates with certain constructible harmonic ratios much as the ear responds to musical consonance.

Write a dialogue between Kepler and a fellow astrologer who still practices strictly within the traditional Ptolemaic system (signs, houses, rulerships, fate-fixing nativities), each defending their own criterion for separating astrology's legitimate core from its superstitious excess. The traditionalist should press Kepler on why isolating certain angles by constructibility and consonance is any less arbitrary than isolating certain zodiacal degrees by convention, since both carve a continuum of possible configurations into a privileged few. Kepler should press back by asking what causal story the traditionalist can offer for signs or rulerships, given that his own admittedly strange resonance theory at least attempts one. Give both sides real technical command of the material, and have them genuinely engage each other's strongest points rather than talk past one another.
