I'm an electrician by trade with no formal background in philosophy, but for the past year I've spent my evenings teaching myself about the mind-body problem, mostly through the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy and a handful of philosophy podcasts. Lately I've gotten stuck on Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument, and I was hoping you could help me reason through something about it that doesn't sit right with me.

As I understand the argument, it runs roughly as follows: every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause (the "causal closure of the physical"), and we have good reason to reject the idea that physical effects are routinely brought about twice over by two separate, complete causes. Put those two claims together, and it looks like there is no causal work left for a mental event to do once its physical basis has already done the job, so mental causation collapses. What puzzles me is that causal closure seems to be part of the very definition of physicalism - if the physical did not form a closed causal system, you would not really be a physicalist at all. So it seems to me that Kim's argument has to be a problem for every physicalist theory of mind in exactly the same way, since they all sign up to that same starting premise. And yet, when I brought this up with a friend who studied philosophy at university, she told me that plenty of physicalists regard themselves as completely untouched by Kim's argument, and that it is really only meant to threaten one specific camp within physicalism. That struck me as a bit too convenient - if the whole argument can be built from a premise that every physicalist accepts, how could only some of them escape its conclusion? Am I missing some real distinction here, or is this just a case of certain physicalists declining to follow the argument's logic where it leads?
