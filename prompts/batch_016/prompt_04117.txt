In my epistemic game theory seminar, we covered Aumann's result that common knowledge of rationality in a finite game of perfect information (such as the centipede game) forces the backward-induction outcome, along with the classic objection -- pressed by Binmore, Stalnaker, and others -- that this premise cannot coherently survive a deviation: once a player fails to stop the game where backward induction says they should, it's unclear why the remaining players are still entitled to treat rationality as common knowledge for the rest of play. I can follow the formal argument, but I'd like a genuinely intuitive explanation of why a purely epistemic assumption is supposed to be strong enough to pin down such a specific, counterintuitive outcome, and of whether the deviation objection reveals a real incoherence in the idea of 'common knowledge of rationality' within a dynamic game, or whether it's just an artifact of how knowledge and counterfactuals happen to be formalized.
