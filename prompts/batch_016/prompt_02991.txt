I'm a senior planning to write my IB Extended Essay on panpsychism, the view that consciousness (or some primitive precursor of it) is a basic, ubiquitous feature of matter rather than something that switches on only in sufficiently complicated brains. A few of us who run our school's informal philosophy club (we don't have an actual philosophy course, so we just meet during lunch on Fridays) have spent the last couple of meetings arguing about the "combination problem," which most introductions present as the single biggest obstacle to the view: if individual particles or fields have tiny shreds of experience, how could those ever add up to one unified experience like the one I'm having right now? While reading around the topic I found that David Chalmers traces this whole problem back to a specific passage William James wrote in 1890, decades before panpsychism became a live position in analytic philosophy once more. I tracked down the original in James's Principles of Psychology, and although I can follow the broad idea, the Victorian sentence-stacking is making it hard for me to pin down exactly what the argument is, and I'd like to get it right before I build a section of my essay around it. Here is the passage, from Chapter VI, "The Mind-Stuff Theory":

"Where the elemental units are supposed to be feelings, the case is in no wise altered. Take a hundred of them, shuffle them and pack them as close together as you can (whatever that may mean); still each remains the same feeling it always was, shut in its own skin, windowless, ignorant of what the other feelings are and mean. There would be a hundred-and-first feeling there, if, when a group or series of such feelings were set up, a consciousness belonging to the group as such should emerge. And this 101st feeling would be a totally new fact; the 100 original feelings might, by a curious physical law, be a signal for its creation, when they came together; but they would have no substantial identity with it, nor it with them, and one could never deduce the one from the others, or (in any intelligible sense) say that they evolved it. Take a sentence of a dozen words, and take twelve men and tell to each one word. Then stand the men in a row or jam them in a bunch, and let each think of his word as intently as he will; nowhere will there be a consciousness of the whole sentence."

Could you help me work through this closely? First, I'd like to understand the precise logical structure of James's objection: is he denying that the "101st feeling" could be numerically identical to the hundred that preceded it, denying that it could be causally or logically deduced from them, or both, and how exactly do the "hundred feelings" and "twelve men" cases work together to make the point? Second, James is explicitly attacking "mind-dust" and what he calls "atomistic hylozoism," not defending panpsychism, so I'm confused about why contemporary panpsychists treat his argument as the founding statement of a problem their own view now has to solve, rather than as an objection to a nineteenth-century theory that nobody holds anymore. Finally, I'd be curious for your own take: does a proposed solution like Philip Goff's "phenomenal bonding" relation between micro-subjects actually defuse James's argument, or does it just give a new name to the very "101st feeling" that James thought was unintelligible?
