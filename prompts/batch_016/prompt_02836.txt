I'm drafting a novel set at the constitutional convention that follows the end of a long civil war in an invented federation I'm calling Kestria, and I've stalled on a scene I can't yet hear in my own head. Two of my delegates are arguing past midnight, on the eve of the floor vote on the new bill of rights: Imre Vass, a constitutional theorist who spent his years of exile translating Elster's work on precommitment, and Dalia Reyes, a human-rights litigator who chairs the drafting subcommittee and made her name arguing around entrenched clauses that had long outlived whatever justice they were once meant to secure. The clause under debate can be amended only by a nine-tenths majority ratified across three successive parliaments, and its supporters have taken to calling it, only half-jokingly, "the Ulysses clause." Imre wants to defend that name in earnest: a sober, deliberating constitutional majority, he thinks, is entitled to do to itself exactly what Ulysses did to himself — bind its own future hands against a danger it can already see coming. Dalia's opening line, which she has clearly used before, is lifted from Jefferson's 1789 letter to Madison: that the earth belongs to the living, not to the dead, and that a living generation can bind only itself.

My writing group's recurring complaint about the convention chapters is that they read like position papers with stage directions, so I'd like this scene to actually sound like two people who respect each other's minds and are still, after several hours of this, not agreeing. Please write the scene itself, not a summary of it: a real exchange of five or six moves on each side, in which the two of them answer what the other has just said rather than restate their opening positions. I especially want Dalia to press the specific disanalogy she is driving at — that Ulysses is a single continuous person binding his own later self, with exact knowledge of the danger and complete authority over his own release, whereas a constitution binds different people, most of them not yet born and none of them consulted, against dangers the framers can only guess at. And I want Imre to have a genuine answer to that rather than a dodge, whether by relocating what counts as the "self" that does the binding, by proposing some mechanism for telling foreseeable panic apart from legitimate future disagreement, or by giving up the strong version of the analogy for a narrower one he can still defend. Dalia, in turn, should take whatever he actually offers seriously rather than simply repeat that entrenchment is dead-hand rule. Neither should land a clean knockout or quietly concede; I haven't decided myself whether the clause should pass, and I'd like the scene to be good enough that a reader couldn't tell either.
