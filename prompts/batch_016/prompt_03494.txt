In my philosophy of logic class we spent a session on Lukasiewicz's three-valued logic, and the professor mentioned, almost as an aside, that Lukasiewicz basically got the idea from Aristotle -- that in the sea battle passage in the De Interpretatione, Aristotle had already worked out that a statement like "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is neither true nor false today, so he'd essentially discovered the third truth-value ("indeterminate") over two thousand years before Lukasiewicz gave it a formal notation. I skimmed the passage myself afterward and it does seem to say that, and Aristotle even goes on to say that "there will be a sea battle tomorrow, or there won't be" is necessarily true even though neither half is -- which seemed to me like it just confirms the picture, since thats exactly what you'd expect from OR-ing two indeterminate things together.

I've been repeating this to people as a fun bit of trivia (Aristotle invented three-valued logic, 2000 years early!) but it feels almost too clean, so I wanted to run it by you before I embarrass myself further with it. Is that basically an accurate way to describe what's happening in that chapter, or am I flattening something that's actually way more contested -- and if it really is that clean, why do people still write entire papers arguing about how to read that passage instead of just agreeing Aristotle already nailed it?
