I'm taking a seminar this term on eighteenth-century political economy, and for the last two weeks we've been working through Book IV of Smith's *Wealth of Nations*. The immediate occasion for this note is a comment a classmate made in our discussion section: she said Smith's opposition to colonial empire in Chapter 7 was "just economics, not ethics," and that struck me as too quick, though I couldn't immediately say why. For our short response paper (two pages, so I have to be disciplined about scope), I've sketched the outline below and would like your comments before I commit to a full draft.

My working thesis is that Smith's argument in Chapter 7 actually runs on two tracks that he never quite reconciles. The first is essentially a rent-seeking argument in eighteenth-century dress: the monopoly of the colony trade, and chartered companies like the East India Company above all, enrich a narrow class of merchants at the expense of the nation as a whole, through higher prices, misallocated capital, and the cost of colonial defence. This is the register of his line that founding "a great empire for the sole purpose of raising up a people of customers" is not, in fact, "a project altogether unfit for a nation of shopkeepers," but is "extremely fit for a nation whose government is influenced by shopkeepers" — which I read as a deliberate distinction between the interest of the nation and the interest of a faction that has captured its government. It's also, I think, what motivates his otherwise startling proposal, later in the same chapter, to give the American colonies representation in Parliament in proportion to what they contribute in tax revenue: a constitutional fix for what is ultimately diagnosed as a problem of misaligned incentives. The second track shows up only occasionally and is a different kind of argument altogether. When Smith turns to the natives of the East and West Indies, he says the benefits of the discovery of America and the Cape route "have been sunk and lost in the dreadful misfortunes which they have occasioned," brought about by nothing more than a temporary "superiority of force" that let Europeans "commit with impunity every sort of injustice" (he makes a similar aside in Book IV, Chapter 1). That isn't a claim about inefficient capital allocation; it reads as a much more direct moral judgment. My claim is that Smith's actual remedies — ending the monopoly, incorporating the colonies constitutionally — would resolve the first problem while leaving the second completely untouched, and that he doesn't seem to register this as a gap in his own argument.

I'd appreciate your thoughts on a few things: whether this "two tracks" reading holds up as an accurate account of the chapter, or whether I'm manufacturing a tension a more careful reading would dissolve; whether "rent-seeking" is too anachronistic a frame to be useful here, given that the term postdates Smith by two centuries; and whether there's an obvious complicating passage I should be dealing with but haven't mentioned. I'd also honestly like to know whether this reading is worth building a paper around, or whether it's a fairly well-worn point in the secondary literature that I'd just be rediscovering.
