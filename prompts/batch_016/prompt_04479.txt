Ok so I've been slowly working through Jaynes' Probability Theory: The Logic of Science (like half of LW seems to have done at some point) and I finally got to his resolution of Bertrand's chord paradox. His move is basically: since the problem never specifies the size, orientation, or position of the circle, the "ignorance" distribution over random chords has to come out invariant under rotations, scalings, and translations of the circle, and that requirement turns out to pin down a unique answer (the "random radius" method, if I'm remembering the name right). He presents this as basically rescuing the principle of indifference from being totally arbitrary, at least for problems that are "well posed" in his sense.

I brought this up with a friend whos much more on the subjectivist side of things (Savage, de Finetti, that whole camp) and she wasn't buying it at all — her point was that choosing *which* invariances count as the relevant symmetries of "the" problem is itself a substantive modeling choice, not something forced on you by pure ignorance, and that people have apparently cooked up other randomization procedures that seem just as natural but break one of the three invariances and land on a different answer. So the uniqueness result is doing a lot less work than it first looks like. She also brought up the old wine/water paradox (all you know is the ratio of wine to water is somewhere between 1/3 and 3, and different "obvious" ways of being indifferent give different probabilities for the same event) as basically the same disease in a different costume.

We never really settled it, it just fizzled out into "agree to disagree" over drinks, which is unsatisfying. Could you write me a dialogue between two characters standing in for these positions — one, an objective-Bayesian type who thinks Jaynes' invariance move genuinely rescues indifference for well-posed problems, and two, a subjectivist/skeptic who thinks it just relocates the arbitrariness one level up without eliminating it? I dont want a Simplicio situation where one of them is secretly just there to lose; they should be evenly matched, each getting to make the strongest version of their case, and actually responding to the specific thing the other one just said rather than delivering pre-packaged speeches past each other.
