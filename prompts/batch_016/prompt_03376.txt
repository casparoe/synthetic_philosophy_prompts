I'm currently drafting a novel centered on two theoretical cosmologists — former doctoral collaborators turned fierce professional rivals — who each champion a different candidate theory about the very early universe. One theory implies a cosmos with a merely astronomical number of observers; the other, reached from a slightly different set of symmetry assumptions, implies a cosmos so much larger that its observers would outnumber the first theory's by many orders of magnitude. Midway through the book, at a conference dinner, their scientific rivalry curdles into something more personal, because their real disagreement turns out not to be about the physics at all, but about how to reason anthropically once the physics runs out of ammunition. One of them accepts the Self-Sampling Assumption and is willing to live with the Doomsday Argument's grim implications for how long humanity has left; the other holds the Self-Indication Assumption and keeps being accused of being Bostrom's presumptuous philosopher in disguise, blithely inferring the size of the universe from an armchair. (The premise, I'll admit, was partly inspired by watching two cosmologist friends of mine nearly come to blows over this exact question at a wedding reception.)

Before I attempt to write the scene myself, I'd like to see the argument actually played out by someone who can hold both positions at full strength, since the popular treatments I've read tend to flatten one side into a punchline. Could you write the dialogue between the two of them? Invent names, staging, and any small stage business you like — a hotel bar after the dinner is the mental picture I have. I want each of them to be able to state the strongest form of her own view and to press the other on its real cost: the self-sampler forced to explain why her own birth rank should tell her anything at all about the far future, the self-indicator forced either to bite the presumptuous-philosopher bullet honestly or to explain precisely why it isn't the reductio it looks like. Ideally, somewhere in the exchange, a fine-grained distinction — about reference classes, say, or about what exactly counts as an "observer" — genuinely shifts the ground under one of them, rather than the two of them simply restating their priors at each other for three pages.
