My friend and I spent much of last night arguing about what Bell's theorem actually forces us to give up, and neither of us convinced the other. She insisted that any theory reproducing the quantum-mechanical predictions must be genuinely nonlocal — that Bell's theorem amounts to a proof that some influence propagates outside the light cone, whatever interpretation you favor. I argued the Everettian line instead: that Bell's proof presupposes measurements have a single, determinate outcome, and once that assumption is dropped — as many-worlds drops it for entirely independent reasons — the theorem no longer applies, so a fully local theory remains available. She wasn't convinced, and suspected I was just relocating the nonlocality into the structure of the universal wavefunction rather than eliminating it.

Could you write the dialogue we ought to have had, between a nonlocality theorist and a local Everettian, in which each actually engages the other's strongest move? I want the nonlocality theorist to press on how the Everettian explains why measurement outcomes on the two wings end up correlated in just the pattern quantum mechanics predicts, without covertly relying on some nonlocal or holistic structure — and I want the Everettian to give a substantive answer, not just a restatement of "Bell's proof assumes unique outcomes." Feel free to draw on the distinction, due to Jarrett and Shimony, between parameter independence and outcome independence, if it helps pin down exactly where the disagreement bites.
