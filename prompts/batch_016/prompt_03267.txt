I'm several drafts into a locked-room mystery in which the victim's death is designed, from the outset, to be causally overdetermined: the coroner's report will eventually show that the poison slipped into his nightcap was, entirely on its own, sufficient to kill him within the hour, and that the blow to the skull he received shortly afterward, delivered independently by a second and unconnected party, would have killed him regardless. Neither culprit is aware of the other, and part of the pleasure I want the reader to have is discovering that the detective's search for "the" cause of death was misconceived from the start. While I was trying to work out how to make this device feel earned rather than gimmicky, an old friend of mine who is finishing a doctorate in metaphysics pointed out, with some amusement, that my plot is a fairly clean illustration of "overdetermination," and that a very different-looking version of the same puzzle shows up in her own subfield, under the heading of metaphysical grounding: the relation people have in mind when they say some fact obtains "in virtue of" some other, more fundamental fact or facts.

She mentioned that most grounding theorists accept, in principle, that a single fact needn't have a unique full ground, but that the standard illustration is a fairly toothless one: a true disjunction such as "the vase is chipped or it is cracked" is fully grounded by either true disjunct on its own, and once that's explained it feels less like two independent grounds and more like two descriptions of the same situation, closer to a semantic quirk than to my two unwitting killers. That contrast has stuck with me, and I'd like your help exploring it properly, since she was too busy with her dissertation to indulge me further.

Could you construct a genuinely different example: a single, ordinary-seeming fact that is fully grounded twice over by two sets of more fundamental facts that are metaphysically independent of one another in something closer to the way my poisoner and my assailant are independent of each other, rather than by two logically related descriptions of one and the same state of affairs? I'd like the example to survive the obvious objections — that it secretly reduces to two distinct grounded facts wearing a single description, or that it's simply my murder plot re-described in the timeless idiom of grounding rather than the tensed idiom of causation. Once you have the example, I'd be grateful if you could spell out exactly why a case like this is awkward for the idea that grounding claims are supposed to function as real metaphysical explanations, and whether grounding theorists have any principled way of ruling such cases out, short of simply stipulating that grounds must be unique.
