I recently read Tolstoy's essay "What Is Art?" (completed in 1897) for the first time — I'd previously known him only as a novelist — after seeing it invoked in a LessWrong subthread about the flood of AI-generated content now clogging the internet. It turned out to be a much more specific and interesting argument than the popular caricature of "Tolstoy denounced fancy art in favor of simple peasant stories." His actual position is that art is the deliberate activity of evoking a feeling in oneself and then transmitting it to others, through external signs such as words, sound, or color, so that they are "infected" by that same feeling. The strength of the infection, and hence a work's merit as art, depends on the individuality of the feeling conveyed, the clarity of its transmission, and, above all, the sincerity of the artist — the sheer force with which he actually felt what he is transmitting. Crucially, Tolstoy also thought ordinary, un-theorized people were the best judges of whether real infection had occurred, since he believed the taste of the leisured and educated classes had been corrupted by professional criticism and by the habit of praising work because it was fashionable rather than because it was genuinely felt. This combination of views is what lets him dismiss Beethoven's Ninth Symphony, Wagner, and much of Shakespeare as false or inferior art, praise Dickens and Uncle Tom's Cabin instead, and, notoriously, disown most of his own fiction — including War and Peace and Anna Karenina — as bad art by his own later standard. It's also, incidentally, close to the mirror image of the costly-signaling accounts of art (Geoffrey Miller, Robin Hanson) that get a lot of airtime in these circles: for Tolstoy, sincere felt emotion is the whole point of art, not a status marker layered on top of it.

None of this was written with anything like a computer in mind, so I'd like your best reconstruction of how Tolstoy's actual arguments — not the popular gloss on them — would bear on two cases he never had to confront. First, generative AI models now produce images, music, and prose that plenty of ordinary viewers, with no particular stake in art-world fashions, report finding genuinely moving, even though there is no felt emotion anywhere upstream of the output. Second, engagement-optimized social media feeds are arguably the most "infectious" media ever produced, in the literal, measurable sense of that word, yet they are shaped by A/B-tested metrics rather than by any individual's lived experience.

Given that Tolstoy explicitly treats ordinary people's reactions as strong evidence of real infection, would his own criteria commit him to conceding that AI-generated content can be genuine art, or does his notion of sincerity block that conclusion regardless of audience response? And more generally: do you think sincerity is doing independent normative work in his theory, or is it something he cares about only instrumentally, as a reliable cause of infectiousness? Those two readings seem to me to point toward fairly different verdicts on both machine-made and algorithm-mediated art.
