I'm developing a character for a novel who is modeled closely on the philosopher G.E.M. Anscombe, and I need to write a scene in which she reacts, in her own idiom, to a case she never had occasion to address: the American strikes of June 2025 (Operation Midnight Hammer) on Iran's Fordow, Natanz, and Isfahan nuclear sites. I don't want a generic "philosopher reacts to the news" voice, so I'd like help reasoning through her actual position first.

In "Mr Truman's Degree," Anscombe's charge against Truman was not that Hiroshima and Nagasaki were disproportionate, but that they were murder in the strictest sense: the civilian deaths were the chosen means of breaking Japan's will to fight, not a foreseen side effect of hitting some other target, and no bookkeeping about net lives saved could change that. Her wider work supplies the machinery behind this verdict — the intention/foresight distinction at the heart of double effect, and a technical sense of "innocent" (roughly "not engaged in harming," rather than "morally blameless") that lets her separate who may permissibly be killed from any judgment of personal guilt.

The 2025 strikes used bunker-busting munitions against hardened, largely underground enrichment and conversion facilities rather than cities, with the declared goal of setting back a weapons program rather than killing anyone — though such sites are never simply empty of people, and harm to staff or nearby residents was foreseeable to planners even where it was minimized in practice.

Working strictly from Anscombe's own apparatus: does the intention/foresight distinction let double effect succeed here in a way she denied it could for Truman, or is there a redescription of the strikes under which both cases collapse into the same verdict? And does the sincerity of the declared aim even settle the matter for her, or would she insist, as she did against Truman's defenders, on asking what was actually being chosen, independent of what was officially declared?
