I'm a novelist, and I recently finished working through *Middlemarch* with a small reading group I've belonged to for years. Afterward, two of the members, Owen and Nadia, fell into an argument over coffee that I haven't been able to let go of, partly because it bears on a decision I need to make in my own work-in-progress, which follows a woman who leaves an evangelical community in early adulthood and spends the rest of the book looking for something to put in its place. Underneath their argument was really a question about George Eliot's own settled view: whether she thought the kind of spiritual ardor that used to find its outlet in religious vocation could be adequately rehoused in ordinary secular sympathy and duty, or whether she thought the modern world had simply closed off the conditions under which that ardor could ever be fully realized again.

Owen's opening move was that the ending of the novel is a genuine, if understated, vindication: Dorothea's marriage to Will and the unrecorded good she goes on to do are a real and sufficient channel for the same energy that made the historical Saint Theresa a reformer, and that the Prelude's description of latter-day Theresas left "foundress of nothing" names a type Dorothea is finally allowed to escape, not a description that still applies to her. Nadia answered that this gets the book's architecture backward: both the Prelude and the Finale go out of their way to stress how narrow and ill-fitting Dorothea's circumstances were for a nature of her scale, so the diffuse, untraceable good she ends up doing reads as compensation for a thwarted life, not as evidence that nothing was thwarted. Owen came back by pointing to how the narrator generalizes at the very end, tying Dorothea's quiet effect on those around her to a claim about how any good at all enters the world -- which, he argued, scales everyone up to her level rather than scaling her down to a consolation prize. Nadia's rejoinder was that this move can't do double duty: if that closing claim is simply a universal law about how goodness works for everybody, it can no longer also serve as the special proof Owen needs that Dorothea specifically got the full version of the life she was suited for, rather than a diminished one dressed up in fine language.

Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who has the better argument at each stage, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also be glad to know what you think both of them are missing about how Eliot actually treats displaced religious longing in this novel, since I'd like a clearer view of her position before deciding how my own character's later, quieter life should read on the page.
