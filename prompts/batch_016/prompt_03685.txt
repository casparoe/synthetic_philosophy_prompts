So I just got certified as a lifeguard, and during in-service training last week our instructor threw a scenario at us: you're the only guard on duty, backup is a good ten minutes out, and you suddenly get two problems at once — a single swimmer struggling off the north dock, and a group of four caught in the same rip current off the point, both about the same distance from your chair. You can only get to one group before conditions make going for the second too dangerous to try. He said he wasn't handing us a right answer, he just wanted us arguing about it, and two people in my cohort, Nadia and Colin, kept going well after class ended, first in the parking lot and then over food. It's stuck with me since I'll actually be solo on the stand some evenings this summer.

Colin's first move was that you obviously swim for the four, since four lives outweigh one, and saying otherwise means treating the lone guy's life as worth more than four other people's put together, which he called indefensible. Nadia said "outweigh" is doing a lot of unearned work there — no single person actually experiences "losing four," each of the four only stands to lose their own one life, same as the lone swimmer loses his, so treating the bigger group as a bigger loss assumes the very thing that needs proving. She said if the two truly can't both be saved, giving each side an equal shot, even a literal coin toss, respects that everyone's claim to be rescued is just as serious as everyone else's, instead of writing the minority off the second they're outnumbered. Colin shot back that this proves too much: if numbers never get to matter, saving one person is exactly as good as saving a hundred, since its always "one life versus one life" from each person's own point of view no matter how big the other group gets, and that can't be right. Nadia answered that she wasn't saying numbers never matter, only that they shouldn't get to automatically decide it before anyone's asked whether that's fair to the minority, and that a coin flip isn't some empty gesture, it's the only option that doesn't just cancel the one guy out. Colin closed with: in a real emergency, "giving everyone a fair shot" just means more total people end up dead for nothing, and calling that fairness instead of a cost doesn't make it one. Nobody budged, and our instructor just laughed when I told him about it later.

Could you go through this move by move and say who's got the stronger case at each point, not just who wins overall? I'm especially stuck on whether Nadia's coin-flip idea actually holds up or whether it's just a nice-sounding way of dodging the math, and whether there's some consideration neither of them raised that should actually settle it.
