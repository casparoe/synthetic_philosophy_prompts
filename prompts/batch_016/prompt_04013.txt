ok so this is a weird one but my grandma died a few weeks ago, and while we were clearing out her house i found her mother's old Book of Common Prayer (like from the 1930s), with the Thirty-Nine Articles printed in the back. Article XIV completely stopped me:

"Voluntary works, besides, over and above God's commandments, which they call Works of Supererogation, cannot be taught without arrogancy and impiety: for by them men do declare, that they do not only render unto God as much as they are bound to do, but that they do more for his sake than of bounden duty is required: whereas Christ saith plainly, When ye have done all that are commanded to you, say, We are unprofitable servants."

the reason this stopped me is i took an ethics class last year where "supererogatory acts" (good but not obligatory, above-and-beyond stuff — kidney donations, jumping on a grenade, whatever) were treated like this obviously great, uncontroversial category, basically the heroic/saintly part of morality. but this 16th century document is calling that exact idea arrogant and borderline blasphemous. can you actually walk me through the logic here, not just a modern-english translation? like why did the reformers think claiming to do "more than required" is impious instead of admirable, what's the theological move that makes that follow, and does it actually pose a real problem for how philosophers use "supererogatory" today, or is it just apples-to-oranges, like they're using "required" in two totaly seperate senses? been chewing on it since the funeral and can't untangle it myself.
