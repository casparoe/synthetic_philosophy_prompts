He was so scared of the backlash that he never published it in his lifetime, and it didn't actually get printed until almost two hundred years later. He also kept coming back to the topic on and off for the rest of his life, writing more about it in some other unpublished manuscripts from the 1810s.

I read through some summaries/excerpts of his actual arguments (his 18th century prose is rough, so I'm paraphrasing) and wrote out the ones I could find:

1. It's fully consensual and produces pleasure, not pain - so there's no "primary mischief" (his actual term), meaning no real harm to anyone
2. Calling it "unnatural" doesnt really mean anything, since all "unnatural" means is "uncommon," and rarity alone isn't a reason to ban something
3. Ancient Greece and Rome treated it as totally normal, so it's clearly not some freak aberration of human nature
4. The punishment (death) is wildly disproportionate to whatever harm is supposedly being caused
5. Adultery causes way more actual damage (illegitimate kids, etc) but gets punished way less severely, so the law is just inconsistent
6. People are simply disgusted by it, and mere disgust ("antipathy") isn't a legitimate basis for a law, since then the government could criminalize literally anything unpopular (his own example is heresy)
7. Discouraging procreative sex is actually good for society, since Bentham was very worried (very Malthus-brained) about overpopulation
