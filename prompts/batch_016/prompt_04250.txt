Random one inspired by a rabbit hole I fell into this week. I'm sketching out a column on all the resurgent "trade deficits are basically theft" rhetoric you hear in the tariff debates lately, and my editors note back was basically "go read some Hume, pal, this is 270-year-old news." So I've been slogging through through his 1752 essay "Of Money," and I hit a passage that's genuinely messing with my head.

Earlier in the essay Hume comes across as a hardcore money-neutrality guy — at one point he says a country having more coin in circulation is basically like keeping your books in clunky Roman numerals instead of Arabic ones: more digits needed, same underlying reality, no real effect either way. Fine, tracks with the "money is just a veil" idea I already sort of knew. But then, literally in the very next paragraph, he writes this (quoting the actual old text, weird spellings and all): "To account, then, for this phenomenon, we must consider, that though the high price of commodities be a necessary consequence of the encrease of gold and silver, yet it follows not immediately upon that encrease; but some time is required before the money circulates through the whole state, and makes its effect be felt on all ranks of people. At first, no alteration is perceived; by degrees the price rises, first of one commodity, then of another; till the whole at last reaches a just proportion with the new quantity of specie which is in the kingdom. In my opinion, it is only in this interval or intermediate situation, between the acquisition of money and rise of prices, that the encreasing quantity of gold and silver is favourable to industry." He then spends the rest of the paragraph tracing how gold coming in from, say, a shipment of goods sold off in Cadiz trickles down from merchants to workers to farmers, making everybody busier and a bit richer, all before prices actually catch up to the new money.

So now I'm stuck on this: is that just a flat contradiction, or is Hume quietly drawing some distinction — level versus rate of change, short run versus long run, something about who gets the new money first — that actually reconciles "money doesn't matter" with "money briefly matters a lot"? And is this the actual passage people have in mind when they credit Hume as some kind of proto-monetarist or forerunner of sticky-price macro, or is that reading 20th century economics backward onto an old essay that doesn't quite support it? I'd love a proper close reading of the logic here rather than a highlight-reel summary of the essay.
