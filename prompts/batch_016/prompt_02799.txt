A friend of mine who's doing a history PhD sent me down a total rabbit hole last week. Apparently Jeremy Bentham -- yes, THE greatest-happiness-principle utilitarianism guy -- secretly wrote a ~60 page manuscript around 1785 arguing that England should decriminalize sodomy (a hanging offense back then). He goes through all the standard arguments of his era for why it should stay illegal -- weakens men, threatens population growth, whatever Montesquieu and Voltaire were claiming -- and picks them apart one by one on pretty rigorous utilitarian grounds, citing ancient Greece and Rome and everything. Wild stuff to be writing in the 1780s, tbh. Thing is, he was so scared of the backlash that he never published it -- it just sat in his papers until a piece finally got printed in 1931, and the fuller manuscript wasn't transcribed until a historian named Louis Crompton did it for an academic journal in the late 70s. He apparently returned to the same theme decades later too (something like 1814-17), explicitly framing it as defending "the principle of utility" against what he called "the principle of antipathy" (and "the principle of asceticism" while he was at it).

Here's the bit thats been stuck in my head though, now that I've read past the "huh, neat, gay rights guy in 1785" headline. Benthams whole argument basically boils down to: the real reason people want this punished is "antipathy" (i.e. disgust), and disgust isn't a legitimate basis for law, because the disgusted person isn't actually harmed by something happening privately between consenting adults somewhere else. That tracks with his general theory too -- in his big book on morals and legislation, he explicitly throws out "the principle of sympathy and antipathy" as a fake rival to the principle of utility, basically accusing anyone who uses it of just dressing up their own unexamined gut feelings as moral law. Fine so far. But isn't disgust still a real, felt pain sitting in someone's head? If Bentham's whole system is "sum up all the pleasure and pain, no matter whose it is, and that's what determines right and wrong," and a big chunk of 1780s England is genuinely distressed by the mere thought that this is happening somewhere, doesn't a strict hedonic calculus have to count that as a cost, same as any other pain? It feels like he's hardcoding a special-case exception into the system instead of deriving it from the same rules as everything else, which is suspiciously close to the exact move he mocks the sympathy-and-antipathy crowd for making.

Could you write me an essay that actually digs into this tension instead of hand-waving past it? Lay out the strongest version of the argument that Bentham is right to exclude aggregate disgust from the felicific calculus (there's presumably some principled way to do this -- maybe something about which preferences count as legitimate "input" in the first place, or a distinction between different orders of pleasure and pain -- I'm just a backend dev, not a philosopher, so I don't know the technical term for it), and then the strongest version of the argument that a consistent hedonistic utilitarian genuinely can't exclude it and has to just bite the bullet. Then actually pick a winner, or at minimum tell me the precise conditions under which Bentham's move works versus when it collapses. I don't want a "well, both sides have a point" non-answer -- I want an actual position, defended.
