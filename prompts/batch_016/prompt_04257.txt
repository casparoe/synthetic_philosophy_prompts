ok so im a stats PhD student and this came up in kind of an annoying way in our Bayesian foundations reading group last week, and now I can't let it go. We were talking about reference priors and somebody brought up Bertrand's chord paradox as the standard cautionary tale against "uniform ignorance priors" on continuous spaces -- you know, the fact that "pick a random chord of a circle, what's the probability it's longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle" gives you 1/3, 1/2, or 1/4 depending on whether you randomize over the endpoints, the radius, or the midpoint, even though each method feels like a perfectly innocent application of the principle of indifference (POI). My advisor's line is that Jaynes basically closed this out in "The Well-Posed Problem" (1973): the physical setup people actually have in mind (think: tossing straws onto the circle from far away) picks out a specific invariance group -- rotation, translation, and scale -- and only the "random radius" method (p = 1/2) respects all three, which also happens to be the one that matched real straw-tossing experiments. So on her view POI is basically vindicated, you just have to be careful about specifying what exactly you're being indifferent over.

I'm not so sure though, mostly because of two things I read afterwards. One is van Fraassen's "cube factory" version of the same problem (a factory makes cubes with side length somewhere between 0 and 1 foot, and being indifferent over side length vs. surface area vs. volume gives you different distributions), which suggests this isn't just a one-off quirk of circles and chords. The other is Shackel's 2007 Phil of Science paper, which -- if I'm remembering it right -- argues that neither Jaynes's invariance move nor Marinoff's earlier "the original problem was just ambiguous, and each disambiguation is fine on its own terms" diagnosis actually rescues POI as a general, content-having principle, since "invariance under whichever group happens to be physically salient" is itself extra information that mere ignorance doesn't hand you for free. That seems like kind of a big deal and not just a fun logic puzzle, since a good chunk of objective/reference Bayesian practice (Jeffreys priors, MaxEnt priors, etc) leans on some version of "symmetric ignorance determines a canonical prior."

So here's what I actually want: an essay that takes a real side instead of both-sidesing it into the ground. Does Jaynes's resolution of Bertrand's paradox rescue the principle of indifference as a genuine, free-standing requirement of rationality for continuous parameter spaces, or does it just push the problem up a level -- to justifying the invariance group -- in which case POI was never really doing any of the work? Go through the strongest version of both my advisor's reading and the skeptical one, actually engage with the van Fraassen and Shackel-style objections instead of just gesturing at them, and then commit to a verdict. A conditional verdict is fine ("POI is rescued exactly when ___, and not otherwise") as long as it's a specific, defensible thesis and not a shrug.
