For the past few weeks, my AP Computer Science Principles class has been working through a unit on the societal impact of algorithms, and this week our teacher gave us a case study modeled loosely on a real-world controversy involving a criminal-justice risk-assessment tool. The tool assigns defendants a numerical score estimating how likely they are to reoffend, which judges can factor into bail and sentencing decisions. The company that built it defends its fairness by pointing out that, among all defendants who receive the same score, the actual reoffending rate ends up essentially identical across racial groups — so a score of, say, 70 percent means the same thing no matter who you are. A civil-rights organization disputes this, arguing that among defendants who never reoffended, one racial group was considerably more likely to have been wrongly scored as high-risk, while among those who did reoffend, the other group was considerably more likely to have been wrongly scored as low-risk.

Our teacher then had us try to design our own simplified scoring rule on a toy dataset to resolve the disagreement between the company and the organization, and no matter how my table group adjusted the thresholds, fixing one side of the complaint always broke the other — unless the two groups happened to reoffend at exactly the same underlying rate in our data, which felt more like sidestepping the problem than actually solving it. A classmate insists this only shows that our particular model was too crude, and that a large enough tech company could engineer its way around the issue given enough data and computing power. I suspect he's missing something more fundamental, but I can't quite articulate why. Is there a principled, provable reason this tension can never fully be engineered away, and if so, does it mean that one of these two notions of fairness deserves more weight than the other, or does it mean that neither one is really capturing what we ought to mean by calling an algorithm "fair"?
