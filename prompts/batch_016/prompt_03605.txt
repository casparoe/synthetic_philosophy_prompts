I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums since the Overcoming Bias days, and a few months ago I picked up some freelance work doing RLHF-style preference labeling for one of the frontier labs. The job is simple enough: I'm shown two model completions to the same prompt and asked to mark which one is "better" along a few axes (helpfulness, harmlessness, and so on), and these judgments eventually get distilled into the reward model that the policy is optimized against. The onboarding materials are quite explicit that, for training purposes, this is simply what "better" amounts to — not some independent quality the completions possess on their own, but whatever raters like me converge on once we've internalized the guidelines.

That framing never bothered me until a friend of mine, also a longtime lurker on the alignment-adjacent parts of the internet, pushed on it over dinner. She granted that Goodhart-style failures — reward hacking, sycophancy, raters getting fooled by confident-sounding nonsense — are real and well-documented practical problems, but said she was after something more basic: if "good response" just means "whatever raters converge on," then a claim like "the raters were systematically mistaken about which response was better" seems to stop making sense, since there is no longer any independent fact for them to have gotten wrong. That conclusion seems clearly false, though, since we obviously want to allow that an entire rater pool could be fooled. So, she pressed, perhaps "better" has to name something the ratings are aiming at rather than something they constitute, which would make the ratings merely fallible evidence of quality rather than its definition. I tried to defuse this by noting that the guidelines are built precisely to approximate some idealized, fully-informed version of human judgment, hoping the idealization would do the needed work, but she pointed out that this just relocates the same question one level up: is a completion good because idealized judgment would endorse it, or would idealized judgment endorse it because the completion is good?

We went back and forth for the rest of the evening without really landing anywhere, and this does not feel to me like a merely verbal dispute: the answer seems to bear on whether the entire training setup is aiming at something coherent even in principle, rather than being a well-engineered proxy with no real target standing behind it. I would be curious how you would adjudicate the disagreement, and, more importantly, whether there is a genuine way of resolving it rather than simply choosing which side to stand on.
