I have been working through some of George Eliot's early journalism on my own, with no formal training in philosophy or literary criticism, and I have gotten stuck on a passage from her 1855 Westminster Review essay "Evangelical Teaching: Dr. Cumming," a sharply critical piece about a popular preacher of the day. Earlier in the essay she insists that she is not going to argue about doctrine at all, only about what she calls the "moral spirit and tendencies" of Cumming's teaching, approaching him, in her own words, "simply as spectators." Then she writes this:

"The idea of God is really moral in its influence—it really cherishes all that is best and loveliest in man—only when God is contemplated as sympathizing with the pure elements of human feeling, as possessing infinitely all those attributes which we recognize to be moral in humanity. In this light, the idea of God and the sense of His presence intensify all noble feeling, and encourage all noble effort, on the same principle that human sympathy is found a source of strength [...] But Dr. Cumming's God is the very opposite of all this: he is a God who instead of sharing and aiding our human sympathies, is directly in collision with them; who instead of strengthening the bond between man and man, by encouraging the sense that they are both alike the objects of His love and care, thrusts himself between them and forbids them to feel for each other except as they have relation to Him."

What I cannot work out is how this squares with her claim to be neutral on doctrine. The distinction she draws between a "moral" idea of God and Cumming's "immoral" one only seems to make sense if you have already granted a fairly specific, substantive theory of what belief in God actually amounts to — namely, that God is at bottom a magnified projection of human sympathy, so that a good God just is an idealized human being extended without limit. That looks like a real theological commitment, not a neutral moral observation about someone else's theology. It is also hard to ignore that she had published her translation of Feuerbach's "The Essence of Christianity," which argues something very close to that, only a year earlier. So is she quietly importing a Feuerbachian theory of religion under cover of a purely moral critique, or is there a way of reading this passage on its own terms that does not already presuppose that theory? I would like to actually understand how the argument is supposed to work here, rather than just get a general summary of her religious views.
