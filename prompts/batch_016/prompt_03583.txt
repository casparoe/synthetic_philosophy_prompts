I'm revising the knowledge unit of my epistemology seminar, and this term I want to push back against a habit I keep seeing in student papers -- and, increasingly, in the chatbot summaries some of them consult when they're stuck -- namely, treating "the Gettier problem" as a single generic template (a justified true belief with some bad luck thrown in) illustrated indifferently by the stopped clock, fake-barn country, or whichever example comes to mind first, as though these were interchangeable stand-ins for what Gettier actually argued. Below is a short passage I've drafted for the coursepack. It tries to recover the specific machinery of Gettier's three-page 1963 paper before showing how a later case complicates the tidy repair his earliest critic proposed. I'd like your comments before students see it.

Draft passage: "Gettier's target is narrower than it's usually presented. He begins by granting his opponents two assumptions central to the traditional analysis: that justification is fallible (a justified belief can still be false), and that justification is closed under known entailment -- if S is justified in believing P, recognizes that P entails Q, and infers Q on that basis, then S is justified in believing Q. Both of his cases exploit this closure principle directly, rather than turning on perception or testimony misfiring in some looser sense. In Case I, Smith is justified in believing the conjunction 'Jones will get the job, and Jones has ten coins in his pocket,' and from it deduces 'the man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket.' The conjunction is false, since Smith himself gets the job, but the deduced conclusion is true anyway, purely because Smith happens to have ten coins in his own pocket -- a fact he doesn't know and that plays no role in his reasoning. In Case II, Smith is justified in believing 'Jones owns a Ford,' and from it derives, by simple disjunction introduction, 'Jones owns a Ford, or Brown is in Barcelona.' Jones, it turns out, owns no Ford at all, yet Brown really is in Barcelona, by sheer coincidence. In both cases, then, a false belief is doing the justificatory work -- which is exactly why the earliest published reply, Clark's 1963 proposal that knowledge is justified true belief that does not rest on any false step along the way (later writers call this the 'no false lemmas' or 'no false grounds' requirement), disposes of both cases in a single stroke. What it does not handle is Goldman's barn-façade case from over a decade later: Henry, looking straight at the one genuine barn in a landscape dotted with convincing façades, forms a true, justified, entirely non-inferential belief that he sees a barn, and is still denied knowledge, with no false belief anywhere in the chain for the repair to rule out. So when introductory treatments reach for the stopped clock or the fake barn to illustrate 'the' Gettier problem, they are usually reaching for cases doing a different kind of philosophical work than Gettier's own -- and, in the clock's case (Russell's, predating Gettier by fifteen years), one that was never built out of his closure-and-fallibilism argument to begin with."

I'd welcome your reactions on three things in particular. First, accuracy: have I represented the two original cases correctly, and is it actually right that they share a common false-lemma structure that Goldman's case lacks, or am I smoothing over some disanalogy between Case I and Case II themselves? Second, is the contrast I'm drawing between Gettier's "logical" cases and the more naturalistic later ones principled, or overstated -- couldn't someone reconstruct an implicit false lemma in the clock case too (something like 'this clock is currently running'), which would put it closer to Gettier's own examples than I'm suggesting, and further from the barn case? Third, I'm undecided about whether to append a closing paragraph on Zagzebski's argument that any analysis of knowledge as justified true belief plus a further fallible condition remains Gettierizable in principle: does that generalization actually go through, and would presenting it at this point clarify the stakes for students or bury them in an abstraction before they've absorbed the concrete cases?
