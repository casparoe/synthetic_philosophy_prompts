so i've been slowly working through Hume's first Enquiry on my own time (no philosophy background, just found a free copy online and got kind of hooked), and i hit a snag in the "Of Miracles" section that's been bugging me for days. here's the bit, copied straight from the text:

"The Indian prince, who refused to believe the first relations concerning the effects of frost, reasoned justly; and it naturally required very strong testimony to engage his assent to facts, that arose from a state of nature, with which he was unacquainted, and which bore so little analogy to those events, of which he had had constant and uniform experience. Though they were not contrary to his experience, they were not conformable to it."

and then like two paragraphs later he writes:

"A miracle is a violation of the laws of nature; and as a firm and unalterable experience has established these laws, the proof against a miracle, from the very nature of the fact, is as entire as any argument from experience can possibly be imagined... There must, therefore, be a uniform experience against every miraculous event, otherwise the event would not merit that appellation."

what i can't work out is how these two bits are supposed to fit together. the prince gets credit for reasoning "justly" even though he ended up dead wrong about the ice, and hume's excuse is that ice was merely "not conformable" to the prince's experience rather than "contrary" to it. fine. but two paragraphs later he's using that exact same kind of thing, an event nobody present has ever witnessed, to build what's supposed to be a basically airtight proof against miracles ever being credible. so what actually seperates an unprecedented event into the harmless "not conformable" bin versus the deadly "contrary to a law of nature" bin? from inside the prince's head, "water turns solid when its cold" would've looked just as exceptionless as "corpses dont get back up" looks to us, he'd never seen an exception either. is there a real principled test buried in here for sorting the two cases before you already know the answer, or is hume just quietly putting things in whichever bin gets him the conclusion he wants? i already know the general "extraordinary claims need extraordinary evidence" summary, i want to know if this specific move actually holds up.
