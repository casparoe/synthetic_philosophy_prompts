I'm finishing the historical chapter of my philosophy MA thesis on Anscombe's theory of intention, and something has been nagging at me. Some commentators trace the germ of "Intention" -- her enormously influential 1957 monograph on action theory -- back to the practical argument she made a year or so earlier in "Mr. Truman's Degree," the pamphlet in which she opposed Oxford's decision to award Truman an honorary doctorate. I used to treat the pamphlet as mere biographical color for the "real" philosophy, but I've started to suspect the influence runs the other way: the technical machinery of intention she later develops looks built to make good on the claim she'd already staked out in the pamphlet, that choosing to kill the innocent as a means to your ends is always murder. This stopped being a purely academic question for me over the holidays, when I brought up Anscombe's charge at dinner and ended up in a heated argument with my grandfather, who was a young private slated for the invasion of Kyushu when the war ended and is adamant that the bombs saved his life.

I'd like your help thinking through the argument properly, in essay form, rather than relitigating the generic "was the bomb justified" debate. Anscombe's case is more specific than "many civilians died, therefore murder": via the doctrine of double effect, she explicitly allows that attacking a legitimate military target while merely foreseeing civilian deaths is not murder, and she reserves the charge of murder for cases where civilian death is the means to victory, which is how she reads Hiroshima and Nagasaki. She also insists that the deeper injustice was the Allies' demand for unconditional surrender, not the bomb as such. Please write an essay that engages this specific machinery -- the intended/foreseen distinction and whether it can bear the weight she puts on it here, plus her point about unconditional surrender -- against the strongest consequentialist or "supreme emergency"-style reply (in the spirit of Michael Walzer) that weighs the bombs against the alternatives actually on the table in 1945. Don't leave me with "there are good arguments on both sides": reach an actual verdict on whether Anscombe's charge of murder holds up, and defend it. A qualified verdict is fine (for instance, "murder by her own premises, but her premises are too strong," or the reverse), as long as you commit to one.
