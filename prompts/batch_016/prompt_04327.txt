I'm a novelist at work on a book in the vein of the real Han van Meegeren affair: my protagonist is a gifted forger of a (fictional) Dutch Golden Age master, and by the premise of the story her fakes are perceptually and technically indistinguishable from the originals, even under expert scrutiny. Late in the book she debates an art historian over whether her paintings are truly worth less than the "authentic" ones, and I want that scene to reflect genuine philosophical positions rather than a straw man.

Here are six views philosophers have taken on why, if at all, a perfect and undetectable forgery is worth less than the object it copies:

- Goodman's autographic/allographic view: painting is an "autographic" art whose identity is constituted by its history of production, so a forgery is strictly a different work; this contrasts with "allographic" arts such as music or literature, where a correctly executed copy simply is another instance of the same work.
- Lessing's originality view: a forgery's aesthetic properties may be indistinguishable from the original's, yet it still lacks originality, and originality is itself a source of value independent of any perceptible quality.
- Danto's art-historical view: two indiscernible objects can differ in meaning and value because of their different places in the history and development of artistic styles and ideas, rather than because of any manifest property.
- Dutton's misrepresented-achievement view: forgery is an "artistic crime" because it misrepresents the actual creative feat behind the work, and our admiration is properly directed at achievements, not merely their sensory results.
- Benjamin's aura view: authenticity rests on an object's unique presence within a particular history and tradition, an "aura" that even a flawless copy cannot possess.
- The deflationary view: none of the above tracks a genuine value-making property, and our preference for originals is best explained psychologically and economically, as scarcity-driven status-seeking rather than sound aesthetic judgment.
