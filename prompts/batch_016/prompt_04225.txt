So this happened at work during a model review and it's been stuck in my head since. Two people on my team got into it over which of two models to ship for a forecasting task, and it somehow turned into a full philosophy arguement. Trying to write down what actually got said, move by move:

(1) Owen wanted to ship the smaller model (200k params vs the other one's 4M) and said "Occam's razor, simpler model, less overfitting, done."

(2) Fatima said param count isn't actually what simplicity means -- the rigorous version is Kolmogorov complexity, the length of the shortest program that outputs your data, and the bigger model can genuinely be "simpler" in that sense if it compresses the training set way better than the small one, which needs a pile of hand-tuned features bolted on to handle edge cases.

(3) Owen said that's a nice theory but useless in practice, since Kolmogorov complexity is literally uncomputable -- nobody can actually compute it for either model -- so param count is at least a number you can compare.

(4) Fatima said uncomputable doesn't mean useless, that's the whole reason MDL (minimum description length) exists, as a practical stand-in. And she said there's an actual theorem behind this -- Solomonoff's result that weighting hypotheses by something like 2^-complexity and updating on evidence provably converges on the true data-generating process, if its computable. So "prefer short descriptions" has real math behind it in a way "prefer fewer params" doesn't.

(5) Owen said sure, but that theorem only works once you've fixed some universal turing machine or language to measure length in, and two people could pick different ones and rank the exact same two models in opposite orders. So the "rigorous" version just moves his arbitrary choice down a level, it doesn't get rid of it.

(6) Fatima said there's literally an invariance theorem for that -- complexity measured in any two universal languages differs by at most some fixed constant that doesn't depend on the data. So it's not arbitrary, it converges.

(7) Owen said a constant can still be enormous, and they don't have infinite data, they have one training set with 50k rows -- "it converges eventually" isn't an answer to "which model do I ship Monday."

Nobody conceded, we just moved on to the next ticket. Can you go through this move by move, 1 through 7, and say who's actually got the better argument at each step -- not just an overall winner -- and flag anything you think both of them are missing?
