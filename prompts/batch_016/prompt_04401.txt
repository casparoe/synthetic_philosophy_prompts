In a graduate seminar on virtue ethics that I teach, we recently spent a week pairing Aristotle's account of habituation with Part Two of Franklin's *Autobiography*, where he lays out his "bold and arduous project of arriving at moral perfection" via the famous thirteen-virtue chart. Two students stayed behind afterward to keep arguing about it while I packed up, and I ended up listening to the whole thing rather than getting any grading done. I've reconstructed their exchange as faithfully as I can below, in six numbered moves, since I found myself nodding along with each of them in turn and never did sort out privately who had the better of it.

(1) Nadia opened by arguing that the project is best described not as a method for becoming virtuous but as a method for becoming well-regulated: Franklin only added Humility to the list after a Quaker friend told him he was widely seen as proud, and by his own admission he achieved only "the appearance" of the virtue and never "the reality" -- a method whose own author reports success solely at the level of appearance, she said, hasn't delivered what it advertised, by its own lights. (2) Simon replied that this holds Franklin to the wrong standard: Aristotle's habituation story is precisely that we become virtuous by first performing the relevant acts, often against inclination, until disposition catches up with behavior, and Franklin says as much when he reports that his new manner of disputing, adopted "with some violence to natural inclination," became so habitual that for fifty years no one heard a dogmatic phrase escape him -- that's not a confession of failure but a progress report exactly where the theory predicts one should be. (3) Nadia answered that Franklin gives us more than a progress report: writing decades later, he says pride is still "alive" and will "every now and then peep out," and adds that even complete victory over it would just furnish a fresh occasion for pride ("I should probably be proud of my humility"). That, she said, is a structural admission, not an update: for a sufficiently self-monitoring agent, achieving humility becomes visible to the agent as an achievement, which is itself food for pride, so nightly self-scoring may be exactly the wrong tool for this particular virtue. (4) Simon countered that this proves too much -- noticing you had become generous would make you proud of your generosity too, which is no argument that generosity is unattainable -- and that it also misreads Franklin's own gloss on humility, which is explicitly behavioral and civic (forbearing "direct contradiction," retiring words like "certainly" for "I conceive"), not a claim about extinguishing self-regard; the regress only threatens a monkish conception of the virtue Franklin never signs up to. (5) Nadia generalized the worry: if each of the thirteen virtues gets quietly redefined into whatever a five-minute evening review can score, the shrinking spots on the chart track improving self-presentation rather than improving character -- and, tellingly, the one virtue Franklin confesses he never mastered even in old age, Order, is also the one least amenable to this kind of redefinition, since a desk is either organized or it isn't. (6) Simon answered that this cuts the other way: Franklin's refusal to fudge his record on Order -- via the story of the customer who settles for a "speckled axe" rather than finish the grinding, which Franklin pointedly declines to make an excuse for himself -- is exactly the honest self-assessment Nadia's own worry says a gamified method should be incapable of producing; and the whole scheme is less atomistic than her "checklist" framing suggests, since Franklin sequences the thirteen because he expects earlier virtues to causally enable later ones (Temperance first, for the "clearness of head" that vigilance requires; Frugality and Industry making Sincerity and Justice "more easy"), cycling the full list repeatedly rather than retiring each virtue once achieved.

Please go through moves (1) through (6) and say, for each, who has the better of it and why -- both of them say genuinely defensible things at various points, so I'm not looking for a rout. Then note anything you think both of them are overlooking entirely. Finish with a verdict on its own line, in exactly this format: \answer{Nadia} or \answer{Simon}, whichever one you think has the more defensible position overall.
