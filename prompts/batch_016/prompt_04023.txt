I'm putting together a session for my grad seminar on practical rationality and want to pressure-test an argument before I inflict it on the students. You know the standard money-pump story: if your preferences over A, B and C are cyclic (A over B, B over C, but C over A), a sufficiently patient bookie can walk you through a sequence of trades -- each one a trade you'd make given just the two options on offer at that moment -- charging you a small fee each time, and leave you back where you started, over and over, for as much money as they like. This is usually presented as a knock-down case that cyclic preferences aren't merely imprudent but flatly irrational, on a par with holding inconsistent beliefs.

Thing is, I've never fully bought that this settles it, mostly because of the resolute-choice move out of McClennen: an agent who just commits to a plan at the outset of the sequence and sticks to it, even at the node where they'd "locally" prefer to defect, never gets pumped. A colleague of mine down the hall thinks this is basically a cheat -- refusing the trade you'd prefer in the moment just IS a further bit of incoherence, so all resoluteness shows is that one irrationality can be used to patch over another, which isnt much of a vindication. I go back and forth on this, honestly, but I'd like to see the strongest version of the pro-resolute-choice case laid out properly, since most treatments I've seen just assert that resoluteness "solves" the problem without doing the work.

So here's what I want: argue, as rigorously as you can, that a resolute chooser with cyclic preferences is not thereby irrational, and that the money-pump argument therefore fails to establish transitivity (or even mere acyclicity) as a requirement of practical rationality. Don't just wave at resoluteness -- actually engage with the mechanics. In particular, say something about why declining the trade at a given node doesn't require that your preference at that node has secretly changed (which would just concede that the intransitivity was never real to begin with), and how this differs, if it does, from the ordinary sophisticated backward-induction story we tell about agents whose preferences change over time because of something like hyperbolic discounting -- where its uncontroversial that planning ahead is rational precisely because the future self really does have different preferences.
