I'm about two-thirds of the way through a novel set in the 1660s, in and around the Port-Royal circle, built around the relationship between an ageing natural philosopher devoted to Cartesian mechanism and a younger protégé who assists him with anatomical demonstrations on live animals and is quietly tormented by what he witnesses. I want the older man's justifications to rest on genuine Cartesian doctrine rather than the flattened version most readers will bring to the page — the notion that Descartes simply thought animals were clockwork automata that felt nothing whatsoever. My own reading of the Discourse and the later correspondence suggests the real position is considerably more tangled: in the 1649 letter to Henry More, Descartes is willing to grant animals "life" and even "sensation, in so far as it depends on a bodily organ," while insisting he is speaking only of "thought" when he denies them minds; and back in Part V of the Discourse, he tries to fix the boundary between man and beast-machine with two distinct tests, one turning on the capacity for new, contextually apt speech rather than fixed responses, the other on reason's standing as "a universal instrument that can be used in all kinds of situations," as against organs that "need some particular disposition for each particular action."

What I would like help untangling, so that my older philosopher can hold his own against a sharp interlocutor rather than simply reciting slogans, is threefold. First, if an animal can have sensation "depending on a bodily organ" without having thought, what exactly is being withheld from it — is there a principled way, on Descartes' own terms, to say that a dog's yelp is mere bodily commotion with nothing it is like to undergo, rather than the very same event with an extra piece of machinery (a soul) merely watching from the wings? Second, are the language test and the universal-instrument test meant as conceptual, in-principle claims — that no arrangement of matter could ever do these things — or more like empirical wagers about the limits of what seventeenth-century mechanism happened to be able to build, which would leave the whole edifice hostage to future engineering? And third, does the concession of "mere sensation" put real pressure on Descartes' official two-substance picture, nudging him toward some third category between pure extended body and thinking soul — what a few later commentators have been willing to name outright as a kind of "trialism" — even though he never owned up to any such category himself?
