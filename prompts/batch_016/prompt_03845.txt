I'm a novelist working through a plotting problem that I suspect is really a philosophical one in disguise. In the story, two of a wealthy man's children, each unaware of the other's scheme, poison their father's wine at the same family dinner—one with a fast-acting toxin, the other with a slower one that would have killed him just as surely, only a couple of hours later. He collapses and dies almost at once, long before the second poison could ever take effect. I want my detective, and my reader, to conclude that only the first child actually killed him, and that the second is guilty of nothing worse than attempted murder. But if I imagine the detective asking the first child, "If you hadn't poisoned him, would he still be dead by the end of the night?", the honest answer is yes—the second child's poison would have finished the job a little later regardless. So the first child's act seems to have made no difference to whether their father died that night, which sits badly with calling it the thing that actually killed him. I'd rather this asymmetry not rest on authorial fiat alone: is there a principled basis for it, or should I be writing both children as equally responsible for his death?
