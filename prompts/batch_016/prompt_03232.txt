So a couple weeks back I went down this rabbit hole reading about Kurt Gödel (the incompleteness theorem guy) and found out that in the early 60s, when he was already this super famous mathematician at Princeton, his mom straight up asked him in a letter whether he actually believed they'd see each other again after she died. He wrote back this whole serious, multi-letter argument for why yes, there's definitely an afterlife - but its not a religious argument at all, its more like: the universe is fundamentally rational and ordered (which is basically the whole premise behind doing science and math in the first place), and a truly rational universe would never build a person up with all this potential - relationships, self-improvement, learning from screwing up, etc - only to snuff it out with barely any of it cashed in. He compares it to pouring the foundation for a house and then just abandoning the whole project forever, which doesn't make sense if reality is actually orderly instead of random junk. The interesting part is he's very explicit this is NOT reincarnation - you don't get sent back for another round on earth - instead, in whatever the "next" thing is, you finally get to properly understand and learn from the mess of this life, with total certainty, like there's no more room for self-deception. And apparently this all connects to him thinking materialism is just wrong, and that a mind can't be fully explained as a physical setup of neurons and wiring, which he thought his own incompleteness theorem backed up somehow.

Anyway, the guy died in 1978, so obviously he never had to deal with stuff like mind-uploading (copying your brain/mind into a computer so "you" keep going forever, the whole Kurzweil singularity thing) or the simulation hypothesis (the idea that we're probably already living inside somebody else's computer simulation) - both of which get thrown around all the time now as basically secular stand-ins for an afterlife. I brought this up with some friends at a bar and nobody could tell me anything useful, but it's been stuck in my head since, because it seems like those two ideas might actually clash with Gödel's real argument rather than confirm it. Like, if he genuinely thought a mind can't be reduced to some finite physical arrangement of parts, wouldn't he have to say an "uploaded" copy of you isn't really you, just an incredibly detailed impersonation, no different in principle from a recording? And separately, even if we ARE in a simulation, that doesn't obviously satisfy his "unfulfilled potential has to get cashed in somewhere" requirement, unless whoever's running the simulation specifically bothers to preserve you after you die inside it, which is a whole extra assumption he never needed to make.

So here's what I'm actually asking: going purely off his own specific reasoning, not just "well he was a genius so he'd probably be open-minded about future tech," do you think Gödel would see mind-uploading and/or the simulation hypothesis as basically vindicating what he told his mother, flatly contradicting it, or just being irrelevant to it? And if you had to pick, which of the two do you think his own logic would make him find more philosophically threatening or interesting, and why?
