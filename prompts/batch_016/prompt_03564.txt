So I was on the phone with an old friend from my MFA program who somehow ended up doing a PhD in decision theory (don't ask), and she told me about something called the absent-minded driver problem that I havent been able to shake since. The setup: you're driving on a highway with two exits that look completely identical from inside the car, you need to take the second one to get home, and because you're "absent-minded" (I think thats the actual technical term, something to do with what they call imperfect recall) you literally cannot tell, once you're sitting at an intersection, whether it's the first one or the second one in front of you. So whatever rule you use for exit-or-continue has to be the exact same rule both times, since as far as you can tell it's the exact same moment happening twice. The part that really got me: apparently this comes out of a real economics paper from the 90s (Rubinstein, with Aumann and a couple of coauthors arguing back at him), and the probability of continuing that looks best if you work it out in advance, before you've even started driving, is something like two-thirds — but that is NOT the same answer you get if you imagine yourself actually sitting at the junction, trying to reason it through from inside that moment. Your "planning self" and your "in-the-moment self" are doing the same math and landing in different places, and apparently there's still real disagreement among the people who study this about whose reasoning is actually the mistake.

I want to build a short story out of this for a collection I'm slowly putting together on memory and choice, but I don't want it to just be "confused guy at an intersection" — that would waste everything interesting about the idea. Could you write me one, maybe 800-1200 words? It doesn't have to literally be a highway, transplant it into a different setting entirely if something else earns its keep better, as long as the bones survive: two moments that feel completely identical from the inside to whoever's living them, a choice that has to be made the same way both times because there's no way to tell the moments apart, and an ending that leaves the reader actually feeling the gap between "the smart plan" and "the smart thing to do right now," instead of a character just explaining the paradox out loud.
