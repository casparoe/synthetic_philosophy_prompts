So I went down kind of a wiki hole on democratic theory a while back (it started because a friend who's really into local politics sent me a news story about a fight kind of like this one) and got stuck on this thing political theorists apparently call "the boundary problem." The basic idea seems to be that before a group can democratically decide anything, somebody already has to have decided -- non-democratically -- who even counts as being in the group that gets to vote, and there's no real consensus on how you're suppose to draw that line. The two answers I kept running into were something like (1) everyone whose interests are substantially affected by a decision should get a say, and (2) everyone who'd actually be subject to or coerced by the resulting law/policy should get a say. Apparently both run into real trouble once you push on them: the first one is supposedly circular somehow (you can't know who's affected without already knowing what's being decided), and the second one, at least according to some guy named Abizadeh who wrote a whole paper about borders and immigration, technically balloons outward until the "demos" is basically unbounded, once you take it seriously.

Could you write me a dialogue between two political philosophers who each hold one of these views, arguing it out over a concrete case? Say: country A wants to approve a big hydro dam, built and operated entirely on its own territory and voted on only by its own citizens -- but the reservoir will flood farmland and wreck the fishing grounds of an indigenous community living just across the river in country B, and will keep messing with fish spawning patterns for decades after, so it's not even just people alive today who'd be affected. I want the "affected-interests" philosopher arguing that the villagers in B clearly deserve some kind of vote or veto, and the "coercion" philosopher pushing back that they're simply not subject to A's laws so they don't belong in A's demos, no matter how much they stand to loose. Then I want the two of them to actually engage with each other's strongest moves instead of just repeating themselves louder -- including whatever this circularity thing and the "basically everyone gets included" thing actually cash out to once you apply them to the dam case specifically. I don't want either one to just cave or come off as a strawman -- both sides should be argued about as well as they realistically can be.
