I'm a retired astrophysicist who's fallen hard into philosophy of religion in retirement, and I've hit a snag I can't shake. Turns out Aquinas himself thought you *can't* actually prove, by reason alone, that the universe had a beginning in time — he says that's known by faith alone, and that an eternal-but-still-created world isn't even a contradiction. Which is suprisingly bad news for William Lane Craig's Kalam cosmological argument, since that whole approach leans on an infinite past being impossible (these days dressed up with Big Bang cosmology and the Borde-Guth-Vilenkin theorem). So here's what's bugging me: if you sat Aquinas down and walked him through the modern scientific case for a finite past, would he finally come around to something like Craig's argument, or would he shrug and say his own contingency-style proofs (the ones that don't care whether the past is infinite) were the better bet all along? Don't just give me a verdict — walk me through his actual reasoning.
