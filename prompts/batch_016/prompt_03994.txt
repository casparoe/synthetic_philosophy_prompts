I was rereading some old Overcoming Bias / LW posts about Knightian uncertainty and why "just maximize expected utility" isn't the automatic trump card people sometimes treat it as, and it got me thinking about Rawls's veil of ignorance again — not the watered-down "imagine you don't know who you'll be" version that gets trotted out in every intro ethics class, but the actual argument buried in A Theory of Justice for why the parties behind the veil would reason via maximin instead of just maximizing expected utility. I wrote up a short case for why I think that argument is shakier than it usually gets credit for, and I want a second opinion before I embarass myself by posting it anywhere. Please actually tell me if I'm wrong or strawmanning somebody here, don't just tell me it's a great post.

Working title: "Rawls's maximin argument might be smuggling in its own conclusion"

Rawls needs the parties in the original position to pick the two principles of justice over average utilitarianism, and his mechanism for getting there is the maximin rule: rank options by their worst possible outcome and choose whichever option's worst outcome is least bad. He's careful to say maximin isn't generally a good decision procedure — it only kicks in when three conditions hold: (1) you have no reliable basis for assigning probabilities to outcomes, (2) the maximin option is one you can actually live with, meaning you don't care much about doing better than the guaranteed floor, and (3) the other options have possible outcomes that are flatly unacceptable. Harsanyi's famous reply is that real uncertainty just calls for the principle of insufficient reason — assign equal probability to landing in any social position — which cashes out to maximizing average utility, not maximin.

Here's my problem with it: Rawls himself later concedes (in Justice as Fairness) that condition (1), the "we can't even assign probabilities" bit, is doing comparatively little work, and that conditions (2) and (3) are what's really carrying the argument. But (2) and (3) aren't claims about uncertainty at all — they're just claims that some outcomes are acceptable and others aren't, full stop, regardless of how unlikely the bad ones are. Once you're allowed to posit that some outcomes are simply off the table no matter their probability, you've basically already assumed something like infinite risk aversion (or a lexical priority on avoiding the worst case), which is most of what you needed the difference principle to say in the first place. That looks less like "thin, formal rationality plus a weird epistemic situation forces maximin on you" and more like "I stipulated something maximin-shaped and now I'm shocked maximin came out." Rawls explicitly denies the parties are dispositionally risk-averse — he compares it to buying insurance, rational given the circumstances rather than a personality trait — but "the stakes are high, so act cautiously" and "some outcomes are lexically unacceptable no matter the odds" seem like very different, and much stronger, claims, and I don't see what's supposed to bridge them.

So: does this seem like a fair reading, or is there an obvious Rawlsian move I'm missing — something about primary goods, or the two moral powers, or publicity/stability considerations — that independently motivates conditions (2) and (3) without just presupposing the conclusion? I have a vague memory of "isn't this just infinite risk aversion in disguise" being a whole minor cottage industry in the secondary literature, but I couldn't tell you whose version of the objection (or whose defense of Rawls against it) is supposed to be the definitive one, so if anything comes to mind there I'd love to know, even without you being able to look it up.
