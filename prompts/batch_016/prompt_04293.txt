Right, so I spent almost 40 years as an industrial chemist before I retired, and these days I've fallen into reading philosophy, which I never really had the patience for back when I was working full time. Lately I've been poking around the early history of the Society for Psychical Research, partly because it amused me to realize that the "Mr. Crookes" who keeps getting mentioned is the same William Crookes I knew from thallium and the radiometer -- funny to think of these serious Victorian scientists taking séances seriously enough to found a whole research society around it. Anyway, I dug up Henry Sidgwick's inaugural address as the Society's first president, delivered in July 1882, and got stuck on this bit near the end of it:

"As regards the question of credibility, the important point to bear in mind is that every additional witness who, as De Morgan said, has a fair stock of credit to draw upon, is an important gain. Though his credit alone is not likely to suffice for the demand that is made on it, his draft will help. For we must not expect any decisive effect in the direction at which we primarily aim, on the common sense of mankind, from any single piece of evidence, however complete it has been made. Scientific incredulity has been so long in growing, and has so many and so strong roots, that we shall only kill it, if we are able to kill it at all as regards any of those questions, by burying it alive under a heap of facts. We must keep 'pegging away,' as Lincoln said; we must accumulate fact upon fact, and add experiment upon experiment, and, I should say, not wrangle too much with incredulous outsiders about the conclusiveness of any one, but trust to the mass of evidence for conviction. The highest degree of demonstrative force that we can obtain out of any single record of investigation is, of course, limited by the trustworthiness of the investigator. We have done all that we can when the critic has nothing left to allege except that the investigator is in the trick. But when he has nothing else left to allege he will allege that."

I think I get the broad strategy: don't try to win with one knockdown case, just pile up so much evidence that skepticism becomes untenable. But the ending seems to undercut the whole plan before he's even finished making it -- he basically admits that no matter how good any single piece of evidence is, the skeptic always has one card left to play, namely just saying the investigator himself is in on the fraud. That move doesn't seem to get any harder to make as the evidence pile grows -- you can say "the investigator is in the trick" about fact number 500 just as easily as about fact number one. So what is accumulating evidence actually supposed to accomplish here, on his view? Is he being knowingly wry/resigned about the whole enterprise, does he think that piling up independent investigators makes the "they're all in on it" explanation itself increasingly implausible (a kind of probabilistic squeeze on the critic), or is there some other move I'm not seeing? I also can't shake the feeling that this is basically the same structure as Hume's argument in "Of Miracles" about testimony always losing out to the prior improbability of the thing testified to -- is Sidgwick likely consciously responding to Hume here given his background as a moral philosopher, or is that reading too much into some off-the-cuff remarks at a society meeting?
