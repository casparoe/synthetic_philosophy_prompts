okay so this week in my decision theory seminar our professor went on this whole tangent about Kelly betting and now I can't stop thinking about it, so I want to actually work through it properly instead of just half-following along like I did in class. Quick recap in case its useful: Kelly showed that if you're making a long sequence of favorable bets, betting the fraction of your bankroll that maximizes the expected log of your wealth is also the strategy that, almost surely, ends up beating literally any other strategy in the long run. Samuelson apparently hated this so much that in 1979 he wrote an entire paper attacking it using only one-syllable words (the very last word breaks the rule -- it's "syllable," which is famously not one syllable, and I'm pretty sure that was the joke). But his actual point was serious: "practically certain to win eventually" isn't the same thing as "what a rational expected-utility maximizer should do," unless your utility just happens to be log, and there's no long-run trick that lets you dodge picking a utility function.

Except then, procrastinating on a different class's reading, I fell down a rabbit hole and found people keep trying to resurrect the idea that geometric-mean maximization is privileged in some deeper way than that. There's the "ergodicity economics" crowd (Ole Peters and co) who flip the accusation around and say its expected-value reasoning that smuggles in an assumption, namely that you're allowed to average over an "ensemble" of parallel copies of yourself, when what actually matters to one person living through time is the time-average of their single path -- and for multiplicative wealth those two come apart. Then separately there's a whole "geometric rationality" sequence by this MIRI/AI-alignment guy Scott Garrabrant that tries to unify Kelly betting with Nash bargaining and Thompson sampling under one roof, and explicitly argues these geometric-expectation-maximizing agents are NOT expected-utility (VNM) maximizers in disguise -- though I also found someone push back with a proof sketch claiming any "geometrically rational" agent can be redescribed as an ordinary expected utility maximizer over some transformed quantity, which if right seems to undercut the whole "genuinely different" claim.
