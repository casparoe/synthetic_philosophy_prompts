I'm a philosophy PhD student working mostly on formal epistemology, but lately I've been pulled into a side project on decision theory and suffering risk, which is how I went down the "surrogate goals" rabbit hole (the Center on Long-Term Risk stuff, originally Baumann and Oesterheld, though people keep adding wrinkles to it). Quick refresher in case it's not on your radar: instead of trying to make yourself immune to threats, you deliberately bolt on some totally arbitrary "surrogate" goal you don't actually care about — the classic example is caring intensely about whether a platinum sphere of exactly 42.82cm diameter ever gets created — and you commit to responding to threats against it exactly as seriously as you'd respond to threats against your real values. The hope is that threateners, including acausal ones (agents or civilizations that never causally touch you but can predict or simulate your policy well enough to threaten it), get deflected onto the meaningless target instead of your actual survival or whatever you care about. Apparently this comes up seriously in discussions of a single AGI having to acausally bargain with other civilizations across the multiverse, which is a genuinely strange thing to have to reason carefully about.

Me and a labmate have been arguing for weeks about whether this trick actually holds up or just relocates the problem one level up. She keeps bringing up Yudkowsky's old reflective-consistency complaint — if the agent can rationally swap in a surrogate goal once, whats stopping it from swapping to a new one the moment the current one becomes inconvenient, forever — plus the credibility problem, since the threatener has to actually believe you privilege the fake goal over the real one, otherwise they just threaten the real thing anyway. I keep thinking these are patchable: dormant triggers that only activate once a threat is actually detected, or just recasting the whole thing in updateless decision theory terms so there's no discrete "self-modification" step for the reflective-consistency worry to even bite on. Neither of us is budging and it's derailed like three lab meetings now.

So could you settle it (or at least dramatize it properly) by writing a dialogue between two decision theorists holding these two positions, really arguing it out? I want them evenly matched — the skeptic shouldn't be missing some obvious fix, and the defender shouldn't get to just hand-wave past the regress and credibility worries — and I want them actually responding to each other's specific points instead of talking past each other. If you can naturally work in the multi-agent coordination problem too (how would independent agents even converge on compatible surrogate goals without being able to communicate) that'd be great, but don't force it. Doesn't need to resolve neatly at the end either; a genuine impasse is a fine outcome if that's where it goes.
