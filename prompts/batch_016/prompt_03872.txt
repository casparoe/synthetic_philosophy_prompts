So I got into it with my friend Alex last night about brain death and it's still bugging me. Started because of some documentary on organ donors and turned into a two hour argument neither of us won.

My point was: if a "whole brain dead" body can still heal wounds, fight off infections, regulate its own temperature, and in one documented case (they call the kid "TK" in the literature) keep doing all that for like 20 years on a vent, then the brain clearly isn't what's holding the organism together as one thing -- so why does the brain failing count as the organism dying? Alex thinks circulatory death is the safer, less philosophically weird standard since its just the heart and lungs stopping, full stop. But afterward I looked into it more and apparently DCD (donation after circulatory death) protocols only wait like 2-5 minutes of no pulse before calling it, and theres a whole literature on how that's only "permanent" cessation, not truly "irreversible" in any strong sense -- which feels suspiciously tailored to make organ harvesting timelines work out.

Anyway, can you compare the brain-death and circulatory-death standards as accounts of when someone's actually died? Which one is more philosophically defensible, or do they both just bend the concept of death to fit transplant logistics? I want an actual verdict, not a "both sides have a point" cop out like we ended on.
