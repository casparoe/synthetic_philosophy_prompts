I've been reporting on some of the more esoteric decision-theory ideas circulating in longtermist and AI-safety circles, and I keep circling back to "evidential cooperation in large worlds" (ECL) — the argument, first floated by Caspar Oesterheld in 2017 under the label "multiverse-wide superrationality," that if the universe (or multiverse) is large enough to contain many agents who reason very much as we do, we have some reason to act cooperatively toward unfamiliar, causally unreachable value systems, on the theory that our choosing to do so is itself evidence that our distant counterparts are choosing likewise. Every explainer I've found, including a couple from researchers at outfits like the Center on Long-Term Risk, illustrates the idea with essentially the same move: Hofstadter's old "superrationality" case of two identical reasoners facing a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, who cooperate because their being the very same algorithm makes an asymmetric outcome close to logically incoherent. That version is admirably clean, but more than one person I've interviewed has admitted, a bit sheepishly, that it's also the easy case — real agents invoking this argument would never be exact copies of one another, only imperfectly and uncertainly similar reasoners, and I don't yet have a real feel for how the logic is supposed to survive once the twins are gone.

So, for a sidebar I'd rather get right than fudge: could you construct a new, concrete scenario — not a pair of identical agents, and not simply the prisoner's dilemma with the serial numbers filed off — involving two parties who never causally interact, whose decision procedures are only partially and probabilistically correlated, and who each have to decide how much weight to put on the other's apparent values in some specific, describable action? I'd like the example to spell out what that "compromise action" actually consists of, to show precisely why a committed causal decision theorist would decline to take it, and to be upfront about exactly where the residual uncertainty over the degree of correlation enters the reasoning, rather than quietly assuming it away. If the case is vivid enough to survive contact with a skeptical copy editor, so much the better.
