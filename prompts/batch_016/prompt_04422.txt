I'm currently taking a seminar on nineteenth-century political philosophy, and this week we're working through the "Estranged Labour" section of Marx's Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844 ahead of Friday's discussion. I feel fairly confident about the four-fold structure of alienation that Marx lays out earlier in the essay (from the product, from the act of production, from one's own species-being, and from other people), but I've gotten stuck on the argumentative pivot that comes right after, where he tries to derive private property from alienated labor rather than the other way around. Our TA mentioned in passing that this move gets read by some commentators as one of Marx's more ingenious dialectical inversions and by others as basically question-begging, which has only made me more unsure what to think.

The passage in question reads: "Private property thus results by analysis from the concept of alienated labor... True, it is as a result of the movement of private property that we have obtained the concept of alienated labor (of alienated life) in political economy. But on analysis of this concept it becomes clear that though private property appears to be the reason, the cause of alienated labor, it is rather its consequence, just as the gods are originally not the cause but the effect of man's intellectual confusion. Later this relationship becomes reciprocal." A page or so on, once Marx turns to wages, the hedging seems to disappear and he simply states that estranged labor is "the direct cause" of private property, without further qualification.

What I can't work out is whether this is a genuine argument or a kind of sleight of hand. Marx openly admits that he only arrived at the concept of alienated labor by starting from political economy's own treatment of private property as a given fact, which sounds like alienated labor is conceptually downstream of private property rather than its origin. Yet he then insists that the true order of explanation runs the other way, and seems to think the analogy with the gods — as effects of human confusion rather than its cause — is what licenses that reversal. Is that analogy actually doing argumentative work, or is it just rhetorical cover borrowed from Feuerbach's critique of religion? And is "later this relationship becomes reciprocal" a genuine resolution of the apparent circularity, or more of an escape hatch? I'd also value help placing this early, rather anthropological derivation of private property alongside the more technical account of exploitation that Marx develops later in Capital through the theory of surplus value — are these ultimately the same argument in a different register, or two distinct claims that survey courses tend to flatten together under the single heading of "alienation"? Any help sorting out the logic here before seminar would be much appreciated.
