For my AP U.S. Government research paper, I've been working on Frederick Douglass's break with William Lloyd Garrison over how to interpret the Constitution, and I'll admit I've gotten a little absorbed in it. After 1851, once Douglass parted ways with Garrison's view that the Constitution was inherently pro-slavery, he leaned heavily on the arguments of Lysander Spooner and Gerrit Smith and began insisting that the framers' private intentions, and the way the document had actually been enforced in practice (fugitive slave renditions, the Dred Scott decision, and so on), were beside the point. In his 1860 Glasgow speech, "The Constitution of the United States: Is It Pro-Slavery or Anti-Slavery?," he lays out a whole list of things he says simply don't bear on the "real question" at issue — not whether slaveholders helped draft the document, not whether they privately meant to protect slavery, not whether courts had in practice given it a pro-slavery reading — because none of that changes what the actual words on the page say. Since neither "slave" nor "slavery" appears anywhere in the text, he insists the Constitution has to be read as a document that nowhere authorizes human bondage.

My debate partner, who cares more about constitutional law than history, is the one who first noticed how strangely this parallels the "history and tradition" test the Supreme Court has leaned on recently, in cases like New York State Rifle & Pistol Association v. Bruen and Dobbs v. Jackson, where a right basically has to be shown as deeply rooted in the historical practices of the founding generation to count as constitutionally protected. That struck me as almost a direct inversion of Douglass's method, since his whole point was that founding-era practices and intentions, produced by a society that tolerated and protected slavery, shouldn't be allowed to override or supply meaning that the bare text doesn't contain. So here's what I'd like help thinking through: if Douglass could see "history and tradition" jurisprudence being used today, would he recognize it as basically the same interpretive move he spent his career arguing against, just relabeled as originalism? Or is there a way to read his own arguments as compatible with, or even supportive of, a history-based test, given that he wasn't purely a textualist so much as someone who reached for whichever interpretive tool best supported an antislavery reading of the Constitution? I'd really appreciate an answer that engages with the actual structure of his reasoning rather than a general statement about how much he valued liberty.
