For my history of science elective, we just finished a unit on the Scientific Revolution, and one detail from it has stuck with me more than anything else in the course so far: Johannes Kepler, the same person who worked out that planets move in ellipses, spent much of his career as a working court astrologer, casting horoscopes for patrons such as the military commander Albrecht von Wallenstein and for Emperor Rudolf II. When I looked into this further, it turned out that his actual views on astrology were far stranger and more specific than "a great scientist who happened to believe in astrology." In a 1610 book called Tertius Interveniens ("Third Man in the Middle"), he cast himself as mediating between people who accepted wildly exaggerated horoscope predictions without question and critics who wanted to throw out the whole discipline. He had already written, in a private letter, that he had discarded the twelve zodiac signs, the astrological "houses," and planetary rulerships as baseless, keeping only the angular relationships between planets, known as aspects, which he later tried to give a physical grounding in Harmonices Mundi by treating them as something like musical ratios that the soul responds to without consciously realizing it. And yet he still described astrology, half-jokingly, as the foolish daughter who pays the bills for her wise mother, astronomy.

I brought this up with some friends in astronomy club, and one of them said it's obvious that Kepler would simply be an ordinary astronomy-side skeptic if he were alive today. That answer feels too quick to me, given how specific and unusual his actual position was. So here is what I would like your best-supported guess on: how do you think Kepler specifically, not just "a 17th-century scientist" in general, would react to two developments he never lived to see? First, that virtually every rigorous, controlled test of astrology conducted since then, including approaches that lean heavily on the very planetary aspects he cared about most, has failed to find any predictive power beyond chance, with the best-known case being a widely cited 1985 double-blind study in Nature in which professional astrologers could not match birth charts to the right people. Second, that the mass-market, sign-based astrology he personally dismissed as fit only for street fortune-tellers is essentially the only version that survived into the present, while his own harmonics-based reform is almost completely forgotten outside specialist history-of-science circles. Would he most likely treat this as a vindication of his instincts, as a disappointment, or would he probably have revised his underlying reasons for believing in planetary influence at all? I would be especially interested in an answer that actually engages with the details of his position rather than defaulting to "he was a scientist, so he would have sided with the skeptics."
