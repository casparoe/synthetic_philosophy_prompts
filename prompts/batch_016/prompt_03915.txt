I spent the better part of four decades doing observational work in stellar astrophysics before retiring, and it's only in the last year or two, largely thanks to a philosophy reading group I joined almost on a whim, that I've started reading about the history and philosophy of my old field rather than just working in it. Kepler has become something of a fixation. I thought I knew him reasonably well already from the physics side: the three laws, the polyhedral hypothesis in the Mysterium Cosmographicum, the long grinding path to the ellipse in the Astronomia Nova. So I was mildly startled to discover how much intellectual effort he poured into astrology, and how central he apparently took it to be to his larger project rather than treating it as a shameful sideline. Like most people, I suspect, the only thing I had ever heard quoted was his remark that astronomy is the wise mother who would go hungry if her foolish daughter astrology didn't bring in the money, which I had always taken, as I gather generations of popular biographers have too, as evidence that he was basically a modern skeptic cynically humoring a superstitious public for the fee.

Reading his Tertius Interveniens and the fourth book of the Harmonice Mundi has complicated that picture considerably. Kepler is scathing about the zodiac signs, the houses (he dismisses their use at one point as "Arabic sorcery"), and horoscopic prediction generally. Yet he retains, and works out in real technical detail, a theory of "aspects," the angles between planets and luminaries, restricted to those grounded in the twelve constructible regular figures. He held that the soul recognizes these a priori, because it was itself built according to the same geometrical archetypes that structure the rest of creation. He even added three new aspects of his own on this basis (the quintile, biquintile, and sesquiquadrate), and he spent years logging weather against planetary configurations as confirming evidence that the earth's own soul, the anima telluris, responds to these angles. The procedure strikes me as uncomfortably close to how he used Tycho's Mars observations to adjudicate between rival astronomical hypotheses. This looks to me like a genuine, if underdiscussed, instance of the demarcation problem: not "is astrology science" in the abstract, but whether one particular thinker's own criteria for good and bad astrology were the same criteria doing the work in his physics. A friend from the reading group, who works on early modern science, thinks I am falling for a fashionable revisionist move. She points to Patrick Boner's book on Kepler's "cosmological synthesis" as exactly the kind of scholarship that overstates the unity of his thought, and insists it is anachronistic to read a real continuity of method and metaphysics into what was, for Kepler himself, a fairly opportunistic and compartmentalized body of belief.

I would like to see the strongest possible case for my side of that disagreement before I concede the point to her. Could you argue, as forcefully as the material supports, for the position that Kepler's reformed astrology of aspects cannot be cleanly excised from his physics and cosmology as an embarrassing or merely pragmatic appendage? My contention, and the one I would like defended, is that it rests on exactly the same architectonic commitments that underwrite the Astronomia Nova and the Harmonice Mundi themselves: geometric archetypes lodged in the soul, causal powers restricted to what is constructible by ruler and compass, and confirmation by patiently gathered empirical data. Please engage directly with the natural objections rather than setting them aside: that the "foolish daughter" remark and his rejection of the zodiac and houses show he was, at bottom, already sorting the wheat from the chaff along recognizably modern scientific lines; and that any astrology-physics parallel is really a flattering reconstruction by sympathetic modern scholars rather than something Kepler himself would have recognized. A tightly argued case of a page or two would suit me better than a balanced survey of the historiography.
