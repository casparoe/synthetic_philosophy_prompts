I'm several chapters into a novel narrated in retrospect by a woman looking back on the year she spent infiltrating, and was eventually won over by, a charismatic self-improvement movement she had originally set out to expose as a fraud. Because the narration is retrospective, she already knows, from the book's opening page, exactly how the story ends: she becomes a devoted follower. Wanting an early scene with some genuine intellectual weight to it, I have her raise this predicament with her old philosophy professor over dinner, and he counters with what he calls the Reflection Principle, which he attributes to the philosopher Bas van Fraassen: the idea that one's present degree of belief in a proposition should match whatever degree of belief one is already certain of holding in it at some later time. Since she is, for the purposes of the scene, fully certain she will end up a true believer, the professor argues that rationality itself obliges her to adopt that conviction now, rather than waiting to be talked into it by the movement's founder.

I find this a wonderfully bleak scene to write — the skeptic argued out of her skepticism before the seduction has even properly begun — but I don't want to fake the philosophy just because it makes for good drama, and a couple of philosopher friends have told me just enough about the principle to make me suspect I might be oversimplifying it. Is the professor's argument basically sound, such that my narrator has no principled way to resist it and the honest version of the scene really does end with her capitulating on the spot? Or is there something about where her anticipated conversion is actually coming from that would give a genuinely rational version of her grounds to tell him his argument doesn't apply to her case at all?
