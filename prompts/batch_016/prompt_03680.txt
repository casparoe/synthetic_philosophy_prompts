I was at an ACX meetup a few months back (I've been reading the rationalist blogosphere since the old Overcoming Bias days, so this is very much my scene) and two of the regulars got into a long argument about quantum immortality that I still haven't stopped thinking about. It started because one of them, Marco, said over drinks that he'd basically stoped being scared of dying because of many-worlds: a conscious observer can never actually experience their own nonexistence, and if Everett is right then every outcome of a quantum event happens in some branch, so there's always some branch where whatever quantum fluke he'd need to survive actually occurs -- meaning from the inside, he'll just keep finding himself alive, indefinitely.

Here's roughly how the rest of it went. (1) Sasha said that only works for something like Tegmark's quantum-gun setup, where death is triggered by one clean, instantaneous quantum-random event -- real deaths like car crashes or heart attacks are big, decohered, classical-scale processes playing out over time, so there's no guarantee of a nearby branch where the outcome is "you, but fine" rather than "you, but maimed," or nothing coherent at all. (2) Marco conceded that, but said fine, restrict the claim to clean instant setups -- it still holds there, and that alone is already a philosophically interesting result about how you should anticipate death. (3) Sasha said okay, but even in the clean case the Born rule says the surviving branch's amplitude shrinks every round, and "the amplitude doesn't matter, I'll be there regardless" is smuggling in a branch-counting rule for anthropics, when he presumably uses amplitude-weighted probabilities for literally every other quantum prediction -- he cant have it both ways. (4) Marco replied that subjective anticipation just isn't the kind of thing that scales with amplitude: either some future observer-moment continuous with you exists downstream or it doesn't, and measure is a third-person fact about populations of branches that has no bearing on whether you personally get a next experience at all. (5) Sasha answered that this proves way too much -- by that logic he should be just as willing to play Russian roulette with five bullets loaded in a six-chambered revolver as with just one, since either way, conditional on still being around afterward, "he'll" be there to notice he survived -- and that's clearly nuts, so his own notion of subjective anticipation must be tracking probability whether he admits it or not.

Nobody at the table actually closed the loop, and honestly neither have I -- it feels adjacent to whatever Yudkowsky's old "Living in Many Worlds" post was gesturing at when it argued that many-worlds still has to reduce to ordinary statistical frequencies at the macro level, but I don't remember him ever settling this exact version of the argument. Could you go through moves (1) through (5) and say who's got the better of it at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'm especially curious whether (4) and (5) mark a genuine fork in how anthropic reasoning ought to work under MWI, or whether one side is just confused about what "subjective anticipation" could even mean here. And if there's some premise both of them are quietly taking for granted that's actually the crux, I'd like to hear about that too.
