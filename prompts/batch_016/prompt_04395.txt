I'm developing a piece on the history of "boundary science" — fields that once sat comfortably inside natural philosophy and have since been expelled from it — and Kepler's astrology is turning out to be a far richer case than the anecdote I originally set out to tell. Almost every popular account notes, usually with a knowing smirk, that the discoverer of the laws of planetary motion also cast horoscopes and wrote annual prognostications for money, as though this were simple hypocrisy or financial necessity overriding his better judgment — an impression he occasionally encouraged himself, having once described astronomy as a "wise mother" whose bills get paid by a "foolish daughter." But when you look at a work like Tertius Interveniens (1610), where he explicitly casts himself as a "third man" standing between credulous astrologers and astrology's wholesale critics, it becomes clear he wasn't defending the discipline as it stood. By his own account, he had already discarded the zodiac's twelve signs, the houses, the planetary rulerships, and the triplicities as arbitrary human bookkeeping with no basis in nature, while insisting that the aspects — the angular relationships between planets — were genuine physical causes, which he described as translating astrology into a "science of harmonics" rather than superstition.

What I haven't been able to get a satisfying grip on is the theoretical machinery that let him draw that line with such confidence. It seems to rest on some fairly strange metaphysical commitments: that the earth itself possesses an instinctual soul (his anima telluris) which reacts to the geometric proportions formed by planetary aspects, and that individual creatures have an analogous innate geometrical faculty, imprinted at birth by whichever aspects happen to be in force, which is supposedly why character and terrestrial events can be weakly but genuinely shaped by the heavens without any need for the zodiac. He works all of this out at length in Book IV of the Harmonice Mundi, tying it to the same compass-and-straightedge constructions of regular polygons that underwrite the book's broader theory of cosmic harmony. Could you explain how this argument actually hangs together — why aspects but not signs, why harmonic ratios rather than symbolic correspondence — and give your own assessment of whether it amounts to a genuinely principled distinction, or whether it's better understood as an elegant rationalization that let Kepler keep practicing, and profiting from, an astrology purged only of the parts he found most embarrassing?
