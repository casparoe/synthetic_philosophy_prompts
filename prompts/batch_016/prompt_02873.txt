I spent most of my working life as an atmospheric scientist, evaluating probabilistic forecasts by means of proper scoring rules such as the Brier score, so I was intrigued, on turning to philosophy in retirement, to find this very apparatus repurposed as the engine of an argument about epistemic rationality. James Joyce's "nonpragmatic vindication" of probabilism purports to show, without any appeal to betting behavior or Dutch books, that any credence function violating the probability axioms is accuracy-dominated: guaranteed to be less accurate, in every possible world, than some coherent rival, provided accuracy is measured by a "legitimate" (strictly proper) scoring rule. Two objections have given me pause. First, critics observe that which credence functions dominate which others is highly sensitive to the choice of scoring rule, so the argument's force rests on an independent, non-question-begging account of scoring-rule "legitimacy" that may not be available. Second, Michael Caie has argued that once credences in self-referential propositions are admitted, accuracy-dominance reasoning can itself recommend probabilistically incoherent credences, apparently turning the argument against its own conclusion. Write an essay assessing whether Joyce's vindication of probabilism survives these two challenges. Weigh the considerations on each side, and defend a definite verdict rather than an inconclusive one.
