I'm not a philosopher by training, just someone who picked up Benjamin Franklin's autobiography on my own a couple of months ago after getting curious about how much of today's habit-tracking, self-improvement culture actually traces back to him. The section that has really stuck with me is his account of his "bold and arduous project of arriving at moral perfection": he settles on thirteen virtues, builds a little chart to mark each day's failures against each one, and works through them one at a time for a week apiece, arranging the order so that mastering the earlier virtues would make the later ones easier to acquire.

What I keep coming back to, though, is a detail near the end of that section that I haven't seen discussed much. Franklin admits his list originally had only twelve virtues, and that he tacked on Humility as the thirteenth after a Quaker friend told him he came across as insufferably proud in conversation, giving it the precept "imitate Jesus and Socrates." He then confesses that he never had much success acquiring the "reality" of humility, only its outward appearance, and he closes the discussion with the joke that even if he had somehow conquered pride completely, he would probably end up being proud of his humility. When I mentioned this to a friend, she laughed it off as a throwaway line, but the more I sit with it, the more it looks like a real crack in the method: keeping score of your own humility on a chart, and feeling satisfied when that line stays clean, seems like exactly the kind of self-congratulation humility is supposed to rule out, in a way that never comes up when you're scoring yourself on frugality or cleanliness.

I'd like you to argue against my suspicion as rigorously as you can. Make the strongest case you can that Franklin's joke about being proud of his humility does not actually show that his week-by-week, chart-based method breaks down when applied to humility, and that his handling of this thirteenth virtue is no less defensible than his handling of the other twelve. Please take the self-undermining worry seriously rather than waving it away, and feel free to make use of his own distinction between the "reality" and the "appearance" of a virtue if it helps your case.
