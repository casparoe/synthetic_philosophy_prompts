I'm working on a piece about nineteenth-century anticipations of today's anxieties over media, virality, and public opinion, and I've run into a passage I can't fully untangle. It comes from the closing section of Kierkegaard's 1846 review of the novel "Two Ages" — the section usually excerpted and published on its own as "The Present Age" (I'm reading it in Alexander Dru's translation):

"More and more individuals, owing to their bloodless indolence, will aspire to be nothing at all — in order to become the public: that abstract whole formed in the most ludicrous way, by all participants becoming a third party (an onlooker). [...] If I tried to imagine the public as a particular person [...] I should perhaps think of one of the Roman emperors, a large well-fed figure, suffering from boredom, looking only for the sensual intoxication of laughter, since the divine gift of wit is not earthly enough. And so for a change he wanders about, indolent rather than bad, but with a negative desire to dominate. [...] In the same way the public keeps a dog to amuse it. That dog is the sum of the literary world. If there is some one superior to the rest, perhaps even a great man, the dog is set on him and the fun begins. The dog goes for him, snapping and tearing at his coat-tails, allowing itself every possible ill-mannered familiarity — until the public tires, and says it may stop. [...] The levelling is therefore done by a third party; a nonexistent public levelling with the help of a third party which in its insignificance is less than nothing, being already more than levelled. And so the public is unrepentant, for it was after all not the public that acted but the dog; just as one says to children — the cat's mother did it. [...] when their betters are held down by the insignificant and the insignificant by itself, then no one is quit of anything."

I follow the general thrust — that "the public" is an abstraction manufactured by the press, which lets people pass judgment on others without ever being personally accountable for it. What I can't quite reconstruct is the internal logic of the extended figure he builds around that claim. Why a bored Roman emperor specifically, rather than a tyrant or a mob? What work does the dog do that the emperor alone couldn't, and why call the dog "the sum of the literary world" instead of simply an instrument of the public? When he says the levelling is "done by a third party" that is "less than nothing, being already more than levelled," is he suggesting the press itself is degraded by playing this role, or is he making some other point about where responsibility actually ends up sitting? And how does the closing aside about children blaming "the cat's mother" sharpen his argument about repentance? I'd appreciate a close reading that shows how these pieces are meant to lock together into a single argument, rather than a general summary of Kierkegaard's views on the public.
