Quick one from a rabbit hole I fell into while reporting on the recent explosion of interest in invertebrate consciousness -- octopuses, bees, crabs, the whole "does it feel like something to be a crab" debate that's spilled out of academic journals into actual animal welfare policy lately. Everyone who works on this stuff eventually cites "Morgan's Canon," the rule from C. Lloyd Morgan's 1894 book "An Introduction to Comparative Psychology" that you shouldn't explain an animal's behavior by appeal to a higher mental faculty when a lower one will do. What almost nobody quotes is the rest of the chapter that Canon comes from (it's literally the closing paragraph of Chapter III, "Other Minds than Ours") -- and that's where Morgan actually works out, in a much weirder way, how much we're entitled to infer about animal minds in the first place.

The setup: imagine a chronometer that's somehow been gifted with intelligence, and has to figure out whether other timepieces have anything like a mind, reasoning only from its own internal workings, since it can't get inside theirs. Morgan then extends this into the bit that's actually tripping me up, comparing what the chronometer can infer about an identical chronometer, vs. a simpler-but-similar chronometer, vs. a kitchen clock, and mapping that onto what we can infer about other people, other vertebrates, and invertebrates. Here it is, cleaned up a little from a scanned copy but otherwise verbatim:

"The chronometer, we will suppose, is acquainted through introspection with its own psychology, and is able to take to pieces the works of other timepieces. It finds a number of chronometers whose works are all practically identical, and as it believes, but cannot demonstrate without taking itself to pieces, just like its own; and it is led to the inference that their psychology is similar to its own. It finds also a number of other timepieces whose works are constructed on similar principles, and differ chiefly in their being less highly finished and somewhat less complex; and it is led to the inference that their psychology, though less developed and less complex than its own, has probably been evolved on similar lines. But when it comes to the kitchen clock, it finds certain general similarities, cog-wheels and chains and so forth, but it also discovers principles of construction so different, the weights and the pendulum being so unlike its own balance-wheel and escapement, that it hesitates to draw any positive and definite conclusions. It sees that though the psychology of the kitchen clock may be closely analogous to its own, it may be quite different. It refuses to express a definite opinion on the psychology of the kitchen clock.

To apply the analogy in this modified form. Man, by anatomical and physiological research, has found in other men cerebral hemispheres with sensory-centres, control-centres, and so forth, similar to those which he believes that he individually possesses; and he infers that their psychology is of like nature to his own. He also finds in other vertebrates cerebral hemispheres, with sensory-centres and so forth, differing from man's chiefly in mass and complexity; and he infers that their psychology, though less developed and less complex than his own, has probably been evolved on similar lines. But when he comes to the insect, the crustacean, the mollusc, not to mention the worms, the sea-anemone, or the amoeba, he finds nervous systems so different in types of structure from his own, that he hesitates to draw any definite and positive conclusions concerning the psychical states of these animals. It is true that there are nerve-fibres and nerve-cells; but the manner of their arrangement is so different from that of the vertebrates to which he belongs, that the careful student of zoological psychology is forced to conclude that, though the psychical states of insects and crustacea may be similar to those of man, they may be markedly dissimilar.

It may indeed be contended that community of environment — the joint-tenancy of the same world — must necessarily beget community of psychical faculty to meet the requirements of that environment. But while admitting the soundness of this argument so far as it goes, I venture to submit that it does not go far. For why should the community of psychical nature be greater than that of physical nature? The anatomy or the physiology of insects, for example, differs tolerably widely from that of man — why then should he suppose that their psychical endowments are more closely similar?"

What I can't quite pin down is what work the three-step comparison (identical chronometers -> similar-but-simpler chronometers -> structurally different kitchen clock) is really doing, as opposed to just restating "structural similarity licenses inference to similarity of mind, and dissimilarity undermines it" three times with more decoration. Is there an actual qualitative break between the "simpler chronometer" case and the "kitchen clock" case, or is it a difference of degree dressed up as one of kind? And is his slap-down of the "joint-tenancy" objection at the end actually any good, or is he just asserting that psychical convergence needs the same kind of evidence as physical convergence without saying why that should be true? I ask because that objection is basically the modern argument for taking octopus cognition seriously -- convergent evolution producing complicated minds via a totally different neural architecture (funnily enough, Peter Godfrey-Smith's octopus book is literally called "Other Minds" too) -- so I'm curious whether Morgan's 1894 dismissal of it actually holds up, or whether hes just begging the question against the best objection to his own view.
