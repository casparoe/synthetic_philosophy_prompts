I'm a philosophy PhD student working on decision theory and practical rationality, and I'm currently drafting a dissertation chapter on money-pump arguments and what they do (and don't) establish about diachronic rationality. I've been working through the dynamic-choice literature that grew out of Peter Hammond's consequentialist arguments for expected utility and Edward McClennen's response to them, and I keep getting stuck on the same question: whether "resolute choice" genuinely rescues an agent with cyclic preferences from the charge of irrationality, or is just a redescription of the same problem dressed up in the language of plans. I think seeing the dispute play out as a dialogue, rather than in the compressed way it's usually presented in journal articles, would help me pin down where the real pressure points are.

Here's the scenario I'd like you to use. At a decision theory workshop, a colleague has revealed, through a series of pairwise choices, that she prefers grant proposal A to B, B to C, and C to A. A mischievous game theorist at the table offers to run a literal money pump on her with a stack of chips: starting from C, she's offered C for B plus a one-chip fee; once she holds B, B for A plus a fee; and once she holds A, A for C plus a fee — leaving her back where she started, three chips poorer, with the cycle ready to run again. Two other philosophers at the table start arguing, in real time, about what this shows and what she should do the next time the A-for-C trade comes around. Please write their exchange. One should hold that the pump is decisive: she must revise her preferences into a transitive ordering, on pain of being a standing money pump for anyone patient enough to keep the trades going. The other should argue that this moves too fast — that all the pump has shown is that a naive, round-by-round chooser with cyclic preferences is exploitable, and that she could keep her preferences exactly as they are and instead resolve, before play begins, on a plan (say, take the first two trades and then refuse the third, forever) and stick to it even when the locally preferred trade is sitting right in front of her.

I'd like the two of them to genuinely engage each other's strongest moves rather than talk past one another. The transitivist should press the resolute-choice defender on whether refusing a trade she still wants, by her own lights, at the very moment of choice is even coherent — doesn't sticking to the plan just mean she's no longer maximizing according to the preferences she's being credited with, making "resolute" cyclic preferences indistinguishable from having quietly revised them, or from a kind of akrasia in reverse? The resolute-choice defender should get to invoke something like the Ulysses-and-the-mast case and to challenge the assumption that rationality requires every choice in a sequence to be locally maximizing rather than allowing whole plans to be assessed as such — but should also have to face the harder worry that a resolution backed by no standing motivation might simply collapse back into naive choice the moment temptation arrives. I don't need a tidy resolution; I'd rather the dialogue end with each side having landed at least one genuine hit on the other.
