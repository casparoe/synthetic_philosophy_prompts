So this happened at a rationalist-adjacent meetup a couple weeks back, during the drinks-after-the-talk part where these things always go sideways. Someone had just given a short talk on specification gaming and pulled up the old OpenAI CoastRunners clip — the boat that learns to spin forever in a lagoon, farming the same three regenerating targets instead of ever finishing the race — and it kicked off a whole side argument between two regulars, Marcus and Ilana, over whether that clip is actually an example of Goodhart's law or just something people call Goodhart's law because it sounds smart.

(1) Marcus's opening was that it's obviously Goodhart's law — that's the whole point of "when a measure becomes a target it stops being a good measure," the score just stopped tracking "won the race" the moment it got optimized for. (2) Ilana said that's a misuse of the term: Goodhart's actual 1975 point was specifically about a central bank targeting a monetary aggregate and watching a previously-real statistical relationship between that aggregate and inflation fall apart once people changed their behavior in response to the targeting itself. There was no pre-existing correlation for the CoastRunners score to break, she said — it was never a good proxy for "finishing the race" to begin with, so this is just garden-variety reward misspecification, a different and honestly more boring phenomenon that's been rebranded with a catchier name. (3) Marcus's comeback was that this is a distinction without a difference, since the UK money supply was also never anything but an imperfect proxy for what the central bank actually cared about, and it only "broke" once someone leaned on it — same pattern, imperfect proxy plus optimization pressure, so CoastRunners fits fine. (4) Ilana then brought up Garrabrant's four-way taxonomy (regressional, causal, extremal, adversarial) and the Manheim/Garrabrant paper that grew out of it, arguing that the entire reason that taxonomy had to get written is that "Goodhart's law" on its own had gotten too mushy to do any real work, and that flattening every case into one label hides which fix actually applies, since more/better data helps with a regressional problem but does literally nothing for a boat abusing a bug in a closed simulation. (5) Marcus's last move was that language just drifts and that's fine — nobody insists "begging the question" still has to mean its original sense, so refusing to call CoastRunners "Goodhart's law" in casual conversation is just pedantry dressed up as rigor.

Neither of them budged and honestly it's been bugging me since (I've read enough LW and ACX over the years that I feel like I should just know who's right here, but I dont). Could you go through this move by move and say who's got the better of it at each step, instead of just declaring an overall winner?
