Since retiring from a career in experimental physics, where a great deal of my work involved Bayesian filtering of noisy detector data to extract weak signals from background noise, I have been spending my afternoons in a small reading group of fellow retirees working through classic papers in formal epistemology. This week we took up Bas van Fraassen's Reflection Principle, and I confess I cannot see what the philosophical fuss is about. As I understand it, the principle holds that one's current credence in a proposition A, conditional on the supposition that one's credence in A at some later time will take a specific value r, should itself equal r. But this looks to me like nothing more than the tower property of conditional expectation that I relied on constantly when combining successive rounds of measurement: E[E[X | F_t] | F_s] = E[X | F_s] for any earlier time s and later time t, where F_s and F_t denote the information accumulated by those times. If one's later credence is simply one's current credence updated by conditioning on whatever evidence arrives in the interim, then Reflection seems to fall straight out of the definition of conditional probability, no different from the internal consistency any coherent forecaster already satisfies as a matter of course.

Given this, I am at a loss as to why van Fraassen thought he needed an entire diachronic Dutch book argument to defend the principle, and why Christensen and other commentators have devoted so much effort to constructing purported counterexamples to what looks, from where I sit, like an elementary theorem of the probability calculus rather than a substantive and contestable norm of rationality. Could you help me see what I am missing here? I would be especially grateful if you could point to precisely where the analogy with my old tower-property calculations breaks down.
