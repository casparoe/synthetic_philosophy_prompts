I recently began my third year as a PhD student in social psychology, and I have just been put in charge of drafting statistical guidelines for my department's newly formed "research reform" committee - a task I inherited after our director grew alarmed following a spate of articles on the replication crisis. While gathering background material for this, I went back through my advisor's old lab notebooks from roughly a decade ago and discovered that the standard procedure at the time was to run a significance test every week or so during data collection and stop as soon as p dropped below .05, sometimes extending a study by dozens of participants if an early look came back null. Nobody thought twice about this at the time, but a few of the papers built on that practice are ones I am currently trying to extend for my own dissertation, so my interest in how worried to be is more than academic.

My advisor's proposed fix, which she wants the committee to adopt as binding policy for all future confirmatory studies in the department, is to require Bayesian analysis using Bayes factors instead of p-values, on the grounds that Bayesian updating obeys the likelihood principle and is therefore mathematically indifferent to when or why the experimenter decided to stop collecting data - unlike significance tests, which are demonstrably distorted by this kind of "peeking." That argument is appealing, but in reading further I have run into discussions of the Jeffreys-Lindley paradox, in which a result that looks decisively significant under classical testing can correspond to Bayesian evidence that actually favors the null, depending on the prior assigned to the alternative hypothesis - which makes me suspect that simply swapping frameworks is not the clean fix my advisor thinks it is. Before I put language in front of the committee that will bind how the whole lab analyzes data for years to come, could you lay out the most important implications of the likelihood principle and the Lindley paradox for this specific proposal - that is, whether requiring Bayes factors really would have protected our old studies from the stopping-rule problem, and how much weight a policy like this can actually bear compared with complementary reforms such as preregistration?
