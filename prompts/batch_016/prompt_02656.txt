I spent about thirty-five years as a field biologist studying corvid and parrot cognition before I retired, and now that I've got time on my hands I've been teaching myself philosophy, which I never had time for during my working life. Lately I've been slowly working through some material on classical Indian epistemology, specifically the Nyaya school's theory of the pramanas (the recognized "instruments" of knowledge), and I've gotten a bit stuck on their treatment of shabda, verbal testimony. Gautama's old definition is that testimony is the statement of an apta, someone trustworthy who both knows the truth of a matter and actually means to communicate it honestly, and later on Gangesha, in the testimony chapter of his Tattvacintamani, works out this whole apparatus of conditions a sentence has to satisfy -- akanksha, yogyata, sannidhi, tatparya -- before it can generate real knowledge in a hearer.

The bit that really got me, for obvious personal reasons, is a case Gangesha discusses of a parrot that's been trained to utter a sentence which happens to be true. Since the parrot doesn't know what it's saying and isn't trying to inform anyone of anything, he apparently classes this as mere "apparent testimony" (shabdabhasa) rather then the genuine article -- and, amusingly, the rival Prabhakara Mimamsa school disagreed and said the parrot's utterance is just as good as a human's, since on their view speaker intentions don't matter at all, only whether the hearer walks away with a true belief. Two very serious philosophical schools, arguing about parrots. Delighted me to no end.

Anyway, this got me thinking about chatbots. Say you put a modern LLM in front of Gangesha and had it answer some question accurately and fluently -- is that just the parrot case scaled up (pattern-matched words, nobody home, no apta forming any intention to tell the truth), or does it actually complicate things for him? I ask because I've seen it argued -- by a scholar named Das, I think, though I'm working from a secondhand summary -- that Gangesha's actual theory of testimony is more "localist" than the apta-talk suggests, i.e. what generates knowledge on his view is really the truth-conducive structure of the sentence plus the hearer's grasp of it, with facts about the speaker mattering only further upstream. If that's right, it's not obvious to me the parrot verdict transfers cleanly to an LLM. Could you reconstruct, as carefully as you can from Gangesha's actual apparatus and not just a kind of "ancient guy meets robot" vibe, what his considered verdict on LLM-generated testimony would probably be, and where the real pressure points in his own theory are?
