My younger brother and I jointly inherited our late aunt's lake house in northern Wisconsin, along with eleven acres of adjoining land and several hundred feet of lake frontage that a regional developer wants to buy for a small condominium project. The developer's offer is generous but expires in three weeks, and because we own the property jointly, it requires both of us to agree on a single asking price; neither of us can sell our half alone. Neither of us has commissioned an appraisal yet, so we genuinely do not know whether the land is worth closer to $700,000 or $1.3 million. The county tax assessment is years out of date, and comparable sales nearby vary enormously depending on frontage and zoning details that neither of us has looked into yet.

I mentioned all this to a friend of mine who spent several years in mergers and acquisitions, expecting sympathy, and instead got pulled into a long, fairly heated argument. His advice was that I should call my brother today, before either of us has real numbers, state a "final" price I am willing to accept, mention it to a few relatives so that backing down would be embarrassing, and then simply decline to negotiate further. His reasoning was that whoever locks in an irreversible position first effectively wins the negotiation, and that spending time gathering information just hands my brother the chance to do exactly that to me first. I objected that this seemed reckless precisely because we do not yet know the facts, and that if my brother thinks anything like I do, he might answer my ultimatum with an equally rigid one of his own, about the furniture or the boat, leaving neither of us able to back down without losing face and quite possibly costing us the developer's offer altogether. He did not think this undercut his advice at all; he said it just meant I needed to move faster and commit harder than my brother would.

I still cannot quite articulate what, if anything, is wrong with "commit first, commit hard, and commit before you know the facts," as opposed to it merely being risky in this particular case. Is my friend basically right that this is simply how rational bargaining works and I am just being squeamish about it, or is there something genuinely unsound about the strategy itself, independent of how things happen to turn out for me? I would like to understand what is actually going on here before I decide what to do about my brother and the lake house.
