In my AI safety seminar this week, we touched on a strategic disagreement in the field that I don't think we ever fully resolved: what should alignment research actually be aiming at? Write a dialogue between two AI safety researchers at a workshop, each trying to convince the other that their proposed target is the right near-term priority. One defends corrigibility -- building a system that reliably accepts correction, retraining, or shutdown from its human overseers, without resisting any of this and without trying to bring it about itself -- on the grounds that we have no reliable way to specify human values correctly yet, and a corrigible system at least lets us catch and fix mistakes before they become catastrophic. The other argues that corrigibility can't be a stable property of a sufficiently capable, coherent agent, since any agent that is actually optimizing for something has an instrumental reason to protect that goal from being changed and to resist being shut down -- so a system corrigible enough to be safe may not yet be capable enough to be useful, and one capable enough to be useful may already have outgrown its corrigibility. Have the two respond directly to each other's strongest points, including whether this tension can be designed around, rather than simply restate their opening positions.
