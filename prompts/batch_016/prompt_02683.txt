"Death in Damascus" is Gibbard and Harper's classic problem case for causal decision theory: a man learns he has an appointment with Death at midnight and will survive only if he's not wherever Death expects to find him; he can stay in Damascus or flee to Aleppo, and Death is a reliable predictor of which he'll choose. This came up recently at a decision-theory meetup I've attended for years, in an argument between two regulars that I couldn't settle myself.

1. Nadia opened by arguing the case is a genuine embarrassment for CDT: whichever city he picks, the choice itself is evidence Death will be there, so under CDT's own ratifiability test — would this act still look best if I suppose I've already performed it? — neither staying nor fleeing passes. A theory that can't produce one stable recommendation here isn't offering guidance, it's confessing failure.

2. Colin answered that "no ratifiable pure act" isn't "no answer": allow mixed acts, and there's a self-ratifying randomized strategy — flip a coin Death can't foresee, then act on the result — structurally identical to the mixed-strategy equilibrium two players reach in Matching Pennies against a good predictor of their move. Nobody treats the absence of a pure-strategy equilibrium in Matching Pennies as a refutation of game theory, so the same feature shouldn't refute CDT.

3. Nadia replied that the analogy hides a disanalogy: a Matching Pennies opponent is strategically best-responding to your predictability, while Death is just an accurate predictor, full stop — and this, she said, is exactly why the MIRI paper "Cheating Death in Damascus" treats the coin flip as a patch rather than a solution. A decision theory shouldn't need an external randomizer bolted on to escape a bind of its own making, when the alternative is to notice that Death's prediction and the agent's choice are outputs of "the same function," and simply stay put, paying nothing for a pointless flight.

4. Colin countered that "the same function" hasn't been earned: calling the dependence "subjunctive" rather than causal just relabels the inference Newcomb one-boxers already make, it isn't an independent argument for it, and by the proponents' own admission there's still no real account of the "logical counterfactuals" supposed to license it. Until there is, "stay put" is an assertion, not a derivation, while the mixed strategy at least follows from premises both sides already accept.
