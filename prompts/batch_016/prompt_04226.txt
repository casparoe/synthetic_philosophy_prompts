I don't have any formal background in mathematics or computer science, but for a while now I've been teaching myself about AI safety through blog posts, old forum threads, and the occasional technical paper that I work through slowly. Recently I spent a lot of time on Löb's theorem and what people in that world call the "Löbian obstacle" — the result that a formal system generally can't prove a statement like "if I can prove P, then P is true" unless it can already prove P outright, which turns out to cause real trouble when a proof-based AI agent tries to certify that a successor version of itself can be trusted.

What struck me is that, from everything I've read, the obstacle specifically kicks in when the successor's reasoning system is as strong as, or stronger than, the original agent's; a system can, in principle, still certify a strictly weaker successor as safe. That seems like a fairly devastating result for the whole idea of an "intelligence explosion," since the entire point of recursive self-improvement is that each successive version is smarter, and therefore presumably reasons in a strictly more powerful way, than the one before it. So it looks to me like Löb's theorem hands us an actual mathematical guarantee that a self-improving AI could never certify its own increasingly capable successors as safe, meaning the "foom" scenarios that people like Yudkowsky worry about are ruled out by logic before they could even get going. A couple of friends I've mentioned this to think I'm over-extending a narrow technical result, but the reasoning seems pretty airtight to me. Where exactly does it break down?
