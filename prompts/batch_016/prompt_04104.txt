I've spent a good part of this past year reading about the history of money for fun, partly because of some long-running arguments I keep having online about whether currency needs to be "backed" by something real, like gold, in order to count as legitimate. That interest eventually led me somewhere I didn't expect: eighteenth-century philosophy. I had always known George Berkeley only as the "to be is to be perceived" philosopher, so I was surprised to learn that he wrote an entire book of numbered questions, The Querist, largely concerned with credit and banking in Ireland, in which he argues that gold and silver have no special claim to being money at all. On his view, the "true idea" of money is really just a ticket or counter for reckoning and transferring goods, so that paper backed by nothing more than public confidence could serve just as well. What surprised me even more was learning that John Locke, decades earlier, had written at length on this very same question and ended up in almost the opposite place: in his pamphlets from the 1690s recoinage debates, he insists that money works as a "counter" only because it also carries an intrinsic value fixed by the actual quantity of silver it contains, so that raising a coin's face value without changing its silver content amounts to quietly cheating everyone who holds contracts in that currency.

It strikes me as strange that two thinkers usually grouped together as founders of British empiricism could end up so far apart on something as basic as what money actually is, and I haven't come across anything that really explains why they diverged. Could you compare Locke's and Berkeley's positions in some depth: where exactly the disagreement lies, and whether it is ultimately just a difference of economic opinion, or whether it grows out of some deeper difference in how each of them understood signs, representation, or the reality standing behind our ideas? And once you've laid that out, I would value your own judgment on which of the two views comes closer to how money actually functions in a modern economy built on fiat currency and electronic credit rather than gold coin, and why.
