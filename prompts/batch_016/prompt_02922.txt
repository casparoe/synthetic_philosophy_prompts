I spent thirty-five years as a geophysicist before I retired, and now that I've got the time I've been working through Bayesian epistemology, partly because it's the closest philosophy gets to what I actually did for a living (updating beliefs about what's underground from noisy seismic data). Lately I've been chewing on van Fraassen's "Reflection Principle" -- roughly, the idea that if you're already certain what credence you'll assign to some claim at a later time, your current credence should match it now, otherwise you're exploitable via a "diachronic Dutch book," a sequence of bets offered at different times that's rigged so you loose money no matter how things turn out. I brought this up at the little philosophy group four of us retirees run out of the library, and two of the members, Owen and Renate, got into an argument about it that's been bugging me ever since because I honestly can't tell who came out ahead.

Owen's opening move (1) was that Reflection is just formalizing something obviously sensible: your future self, having lived through more of your life and learned more in the meantime, is like an expert witness, and you should defer now to what you know the expert will eventually say. Renate answered (2) with a case she'd come across: suppose you know for certain that in an hour you're going to take a pill that'll make you fully convinced you can breathe underwater, even though the pill does nothing whatsoever to your actual lungs. Reflection seems to say your credence in "I can breathe underwater" should already be rising now, which is plainly absurd. Owen said (3) that's not a fair test, since van Fraassen only meant Reflection to cover cases where your opinion is expected to shift through genuine learning, not through a pill that just scrambles your judgment -- add that proviso and the pill case is excluded by definition. Renate came back (4) saying that fix needs some independent, advance way of certifying which future belief-shifts count as "genuine learning" rather than corruption, and the Dutch book argument itself can't supply this, since a bookie can walk you into a sure loss using only your current beliefs whether your future self got there by real evidence or by the pill -- so the "you'll be exploited" argument is blind to exactly the distinction Owen needs it to draw. Owen's reply (5) was that every diachronic updating rule, including ordinary conditionalization, quietly leans on some background assumption about what counts as a legitimate learning experience, so Renate's complaint isn't a special problem for Reflection, it's a problem for the whole Bayesian picture, and its unfair to single Reflection out for it.

Could you go through moves (1) through (5) one at a time and say who's got the better of it at each step, not just who wins overall? I'd also really like to know whether Owen's move (5) actually gets him out of Renate's circularity charge in (4), or whether conditionalization's background assumptions are relevantly different from the one Reflection needs here -- that's the part where I keep going back and forth and can't settle it myself.
