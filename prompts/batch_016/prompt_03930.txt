I'm a software engineer without any formal background in philosophy, but I recently got pulled into a debate after a friend who's finishing her PhD in epistemology brought up Timothy Williamson's argument against the "KK principle" — the thesis that if you know that P, you're thereby in a position to know that you know P (she wrote it out as Kp → KKp). She sketched the setup from his book *Knowledge and Its Limits*: imagine someone who feels freezing cold at dawn and warms up so gradually that by noon they feel hot, with a "case" recorded every millisecond such that no two consecutive cases are discriminable from one another in how cold the person feels. The argument seems to hinge on the idea that knowledge requires some kind of margin for error, or "safety" — roughly, that your belief couldn't easily have been false, or wouldn't be false in any sufficiently similar neighboring case — and then uses the indiscriminability of adjacent cases to chain from the freezing-cold case all the way to the hot case, deriving a contradiction. The upshot is supposed to be that "feeling cold" isn't luminous (you can be in that state without being in a position to know you're in it), and that essentially the same reasoning ends up sinking the KK principle more generally.

I followed each individual step she wrote down, but the argument as a whole hasn't really clicked for me — it has the flavor of a sorites paradox, and I suspect some premise is doing more work than it looks like it's doing. I'd appreciate help building real intuition for it, rather than just being walked through the symbols again. Specifically: (1) Why think knowledge requires this strong, no-false-belief-in-any-neighboring-case notion of safety, rather than just reliability on average — what would go wrong if we rejected that premise instead of accepting the argument's conclusion? (2) The "feeling cold" case is about a raw sensation; why does showing that it isn't luminous tell us anything about the KK principle for an arbitrary proposition P, given that "knowing that P" seems like a very different kind of state from a bodily sensation? (3) Is there anything special about introspection or self-knowledge going on here, or would a structurally identical argument equally undermine, say, ordinary perceptual knowledge of a continuously varying external quantity, like the height of a growing tree? I'd like to come away actually seeing why the argument is supposed to work, not just able to recite its premises.
