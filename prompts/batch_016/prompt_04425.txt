For a piece on the limits of expected-value reasoning, I've been digging into the long-running dispute over the Kelly criterion. The Kelly rule — stake the fraction of your bankroll that maximizes the expected logarithm of wealth — is usually defended on the grounds that it maximizes long-run growth with probability one, but Paul Samuelson called this reasoning a "fallacy" and underlined his scorn by later writing an entire journal article in one-syllable words, insisting that log-utility betting is just one arbitrary preference dressed up as a mathematical law. The "ergodicity economics" reply associated with Ole Peters tries to rescue Kelly on different grounds: ordinary expected-value reasoning, it says, quietly assumes time averages equal ensemble averages, an assumption that fails once wealth compounds, so a bettor who actually lives through repeated rounds ought to use geometric rather than arithmetic averaging as a fact about the dynamics, not a matter of taste. More recently, Scott Garrabrant has generalized this into a broader "geometric rationality," extending geometric-expectation maximization to cases like Nash bargaining and Bayesian updating, while explicitly conceding that doing so means giving up the independence axiom behind ordinary expected-utility theory. Write an essay adjudicating this dispute: does the ergodicity argument actually make geometric-growth maximization non-optional for an agent facing repeated multiplicative bets, rather than merely one defensible utility function among others, and does whatever answer you reach carry over to the domains geometric rationality has been extended to, or does it stay confined to compounding wealth? Weigh the strongest arguments on each side, but end by defending a specific verdict rather than surveying them.
