So this happened at my math team practice and I haven't been able to stop thinking about it. Our coach put up this old probability/philosophy puzzle called the "Judy Benjamin problem" as a bonus brain-teaser before we got into the actual practice set. Setup: a soldier gets dropped into a region split into Red territory and Blue territory, and each of those is split again into "Headquarters Company" area and "Second Company" area, so there's 4 equally likely quadrants to start (25% each). Then right before her radio cuts out she gets told: "if you're in Red territory, the odds are 3:1 you're in the Headquarters area" -- and that's literally the only new info she gets. Question is how she should update her probability of being in each of the 4 quadrants now.

Two of my teammates, Dan and Aisha, got into an actual argument over it walking home and neither one backed down. Dan said it's obvious -- the message is only talking about the split inside Red, so you leave Red vs Blue at 50/50 like before, and just redistribute Red's 50% into 37.5% HQ and 12.5% Second (matching the 3:1), leaving both Blue quadrants untouched at 25% each. Aisha said she looked it up and the actual standard method for updating on this kind of info (something called minimizing relative entropy -- I guess an info-theory thing, we haven't touched that in class) doesn't give that answer at all, it bumps Blue up to something like 53% overall, even though the message never said one word about Blue. Dan said that's gotta be broken, because info that's 100% about the internal Red split can't possibly tell you anything about Red vs Blue overall, thats like saying your grade changing in math should shift your grade in a totally unrelated class. Aisha admitted it sounds insane but said the math doesn't care how it sounds, and maybe our confidence that "it's obviously still 50/50" is exactly the kind of intuition that's usually wrong in these problems, kinda like Monty Hall. Dan wasn't having it and said if a formula spits out something that violates a principle that basic, that's a strike against the formula, not against the intuition.

Neither of them convinced the other and our coach just laughed and said "great, now you know why people write whole papers about this" instead of actually settling it. Can you go through their back-and-forth move by move and say who's right about what (not just who wins overall), and point out anything either of them is just flatly getting wrong, or anything important that both of them are missing? I'd also really like to know whether this puzzle actually has a settled answer, or if it's genuinely one of those cases where smart people can reasonably disagree about how you're even supposed to update on conditional info like that.
