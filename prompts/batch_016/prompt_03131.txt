I started a new job about a month ago doing technical policy/governance work at an AI company - mostly writing internal memos, nothing glamorous - and I've been trying to cram on alignment theory in the evenings so I don't sound clueless in meetings. This week I fell down a rabbit hole on "coherence arguments," which is apparently a big part of why people think advanced AI systems will end up behaving like expected-utility maximizers with stable goals. The basic idea, as I understand it: if an agent's preferences aren't "coherent" (consistent, transitive, complete, whatever combination of those words applies), it can get money-pumped - like if it prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A, someone can cycle it through trades and skim a fee off it each time until its broke - so any sufficiently smart, self-improving system should route around that kind of free exploitability as it gets more capable.

Here's why this actually matters to me and isn't just a fun math fact: in a meeting this week, one of the senior researchers used this to basically declare the corrigibility debate settled. Her argument was that since coherence is mathematically forced on any sufficiently capable agent, its essentially a law of nature that a smart enough AI will resist having its goals edited or being shut down, since letting that happen would score worse by its own current utility function - meaning all the "build an off-switch the AI won't fight" research is fighting math itself, not solving a real engineering problem. Kind of a depressing thought to have a month into a job you took partly because you cared about that research.

Someone else in the room pushed back, saying something about the money-pump arguments quietly assuming completeness instead of actually proving it, and that coherence and corrigibility might not even be in tension in the first place - but I couldn't really follow the objection and didn't want to ask a dumb question in front of everyone. So: is "coherence theorems prove shutdown-resistance is inevitable, therefore corrigibility research is pointless" actually right? And if not, could you walk me through exactly where the reasoning breaks, ideally in terms I could go explain back to my team without embarrassing myself?
