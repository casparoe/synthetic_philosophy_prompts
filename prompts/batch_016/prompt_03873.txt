I'm working on a novel set partly in West Germany in the years just after the war. In my research I came across the real "grudge informer" case that H. L. A. Hart later wrote about: in 1944 a woman denounced her husband to the authorities for derogatory remarks he'd made about Hitler while home on leave, apparently wanting to be rid of him. He was sentenced to death but was sent to the front instead, and survived the war. In 1949 she was prosecuted for unlawfully depriving him of his liberty; her defence was that she had simply relied on a statute validly in force at the time. The court held that the Nazi-era statute was, in its usual translation, contrary to "the sound conscience and sense of justice of all decent human beings," and convicted her.

I mentioned this to two friends, Nora and Sam, and they argued about it long after the rest of the table had moved on:

(1) Nora: The court got it right. A rule that makes a private complaint about the head of state a capital offence isn't law in any sense that could shelter her, so convicting her now isn't retroactive punishment of a lawful act — it was never a lawful act.

(2) Sam: Saying it "was never law" just avoids the truth. By every ordinary mark it was law: enacted, published, applied for a decade. It's more honest to admit we're retroactively punishing an act that was legal when she did it, and to do that openly, through a frankly retroactive statute, rather than pretend the old rule never existed.

(3) Nora: That "ordinary mark" is exactly what's in question. Plenty of regimes enact things properly; no one living under this one thought it commanded real law's obedience. Insisting on the label because the paperwork was in order isn't honesty, it's a quieter fiction — that form alone certifies a rule as law regardless of content.

(4) Sam: That proves too much. Once "wicked enough" can strip a rule of legal status, any judge can disregard any statute they personally find monstrous. The formal test earns its keep by not depending on anyone's moral judgment — and here it isn't even close: everyone in 1944 knew exactly what the statute forbade.

(5) Nora: Did they? No one could have predicted, from the wording alone, that a remark to one's own spouse counted as capital treachery — that took secret directives and terror prosecutions that had already abandoned consistent, public application. The system fails your own test for a working legal order; that's the real reason to hesitate over the word "law," not squeamishness about content.

(6) Sam: Even so — if inconsistency elsewhere can retroactively unmake a rule that was clearly worded and consistently applied to this husband, we're still just deciding, case by case, which enactments we'll keep honouring. That should be a legislature's call, made openly in a statute passed for the purpose, not a court's, reached by quietly declaring the old rule never existed.

Could you adjudicate this move by move — who has the better of it at each step, not just overall — and note anything you think both of them are missing?
