I spent my career as an experimental physicist working on optics (lasers, waveguides, that sort of thing), and in retirement I've fallen into a philosophy reading group at my old university that's slowly working through Leibniz's Theodicy — they're nice enough to let a retiree sit in. What hooked me wasn't the theology so much as something I stumbled on almost by accident: Leibniz was one of the people who helped get the ball rolling on the idea that nature "chooses" extremal paths — least time for light bending through a lens, least action in mechanics. In some essay of his (Tentamen Anagogicum, I think, though I can barely pronounce it) he says flat out that this is the very same principle by which God picked out the best of all possible worlds to actualize, the way a ray of light finds the quickest route between two points. Lovely idea. But it also set off an alarm bell from my old day job, because in plenty of real optimization problems there's no minimum to find at all — just an infimum you can keep sneaking up on forever without ever landing on it. So I went looking to see if anyone had raised the equivalent objection against Leibniz, and sure enough: apparently there's a whole line of argument that there might not be any "best possible world" in the first place, since for any world, however good, you can always conceive of a slightly better one — so God couldn't have picked "the best" any more than a ball can settle at the bottom of a bowl that has no bottom. And apparently some people try to save the doctrine anyway, for instance by having God actualize an entire multiverse of every sufficiently good world instead of just one "best" one.

Could you write me an essay that actually engages with this, rather than the standard Voltaire-and-earthquakes routine I've already read a dozen versions of? Lay out the strongest case that there is no best possible world, then the best moves available to a Leibniz-style optimist for rescuing the doctrine anyway (the multiverse idea, denying that "better than" has to bottom out in a unique maximum, restricting which worlds even count as eligible for comparison, or whatever you think is strongest), and then actually adjudicate between them. I don't want "its complicated" as the ending — give me an actual verdict, even a hedged one like "the doctrine survives, but only in some watered-down form," and argue for it rather than just asserting it.
