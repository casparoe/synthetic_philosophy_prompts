I'm part of a small reading group working through Spinoza's political writings, and after last week's session on the Political Treatise, a friend from the group and I ended up arguing for the better part of an evening about something that has been bothering me ever since. In the Theological-Political Treatise, Spinoza calls democracy "the most natural" form of state, arguing that in a democracy no one transfers their natural right to another so completely as to be shut out of any future say over it -- each person remains, in principle, part of the sovereign body itself, so democracy alone avoids permanently concentrating everyone's right in the hands of one person or a few. But in the unfinished final chapter of the Political Treatise, where he actually specifies who counts as a citizen, Spinoza excludes women from citizenship -- that is, from any share in political power -- and, unlike his treatment of servants or foreigners, whose exclusion he ties to contingent social dependency, he explicitly insists that women's exclusion is natural rather than conventional. His argument is comparative: if women had, by nature, the same capacities of mind (ingenium) and strength of character as men, we should expect to find at least one society, somewhere in recorded history, where the sexes rule jointly or where women rule and men are raised to be the lesser party; since no such society exists, the inequality must be natural rather than a mere accident of custom. He even takes up the Amazons as a possible counterexample and dismisses them by claiming they held on to power only by killing their male children at birth, so that no adult men were ever around to reassert dominance. A few sentences later, the chapter -- and the treatise -- breaks off.

My friend's position was that this isn't a stray prejudice we should try to explain away: it follows from Spinoza's own definition of natural right as strictly coextensive with power. If he sincerely believed, however wrongly, that women collectively lacked the power to govern themselves and others, then withholding a share of political right from them isn't a departure from his naturalism -- it's that very naturalism operating exactly as designed on a false empirical premise; the theory is fine, the input is bad. I argued that this can't be right, because the inference he actually makes -- from "no society has ever shown X" to "X is naturally impossible" -- is precisely the sort of superstitious, quasi-teleological reasoning that the appendix to Ethics I spends its time teaching readers to catch themselves making, and a notion of power grounded in reason and cooperative self-preservation gives no principled reason to expect it to track sex in the first place. On my view, the passage doesn't apply Spinoza's system correctly so much as violate standards he defends everywhere else in it. We went back and forth for a long time, and neither of us gave any ground.

Could you write an essay that actually works through this rather than leaving it as a draw? Lay out the strongest version of each reading -- that the exclusion of women is a consistent, if repugnant, application of Spinoza's naturalized theory of right and power, and that it is instead a genuine internal inconsistency, a point where his own argument fails by his own standards -- and then defend one of them. I'd also like you to address what, if anything, this implies for the more celebrated argument in the Theological-Political Treatise: does the Political Treatise passage merely sit awkwardly next to the claim that democracy is the most natural regime, or does it actually threaten that claim?
