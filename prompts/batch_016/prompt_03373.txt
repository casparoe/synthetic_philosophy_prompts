Fitting-attitude (or "buck-passing") analyses of value hold that for something to be good, admirable, or otherwise valuable just is for there to be reason to take up some favoring attitude toward it — to desire it, admire it, and so on. A well-known difficulty for this view is the "wrong kind of reasons" (WKR) problem, usually introduced via some version of a case that became famous through Wlodek Rabinowicz and Toni Rønnow-Rasmussen's 2004 paper "The Strike of the Demon": an evil demon threatens to torture a great many people unless you come to admire him. This threat plainly gives you a weighty reason to admire the demon, yet nobody wants to say that the demon has thereby become admirable, or that your admiration would be fitting. Defenders of the buck-passing view therefore owe us some principled way of excluding reasons like this one — reasons grounded merely in the good or bad consequences of having an attitude — while still allowing that reasons grounded in an object's actual qualities can make it valuable. The trouble is that the two best-known strategies for drawing this line both run into serious difficulty: Derek Parfit's proposal that only "object-given" reasons, and not merely "state-given" ones, can be of the right kind has been argued by Mark Schroeder and others to collapse, on the grounds that state-given reasons turn out to be far more common, and far harder to tell apart from ordinary right-kind reasons, than the distinction can accommodate; and the alternative move of saying that a threat gives you reason only to want to have the attitude, not reason to actually have it, seems to founder on cases where the threatened party could simply decide, at will, to have the attitude in question.

I've been working through the responses to this problem for a few weeks now and have yet to find one that strikes me as more than a temporary patch. I'd like you to argue for the position that the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem has no principled solution, and that this is not a peripheral technical embarrassment but a genuinely fatal problem for the entire project of analyzing evaluative properties in terms of reasons for attitudes. Please engage directly with the object-given/state-given proposal and explain concretely why it fails, rather than simply asserting that it does, and address whether the structurally identical problem that arises for reasons for belief — where evidentialists distinguish genuine, truth-directed reasons to believe something from merely prudential ones, of the sort highlighted by Pascal's wager — shows that the difficulty is a completely general feature of the concept of a normative reason, rather than a special defect of value theory that some sufficiently clever fix might repair.
