I'm a software engineer whose closest brush with moral philosophy came from a year spent building software that supported targeting decisions for a defense contractor, which left me unsettled in ways I couldn't quite articulate until I came across the debate over the 'moral equality of combatants.' The traditional view, defended by Michael Walzer, holds that soldiers who fight within the laws of war (jus in bello) are moral equals regardless of which side's cause is actually just, since responsibility for the war itself rests with political leaders rather than the people who fight it. Jeff McMahan's revisionist critique denies this, arguing that a combatant fighting for an unjust cause lacks the standing to kill an opposing combatant who fights justly, however scrupulously the unjust combatant follows the rules or however blameless they are for not knowing their cause is unjust.

Write a dialogue, set backstage after a public panel on autonomous weapons, between a defender of Walzer's equality thesis and a defender of McMahan's revisionism, working through a single hard case: a conscripted, non-culpably ignorant soldier who kills a just combatant while fully complying with the laws of war. Have each press the other on the toughest points raised against their own view — the analogy to individual self-defense, the difference between being excused and being permitted, and the practical cost of abandoning combatant equality as a shared convention — and don't let either one come out on top too easily.
