That argument reminded me of something a friend doing a history PhD once mentioned in passing: a mid-20th-century debate in philosophy of history over whether historical explanations work like scientific ones, by citing general laws, or whether they're doing something else entirely, namely telling a story. I read around a little (Hempel's original 1942 paper, plus secondary material on Dray, Danto, and Mink) and ended up drafting the essay below, trying to map that debate onto how we write, and argue about, incident postmortems at work.

Postmortems as History: Root Causes, Explanation Sketches, and Narrative Sentences

Every postmortem template I've used asks, in some form, for "the root cause." In practice this produces a document trying to do two things at once, and I've started to suspect the tension between them is a smaller-scale replay of an old argument in philosophy of history.

One half of a postmortem wants to behave like Carl Hempel's covering-law model of explanation: an event is explained by showing it had to happen, given a general law plus the specific starting conditions. Hempel argued this holds for history too, even though, as he conceded, most historical writing only offers an "explanation sketch" that gestures at the relevant law and conditions without ever spelling them out fully. Our postmortems read a lot like explanation sketches: "the payment service fell over because a config push interacted with an already-degraded connection pool during a traffic spike" gestures at a general pattern (pools degrade under sustained latency; spikes exhaust degraded pools) without stating it as a law, on the assumption that a fuller engineering theory could fill in the gap. The whole point of the "action items" section, adding a circuit breaker, capping retries, alerting on pool saturation earlier, is to convert the sketch into something with real predictive and preventive force, exactly the "filling out" Hempel says a sketch calls for.

The other half behaves more like what William Dray called rational explanation: it reconstructs why it made sense for the on-call engineer to do what she did, given what she could see at the time, rather than deriving the outage from a law connecting the deploy to the crash. Dray's objection to Hempel was precisely that this kind of explanation doesn't need a covering law to succeed; it succeeds by making each step intelligible on its own terms. Arthur Danto's idea of a "narrative sentence" seems even more apt for the root-cause line itself: we write, after the fact, that "the root cause was a schema migration three weeks earlier," but nobody could have described the migration that way at the time, any more than someone in 1618 could have said the Thirty Years' War had just begun. The root cause, in other words, is partly constituted retrospectively, by its place in a story we can only tell once we know the ending, which is a strange thing for a supposedly objective RCA document to rest on.

I initially wanted to conclude that the covering-law framing is the "real" explanation and the narrative is just a human-readable gloss on it, useful for a blameless retro but not doing any actual explanatory work. Louis Mink's argument that narrative provides a distinct, irreducible "configurational" mode of comprehension, grasping a set of events together as parts of one story rather than as instances of a general type, makes me want to resist that conclusion. A postmortem's narrative section may not be a placeholder for a law we haven't found yet; sociotechnical systems might simply be too idiosyncratic, path-dependent, and populated by reasoning agents to support the kind of regularities Hempel's model needs, so the "filling out" he promises may never fully arrive. If that's right, a good postmortem is permanently doing two different cognitive jobs at once, configuring a particular sequence into an intelligible story, and gesturing at generalizable, actionable patterns, and the recurring argument about which sentence belongs under "root cause" versus "contributing factors" is really an argument about which job a given sentence is doing.
