I'm about two-thirds into a novel about an aging, once-celebrated writer who spends his final years disowning most of his own early books (loosely Tolstoy-shaped, if I'm honest), so I've been down a research hole reading his actual nonfiction -- What Is Art?, and then that stranger later essay where he goes through King Lear scene by scene and decides its basically incompetent as drama. Mentioned this at my writers' group last week as a bit of trivia, and two people, Dev and Meg, ended up in a proper argument over whether Tolstoy's takedown of Lear is a serious, principled application of his own theory of art or just an old man being a crank about a play he happened to hate. Neither gave an inch, so I wrote down the gist after.

(1) Dev said it isn't crankery: Tolstoy's theory says good art needs individuality, clarity, and sincerity of feeling, and he shows, scene by scene, that the opening love-test makes no psychological sense and that every king in the play talks in the same pompous register no matter who they are -- so "bad art" just falls out of premises Tolstoy already held. (2) Meg said individuality can't mean literal realism of speech, or all verse tragedy fails by definition -- Racine, Aeschylus, even Tolstoy's own stylized dialogue -- and the storm scenes have flattened audiences for four centuries, which is exactly what "infectious" is supposed to track, so Tolstoy's "unnatural, therefore insincere" move just smuggles in a taste for naturalism the theory never licenses. (3) Dev answered that four centuries of trained critical response isn't neutral evidence, since Tolstoy explicitly argues educated people only feel moved because they were raised from childhood to expect to be -- closer to hypnotic suggestion than genuine infection -- so the real test is an unprepared reader, not a Shakespeare professor. (4) Meg said that proves too much, since the folk legends and religious parables Tolstoy loved also take cultural training to land -- and besides, the more honest explanation is biographical: Lear is the one play about an old man who gives his land and title away and gets humiliated for it, awfully close to what Tolstoy was doing to his own family in those exact years, so of course it's the play he needed to be bad. (5) Dev said the biographical bit is a genetic fallacy dressed up -- explaining why Tolstoy might want a conclusion doesn't show his textual arguments are wrong -- and that the "he'd never nuke his favorites" charge doesn't work either, since Tolstoy also trashed most of his own acclaimed novels on the very same grounds.

Neither of them would concede anything, and I honestly dont know Tolstoy's essay or the play well enough to referee it myself. Could you go through it move by move, say who actually had the better of it at each numbered point -- not just who won overall -- and flag anything you think both of them are missing?
