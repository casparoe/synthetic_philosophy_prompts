I don't have any formal background in philosophy, but I've spent a good part of this year reading about Jain thought on my own, after a colleague used the old story of the blind men and the elephant to explain why my brother and I can never agree about anything. That sent me looking into anekantavada, and from there into syadvada, the idea that a properly careful statement has to be qualified with something like "in some respect" before it can count as true. As I understand it, this was worked out in real technical detail over many centuries, down to a seven-part scheme for how a single claim, such as "the pot exists," can come out true, false, both, or strictly unsayable depending on which angle you take. When I mentioned this to a couple of friends who know more philosophy than I do, both said some version of "oh, that's just relativism" and left it there, which matched my own first impression anyway.

The more I sat with it, though, the more it looked to me like exactly the kind of relativism that refutes itself. If every statement is only true "in some respect," then the statement "every statement is only true in some respect" should itself be only true in some respect, which seems to leave anyone free to reject it from their own angle without ever being wrong, so the whole doctrine quietly swallows its own tail. It is hard for me to believe that generations of Jain logicians who clearly cared about rigor never noticed something this basic, so I suspect the fault is in my own understanding rather than theirs. Could you help me see what I am missing here: is there something about what "in some respect" is actually doing in their logic that blocks this kind of self-refutation, or is "many-sided truth" ultimately just relativism with better branding?
