This term I am teaching Fitch's paradox of knowability in a graduate seminar on realism and anti-realism about truth, where the anti-realist's animating thought is that there can be no truths lying permanently beyond the reach of anyone's possible knowledge. I have run into a recurring pedagogical problem: once students have reconstructed the formal proof for themselves, they tend to treat the various replies to it in the secondary literature as a set of disconnected technical maneuvers, rather than as moves made by two people who are genuinely disagreeing with one another about something. I would like a dialogue I can hand out that restores the dispute to something closer to its natural form.

As background: the paradox begins from the knowability principle - the claim that every truth is such that it is possible for someone to know it - together with the modest concession that at least one truth is not, in fact, known by anyone. Substituting that very truth ("such-and-such is the case, and nobody knows it") back into the knowability principle yields the claim that it must be possible to know this conjunction. But knowing a conjunction requires knowing each of its conjuncts, and because knowledge is factive, knowing the second conjunct - that one does not know the first - entails that one really does not know it. So whoever knew the whole conjunction would have to both know it and not know it, which is impossible. And because that impossibility is secured by logic alone rather than by any contingent fact, it holds in every possible circumstance - which means it was never even possible to know the conjunction in the first place. The upshot, notoriously, is that the knowability principle, together with the near-truism that we are not omniscient, entails the absurd conclusion that every truth actually is known by someone.

Please write a dialogue, set just after this proof has gone up on the whiteboard during office hours, between the anti-realist who defends the knowability principle and a realist colleague who has wandered in partway through. The anti-realist should attempt at least two genuinely different ways of rescuing some non-trivial version of the thesis - for instance, restricting the range of propositions to which it applies, and, separately, questioning whether the derivation is entitled to help itself to classical logic at every step - while the realist contests each proposal on its own merits rather than simply reiterating that the conclusion is absurd, pressing in particular on whether each fix is independently motivated or has instead been cut to the exact shape of this one argument, and on how much of the original anti-realist position survives the repair. The two interlocutors should be equally rigorous, neither should be allowed to win by authorial fiat, and the dialogue may end without a tidy resolution if that is honestly where the exchange leads.
