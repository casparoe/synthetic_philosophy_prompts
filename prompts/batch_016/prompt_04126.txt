I'm about two-thirds into a historical novel set in Fustat in the 1180s, and one of my secondary characters is a physician who's basically a thinly veiled Maimonides (I know, a bit on the nose, but I cant help myself). There's a scene I want to write where he has to talk down a young hazzan who's furious that "the philosophers" are explaining away the Temple sacrifices as basically glorified primitive superstition. So instead of just working off vibes I went back to the actual source, dug up the Guide for the Perplexed, Part III, chapter 32 (Friedländer's old translation, hence the clunky phrasing), and copied out the bit I think my guy would actually say to him:

"Many precepts in our Law are the result of a similar course adopted by the same Supreme Being. It is, namely, impossible to go suddenly from one extreme to the other: it is therefore according to the nature of man impossible for him suddenly to discontinue everything to which he has been accustomed... the general mode of worship in which the Israelites were brought up, consisted in sacrificing animals in those temples which contained certain images, to bow down to those images, and to burn incense before them... It was in accordance with the wisdom and plan of God, as displayed in the whole Creation, that He did not command us to give up and to discontinue all these manners of service; for to obey such a commandment it would have been contrary to the nature of man, who generally cleaves to that to which he is used; it would in those days have made the same impression as a prophet would make at present if he called us to the service of God and told us in His name, that we should not pray to Him, not fast, not seek His help in time of trouble; that we should serve Him in thought, and not by any action. For this reason God allowed these kinds of service to continue."

Two things about this chapter are tripping me up and I don't want my character to just hand-wave through them the way I'm doing right now. First, right before this bit Maimonides spends a whole paragraph on how nerves and brain matter develop gradually from soft to hard in a growing animal, and I honestly can't tell if that's just flavor or if he's actually leaning on it to do real argumentative work about the sacrifices. Second, a page or two later he quotes Jeremiah flatly saying God never commanded burnt offerings at all ("this thing commanded I them, saying, Obey my voice...") and then admits himself that "this passage has been found difficult" — fair, since it seems to contradict half of Leviticus. His way out of the difficulty seems to switch between two different moves, one about "primary" versus "secondary" objects of the law and one about what was literally commanded first at Marah, and I can't tell if those are really the same argument said twice or if he's hedging because neither one fully works on its own. Could you walk me through what's actually going on logically in both spots, and maybe flag whether this kind of gradualist reasoning has a real weak point worth having my hazzan character push back on?
