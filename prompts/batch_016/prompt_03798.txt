I spent about thirty-five years doing condensed matter physics before I hung up my lab coat, and now that I've got time on my hands I've been going down a philosophy of science rabbit hole, partly to procrastinate on cleaning out the garage. Somehow I landed on this whole debate around scientific realism, and specifically an argument I gather is called the "pessimistic meta-induction" -- originally from Larry Laudan, I think. The basic idea, as I understand it: history is littered with theories that were once wildly successful, made loads of correct predictions, and are now known to be flat wrong (phlogiston, caloric fluid, the various luminiferous ethers, circular celestial spheres, and on and on), so by a kind of induction we should expect todays best theories -- dark matter, string theory, whatever -- to eventually join that graveyard too, which means we probably shouldn't believe our current theories are even approximately true.

What's been nagging me is that the comebacks to this argument all seem to pull in different directions and I honestly can't tell which one actually holds up. There's "structural realism," which leans on the fact that Fresnel's equations for light apparently survived, mathematically, right through the transition from the old mechanical ether theory into Maxwell's totally different field theory -- so the idea is we should trust the structure or math of a theory even when the underlying picture of "what's really there" gets thrown out. Then there's a move I've seen called divide et impera, which says the parts of old theories that were actually doing the predictive work got carried forward into the new theories, and only the idle, decorative parts got junked, so the induction is unfairly lumping the good in with the bad. And then, just when I thought I had a handle on it, I ran into a newer and apparently nastier argument from a guy named Kyle Stanford, about scientists historically being bad at even conceiving of the rival theories that eventually replaced their favorites -- which sounds like a deeper problem then just "old theories got shown false."

Could you write me an essay that actually adjudicates between these positions instead of just listing them like a textbook would? I want it to seriously weigh whether structural realism and divide et impera really succeed in defusing Laudan's argument, and whether Stanford's version escapes those same defenses or makes things worse. And please don't let it end on some limp "reasonable people disagree" note -- as an old physicist I want a real verdict, something like whether I should still think of the well-confirmed theories from my own field as approximately true, or whether I ought to retire as a realist right along with retiring from the lab.
