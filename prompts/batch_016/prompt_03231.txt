Okay, I need an outside sanity check because my critique group and I hit a wall on this one and it's driving me a little nuts. I'm about two-thirds through my first real stab at a fair-play locked-room mystery (think Agatha Christie by way of Knives Out), and the snag is pacing, not plot. Every clue my detective needs is on the page by the end of chapter 9 -- the smudged ink on the will, the housekeeper's alibi that doesnt quite square with the tide tables, the fact that the "locked" window actually only locks from the outside. But she doesn't say the killer's name out loud until chapter 16, after turning it all over on a long train ride home. Two people in the group are calling this sloppy: their argument is that the moment she's got all those individual facts in her head, she basically already knows who did it, full stop, so every chapter in between where she "hasn't figured it out yet" is either padding or a straight-up plot hole, since you can't possess every piece of a valid deduction without possessing the conclusion too.

My gut says that's wrong -- if knowing a pile of true things automatically meant knowing everything that logically follows from them, nobody would ever need to sit and actually think, math homework wouldn't exist, and detectives would be out of a job -- but I couldn't turn that gut feeling into a real counter-argument before the meeting ended, and it's been bugging me since. Is there something more rigorous going on here than just "well, sometimes it takes people a while to connect the dots"? I have this nagging feeling that the exact gap I'm describing -- between having all the ingredients of a conclusion and actually having drawn it -- is a real, named problem somewhere outside of fiction, but I can't place it and my googling has gone nowhere. I'd love the actual shape of the idea rather than just reassurance that my structure is fine, partly to win the argument, but mostly because I can't stop noticing the same tension now in other books, and even in myself.
