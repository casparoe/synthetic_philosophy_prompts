I spent thirty-five years as an experimental particle physicist before retiring, and in my newfound free time I have been trying to make up for the philosophical education I never had, mostly by auditing a graduate seminar in metaphysics at the university that once employed me. The seminar has recently turned to the literature on "grounding" and fundamentality associated with people like Jonathan Schaffer and Kit Fine, and I confess I cannot see what genuine work is left for this project once physics has finished its own. The entire history of my discipline has been a patient demonstration that higher-level phenomena are nothing over and above arrangements of whatever sits one level further down: macroscopic matter reduces to molecules, molecules to atoms, atoms to nuclei and electrons, and nuclei further to protons and neutrons, which reduce in turn to quarks. I had always assumed that this chain of composition, extended all the way to whatever a completed theory of quantum gravity eventually posits, simply is the grounding hierarchy that metaphysicians are after. On that assumption, "grounding" is just our new, more fashionable word for the old reductive relation of being made up of smaller and more basic parts, and a finished fundamental physics would settle every question of what grounds what as an automatic by-product.

Two things from the seminar have unsettled this picture without my quite understanding why they should. First, Fine insists that grounding is a distinctively non-causal relation, which puzzles me, since our best physical explanations of why composite systems behave as they do are causal, mechanistic stories told in terms of their parts. Second, and more troubling, Schaffer himself denies that fundamentality runs from small to large at all: he argues that the cosmos as a whole, rather than whatever is smallest, is the one truly fundamental entity, with particles as comparatively derivative fragments of it, invoking quantum entanglement in support of the idea. My instinct is that this must be either a rhetorical flourish or a kind of category error, since it seems to invert everything that a century of reductive success in physics has taught us. I would be glad if you could tell me where my reasoning goes astray: is fundamentality, properly understood, really just the terminus of physical reduction wearing philosophical dress, so that Schaffer's monism is some kind of outlier or confusion about how the physics actually works - or is there a principled reason that grounding and the small-to-large hierarchy of composition could come apart, such that even a completed physics would leave the metaphysician's real work undone?
