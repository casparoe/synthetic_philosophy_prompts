I filed my application for naturalization last month, after eleven years as a permanent resident, and the process has left me oddly preoccupied with a question I can't quite shake: what actually justifies the criteria being used to decide whether I get to join the group of people entitled to govern themselves here? Some reading turned up the fact that this is basically what political theorists call the "boundary problem" — the puzzle of how a demos can legitimately fix its own membership, given that any procedure for doing so seems to presuppose an answer to the very question it's supposed to settle.

Looking for earlier discussions of the issue, I came across this passage from Henry Sidgwick's The Elements of Politics (1891), Chapter XIV ("The Area of Government — States and Districts"), §4, where he tries to work out how membership of a state is acquired in the first place. I can follow the individual sentences, but I'm having trouble reconstructing what the actual argument is supposed to be:

> How, then, is membership of a political society to be determined, if mere local habitation is not sufficient to determine it? There are two obvious alternatives, (1) Birth, and (2) Consent: and the first subdivides again into two, according as "birth" is understood to mean either "birth from parents who are members" or "birth within the territory". The decision in favour of any one of the three principles is, however, of minor importance: because ordinarily all these characteristics are found in combination. Ever since political societies have existed, the quality of membership has been handed down from parents to children, along with the common language, customs, and traditions that constitute the normal bonds of national unity: thus in any modern country the great majority of the inhabitants — born from native parents and on the soil — have been regarded as inchoate members from their birth, and as they have grown up have assumed the rights and obligations of full membership, without any formal act of consent. With regard, then, to this great majority, it would be superfluous and disturbing to admit any doubt as to their membership, so long as they remain within the territorial limits of the state…
>
> We are thus led to consider the conditions under which aliens generally should be admitted to (1) residence and (2) citizenship… Here, then, I shall only point out that the residence of a large number of persons permanently excluded from citizenship within the territory of any community involves an obvious danger of weakening the internal coherence of the community. Accordingly, if a State permits the free immigration of foreigners, it seems expedient that admission to citizenship should be generally open to those resident aliens — provided they have, by sufficiently long, orderly, and unblemished residence within its territory, both shown a settled preference for the social order that it maintains, and acquired a sufficient acquaintance with its laws and political habits to render it fairly probable that they will adequately perform the duties of citizenship.
>
> It remains to settle the minor point, whether parentage or soil is to decide citizenship, in the case of children born in the territory of a state other than that of which their parents are members… we may lay down that — as it is obviously undesirable, other things being equal, that either State should have unwilling members — the children of resident aliens should be allowed to choose their nationality when they come to years of discretion; so long at least as there is no need of discouraging the residence of aliens on account of their numbers.

Could you help me reconstruct the argument here?

1. Sidgwick offers birth-by-descent, birth-by-soil, and consent as candidate criteria for membership, then says the choice between them is "of minor importance" because they usually coincide — and on that basis treats the native-born as members automatically, "without any formal act of consent," while still requiring resident aliens to show a "settled preference" before naturalizing and letting their children choose "when they come to years of discretion." Is there a principled reason for holding insiders and outsiders to such different standards of legitimation, or is this just existing practice dressed up as theory?
2. Stepping back: does starting from the state as an already-constituted "going concern," and asking only how individuals are added to or subtracted from it, let Sidgwick quietly sidestep the regress that later boundary-problem theorists worry about (that any decision procedure for fixing the demos already presupposes a demos to run it) — or does it just relocate that same problem onto the unexamined "great majority" he takes for granted from the outset?
