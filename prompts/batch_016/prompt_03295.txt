I spent my career as a field biologist studying predator-prey and host-parasitoid dynamics, and it is only in retirement that I have started reading philosophy in earnest. Lately I have been re-reading Darwin's 1860 letter to Asa Gray, in which he confesses that he cannot square a beneficent, omnipotent God with the existence of the Ichneumonidae, whose larvae — as I once watched under a microscope — devour caterpillars alive from within. The same unease now animates a strand of contemporary moral philosophy, associated with parts of the effective altruism movement, which treats wild-animal suffering as a serious ethical problem in its own right rather than melancholy scenery. Proponents point not only to predation and disease but to the statistical cruelty of r-selected reproduction, in which the great majority of offspring are expected to die badly within days of birth, and argue that we may eventually have the knowledge and tools — gene drives, habitat engineering, fertility control — to do something about it.

Write a short story that takes this argument seriously rather than satirizing it. Center it on a character or institution with real power to intervene in a specific wild ecosystem, and let the story sit honestly with the tension between reducing suffering and respecting ecological complexity and wildness for its own sake, without resolving that tension too neatly by the end.
