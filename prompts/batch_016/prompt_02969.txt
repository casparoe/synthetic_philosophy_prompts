So my school has this philosophy elective (small school, we get like three weird electives a year) and this week's unit was on political philosophy, so for homework we got a packet with a little bit of Hobbes and then a few pages of excerpts from Pascal's Pensees — not the famous wager part, the section on justice and force. There's a bit where he says "being unable to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just," and another part where he points out that laws flip depending which side of a mountain range or river you're born on ("truth on this side of the Pyrenees, error on the other side"), and calls custom "the mystical foundation" of a law's authority, and says that "not finding justice, we have found force" instead. We were supposed to discuss it in small groups, but in mine it turned into Owen and Layla going back and forth for like 20 minutes while the rest of us just watched, and they never actually resolved it, so now I can't stop thinking about who was actually right.

Here's roughly how it went, numbered so its easier to follow: (1) Owen said Pascal's basically just a cynic here — "we made what is strong just" means "justice" is just a label winners stick onto themselves, so its might-makes-right with extra steps. (2) Layla said thats backwards — Pascal thinks real justice probably does exist, we just can't ever access or agree on it (that's the whole point of the Pyrenees bit), so society falls back on force and custom out of necessity, not because force actually IS justice. More of a sad diagnosis than something he's endorsing. (3) Owen shot back that a "true justice" that never once overrides an actual law or ruler is doing literally zero work — if it can never say "this specific thing is unjust, resist it," then cynicism and skepticism give you the exact same instructions: shut up and obey, so the label doesn't matter. (4) Layla said it's not empty because it cuts both ways: Pascal specifically warns that revolutionaries who think they've found the real natural justice are making the same mistake as whoever they're overthrowing — that's a real claim (nobody's access to justice is special), not just cover for whoever's currently in charge. She also pointed out this whole section is in service of Pascal's bigger project of showing that human reason can't ground itself on its own, which is why he's building toward Christianity later in the book — a different job than what someone like Hobbes is doing with a similar sounding conclusion. (5) Owen said he doesn't care what "job" the passage is doing in Pascal's head — if the actual advice that comes out the other end is "obey the law, don't ask about its origins, and definitely don't trust the people telling you its unjust either," that's functionally telling powerless people to stay powerless no matter how fancy the reasoning behind it is, so Layla's distinction doesn't actually cash out in anything.
