I am a novelist at work on a book set among the merchant and banking families of Dublin in the mid-1730s, a period when the city was chronically short of coin, rents were flowing out of the country to absentee landlords in England, and pamphleteers of every stripe were proposing remedies. In hunting for period texture rather than plot, I have been reading primary sources from the decade, and this led me, rather to my surprise, to George Berkeley's The Querist, published in three parts between 1735 and 1737, not long after Berkeley became Bishop of Cloyne. I had known Berkeley only as the philosopher of esse est percipi, the man who dissolved matter into perception, so it was disorienting to find him composing, in much the same voice, an extended tract on banking and the nature of money, cast entirely as a sequence of unanswered questions rather than a single flat assertion. The run of queries below is the passage that stopped me cold:

23. Qu. Whether money is to be considered as having an intrinsic value, or as being a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, as is variously suggested by writers? And whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?

24. Qu. Whether the value or price of things be not a compounded proportion, directly as the demand, and reciprocally as the plenty?

25. Qu. Whether the terms crown, livre, pound sterling, etc., are not to be considered as exponents or denominations of such proportion? And whether gold, silver, and paper are not tickets or counters for reckoning, recording, and transferring thereof?

26. Qu. Whether the denominations being retained, although the bullion were gone, things might not nevertheless be rated, bought, and sold, industry promoted, and a circulation of commerce maintained?

[Berkeley continues in this vein for some pages more — proportions, denominations, the raising and lowering of coin — before arriving, much later, at this:]

37. Qu. Whether power to command the industry of others be not real wealth? And whether money be not in truth tickets or tokens for conveying and recording such power, and whether it be of great consequence what materials the tickets are made of?

I understand the broad gist: Berkeley wants to sever the assumed link between money and precious metal, so that a country without gold or silver need not therefore be a country without commerce. But before I let my banker character anywhere near this argument at his own dinner table, I would value help with three things. First, how literally should Query 26 be taken — is Berkeley genuinely proposing something like a fiat currency, sustained by convention and denomination rather than metallic backing, which would be a startling position to hold in 1735, or is this simply one provocation among hundreds that he need not be read as fully endorsing? A friend of mine who once worked in finance read it and said flatly, "that's just fiat money," and I can't tell whether he's right or is reading his own categories back into a text that won't bear them. Second, what is the interrogative form actually doing here — is casting every claim as a question merely a clergyman's prudent hedge against meddling in politics, or does it track something more considered in Berkeley's method, a reluctance to assert what can instead be elicited? Third, and here I suspect I may just be inventing a good line for my own purposes: is there a real, defensible connection between this dematerialized view of money as a mere "ticket or counter" and the metaphysics Berkeley is actually remembered for, or would it be an anachronistic embellishment for my character to draw that parallel aloud in 1735?
