A few of us at work have a Slack channel where people debate math, logic, and AI questions for fun, outside our actual jobs. Last week two regulars, Marcus and Elena, got into an extended argument about the Lucas-Penrose argument -- the claim that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show a human mind can't be equivalent to any Turing machine. I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher or logician: I could follow each individual step but couldn't tell who was actually winning. I've reconstructed the exchange as faithfully as I can:

(1) Marcus opened with the classic move: take any consistent formal system F rich enough for arithmetic, and Gödel's theorem hands you a sentence G(F) saying, in effect, "I am not provable in F." F can never prove G(F) -- that would make F inconsistent -- but we, standing outside F, can see that G(F) must therefore be true. Since this works for whatever F you propose as a model of him, he can't be any of them. He added that this argument has never really been refuted, only quibbled with.

(2) Elena pushed back: that last inference only goes through if F is consistent, and Gödel's second theorem says no system as strong as F can prove its own consistency. If F is a formal model of Marcus, he doesn't get to assume Con(F) for free just by introspecting -- he might be consistent, but he can't know it from the inside, so he can't run the argument on himself with the confidence he was claiming.

(3) Marcus said Penrose has a sharper version built exactly to dodge that objection. Suppose I am F. Since I know I'm sound, not just consistent, the larger system F' -- F plus the extra premise "I am F" -- is sound too, because adding a true premise to a sound system can't make it unsound. But then I can see that G(F') is true, using only the assumption that I am F'. So I've done something F' is supposed to be unable to do, and I never had to independently verify any consistency claim from outside -- the contradiction is generated entirely from inside the hypothesis.

(4) Elena said she knew that version too, and that the real flaw isn't the "I am F" step -- it's a quieter premise hiding inside "I know I'm sound," namely that I can unassailably know I'm even consistent, let alone sound. Run a Löb-style diagonal construction on any system, mechanical or not, and the bare premise "this system unassailably asserts its own consistency" already yields a contradiction on its own, with no assumption anywhere that the system is formal. So the contradiction Marcus found was never evidence against mechanism specifically -- it's evidence that nothing gets to unassailably know its own consistency.

(5) Marcus granted that this cuts against his original 1961-style move too, but said Elena still owes an account of mathematical practice: mathematicians do make mistakes, but they catch and correct them against some standard they'd call unassailable, and if mechanism means denying we have anything like that standard, that's a steep price to pay just to save computationalism.

(6) Elena answered that she isn't denying they operate with such a standard, only that operating reliably by it and knowably being reliable by it are different things -- and that gap is exactly where mechanism lives. Penrose needed to show we're "knowably sound," not just sound, and, on her reading, never actually got there.
