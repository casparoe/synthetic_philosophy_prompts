Bit of context: I'm in a philosophy PhD program and our modal metaphysics reading group has been going through the standard Lewis-counterpart-theory-vs-Kripke/Plantinga-style-transworld-identity material this term. This week our organizer threw in a "historical ringer" — a chunk of Leibniz's July 1686 letter to Arnauld about "possible Adams" — on the theory that Leibniz basically had this exact fight three hundred years early, just dressed up in theological clothes. Nobody in the group could agree on how to cash it out though, so I figured I'd ask you. The translation below is a bit stiff (it's from some old reader someone photocopied for the group, no citation on it anywhere), but here's the relevant bit:

"I had said that the supposition from which all human events can be deduced is not that of creating a vague Adam, but that of creating a particular Adam, determinate in all his circumstances, chosen from among an infinity of possible Adams. On which you make two considerable remarks, one against the plurality of Adams, and the other against the reality of merely possible substances. As to the first: you say, with good reason, that it is no more possible to conceive of several possible Adams, taking Adam as a singular nature, than to conceive of several me's. I agree with that — but in speaking of several Adams, I was not taking Adam as a determinate individual, but as some person conceived sub ratione generalitatis [under a general description], under circumstances which seem to us to determine Adam as an individual but which, in truth, do not determine him sufficiently — as when by 'Adam' one understands the first man, whom God places in a garden of delight, from which he departs on account of sin, and from whose rib God draws a woman. (For one must not name Eve, or paradise, taking them as determinate individuals either, or it would no longer be sub ratione generalitatis.)"

Here's my confusion. Leibniz needs "vague Adam" to block Arnauld's objection, which reminds me a bit of the modern "Humphrey" worry about whether a counterpart is really you at all, though it's probably not quite the same complaint — anyway, it looks like Leibniz wants "vague Adam" to name one indeterminate thing that the different possible worlds' Adams then each determinately are. But elsewhere (the complete-concept doctrine) Leibniz is committed to full-blown superessentialism: every predicate true of an individual, including that he sinned, is packed into his one true concept, so an Adam who doesn't sin just isnt numerically the same guy, full stop. That sounds to me like it forces Leibniz into something counterpart-theory-shaped — a bunch of resembling-but-distinct candidate-Adams, one of which God actualizes — rather than anything like real transworld identity for a single Adam. So is "vague Adam" doing genuine metaphysical work here, like some third option between transworld identity and Lewisian counterparts (a subject of predication that's less than a full individual but still somehow one thing), or is it just a description/sortal Leibniz is using to dodge the question, with the "real" metaphysics underneath being counterpart theory whether he likes it or not? Would love your read on what's actually going on in the passage, and wether you think Leibniz's move even works on its own terms.
