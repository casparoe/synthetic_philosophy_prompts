I'm taking a seminar on early modern philosophy this semester, and this week's reading moved past the *Principles of Human Knowledge* and the *Three Dialogues* into a text I hadn't encountered before: Berkeley's *The Querist* (1735–37), his series of queries on Irish economic policy. I was struck by how naturally his monetary views seem to fall out of his immaterialism. In Query 23 he asks whether "the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter," and shortly afterward suggests that gold, silver, and paper are all just "tickets or counters for reckoning, recording, and transferring" value, rather than valuable in themselves. He goes further and argues that a kingdom could be "five times richer" than it currently is with "not one ounce of gold or silver" within its borders, provided credit circulates briskly enough to sustain industry — and he repeatedly promotes a national bank, at one point calling it "the true philosopher's stone in a State," as the institution best suited to manage that circulation.

A few of us got to arguing after class about what Berkeley would have made of Bitcoin, and I'd like a more careful answer than we managed to work out ourselves. On one hand, cryptocurrency seems to vindicate exactly the point he was making against the "vulgar" prepossession for bullion: that money is fundamentally a sign or token within a social practice of reckoning, with no need of any intrinsic backing. On the other hand, nearly everything else he valued about money seems to cut against it — his insistence that credit be actively and visibly managed by an accountable central institution, his impatience with money "lying dead" instead of being put to productive use, and his general assumption that a sign system like this needs some trusted authority standing behind it and vouching for it. A fixed-supply, deliberately ownerless ledger with no central manager seems about as far from his proposed national bank as one could get while still counting as a mere "ticket or counter."

I have to write a short reflection paper for this course, and I'd like to use your answer as a starting point, so please don't just give me a one-line verdict. Could you work through how you think these two strands of his thought — the anti-metallist theory of money as sign, and the pro-centralization theory of credit as something requiring active, accountable management — would actually balance out in Berkeley's assessment of Bitcoin specifically? And, if it's not too much of a tangent, I'd also be curious whether you think he'd regard something like a central-bank digital currency as a much closer realization of his own proposals than Bitcoin ever could be.
