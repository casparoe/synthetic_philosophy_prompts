I'm taking a grad seminar on phil of language this term and we spent a week on "the unity of the proposition" problem, which the professor kept saying traces back to F.H. Bradley's regress argument in Appearance and Reality (1893). Annoyingly she never actually assigned us the primary text, just secondary lit that name-drops it, so I went and dug up the original chapter myself (its public domain, wasn't too hard to find). Now I'm stuck on the actual passage where Bradley lays out the regress, because his 1890s prose is rough going — lots of "eddies" and "links" and rhetorical asides, and I keep losing track of what's actually being argued versus what's just colorful language. Here's the bit that's tripping me up:

"We may briefly reach the same dilemma from the side of relations. They are nothing intelligible, either with or without their qualities. In the first place, a relation without terms seems mere verbiage; and terms appear, therefore, to be something beyond their relation. At least, for myself, a relation which somehow precipitates terms which were not there before, or a relation which can get on somehow without terms, and with no differences beyond the mere ends of a line of connection, is really a phrase without meaning. It is, to my mind, a false abstraction, and a thing which loudly contradicts itself; and I fear that I am obliged to leave the matter so. As I am left without information, and can discover with my own ears no trace of harmony, I am forced to conclude to a partial deafness in others. And hence a relation, we must say, without qualities is nothing. But how the relation can stand to the qualities is, on the other side, unintelligible. If it is nothing to the qualities, then they are not related at all; and, if so, as we saw, they have ceased to be qualities, and their relation is a nonentity. But if it is to be something to them, then clearly we now shall require a new connecting relation. For the relation hardly can be the mere adjective of one or both of its terms; or, at least, as such it seems indefensible. And, being something itself, if it does not itself bear a relation to the terms, in what intelligible way will it succeed in being anything to them? But here again we are hurried off into the eddy of a hopeless process, since we are forced to go on finding new relations without end. The links are united by a link, and this bond of union is a link which also has two ends; and these require each a fresh link to connect them with the old."

Could you walk me through, step by step, what's actually regressing here? Like, is it the relation itself that supposedly needs an infinite chain of further relations just to exist, or is it more that any attempt to say how a relation connects to its terms is what kicks off the infinite chain? And separately — since this is apparently the ancestor of Russell's worry about why "Socrates is wise" counts as one unified claim and not just the word "Socrates" sitting next to the word "wise" — can you spell out exactly how the two problems connect? Are they genuinely the same regress wearing different clothes, or more like cousins that just get lumped together in lit-review paragraphs because they both involve relations somehow?
