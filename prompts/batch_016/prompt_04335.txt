I spent my whole career as an entomologist, mostly working on parasitoid wasps, and now that I'm retired I've finally got time to read all the philosophy I skipped over during the research years. Lately I've been working slowly through Leibniz's Theodicy (in translation — my Latin never got that far), and I keep snagging on the same worry, which I cant shake and which my old reading-group friends are getting pretty tired of hearing about over coffee.

Leibniz sorts evil into three kinds — metaphysical (mere finitude/imperfection), physical (suffering), and moral (sin) — and argues that God picked this particular world because, taken as one giant interlocking package (his "compossibility" idea), its evils are outweighed by, or even instrumental to, the greatest overall harmony and perfection achievable: the famous "best of all possible worlds." Fine, as far as it goes. But here's what nags at me: Leibniz wasn't indifferent to earth history — he even wrote his own little geological treatise, Protogaea, reasoning about rock strata and fossils — yet he obviously had zero inkling of what we now know, which is that predation, parasitism, and mass extinction have been running continuously for a few hundred million years, almost all of it long before any rational creature existed to be improved, tested, or "soul-made" by any of it. It's the same thing that later spooked Darwin when he thought about the very wasps I used to study, laying eggs in caterpillars that get eaten alive from the inside out — he admitted to a correspondent that he "cannot persuade myself that a beneficent & omnipotent God would have designedly created" them for that purpose.

So here's my actual ask: using only Leibniz's own conceptual toolkit — the harmony/perfection talk, monadic perception, sufficient reason, all of it — not modern replies to theodicy in general, can you reconstruct how he'd genuinely try to absorb a few hundred million extra years of apparently pointless, unwitnessed animal suffering that predates any Fall, any free will, any rational soul whatsoever? Does he actually have the resources to swallow that without damage, or is this the one rock his optimism can't get over? I'd love your best guess at the essay he'd write if you handed him a modern paleontology textbook.
