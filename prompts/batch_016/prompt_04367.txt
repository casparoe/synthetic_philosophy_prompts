Been meaning to ask this somewhere for a while. I fell down a rabbit hole a couple weeks ago re-reading old Overcoming Bias and early LessWrong posts on Pascal's Mugging (the one where the mugger says he'll use his powers from outside the Matrix to simulate and kill 3^^^^3 people unless you hand over five bucks), and I think I might've actually found the fix that everyone online keeps overcomplicating for no reason.

Here's my thinking: the "paradox" only really bites if you round the mugger's probability of being legit all the way down to literally zero, and yeah, that's a no-no, since it breaks Cromwell's rule (the whole "leave a little room for the moon being made of green cheese" thing). But if instead you just assign his story some tiny-but-strictly-positive probability, like 10^-30 or whatever number feels sufficiently absurd, then you've dodged the zero-probability objection completely and the paradox just... resolves itself? Seems like it was only ever a problem for people sloppy enough to round all the way down to exact zero. My roommate, who's way more into decision theory then I am, swears this doesn't actually fix anything, and started going on about some "leverage penalty" that Yudkowsky and Robin Hanson apparently had to invent for exactly this reason, but he couldn't really explain why swapping in a tiny positive number instead of zero doesn't just close the case. So which is it, is he right that somethings still broken here (and why), or did I genuinely spot a hole that a bunch of LW old-timers somehow missed?
