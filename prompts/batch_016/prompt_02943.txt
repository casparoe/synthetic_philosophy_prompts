I've never studied philosophy or economics formally, but I enjoy puzzling over this kind of thing in my spare time, and a few months ago a friend who is much more mathematically inclined than I am introduced me to the St. Petersburg paradox. The setup is simple: you flip a fair coin repeatedly; the pot starts at $2 and doubles every time the coin comes up heads, and the game ends and pays out the moment the coin comes up tails. Because there is always some shrinking but never-zero chance of an extremely long run of heads before a tail finally appears, the mathematically expected payout of this game is infinite, yet almost no one would sensibly pay more than a modest sum to play it. That gap between an infinite expected value and people's very finite willingness to pay is what makes it a paradox in the first place.

Reading further on my own, I learned that Daniel Bernoulli proposed a resolution to this back in the eighteenth century: money has diminishing marginal value to a person, so if you measure outcomes in "utility" (say, on something like a logarithmic scale) instead of raw dollars, the expected utility of the game comes out small and finite, which matches how people actually behave. This strikes me as a completely general fix rather than a clever patch for one particular case: as long as we agree that a rational agent maximizes expected utility rather than expected money, and that utility grows more slowly than money does, it seems like no version of this doubling game, however its payouts are dressed up, should ever be able to generate an infinite or otherwise paradoxical expectation again. I have been mulling this over partly because I like thinking about how to reason through very lopsided bets in my own life, such as career or investment decisions with a small chance of a huge payoff, and I would like to know whether I can lean on this utility-based reasoning with a clear conscience. So is Bernoulli's move really the end of the story for the St. Petersburg paradox, or is there some deeper reason that decision theorists don't consider the matter fully settled?
