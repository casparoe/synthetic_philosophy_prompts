so i've been reading up on old economic writers for fun and found out George Berkeley (the philosopher who argued things only exist if someone's perceiving them) also wrote this weird book called The Querist thats basically just hundreds of numbered questions about money and banking in Ireland. a bunch of them are him asking whether money has any value in itself, or whether its really just a marker for keeping track of real wealth -- and at one point he flat out asks whether it matters "what materials the tickets are made of." his whole angle was that real wealth is industry/labor, gold and silver are just tokens people happen to trust, and he wanted Ireland to set up a national bank backed by public credit instead of hoarding metal.

that got me wondering what he'd make of bitcoin specifically (not crypto in general, bitcoin). part of me thinks he'd be thrilled, since its literally a ticket with zero intrinsic value, which is exactly what he argued money should be. but his whole fix for "money doesn't need to be metal" was backing it with a national bank and public trust, whereas bitcoin is specifically designed to not need a bank or government or anybody in charge. he was also pretty harsh about south sea bubble type schemes, basically calling that kind of speculation gambling dressed up as finance.

so using his actual arguments in the querist, not just the vibe that he already thought value is kind of made up -- do you think he'd see bitcoin as finally the pure ticket-money he wanted, or would he think it completely misses the point of what tickets are even for?
