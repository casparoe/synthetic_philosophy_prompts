I am taking a decision theory seminar in the philosophy department this term, and a few weeks ago we spent a session working through Nover and Hájek's 2004 paper "Vexing Expectations," which introduces what they call the Pasadena game. The setup is as follows: a fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads, and if that happens on toss n, the payoff is (-1)^(n-1) · 2^n/n, so the sequence of possible payoffs runs 2, -2, 8/3, -4, and so on, growing without bound while alternating in sign. What makes this stranger than the classic St. Petersburg game, where the expected payoff is merely infinite, is that the Pasadena series is only conditionally convergent: by the Riemann rearrangement theorem, its terms can be reordered to sum to any value whatsoever, or to diverge to positive or negative infinity. The game therefore does not simply have a very large or infinite expected value; it provably has no determinate expected value at all, unless one first makes an arbitrary decision about how to order infinitely many terms.

This puzzle has stayed with me partly because of something unrelated to the course itself: I help run my university's effective altruism discussion group, where much of our debate about comparing interventions — a well-evidenced global health charity, say, against a speculative biosecurity or AI safety project with a small estimated probability of an enormous payoff — ultimately comes down to whoever can defend the higher expected value. We have generally assumed that, however difficult the relevant probabilities and payoffs are to estimate in practice, there is at least some fact of the matter about which option has the higher expectation, even when we cannot calculate it precisely.

Could you describe the most important implications of the Pasadena paradox for this kind of expected-value reasoning about high-stakes, long-shot causes? I am especially interested in whether the paradox is really just an exotic curiosity confined to games with literally infinite state spaces, or whether it points to a deeper problem with treating "expected value" as a well-defined quantity that we are merely bad at estimating, in real cases such as ours where the tails of the relevant probability distributions are genuinely open-ended.
