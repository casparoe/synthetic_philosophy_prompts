"Jupiter, having surveyed [the possibilities] before the beginning of the existing world, classified the possibilities into worlds, and chose the best of all... I will show you some, wherein shall be found, not absolutely the same Sextus as you have seen... but several Sextuses resembling him... You will find in one world a very happy and noble Sextus, in another a Sextus content with a mediocre state, a Sextus, indeed, of every kind and endless diversity of forms."

[she then walks him through a couple of these alternate worlds, then:]

"The halls rose in a pyramid, becoming even more beautiful as one mounted towards the apex... Finally they reached the highest one which completed the pyramid, and which was the most beautiful of all... 'We are in the real true world,' [the Goddess said], 'and you are at the source of happiness... Here is Sextus as he is, and as he will be in reality. He issues from the temple in a rage... You see him going to Rome, bringing confusion everywhere, violating the wife of his friend... If Jupiter had placed here a Sextus happy at Corinth or King in Thrace, it would be no longer this world. And nevertheless he could not have failed to choose this world, which surpasses in perfection all the others... You see that my father did not make Sextus wicked; he was so from all eternity, he was so always and freely. My father only granted him the existence which his wisdom could not refuse to the world where he is included; he made him pass from the region of the possible to that of the actual beings.'"

ok so here's my question. how is that last part not a total cop out? the goddess is saying Sextus was "wicked from all eternity" and "always," like before God ever decided to make anything, AND that God "could not have failed" to choose the world with this exact evil Sextus in it once he decided to be as wise/good as possible. so it sounds like there was never any point where this specific Sextus could've turned out differently — his whole evil life is basically baked into "which Sextus he is" from the start, and God was pretty much logically forced to include him once he went with the best world. but then she also says he did all that "freely." those seem like they can't both be true? if literally zero possible version of events has this particular Sextus not doing the crime and getting exiled, in what sense did HE decide anything?

my teacher's whole summary of leibniz was just "best of all possible worlds means a little evil is worth it for the greater good," but this passage seems to be making a totally different and way weirder point about how individual people and their choices even work. can someone explain what leibniz's actual reasoning is supposed to be here, and whether it actually holds together or if its just a fancy way of dodging the question?
