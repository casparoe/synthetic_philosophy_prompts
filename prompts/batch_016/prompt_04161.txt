I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of language, and a disagreement from this week's session on Kripke's Naming and Necessity spilled over into my office hours, where two of my second-year PhD students, Nora and Theo, spent the better part of an hour arguing about it without either one budging. The question was whether Kripke's causal-historical theory of reference actually solves Frege's original puzzle about identity statements -- why "Hesperus is Phosphorus" seems to be a substantive astronomical discovery while "Hesperus is Hesperus" is trivial -- or whether it merely relocates that puzzle elsewhere. Both of them later cornered me separately to ask who had won, and I honestly don't think it's as one-sided as either believes, so I'd rather have an outside verdict than referee my own students.

Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it. (1) Theo opened by claiming that Kripke's apparatus fully dissolves Frege's puzzle: since "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" are both rigid designators of the same object, "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is necessarily true, and its felt informativeness is completely explained by the fact that this necessity was knowable only a posteriori -- so there is no further work for Fregean sense to do. (2) Nora objected that this explains why the sentence isn't knowable a priori, but that was never the explanandum Frege cared about; what needs explaining is why a fully rational speaker can sincerely assent to "Hesperus is Hesperus" while dissenting from "Hesperus is Phosphorus," a fact about cognitive significance rather than modal status, and rigidity by itself says nothing about that. (3) Theo replied that this objection just restates Kripke's own puzzle about Pierre, which Kripke introduced precisely to show that substitution failures in belief contexts arise even between codesignative names that are, by the causal theorist's own lights, semantically on a par -- so the difficulty is a general fact about our naive theory of belief ascription, not a special defect of the causal theory of names. (4) Nora granted that this was indeed Kripke's own moral, but argued it supports her rather than him: if the puzzle survives untouched by the move from Fregean sense to rigid designation, then the causal theory hasn't solved Frege's puzzle in the sense that mattered to Frege -- it has only settled a modal question Frege never asked, while the original question about cognitive significance has simply been relocated into the theory of attitude ascriptions, where it is exactly as open as before. (5) Theo answered that relocating a puzzle is not the same as failing to solve it: Frege's puzzle was always two puzzles traveling together, a modal one (how can an identity statement be both necessary and informative?) and a cognitive one (why does substitution fail in attitude contexts?), and Kripke deserves credit for cleanly prying these apart and fully closing the first, even though the second remains open for Fregeans and Millians alike. (6) Nora closed by insisting that this concedes her point rather than answering it: Frege introduced sense specifically to solve the cognitive puzzle, not a modal one -- he had no considered doctrine of metaphysical necessity, since the very distinction between necessity and apriority is a Kripkean contribution -- so crediting Kripke with "solving Frege's puzzle" while granting that its cognitive half remains untouched gets the history backwards, and amounts to admitting that on the question that actually animated Frege's essay, we have made no progress since 1892.
