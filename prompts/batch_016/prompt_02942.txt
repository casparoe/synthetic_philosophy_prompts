I'm a retired geneticist - spent a good chunk of my career running Mendelian randomization studies, basically using genetic variants as a stand-in for randomized interventions we couldn't ethically run ourselves. Now that I've got time to actually read philosophy instead of just borrowing its tools without asking where they came from, I noticed Hume gives two definitions of 'cause' back to back near the end of Enquiry Section VII: first the regularity one (constant conjunction), then, almost as a throwaway, a counterfactual one - "if the first object had not been, the second never had existed." Lewis apparently ran with that second line and built his whole possible-worlds theory of causation on it, while Woodward and Pearl branched off and cashed out the counterfactuals through hypothetical interventions on a causal graph instead, partly because Lewis's similarity-between-worlds semantics gave the wrong verdict in some cases.

So if you could sit Hume down and walk him through the whole split - do-calculus, structural equations, possible worlds, all of it - which camp do you think he'd recognize as actually capturing what he meant by that second definition? And would he even grant either side the extra machinery it needs (possible worlds for Lewis, a primitive notion of 'intervention' for Woodward), given how stingy he was about accepting anything not traceable back to an impression? Not fishing for the standard textbook gloss on Hume here, geniunely curious what you'd say.
