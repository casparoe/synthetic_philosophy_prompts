I've been slowly working my way back through Eliezer Yudkowsky's old Quantum Physics Sequence on LessWrong, and the detail that has stuck with me isn't the main case for many-worlds but a complication he mentions only in passing and credits to Robin Hanson: the "mangled worlds" proposal. As I understand it, the idea is that decoherence between branches is never perfectly exact, so branches of very low amplitude can get "mangled" — effectively scrambled or destroyed — by interference bleeding in from the much larger-amplitude branches around them, which would put a practical floor on how thin a branch's measure can get while still supporting anything like a stable, coherent observer. This has struck some people who write about quantum immortality as bad news for the usual quantum-suicide argument: the standard reasoning says that after a Tegmark-style quantum gun has failed to kill you a hundred times running, you should expect to find yourself, however implausibly, in the branch where you survived — but if branches thin enough to contain "a hundred consecutive survivals" are exactly the ones too thin to cohere, it's unclear there is anyone there to do the finding. You might not get a clean thread of immortal experience so much as something that stops being a well-defined branch before it stops being a life.

I'd like you to write a short story of roughly 1500 to 3000 words that takes this specific worry seriously, rather than defaulting to the more familiar trope of someone simply waking up having survived Russian roulette yet again, indefinitely. You're free to invent the protagonist and the exact apparatus — perhaps a lone physicist testing the hypothesis on herself, perhaps an informal experiment within a rationalist community that thinks it already knows how this is supposed to go — but I'd like at least part of the story told from close enough to the protagonist's point of view that the reader has to sit inside the ambiguity of what a "mangled" branch would even be like from the inside, if "inside" is still the right word by that point. Please don't resolve the physics one way or the other; Hanson's proposal is itself speculative and contested among the people who think seriously about it, and I'd rather the story earn its unease honestly than tidy everything up at the end.
