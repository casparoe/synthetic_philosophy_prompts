So I went down a rabbit hole a while back reading about "program equilibrium" - I think it started with a Tennenholtz paper from 2004, the idea being that instead of just picking a move in a game, you submit a whole program that gets to read the other player's source code before deciding what to do. Then there's this newer branch of the literature (FairBot, PrudentBot, all built on Lob's theorem / provability logic) that claims to get two programs to provably cooperate in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma without the infinite regress of "I cooperate if you cooperate if you cooperate...". As a dev this reminds me a lot of reproducible builds or smart contracts, that whole "you don't have to trust me, just read the code" pitch.

I want an essay that actually commits to a position: does program equilibrium (the Lobian/provability-logic version specifically, not just the trivial "cooperate iff your source is byte-identical to mine" CliqueBot version) really solve the philosophical problem of getting rational cooperation without an external enforcer, or is it basically a clever party trick that only works for a handful of hand-crafted agents in a toy setting and doesn't generalize? Please actually engage with the strongest stuff on both sides - the common-knowledge-of-source-code assumption, what happens once you allow arbitrary/undecidable programs instead of the specific modal agents, and the fact that FairBot had to get patched into PrudentBot the moment someone noticed it happily cooperates with plain old CooperateBot. Don't hedge with "it's complicated" at the end, actually pick a side and argue for it.
