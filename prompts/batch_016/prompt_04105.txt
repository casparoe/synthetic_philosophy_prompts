A few months back I got pulled into an ugly pile-on in the comment section of a local Facebook group, over something as small as a disagreement about a proposed bike lane, and it left me more rattled than the actual issue seemed to warrant. Trying to figure out why comments from a few dozen people I'd never met could feel so oppressive, I went down a bit of a rabbit hole and ended up reading an old paperback of Kierkegaard's "The Present Age" — the long closing section of his review of a novel called "Two Ages" — which I gather he published while he was in the middle of being mocked in a satirical Copenhagen newspaper called The Corsair, complete with cartoons of him as a hunchback with uneven trouser legs. What stuck with me wasn't the biographical parallel so much as his actual argument: that "the public" is a kind of phantom or abstraction that only comes into being through the press, that it can never be pinned down or held responsible the way an actual assembly of real people could be, and that "leveling" works precisely by dissolving individuals into this faceless, opinion-having mass that never has to answer for anything.

I'd like you to spell out what you think are the most important implications of that specific strand of Kierkegaard's thinking — not the more general, frequently-quoted material about subjectivity, anxiety, or the leap of faith, but the argument about the press and the public itself — for how an ordinary person today should think about participating in online comment sections, social-media pile-ons, and local forums like the one that got under my skin. Does his framework give any real guidance on when engaging with "the public" is worthwhile versus when it's better to just log off, on whether posting under one's real name rather than anonymously changes anything on his account, or on how to tell a genuine community apart from a mere aggregate of onlookers? I'd rather you be honest about where his nineteenth-century diagnosis maps awkwardly onto algorithm-driven social media — his picture seems to assume something like a single, unified "press," which doesn't obviously match a world of competing platforms and recommendation engines — than have you pretend the fit is perfect.
