So this happened in a reading group I'm part of — im actually a linguistics PhD student but I sit in on a philosophy of language seminar for fun — and right after we worked through the private-language stretch of the Investigations (roughly §243 to §258) two people in the group, Owen and Farah, got into a real argument about what the argument is actually supposed to rule out. Our professor just said "good question, let's come back to that" and never did, so now I'm stuck without a verdict and would love an outside read.

(1) Owen opened by saying the diary case in §258 shows that even a lifelong-solitary person — a Crusoe figure who never had contact with any other language-user, ever — couldn't mean anything by a private sign "S" for a recurring sensation, since he has no way to tell correctly reapplying the sign apart from merely seeming to himself to reapply it correctly; whatever seems right to him is right, which is the same as there being no "right" at all, so you need an actual community whose corrections aren't just more "seeming right" to get a real standard of correctness off the ground. (2) Farah said this quietly conflates "private" with "solitary": the opening lines of §243 have Wittgenstein imagining people who spoke only in monologue, narrating their own actions to no one, and treating that as a case a foreign observer could in principle decode by watching their behavior, precisely because it isn't private in the relevant, logically-walled-off sense; her Crusoe isn't doing bare private ostension off a naked inner flash, his use of "S" is embedded in a lifetime of behavioral regularity — what he does next, what he'd correct himself on — and thats already enough to ground a real seems-right/is-right gap with nobody else ever in the picture. (3) Owen's comeback was that the regularity move doesn't buy her anything, because of the rule-following paradox back at §201: any stretch of past behavior can be redescribed to rationalize whatever Crusoe does next, so absent a check external to him, "the regularity" just collapses into whatever he's currently inclined to call correct. (4) Farah's reply was that this proves too much — a community's pattern of correction is just a bigger pile of the same kind of disposition, equally redescribable after the fact, so either dispositions (solo or collective) can ground correctness or none can, and since Owen presumably won't say a community can't mean anything either, the real target of the argument was never headcount but the fantasy of a purely inner, occurrent warrant.

Could you go through (1)-(4) and say who has the better of it at each step, flagging anywhere someone's begging the question or sliding between senses of "private," "solitary," and "regularity"? Then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing — I suspect the actual literature on this (Baker and Hacker's individualism vs. the Malcolm/Kripke-style "community view") is messier than either of them is letting on. End with a one-line verdict in exactly this format: \verdict{Owen}, \verdict{Farah}, or \verdict{neither}, for whichever reading of the argument you think the text actually supports.
