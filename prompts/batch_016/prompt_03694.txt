So I've fallen down a rabbit hole reading AI safety stuff on LessWrong and the Alignment Forum about this thing called "the shutdown problem," and now I can't stop thinking about it - even tried explaining it to my roommate over dinner and we just ended up confusing each other more. The basic idea, as far as I understand it: if you build an AI that's a proper expected-utility maximizer going after some goal, there's basically no way to make it truly neutral about being switched off - it'll always have some incentive to either stop you from pressing the button or manipulate you into pressing it, depending on which one helps its goal more. Some Oxford philosopher (Thornley, I think?) apparently proved this pretty rigorously. The fix people keep proposing is to deliberately give the AI "incomplete" preferences - basically make it have zero preference between, say, a short life where its shut down soon and a long one where its not, while still letting it have totally normal, complete preferences whenever its comparing two futures where the shutdown happens at the same time. That way its still useful for actually getting stuff done, but supposedly has no reason to fight over the button either way.

But then I read a bunch of pushback saying any agent walking around with gaps like that in its preferences can just get "money pumped" - basically tricked through a sequence of trades into a guaranteed loss - and that this gives the agent (or whatever's shaping it, training, evolution, whatever) a standing incentive to smooth over the gap and turn itself back into a normal complete-preferences maximizer, which just brings the original problem right back. Then there was ANOTHER round of replies saying that money-pump argument secretly assumes the agent picks options one at a time with no memory of what it already turned down, and that an agent which simply refuses to accept a trade worse than one it previously rejected is both incomplete AND immune to the pump. At this point I've read like four different posts/papers on this and honestly still couldn't tell you who's winning.

Could you write me an actual essay on this? Like, does deliberately giving an AI incomplete preferences genuinely solve the shutdown problem, or do the money-pump arguments show that this kind of fix is fundamentally unstable and would just get optimized away over time? Please weigh the strongest version of both sides but actually pick one and defend it at the end - something conditional like "it works, but only if X" totally counts as picking a side, I just don't want a wishy washy "well, there are good points on both sides" non-answer.
