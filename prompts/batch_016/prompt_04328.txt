I've spent the past few weeks working through material from the Newton Project's digitization of Isaac Newton's private papers for a feature I'm writing, and I keep running into a question that the usual "did you know Newton secretly obsessed over alchemy and Bible codes" framing never really resolves. Newton concluded, apparently already in the early 1670s, that the doctrine of the Trinity was a corruption introduced into scripture centuries after Christ. To keep his Cambridge fellowship without being ordained an Anglican priest - which would have meant swearing to a doctrine he no longer believed - he needed a royal dispensation, granted in 1675; in 1690 he quietly sent John Locke a dissertation arguing that two of the New Testament's clearest proof-texts for the Trinity were later interpolations; and he spent decades on a prophetic chronology of the Book of Daniel that eventually yielded 2060 as a date before which the "corrupt," Trinitarian church could not fall. None of this was published in his lifetime. Yet the General Scholium he appended to the Principia contains a natural-theology excursion long enough that, by one scholarly count, more than half of it is theology rather than physics - including a claim that "dominion," not shared substance, is what makes something count as God - and the Opticks floats the idea that infinite space is God's "sensorium," a suggestion Leibniz mocked and that Newton's own defender, Samuel Clarke, had to downgrade to a mere figure of speech.

Newton scholars are genuinely split on what to make of this. Some, in a tradition that includes Richard Westfall's more skeptical remarks about the General Scholium, are willing to grant that the theology was sincere and serious while still doubting that it did much real work inside the physics itself - two intense private obsessions of the same restless mind, rather than two expressions of a single project. Others, like Betty Jo Dobbs on the alchemy side and Stephen Snobelen on the General Scholium, argue for real continuity: that the same impulse - to strip away centuries of institutional corruption and recover a purer original truth, whether in scripture, in nature, or in the "prisca sapientia" of the ancients - runs through the theology, the alchemy, and the mathematical physics alike, and that specific doctrinal commitments, like the dominion argument, leave real traces in how Newton thought about God's relationship to space and time. Even Rob Iliffe, whose "Priest of Nature" is the most exhaustive account of Newton's religious writing we have, resists any simple story of coherence between the two sides of Newton's thought.

Where do you come down on this? Write an essay that actually stakes out a position rather than settling for "it's complicated": a specific, defended verdict on whether Newton's heterodox theology should be understood as substantively continuous with his natural philosophy, or whether the connection is real but considerably shallower than the "unity" camp suggests, or illusory altogether. Please take seriously the strongest version of the skeptical case - including the fact that plenty of religiously orthodox contemporaries, Robert Boyle above all, also freely mixed physics and natural theology, which should make us wary of reading too much heresy-specific significance into the mere fact that Newton did the same - before landing on a clear answer.
