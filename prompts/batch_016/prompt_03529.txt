I'm about two-thirds into a fantasy novel where the god character, my characters call her the Archivist, supposedly knows exactly what every person would freely do in every situation she could ever put them in, and she uses that to hand-pick which situations become real so history goes her way without her ever technically forcing anyone's hand. I got the germ of this off a philosophy podcast episode about Molinism and "middle knowledge" that I only half understood, i just thought it was a great excuse for a deity with plausible deniability. I read the chapter where this gets explained out loud at my writers group last week and it kicked off a whole argument between two of the regulars, Mara and Colin, that carried on at the pub afterward. I mostly just listened because I couldn't keep up with all of it.

Mara's opening move was that the whole premise cheats: if the Archivist knows for certain, before she's even created anyone, exactly what each person would do in each situation, then nobody's actually choosing anything, she's just arranging dominoes and calling it free will. Colin said that's a mistake people make about ordinary foreknowledge in general, knowing in advance what someone will freely do doesn't cause them to do it, no more than a friend who knows you well enough to predict you'll order the same coffee every morning is forcing your hand. Mara granted that for ordinary cases but said the Archivist's is different in kind, not just degree, because her knowledge has to be true logically before there's any actual person with any actual habits for her to be reading, there's no track record yet, so what could possibly make it true in advance that a person who doesn't exist would do one thing rather than another in a situation that also doesn't exist? Colin answered that counterfactuals don't need a real subject sitting around to be true, we say stuff like "if I'd left five minutes earlier I'd have missed the crash" all the time and nobody thinks that requires a spare copy of us to already exist somewhere. Mara's comeback was that ordinary counterfactuals like that are only true, if they're true at all, because they're anchored to a real person's actual settled character, whereas the Archivist's are supposed to be fixed before any character gets settled by anything, which she said sounds less like a fact and more like a fact shaped hole. Colin's response was that maybe not every truth needs something else sitting underneath it holding it up, maybe some facts about what a possible person would freely choose are just brute, and Mara hasn't earned the right to demand a "grounding" for them just because she wants one. Mara's last word was that if that's really the move then Colin's basically conceding the Archivist doesn't know anything at all, she's just got a string of lucky guesses that always happen to land, dressed up in fancier language, which makes her a lot less impressive as a god than the book wants her to be.

I'd love to use whichever side of this is actually stronger for how the book handles the Archivist, so could you go through Mara and Colin's exchange move by move and say who's got the better of it at each stage rather than just picking an overall winner? And if this is a real, named problem in the philosophy of religion literature rather than something they invented at the pub, I'd be curious what the standard reply is, since neither of them seemed to know the field and I want to know what they're both missing.
