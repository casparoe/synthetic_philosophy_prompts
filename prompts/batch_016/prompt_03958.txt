I run a small Maimonides reading group in my department with a couple of postdocs and a rabbinical student, purely for fun, and last week after we finished Guide III.32 two of them (Naomi and Dan) ended up arguing about it over coffee for a good half hour while I mostly just topped up cups and listened. I've been turning it over since and honestly can't decide who had the better of it, so I want a second opinion. Naomi's opening position was that Maimonides is basically right in III.32: the sacrificial commandments are a concession to the fact that the Israelites left Egypt having only ever known worship as animal-sacrifice-at-temples-full-of-idols, and human nature can't flip from one extreme to the other overnight, hence the weaning-an-infant comparison. On her reading sacrifice has no intrinsic value at all, its just scaffolding whose whole job is to redirect that impulse away from idolatry and eventually phase itself out. Dan pushed back with the messianic-era problem: Maimonides, in the Mishneh Torah, rules flatly that the messianic king will rebuild the Temple and get the sacrificial service running again, in the very same section of law where he says the messianic era is one in which idolatry has been wiped out and, quoting Isaiah in the closing lines of the entire Mishneh Torah, "the earth will be full of the knowledge of the Lord." If sacrifice was only ever training wheels for a specific ancient disease, Dan said, it's strange to legislate in advance that the training wheels go back on right when the disease is cured.

Naomi's comeback had two parts. First: a commandment's rationale doesn't have to stay operative for the law to keep binding -- nobody thinks Maimonides expects the detailed reasons he gives elsewhere (III.46) for particular sacrificial laws, like why sheep were fair game because the Egyptians revered the constellation Aries, or why goats were included because other sects worshipped goat-formed spirits, to lapse just because those specific cults are long dead, so why think the whole institution should lapse either. Second, and more interesting: Maimonides is a strict naturalist about the messianic age in the very next chapter of Hilchot Melachim -- nothing about nature or human psychology changes, only Israel's political circumstances do -- so if part of what the concession was managing all along was a generic human pull toward concrete, sensory worship and not just idolatry as such, there's no reason that pull should evaporate just because organized idolatry does. Dan's rejoinder was that this saves the consistency by changing the subject: III.32 is explicit that the target is idolatry specifically, with sacrifice as a strategic device for out-competing and eventually erasing a rival cult (Maimonides even reads the pre-Golden-Calf layer of the law as containing no sacrifices at all). If the real driver were actually a permanent human need for concrete ritual, that's a different and much weaker claim than the one Maimonides is actually making in III.32, and Naomi hasn't earned the swap, since nothing in his account of why the concession was needed in the first place appeals to ritual-craving in general rather than to the specific pull of the idolatrous cults around Sinai.

They were still going at it when I had to head out. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right about what at each stage, and whether there's something both of them are overlooking?
