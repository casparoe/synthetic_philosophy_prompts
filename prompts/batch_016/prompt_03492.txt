After six years, my co-founder and I are dissolving our small consulting partnership. For tax reasons we've agreed to split the remaining client receivables over four sequential quarterly payouts rather than one lump sum; each quarter, one of us -- alternating -- is responsible for reconciling the books and reporting what's owed. A friend finishing a PhD in economics pointed out that this has the same structure as the "centipede game": whoever handles the final payout has every incentive to shortchange the other, and standard backward induction says that incentive unravels all the way back to the start -- so neither of us has any rational basis for trusting the other even now, on the first payout.

That conclusion strikes me as absurd, but resisting it isn't just wishful thinking. There's a genuine, unresolved dispute among game theorists and philosophers -- sometimes called the backward induction paradox -- over whether backward induction really is the rationally mandated solution to finite games like this one. It turns on hard questions about what a player may infer about an opponent's rationality once play reaches a stage that backward-induction reasoning itself says should never occur. Please write an essay laying out the strongest case for backward induction being correct here, the strongest case against it, and then defend an actual verdict rather than concluding that both sides have a point.
