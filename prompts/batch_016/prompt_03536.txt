I'm preparing a session on Descartes and animal minds for my early modern seminar, and a conversation with a colleague in cognitive science has left me wanting a second opinion before I teach it. Everyone arrives already knowing the headline view — that Descartes treats animals as mere automata, lacking souls and genuine thought — but the material I actually want to build the class around is more specific: the pair of independent tests he offers in Part V of the Discourse on the Method for telling a genuine thinking being apart from any machine built to imitate one, however cleverly.

The first is a linguistic test. Descartes grants that a machine could be built to produce fixed verbal outputs keyed to particular stimuli — crying out when struck, say, or asking what you want when touched in a certain spot — but insists that no machine could flexibly rearrange its words so as to respond appropriately to the sense of whatever might be said to it, on any subject whatsoever, the way even the dullest human being manages to do. The second is a versatility test: even a machine that outperformed us at some narrow task would inevitably come apart at others, because — unlike reason, which he calls a "universal instrument" serviceable in every circumstance life presents — a machine needs some distinct arrangement of parts suited to each particular action. Descartes treats passing both tests as necessary for genuine thought, and takes it as obvious that no animal, and no machine buildable in his century, could pass either one.

Here is where I would value your help. Suppose Descartes were somehow confronted with a contemporary large language model: a system whose output is fluent, contextually apt, and topically unconstrained in just the way he thought only a genuine thinker could manage, and so, prima facie, a far stronger showing on his first test than he ever imagined a mere mechanism achieving. Reasoning strictly from Descartes' own stated commitments — his substance dualism, his conception of the rational soul as simple and indivisible, and his specific rationale for treating flexible language use as diagnostic of that soul — rather than from what we ourselves happen to believe about such systems, what do you think he would be forced to conclude? Would he have to grant that the first test is passed and that something soul-like is therefore present; would he instead retreat to the second test, arguing that fluent conversation is compatible with failing badly enough at other things — sustained embodied action, say, or reliably avoiding confabulation — that the system still comes apart on versatility overall; or does his own framework leave him without any principled way to hold the line? I'd like a careful reconstruction of the strongest response you think he would actually give, explicit at each step about which metaphysical commitment is doing the work, and an honest assessment of whether that response amounts to a genuine application of his views or starts to look like an ad hoc patch.
