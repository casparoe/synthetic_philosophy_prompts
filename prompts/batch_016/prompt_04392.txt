ok kind of a dumb rabbit hole but I've been rereading old Hanson posts on futarchy (the "vote on values, bet on beliefs" thing -- I think I first ran into it via Inadequate Equilibria, or maybe it was just years of Overcoming Bias comment sections, honestly can't remember). anyway I keep noticing the same 7-8 objections get trotted out every time futarchy comes up, but nobody ever actually weighs them against each other. some of these feel like they'd sink the whole project even under a best-case implementation, others feel more like mechanism-design annoyances you could patch with enough cleverness.

here's my list, paraphrased from years of comment-section osmosis (crypto DAO people actually trying to build this stuff now, e.g. MetaDAO, run into versions of half of these too):

1. Corruptible metric — whatever scalar becomes the official "national welfare" score turns into a Goodhart target the moment trillions in trades hinge on it.
2. Plutocracy — trading takes capital, so the rich's "beliefs" dominate the market; it's one-dollar-one-vote wearing an epistemics costume.
3. Cheap manipulation — anyone who cares more about which policy wins than about profit can just eat an expected loss to move the price, exactly on the questions people care most about.
4. Thin conditional markets — most real policy questions won't draw enough informed traders on the branch that doesn't get chosen, so you never get a clean signal on what would've happened.
5. Perverse scoring inside decision markets — if a trader expects policy A to win anyway, they can profit more from trashing B's price than pricing it honestly, since only the implemented policy actually gets scored.
6. Existential risk gets discounted — decades-out bets don't attract patient capital, and bets don't pay out if civilization ends, so tail risk is structurally underpriced.
7. Values and beliefs aren't actually seperable — the Tyler Cowen-ish objection that what people say they want is shaped by what they think is achievable, so "vote on values, bet on beliefs" smuggles in more of a values judgment than it admits.
8. Legitimacy cost — even a perfectly accurate market might tank public buy-in, since voters no longer get a real say in which policy gets chosen, only in the abstract value function.
