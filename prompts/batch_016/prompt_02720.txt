In Anarchy, State, and Utopia, tucked between the experience machine and the entitlement theory for which he is far better remembered, Nozick devotes a surprisingly sustained stretch of argument—running through the sections "Constraints and Animals," "Underdetermination of Moral Theory," and "What Are Constraints Based Upon?" (roughly pp. 35–49)—to the moral status of non-human animals. He floats, and then worries at, a position he labels "utilitarianism for animals, Kantianism for people": aggregate animal welfare in the ordinary utilitarian fashion while protecting human beings with stringent, non-aggregative side constraints. Almost immediately he notices the strain this puts on his own construction: since a person's gain from sacrificing an animal will typically swamp the animal's loss, his own utility-monster-style worry seems to recoil on him, leaving animals "too subordinate" to persons. He then imagines beings from another galaxy who might "stand to us as it is usually thought we do to animals," sketches a hierarchy of possible moral statuses, and spends several pages hunting for the elusive characteristic—which he labels "M"—that could justify treating some beings as aggregatable resources and others as inviolable, eventually gesturing toward the capacity to shape one's life by an overall conception, and so toward something like a capacity for a meaningful life, without ever quite deciding whether this settles the matter.

I am assembling material for a graduate seminar on moral status and would rather hand students a genuine argument than the usual pro-and-con exchange about animal rights. Could you write a dialogue between two philosophers who both know this stretch of Nozick closely: one who thinks his search for M can be completed in a way that vindicates something like his original asymmetry—real but categorically lesser protections for animals, grounded in a difference of kind rather than degree—and another who thinks that taking Nozick's own hierarchy-of-superbeings argument, together with his trouble in pinning down M, seriously shows that no such asymmetry can be stabilized, and that consistency instead pushes toward far more stringent constraints on our treatment of animals than "utilitarianism for animals" allows. I would like the two of them to be equally sharp and to genuinely engage each other's specific moves—the threshold problem, the challenge of marginal cases, and what, if anything, stops the superbeings from redeploying against us the very reasoning meant to justify our treatment of animals—rather than each simply restating a favorite conclusion.
