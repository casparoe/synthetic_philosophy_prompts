I've hit a snag with two bits that are supposedly gesturing at the same idea but read to me like they're pulling in opposite directions, and I don't have any formal philosophy training to sort it out on my own.

The first is the famous "hinge" passage, from around the halfway point of the book: "341. That is to say, the questions that we raise and our doubts depend on the fact that some propositions are exempt from doubt, are as it were like hinges on which those turn. 342. That is to say, it belongs to the logic of our scientific investigations that certain things are in deed not doubted. 343. But much earlier in the book there's this river-bed image that, as far as I can tell, is supposed to be describing the same basic phenomenon: "96. The mythology may change back into a state of flux, the river-bed of thoughts may shift. But I distinguish between the movement of the waters on the river-bed and the shift of the bed itself; though there is not a sharp division of the one from the other. 99. And the bank of the river consists partly of hard rock, subject to no alteration or only to an imperceptible one, partly of sand, which now in one place now in another gets washed away, or deposited."

Is Wittgenstein just being loose with his own metaphors, or is there an actual way of reading "the hinges must stay put" and "the river-bed may shift" together without them contradicting each other — like maybe "stay put" just means "relative to whatever's being doubted right now," not "forever, full stop"?
