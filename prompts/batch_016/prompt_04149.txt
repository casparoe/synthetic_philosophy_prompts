Working on a piece about the St. Petersburg paradox and don't want to lean on the tired "diminishing marginal utility of money" line, since that doesnt actually fix things: Menger showed that for any unbounded utility function you can rebuild the game with prizes stated directly in utility units, so expected utility is infinite again -- only utility that's bounded on both ends escapes this. Then theres the "Pasadena game" (Nover & Hájek), where summing the payoffs in the natural coin-flip order gives a tidy finite answer, but the sum is only conditionally convergent, so reordering those same terms can get you any total whatsoever, including +/- infinity. And the "ergodicity" crowd (Ole Peters etc) now argue the fix isn't about utility at all -- what matters is the time-average growth a real person gets from playing repeatedly, not the expectation-across-possible-worlds that "expected value" computes. So: once you take these extensions seriously, does the paradox just show we need bounded utility, or does it actually break expected utility theory as a normative theory of rational choice? Write an essay weighing the live positions and commit to a real verdict -- conditional answers are fine, just don't land on "smart people disagree."
