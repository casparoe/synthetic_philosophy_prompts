I'm a retired analytical chemist -- spent about 35 years in a lab before I hung up my coat, and now I've fallen down the rabbit hole of reading philosophy for the first time, which is equal parts fun and humbling. A few of us from the old department get together once a month to read something short and argue about it over coffee. Last month's pick was an epistemology piece that mentioned something called "the dogmatism paradox," which apparently goes back to Saul Kripke: roughly, if I really *know* that h is true, then I already know that any future evidence against h has to be misleading, and since I know it's misleading, I'm entitled to just disregard it whenever it turns up. Two of the regulars, Frank (a retired immunologist) and Delia (ran a stats consulting shop for years), got into a proper argument about it using a case Frank made up on the spot: a PI who's lab's flagship result is, in her own mind, rock solid, so she won't even skim a new preprint picking apart the methodology. Kind of reminded me of how dismissively continental drift got treated for decades, actually.

Here's roughly how the argument went, as best I can reconstruct it. Frank opened by saying the whole thing is a reductio: any theory of knowledge that lets a scientist rationally pre-commit to not even reading a serious methodological critique has gone wrong somewhere, full stop. Delia said no -- the logic is just closure playing out honestly: if "I know h" really does entail "any genuine counter-evidence to h is misleading," the conclusion follows validly, and refusing to accept it just because it sounds bad isn't a counterargument, it's just squeamishness. Frank shot back that this can't be right, because it would license every crank -- flat-earthers, the works -- to use the exact same reasoning to wave off inconvenient data. Delia answered that this isn't a real counterexample: cranks don't actually know what they claim, they only feel like they do, so the license only ever kicks in for people who genuinely know, and that's not a cheat, it's just what a factive concept like knowledge requires. Frank replied that from the inside, genuinely knowing and merely feeling sure are indistinguishable, so a norm that only helps people who happen to already be right is useless as actual guidance -- it doesn't tell anyone, in the moment, whether to open the preprint or not. Delia then tried another angle: being entitled in advance to predict that any future critique would turn out flawed isn't the same as being entitled to disregard an actual critique once it exists on your desk, because your total evidence has changed by then and you have to actually look at it. Frank said that just relocates the problem -- if "knowledge" can be undone that easily by evidence which was, by hypothesis, supposed to be misleading, maybe it wasn't stable knowledge in the first place, which makes him suspect the whole setup smuggles in a shaky notion of "knowing" from the start. Nobody at the table settled it, and I havent been able to stop chewing on it since.

Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who's got the better of it at each step, not just declare an overall winner -- and flag anything important you think both Frank and Delia are missing? I don't have any background in this stuff, so please don't assume I already know the standard moves philosophers make here.
