I'm in my school's philosophy club, and a few weeks ago our faculty advisor had us read a short piece on Newcomb's Problem to open a unit on decision theory. It spiraled from there: at lunch one day, two members, Malik and Sasha, got into a long-running argument about something called "acausal trade" — the idea, from that same corner of decision theory, that two agents who can never causally interact with each other (because they are too far apart, because one of them does not exist yet, or because they occupy separate branches of a multiverse) might still coordinate for mutual benefit purely by each predicting how the other reasons. What set them off specifically was whether it could ever make sense for people today to act now on the assumption that some future superintelligent AI would "reward" that behavior later, even though there is no causal channel between us and it and no way to check whether any such deal is actually on.

(1) Malik opened by saying the whole idea is a category error: genuine trade requires the possibility of enforcement, some way for reneging to cost you something even informally, and since acausal "trading partners" never interact, communicate, or verify compliance, calling this "trade" is just a dignified way of saying you were going to do it anyway. (2) Sasha answered that this assumes enforcement has to be causal. If two reasoners can predict each other well enough, she said, the enforcement is logical rather than causal, because your disposition to defect is exactly the fact that determines whether the other party cooperates with you in the first place. She pointed to the classic case of two identical, ideally rational agents in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma who cooperate because each knows the other's reasoning mirrors its own — essentially Hofstadter's "superrationality," which decision theories like functional or timeless decision theory try to formalize — and said acausal trade is just this same idea applied to partners separated by time or space rather than a table. (3) Malik replied that this works for two copies of the same program, but a future AI or an alien civilization is not a copy of you, and you cannot inspect its source code; "predicting" such a partner, he said, is really just projecting your own values onto a blank and calling the projection a negotiating counterpart. (4) Sasha conceded that the epistemic worry is real in practice, but said this makes it a practical objection rather than a conceptual one: messy real-world cases are just noisier versions of the clean case, the way ordinary people's inability to compute exact probabilities does not undermine expected-value reasoning as such. She added that the case people actually care about may be less hard than Malik assumed, since a superintelligence would, by hypothesis, be far better at modeling humanity than humanity is at modeling it, so the epistemic gap he raised is mostly a problem for our side of the trade, not for its side. (5) Malik's comeback was that this last move makes the whole framework unfalsifiable: if any absence of evidence that the deal is on can always be explained away by "it already predicted this and priced it in," then the theory is compatible with literally any behavior on the other end, and something that cannot in principle be checked should not be guiding what we actually do now.

They never really settled it, and I've gone back and forth myself ever since. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each stage, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'm especially curious whether move (5) actually defeats the core idea behind move (2), or whether it only shows that we shouldn't act on acausal trade in a case like this one while leaving the underlying decision theory intact. And if there's an important consideration neither of them raised that changes the picture, I'd genuinely like to hear it.
