I'm assembling a collection of short stories, each centered on a philosopher at the point where private hope and professional method collide, and I'm stuck on the entry for Henry Sidgwick. His ethics arrives, in the end, at what he called the "dualism of practical reason": the admission that duty and self-interest are equally rational demands that can nonetheless conflict, a conflict he found tolerable only on the assumption of some moral order to the universe -- an afterlife, say -- guaranteeing that the two ultimately coincide. Since philosophy alone couldn't supply that guarantee, he spent over a decade pursuing empirical evidence for it as a founder of the Society for Psychical Research. In 1894 he and his wife Eleanor were cautiously encouraged by sittings with the Italian medium Eusapia Palladino in France; when he took a leading part in her sittings in Cambridge the following year, his circle caught her cheating, and that October he stood before the Society's general meeting and withdrew his support. Write a short story, under 1,300 words, in close third person from inside Sidgwick during that Cambridge sitting, in which the very rigor he has spent years insisting on is what forecloses, once again, the one thing his ethics actually needs him to find.
