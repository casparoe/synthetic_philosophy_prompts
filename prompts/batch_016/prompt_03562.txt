So this happened in my formal epistemology reading group (part of my phil PhD program) and I honestly still don't know who won. We were on Jeffrey conditionalization -- updating when evidence just shifts your credence across a partition instead of handing you flat-out certainty -- and someone posed a toy case: you glance at a scarf in bad light and become 60% confident it's green rather than blue, and seperately you're 70% confident the store tag ("forest green") is accurate. Two people, R and M, ran the update both orders -- glance then tag, tag then glance -- and got different final credences that the scarf is green.

R said this basically breaks the rule: if the same two bits of evidence give different answers depending on which you process first, the rule isn't tracking anything objective.

M said that's unfair -- rigidity (conditional probabilities on a partition staying fixed through its own update) is only ever secured for one partition at a time, so drift is expected unless it happens to hold jointly across both. That's not the rule failing, it's just what uncertain evidence is like.

R shot back: fine, but then the rule lets you land on totally different beliefs from identical total evidence purely from bookkeeping order, so it can't be doing real normative work.

M answered that plain Bayesian conditionalization hides the same issue -- it's just invisible since updating on outright certainties trivially commutes -- so Jeffrey's rule is only exposing a general fact about sequential updating, not adding a new flaw.
