In my graduate seminar on philosophy of language, we recently moved from Kaplan's "Demonstratives" to David Chalmers and Frank Jackson's work on epistemic two-dimensional semantics, and I think I've spotted a problem with the latter that I want to raise in my term paper. Kaplan already gave us a two-dimensional semantics for indexicals: an expression's character is a function from context of utterance to content, and its content is in turn a function from circumstance of evaluation to extension. Reading Chalmers and Jackson, their primary and secondary intensions look to me like exactly this same pair of functions, just relabeled and applied more broadly: primary intension is character, secondary intension is content.

If that's right, then there's nothing really new in Chalmers's celebrated discussion of "water" — the claim that its primary intension picks out XYZ at the Twin Earth scenario even though "water" is a rigid designator whose secondary intension picks out H2O at every possible world. That looks to me like a restatement, with a fancier example, of the perfectly familiar Kaplanian point that a term can have one fixed character while the content it expresses still varies depending on which circumstance of evaluation you plug in. And since the two-dimensional argument against materialism about consciousness is built entirely on the gap between primary and secondary intension, it seems to follow that its technical apparatus adds nothing to what Kaplan had already established decades earlier — Chalmers has just imported Kaplan's machinery into philosophy of mind under new names. Before I bring this to my professor, I'd like to know whether the objection actually holds up: is the primary/secondary intension distinction really doing the same work as Kaplan's character/content distinction, just relabeled and generalized?
