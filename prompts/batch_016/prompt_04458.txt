Michael Dummett's 2001 book *On Immigration and Refugees* argues that a country has a limited right not to be "submerged" by immigration, that there is nonetheless a strong presumption in favor of admitting refugees unless specific and weighty reasons justify refusal, and that most of Britain's postwar restrictionist legislation was driven by racism rather than by the legitimate concerns he was willing to grant existed. Dummett died in 2011, several years before small-boat Channel crossings became Britain's dominant immigration controversy and before the 2022 plan (abandoned in 2024 without a single deportation flight) to remove asylum seekers to Rwanda instead of hearing their claims in the UK. Reasoning from the specific distinctions Dummett actually draws rather than from generic left- or right-leaning intuitions about immigration, how do you think he would have judged the Rwanda scheme and the "stop the boats" rhetoric surrounding it: as a legitimate, if narrow, invocation of a state's rights, or as another instance of the racism he diagnosed in earlier legislation?
