I'm developing a feature on the mathematical case for utilitarianism, and Peter Diamond's 1967 rejoinder to Harsanyi's aggregation theorem strikes me as its most persistent unresolved challenge. Harsanyi showed that if a social planner and every individual are expected-utility maximizers, and the planner is indifferent between any two prospects that all individuals are indifferent between, then social welfare must be a weighted sum of individual utilities -- a result widely read as a proof of utilitarianism. Diamond's objection is that this criterion forces the planner to be indifferent between giving some indivisible good to person A outright, giving it to person B outright, and flipping a fair coin between them, so long as the planner is already indifferent between the two certain outcomes -- yet the coin flip strikes most people as obviously superior, since it alone gives each person a genuine chance rather than an arbitrary certainty for one and nothing for the other. The usual moral drawn from this is that Harsanyi's framework cannot accommodate a legitimate ex post fairness value alongside ex ante welfare, and I would like you to resist that moral. Please argue, as rigorously as you can, that Diamond's example reveals no real defect in Harsanyi's theorem or in the utilitarian conclusion it supports -- addressing, in particular, whether the appeal of the coin flip is better understood as a demand that risk- or inequality-aversion be built into the individual utility functions themselves, rather than as evidence of a freestanding fairness value that expected-utility social choice is failing to capture.
