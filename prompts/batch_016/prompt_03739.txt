Been on a weird little philosophical bender this month. I'm reporting out a longer piece on the longevity industry (rapamycin trials, the whole Bryan Johnson "Blueprint" routine, that crowd), and in between interviews I had to re-read Cicero's De Senectute for an unrelated classics podcast thing I said yes to before I knew how busy October would be. Reading them back to back kind of broke my brain, honestly, because they're both answering "what do we do about getting old" but landing in completely opposite places.

Cicero writes the whole thing in the voice of Cato the Elder, who's 84 in the dialogue, walking through basically every complaint people have about old age — that it sidelines you from real work, wrecks the body, cuts off your pleasures, and drags you closer to death — and arguing each one is either overstated or actually a fair trade. The bit that stuck with me is the ship-helmsman line: the old captain steering isn't doing "less" than the young sailors climbing the rigging, he's doing the part that actually matters, and old age is suited to that kind of judgment-based contribution rather than the physical kind. Theres also the random detail of Cato bragging about learning Greek for the first time in his 80s, and the story about Sophocles's sons trying to get him declared senile so they could grab his estate, and him basically clearing his name in court by reading the judges his newest tragedy. The whole treatise treats old age like a different job with its own toolkit, not a worse version of being young. Meanwhile every longevity scientist I've interviewed for this piece treats that entire frame as pretty and basically false — to them aging is a disease process, full stop, all cost and no upside, and the honest thing to do is try to cure it rather than write odes to it.

I'd love to see these two actually fight it out instead of me just summarizing both "views" side by side in the article. Could you write a dialogue between Cicero (arguing straight from the De Senectute playbook) and a present-day longevity scientist or transhumanist — say they end up seated next to each other backstage at some conference — where they really engage each other's specific points instead of just re-stating their own case past one another? I want both of them to land real hits, because after reading both I genuinely can't tell which one is coping and which one is right, or whether that's even the right way to put the question.
