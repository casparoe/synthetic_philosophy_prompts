I'm about halfway through a novel narrated by a fictional research assistant to Einstein at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton in the early 1950s — a young woman ostensibly hired to help him answer his mail who is secretly gathering material for a magazine profile of him. The book's central scene is a long evening in his study, not long after "Why Socialism?" appeared in the first issue of Monthly Review, in which she finally gets him talking, at length and off the record, about why a man so identified with the solitary, uncredentialed genius of his patent-office years would put his name to an argument for collective ownership. There's a minor subplot, lifted from the real FBI file on Einstein, in which an agent cites the essay itself as evidence of disloyalty, so I'd rather not misrepresent what it actually says. I want the scene to do real philosophical work rather than just gesture at "Einstein liked socialism" as a factoid — but I also don't want him to lapse into delivering a position paper with quotation marks around it.

Here is the passage I've drafted for his answer, compressed from a longer version:

"You want to know why a man who has spent his life prizing one mind's free play against a problem should end up arguing for collective ownership. It isn't because I've found some science that makes socialism as certain as starlight bending around the sun — economics is not astronomy, whatever it likes to pretend, and most of what passes for its laws are just the frozen habits of one especially rapacious period of history mistaking itself for permanent human nature. My reasons are humbler and, I think, harder to shake. I look at what unregulated competition does to a person before he's old enough to have chosen anything for himself — the anxious, grasping posture our schools reward — and I call that a moral injury, not merely an inefficiency. And I look at what happens to a democracy once a handful of men own the factories and the newspapers and, at one remove, the parties that stand for election: the citizen keeps his vote and loses the information he'd need to spend it wisely. Planning the economy might cure some of this. I will not pretend it cures all of it — a planned economy policed by an unanswerable bureaucracy could enslave a man as completely as any factory owner ever did, and no one, including me, has yet designed the office that keeps the planners honest."

What I'd value from you: first, whether this is a fair compression of the actual shape of Einstein's argument, rather than a flattering cartoon of it — in particular, whether it's right to make the ethical claim ("moral injury") carry more weight than any appeal to historical inevitability, of the kind you'd expect from a more orthodox Marxist case for socialism, and whether it's a real distortion to skip past the historical scaffolding he builds at the start of the essay — the material about conquering peoples monopolizing land and installing a priesthood to run education — which he uses to argue that existing economic "laws" can't be trusted to predict a socialist future. Second, and separately from accuracy: does the closing worry about bureaucracy feel like something he's earned within the speech, or does it read as a hedge I've bolted on so nobody mistakes him for a Stalinist?
