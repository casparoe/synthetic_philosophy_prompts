I'm a novelist, and an editor has asked me to contribute a short craft essay to an anthology on why readers weep over people who have never existed — the kind of piece meant to sit alongside more practical essays on plot and dialogue, but to take the emotional question seriously rather than just assert that good writing 'makes you feel things.' In preparing to write it, and after a long argument over dinner with two philosopher friends, I went back to the literature on the paradox of fiction: Colin Radford's original observation that we seem committed to three individually plausible but jointly inconsistent claims — that we are genuinely moved by characters and events we know to be invented, that a genuine emotion requires believing in the reality of its object, and that nobody who knows a character is fictional believes any such thing — and the two families of solution that still dominate the debate, Kendall Walton's view that what we feel toward fiction is never real fear or pity but only 'quasi-emotion' produced inside a game of make-believe, and the rival 'thought theories' associated with Peter Lamarque and Noël Carroll, on which ordinary emotions can be triggered by a vividly entertained thought-content with no existence belief required at all. I have no formal training in aesthetics, so before any of this goes near print I would like a sanity check from someone who does.

The paragraph I have actually drafted tries to use something from my own work-in-progress against both camps. Two-thirds of the way through my manuscript, a minor character's death is disclosed in a single flat clause embedded in another character's free indirect thought; of my early readers, every one who has reached that clause has been undone by it far more than by the fully staged, multi-page death of a major character a hundred pages earlier. Here is the paragraph as it stands: 'Neither standard theory predicts this asymmetry. Walton ties the strength of quasi-emotion to how completely a scene establishes the relevant fictional truths, so the more fully dramatized death should win out; thought theories tie it to how vividly a content is entertained, which again favors the dramatized scene over the buried clause. What seems to matter instead is a change of grammatical person: for one clause, the reader is not being told what happened to the character but is inhabiting, through free indirect style, a consciousness for whom the death is already an old, absorbed fact rather than breaking news. This suggests that something like the simulation theories of imagination associated with Gregory Currie and Ian Ravenscroft, on which imagining is a matter of running another person's perspective off-line rather than entertaining a content about them, is better placed to accommodate the example than either make-believe or thought-content accounts, since only simulation is sensitive to whose point of view is being occupied and not merely to what is being imagined.'

I would be grateful for your comments on three things: whether I have represented Walton and the thought theorists accurately enough to survive a philosophically literate reader, or have flattened positions that already have the resources to explain my example without conceding anything to simulation theory; whether the shift to simulation actually earns its keep philosophically or merely redescribes the puzzle in narratological terms; and, if the example strikes you as genuinely telling, what existing objection in the literature it most resembles, since I would much rather cite real precedent than claim a false originality once the essay is in print.
