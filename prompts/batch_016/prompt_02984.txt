I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I recently got interested in the "statue and the clay" puzzle in metaphysics (roughly: is a bronze statue the same object as the lump of bronze it's made of?) after running into some discussion of it online. Digging into the history of the problem, I found that Locke was wrestling with a strikingly similar issue three centuries earlier. Here's the passage, from Book II, Chapter XXVII of "An Essay Concerning Human Understanding" (1689), section 3:

"In like manner, if two or more atoms be joined together into the same mass, every one of those atoms will be the same, by the foregoing rule: and whilst they exist united together, the mass, consisting of the same atoms, must be the same mass, or the same body, let the parts be ever so differently jumbled. But if one of these atoms be taken away, or one new one added, it is no longer the same mass or the same body. In the state of living creatures, their identity depends not on a mass of the same particles, but on something else. For in them the variation of great parcels of matter alters not the identity: an oak growing from a plant to a great tree, and then lopped, is still the same oak; and a colt grown up to a horse, sometimes fat, sometimes lean, is all the while the same horse: though, in both these cases, there may be a manifest change of the parts; so that truly they are not either of them the same masses of matter, though they be truly one of them the same oak, and the other the same horse. The reason whereof is, that, in these two cases — a mass of matter and a living body — identity is not applied to the same thing."

I can parse the individual sentences well enough, but I'm struggling to reconstruct the actual argument. What exactly is the atom-swapping thought experiment supposed to establish, and how does it license the closing claim that "identity is not applied to the same thing" in the two cases? It almost sounds like Locke thinks the oak and the mass of matter composing it are two distinct objects occupying the same place at the same time — a bit like insisting that two distinct objects can occupy the same memory address — which seems like an odd thing to commit to. Could you walk me through the logical steps in plain language, and tell me whether this passage genuinely anticipates the modern "constitution" solution to the statue-and-clay problem (on which the statue and the lump are said to be numerically distinct things that merely coincide), or whether something importantly different is going on here?
