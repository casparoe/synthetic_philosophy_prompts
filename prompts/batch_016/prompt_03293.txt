About eighteen months ago I was diagnosed with retinitis pigmentosa, and my vision has deteriorated to the point where fine visual detail — brushwork, small figures, subtle shifts within a palette — is essentially out of reach for me now. I used to spend a lot of weekends wandering galleries on my own, and the hardest part of the adjustment has been learning to encounter visual art through someone else's eyes rather than my own. My friend Nora has become my regular gallery companion for exactly this reason: she has a good eye and a real gift for description. On a visit to a retrospective last month, she spent close to ten minutes on a single canvas, walking me through the palette, the composition, and eventually her own verdict that the painting was "quietly devastating." I mentioned this at dinner with some other friends that evening, purely as an aside about how vivid the description had been, and it unexpectedly set off an argument I haven't been able to settle since.

Felix, who hadn't been at the museum, said that what I'd actually come away with wasn't knowledge that the painting was devastating, only knowledge that Nora found it so — the way being told a joke is hilarious doesn't let you in on why it's funny (1). Sana pushed back: we accept exactly this kind of secondhand aesthetic knowledge all the time, trusting a friend with excellent taste that a restaurant is superb, or a critic that a novel is beautifully constructed, without having eaten there or read it ourselves; and even remembering a painting you once saw with your own eyes is arguably testimony from your past self, since you aren't perceiving the work at the moment you recall it either (2). Felix granted the memory case but drew a line: there's a difference between Nora reporting the sort of facts that would let me form my own impression — the palette, the places where the brushwork turns frantic rather than calm — and Nora simply handing over her finished verdict; only the former leaves room for my own sensibility to do the work aesthetic judgment is supposed to involve, which is why "I heard it's a masterpiece" has never been treated as equivalent to actually hearing the symphony (3). Sana answered that this distinction quietly collapses for exactly the art that matters most: for much of the collection we'd seen, there is no neutral inventory of such facts from which a third party could reconstruct the aesthetic effect, since the whole isn't recoverable from an independently specifiable list of its parts — and a principle that implies no blind person could, even in principle, know that a painting is great looks less like an epistemological discovery than a way of dressing up an exclusion we should already find suspicious (4).

Neither of them was showing off, and both seemed to me to be onto something real, which is partly why I still haven't been able to resolve it, even after reading a little about what I gather philosophers call the "aesthetic testimony" debate and the "acquaintance principle."
