I'm a PhD student in philosophy currently drafting a dissertation chapter on moral responsibility, and a move in the Frankfurt-cases literature came up in my department's free will reading group last week that I only followed in outline. I'd like a more precise explanation than I've been able to piece together on my own, ideally one that goes beyond just restating Frankfurt's original 1969 case. As I understand the basic dialectic, that original case -- where Black waits for some "prior sign" that Jones is about to choose otherwise, and intervenes only if it appears -- runs into what's sometimes called the dilemma defense, or the Widerker-Kane-Ginet dilemma: either the sign is causally sufficient for Jones's ensuing choice, in which case the example simply presupposes determinism and begs the question against the libertarian, or the sign is not sufficient, in which case Jones retains a robust alternative possibility after all, and the principle of alternative possibilities (PAP) survives untouched.

What I haven't managed to reconstruct on my own is exactly how Pereboom's later "Tax Evasion" cases are supposed to improve on this. My understanding is that, rather than positing a distinct antecedent sign, Pereboom has Black monitor something like a necessary component of the very deliberative process that would (indeterministically) issue in the alternative decision -- intervening just as soon as Joe would start to bring his moral reasons to bear against evading his taxes, instead of waiting on some separate predictive indicator. Could you walk me through, in some detail, why this restructuring is supposed to let the case avoid both horns of the dilemma at once -- avoiding any tacit commitment to determinism, while also avoiding leaving behind a residual alternative that's just a flicker of freedom by another name? I'd also appreciate it if you could lay out the main worry that Widerker presses against this strategy, which I gather turns on Joe ending up only "derivatively" responsible for the evasion in virtue of that very alternative, and tell me whether you think that worry is ultimately decisive against Pereboom's version of the Frankfurt case.
