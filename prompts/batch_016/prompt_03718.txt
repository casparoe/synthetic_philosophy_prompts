I'm putting together a lecture on Wittgenstein's *On Certainty* for my epistemology seminar, and before I commit anything to my notes I want to make sure I have the "hinge proposition" idea right. As I currently understand it, Wittgenstein's hinges — the sort of thing exemplified by the earth's having existed for a very long time, or one's having two hands — are simply the empirical beliefs that happen to enjoy the very highest degree of evidential support any of us ever accumulates. On this reading, there is one continuous scale of certainty running from shaky conjecture, through well-confirmed scientific hypotheses, up to beliefs so massively over-determined by a lifetime of converging experience that doubting them becomes practically unthinkable; hinges simply sit at the top of that scale. This seems to me to fit Wittgenstein's own river-bed image at §§96–99, where he describes the bank of the river as consisting partly of "hard rock" and partly of shifting sand — which sounds like a difference of degree in stability, not a difference in the kind of role that the rock and the sand are playing relative to the water flowing over them.

A colleague of mine who works on Wittgenstein insists that this "difference of degree" reading misses the whole point of the hinge remarks from §341 onward, where he calls certain propositions "exempt from doubt" and says that our doubts presuppose them rather than compete with them for evidential support. I would like to know, in some detail, whether she is right that something important is lost by treating hinge propositions as merely the most evidentially secure members of an ordinary class of empirical beliefs, rather than as playing a different logical or grammatical role altogether — and, if so, how I might make that difference vivid for my students, since simply asserting that hinges are "exempt from doubt" without explaining why tends to strike my seminar as question-begging.
