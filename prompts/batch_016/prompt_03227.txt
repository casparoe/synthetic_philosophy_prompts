In my working life as an experimental physicist, updating beliefs in light of new data meant one thing: conditionalize on the evidence, and it never mattered in which order separate pieces of data arrived — the final posterior came out the same either way. Since retiring, I have been reading in formal epistemology and came across Richard Jeffrey's generalization of conditionalization, designed for evidence that only redistributes one's confidence across a partition of possibilities without ever rendering any single member of it certain. As I understand it, unlike ordinary conditionalization, successive applications of Jeffrey's rule need not commute: updating first on one uncertain observation and then on a second can leave you with different final probabilities than updating in the reverse order. Construct a small, fully specified numerical example — an outcome space, a prior, and two such observations — in which this failure of commutativity actually occurs, and explain what would have to be true of the two observations for the order to stop mattering.
