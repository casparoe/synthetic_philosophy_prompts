I'm reporting a feature on the recent resurgence of public interest in free-will skepticism — fueled in large part by the reception of Robert Sapolsky's *Determined* — and in reading further back into the academic literature than the popular coverage usually goes, I've gotten stuck on Galen Strawson's "Basic Argument" against moral responsibility. Most secondary summaries I've found present it as essentially a more careful restatement of the standard hard-determinist case: you are the product of factors — genes, upbringing, circumstance — that you never chose, so determinism rules out the sort of responsibility that would make punishment or praise truly deserved. But when I went back to Strawson's own writing, he is emphatic that the Basic Argument is not that argument at all, and that it goes through with exactly the same force whether the universe turns out to be deterministic, thoroughly indeterministic, or even hospitable to some form of libertarian agent-causation. Since virtually every other position in this debate turns on the truth or falsity of determinism, I'm struggling to see what is actually doing the work in his version, if not that.

There's also a bit of intellectual family drama tangled up in this that I'd like to understand properly rather than just gesture at for color: Strawson's father, P. F. Strawson, wrote one of the most influential twentieth-century defenses of moral responsibility against the metaphysical worries of determinists, arguing in effect that our ordinary practices of praise, blame, and resentment do not need any "ultimate" metaphysical backing to be justified. The son's argument seems almost purpose-built to outflank exactly that move. Could you walk me through, step by step, the actual logical mechanics of the regress at the heart of the Basic Argument — including the notion of being causa sui, which Strawson borrows explicitly from Nietzsche — and explain why he thinks no amount of indeterminism, or even exotic agent-causal machinery, can halt it? And could you then tell me plainly where a defender of moral responsibility has the best chance of blocking the regress: by denying some specific premise in it, or by following the father's route and denying that "ultimate" responsibility was ever the kind that mattered in the first place?
