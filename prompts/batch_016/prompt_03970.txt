I work as a site reliability engineer at a mid-sized fintech company, and we recently went through a genuinely tense postmortem after an outage caused by a colleague pushing an unreviewed configuration change that took checkout down for the better part of an hour. The retro split the team into two camps: one wanted a strictly "blameless" process that avoids naming names or attributing the failure to anyone's specific judgment, on the theory that this reduces defensiveness and surfaces the systemic conditions that really caused the failure, while the other worried that refusing to ever say a particular decision was careless quietly lets people off the hook and erodes any real sense of accountability over time. Our VP of Engineering, who studied philosophy before switching careers, tried to reframe the whole argument by bringing up P. F. Strawson's work on reactive attitudes and moral responsibility, in particular his distinction between the "participant" and "objective" attitudes we can take toward another person, and the further distinction, much discussed in the secondary literature on him, between conditions that merely excuse a particular act and conditions that exempt a person from the reactive attitudes altogether.

I have no formal philosophy training and only got the two-minute hallway version of all this, but it struck me as more than just a clever analogy for our situation. Could you describe the most important implications of Strawson's framework, and specifically of the participant/objective and excuse/exemption distinctions, for how a team like mine should structure incident postmortems and think about individual accountability afterward? I'd like you to work with the actual mechanics of Strawson's view rather than give me a general tour of the compatibilism-versus-libertarianism debate, and I'd be especially interested in your honest assessment of whether the "blameless postmortem" culture common at companies like Google and Etsy is, on reflection, a fairly faithful application of something like Strawson's picture, or whether it is quietly smuggling in a different and perhaps incompatible theory of responsibility.
