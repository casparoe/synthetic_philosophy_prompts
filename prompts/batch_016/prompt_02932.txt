So I cover science and ideas for a living, and for the last couple of weeks I've been knee-deep in stories about the new tariff fights, where basically every senator and think-tank guy treats the US trade deficit like its a scoreboard we're obviously loosing on. That sent me down a rabbit hole into David Hume's 1752 essay "Of the Balance of Trade," where he goes after the mercantilists with what's now called the price-specie-flow mechanism: a country running a trade surplus pulls in gold, the extra gold pushes up domestic prices, pricier goods make its exports less competitive and imports more attractive, and the surplus corrects itself automatically, no tariffs or government intervention required. I even found some historians arguing Hume's real target wasn't mercantilist protectionism at all but paper credit and public debt schemes — John Law's system and its imitators — which made me wonder if the tidy "Hume = free trade hero" story I grew up with is missing something.

Anyway, here's what I can't shake: that whole mechanism assumes gold-backed money that physically moves between countries until prices equalize, and we just don't live in that world anymore. Currency is fiat, exchange rates float or get managed, the dollar is everybody's reserve currency, and the US has run a trade deficit for something like fifty years straight without Hume's automatic correction ever really kicking in. I ran this by an economist friend and she just shrugged and said "different world, different rules," which felt like a cop-out. So: if you actually sat Hume down and walked him through all of that, what would he say? Would he conclude his own mechanism has simply broken because its gold-standard assumptions no longer hold, would he point to something specific in the modern plumbing — reserve-currency status, capital-account surpluses quietly offsetting the trade side, central banks sterilizing the flows — as the thing gumming up the works, or is there a stubborn version of him who'd insist the deficit is secretly correcting in ways we're just measuring wrong? I don't want the greeting-card version of Hume where he just "loved free trade" — I want someone who'll actually reason through the mechanics of his own argument and figure out where it does, and doesn't, still hold up.
