I spent my career as a geologist -- volcanology mostly -- and one of the rabbit holes I've fallen down since retiring is the early history of the field, which is how I ended up reading about Athanasius Kircher, the 17th-century Jesuit who apparently had himself lowered into the crater of Vesuvius to take measurements before writing his "Mundus Subterraneus." One thing led to another and I found out that Sor Juana Ines de la Cruz -- the Mexican nun everybody cites as an early champion of women's education -- actually names Kircher as one of her own sources in her famous letter, the "Respuesta a Sor Filotea." That sent me off to read up on the whole episode behind that letter, and it turns out to be alot stranger and more specific than the potted version I already had in my head (she owned something like 4,000 books plus a pile of scientific instruments in her cell, for one thing, so this clearly wasnt a woman being kept away from "worldly" things in general).

Here's what really got me: the bishop who kicked the whole fight off -- a guy named Manuel Fernandez de Santa Cruz, writing to her under a fake nun's name, "Sor Filotea," after her critique of a famous old sermon got published without her okay -- wasn't making the crude case that women can't reason. As best I can tell his actual argument leaned on an old scholastic distinction between "curiosidad," an illicit, excessive itch to know things for their own sake, and a proper, useful "studiousness" aimed at your soul's business, and he holds up Boethius as the model to follow, a guy who supposedly moved on from the mere "subtleties" of natural philosophy to the "usefulness" of moral philosophy. Sor Juana's comeback in the Respuesta isn't just "let women study too" -- she goes after the curious/useful line itself, at one point arguing that if Aristotle had done some cooking he'd have written a lot more, since she claims to have worked out genuine physical facts (why an egg sets in hot oil but falls apart in syrup) purely by paying attention in the kitchen. She's also got a separate argument re-reading the bit about women keeping silent in church as being about public preaching specifically, not private study or writing at home. None of that is the flat "yay, education for everyone" story I was expecting going in.

So here's my request: write me a dialogue between the two of them, imagined face to face instead of trading letters months apart, where they actually hash this out properly -- you don't need to write it in faux-archaic prose, I just want the substance of the arguments right. Give the bishop's side its strongest real form (the curious-versus-useful distinction, whatever he'd say about the propriety of a nun publicly taking down a famous preacher's scholarship, the pride angle) rather than a strawman who just thinks women are dim, and have Sor Juana actually engage with that case rather than just delivering her famous lines as a monologue at him -- I'd love it if the egg-and-syrup bit shows up, but I want to see her use it to attack his distinction directly, and I want him to get a real comeback rather than just folding. Ideally by the end it's clearer whether the two of them are actually disagreeing about women's capacity to reason at all, or about something else entirely -- what counts as prideful curiosity, who gets to publicly interpret scripture, that sort of thing.
