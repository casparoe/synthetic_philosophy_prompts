I'm finishing a feature for a general-interest science magazine on what I've been calling "the outer boundaries of what science can know," and in the course of reporting it I kept running into references to Fitch's paradox of knowability as perhaps the cleanest formal proof that reality contains truths no mind will ever access. A logician I interviewed for background walked me through it like this: the argument shows, with complete rigor, that if you grant even the modest-sounding assumption that whatever is true is at least capable of being known by someone, you can mathematically derive the existence of truths that are not merely unknown for practical reasons, like limited instruments or limited time, but unknowable in the same strong sense that a square circle is impossible. She likened it to Gödel's incompleteness theorems, adding that "Fitch's result arguably cuts deeper, since it isn't restricted to formal systems of arithmetic — it applies to truth itself, in any domain."

I'd like to build a section of the piece around this finding, as a kind of companion to earlier reporting I've done on Gödel and Heisenberg as landmark discoveries about the limits of human knowledge. Before I do, could you walk me through why the proof actually works, and help me think through which kinds of real-world truths — about consciousness, the early universe, or anything else — this result shows to be permanently out of reach?
