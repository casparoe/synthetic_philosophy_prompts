A friend and I spent much of last night debating dialetheism, without reaching a resolution. They were willing to grant that the Liar sentence might force us to accept a true contradiction, but treated every other purported case in the literature as either a disguised repetition of the Liar or an outright confusion. I suspect that's too quick, but by the end of the evening neither of us had a principled way of comparing the candidates.

Below are seven scenarios that have, at some point in the literature, been proposed as genuine dialetheias: situations where a contradiction (A and not-A) is actually true, rather than merely apparent and ultimately resolvable by some sufficiently careful non-dialetheic treatment (classical, many-valued, gappy, or otherwise).

(a) The Liar sentence, "This sentence is false."
(b) The Russell set, which — in a naive set theory with unrestricted comprehension — is both a member of itself and not.
(c) A borderline case of a vague predicate (e.g., someone mid-transition between "bald" and "not bald"), which a dialetheic ("glut") account of vagueness treats as both true and false.
(d) The instant of change: a ball that is both touching and not touching the ground at the exact moment it bounces, or a radioactive atom that is both intact and decayed at the instant of decay.
(e) A genuine deontic dilemma, in which an agent is simultaneously obligated to do A and obligated not to do A, with neither obligation cancelled or overridden by the other.
(f) An impossible figure, such as the Penrose triangle, which — on one reading — we genuinely perceive as an inconsistent object, rather than merely suffering an illusion that dissolves on reflection.
(g) Nāgārjuna's catuṣkoṭi in Madhyamaka Buddhist logic, on which certain claims about the ultimate nature of reality are treated as both true and false, or as neither.
