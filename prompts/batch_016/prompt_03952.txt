I'm drafting a novel that includes a short chapter built on a conceit I'm still nervous about: Frederick Douglass, by some device I haven't fully settled on (a found manuscript, a fever dream — it hardly matters), is made to confront a Supreme Court opinion handed down more than a century after his death. I want to earn this scene rather than coast on it, and the obvious risk is that I simply ventriloquize my own politics through a famous name and call it historical imagination. So before I write a line of dialogue, I'd like to pressure-test the premise against what Douglass actually argued, because the more of him I reread, the less sure I am that his real commitments point where his modern admirers tend to assume.

Here is the specific tension I can't resolve on my own. The opinion I have in mind is Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard, where the Court — Justice Thomas's concurrence especially — revived Justice Harlan's language from his Plessy dissent to argue that the Fourteenth Amendment forbids government from classifying anyone by race at all, even for remedial ends. Two strands in Douglass's own thought seem to me to cut toward agreeing with that, not against it: the interpretive method he settled on in 1851, after breaking with Garrison under the influence of Gerrit Smith and Lysander Spooner, and laid out at length in his 1860 Glasgow lecture on whether the Constitution was pro- or anti-slavery — the document never says "slave," and ambiguous language must be read in favor of liberty rather than filled in with the framers' private intentions — and, separately, his 1865 "What the Black Man Wants" speech, where he explicitly rejected special provision for the freedpeople and asked only to be "let alone" and judged as any other citizen would be. But there is also the older Douglass of the 1880s, who watched the Court hollow out the Civil Rights Act of 1875 in the 1883 Civil Rights Cases and denounced the decision at a mass meeting in Lincoln Hall as leaving Black Americans defenseless against private prejudice — which reads like a man discovering, within his own lifetime, that formal equality without government enforcement had already failed him once.

I'd like help thinking through, from the actual textual and rhetorical evidence rather than from a general sense of which side of history Douglass belongs on, how those three data points fit together — and, if you had to commit, which way they leave him leaning on something like Students for Fair Admissions. I would much rather have a careful account of the tension, including the possibility that it doesn't resolve cleanly, than a confident answer in either direction.
