I teach a graduate seminar that pairs Aristotle's writings on exchange with contemporary debates in political economy, and a recent session on Politics I.8–11 alongside the discussion of just exchange in Nicomachean Ethics V.5 left several students unsatisfied with the usual gloss of Aristotle as simply "anti-market." We had worked through the core distinctions: oikonomike, the natural and self-limiting art of household management, versus chrematistike, the unnatural and potentially limitless pursuit of wealth as such; the claim that money (nomisma) is a creature of convention rather than nature, which Aristotle thinks is shown by the very fact that it takes its name from nomos, law or custom, and can be rendered worthless by a change in that convention; and the argument that lending at interest is the most unnatural mode of acquisition of all, since interest, tokos, literally "offspring," treats money as though it could breed money on its own, apart from any exchange of actual goods. One student, who worked in software before returning to graduate school, then asked what Aristotle would make of a currency like Bitcoin: money "mined" into existence by computation, answerable to no polis or lawgiver, whose supply can be expanded still further through staking and lending protocols that generate new tokens without a single good ever changing hands.

I would like your own careful reconstruction of the verdict that Aristotle's actual framework, rather than a simplified paraphrase of it, would issue here. Does a currency generated by decentralized algorithmic consensus, rather than the authority of a polis, still satisfy what Aristotle means by nomisma, or does it fail to meet the conditions under which he thinks something counts as money at all? Is "mining," or the yield produced by staking, best understood on his terms as an even more extreme case of tokos than the usury he already singled out as the most unnatural mode of acquisition, given that it does not even involve lending a good to someone who will put it to use? And does his own anecdote about Thales and the olive presses in I.11 give us any reason to think Aristotle might have recognized something familiar, even admirable, in the ambitions of crypto speculators, rather than simply condemning them? I would rather you work through the tensions in the text than settle on whichever answer sounds tidiest.
