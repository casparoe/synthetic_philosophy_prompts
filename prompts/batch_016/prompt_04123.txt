I don't have any formal training in philosophy, but over the past year or so I've gotten interested in old arguments about what money actually is, mostly through following online debates about cryptocurrency and fiat currency. That curiosity eventually led me to George Berkeley, whom I'd only ever known as the philosopher who coined "esse est percipi" and denied that objects have any material substance behind our perceptions of them. It turns out that later in life, as Bishop of Cloyne, he wrote a strange little book called The Querist, made up entirely of rhetorical questions about Ireland's economy. I read it on Project Gutenberg and got so absorbed that I drafted some notes connecting it back to his metaphysics. A friend who works in finance thought I was reaching, but I'd like a more careful outside opinion before I decide whether there's anything to it.

Here is the heart of what I've written so far. The Querist keeps circling one question: is money valuable in itself, or merely a device for keeping track of something else? Berkeley comes down firmly on the second option, asking whether "the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter," and elsewhere whether gold, silver, and paper are anything more than "tickets or counters for reckoning, recording, and transferring" people's claims on one another's labor. He goes further, asking whether, if the bullion behind a currency vanished but the "denominations" were kept, trade and wages could carry on exactly as before, and whether a nation might possess real wealth "without the help of gold and silver" at all, so long as industry and credit kept circulating. My notes argue that this isn't just shrewd economic commentary but the same philosophical move he makes against material substance, applied a second time to money. Just as sensible objects, on his view, are nothing over and above regular, law-governed patterns of perception, with no hidden "stuff" propping them up, currency is nothing over and above a trusted, regular social practice of recording and transferring claims on labor, with no hidden metal "stuff" needed to back it. Wealth, on this reading, is the industry itself; money is just the notation.

I suspect entire books have probably been written tying Berkeley's economics to his idealism, but I arrived at the parallel on my own, going back and forth between the Querist and the Principles, so I genuinely don't know whether it's a well-worn observation, a defensible but fairly modest one, or a stretch that mistakes a pun on the word "substance" for a real philosophical connection. Could you tell me, first, whether I'm representing Berkeley's actual position in the Querist fairly, and second, whether the link I'm drawing to his immaterialism holds up under pressure or is more superficial than I think? I'd also like to know whether the resemblance I keep sensing to modern arguments for fiat currency and cryptocurrency, that money needs no intrinsic backing, only a reliable practice of trust and use, is genuinely present in Berkeley's text, or something I'm projecting backward onto it. I'd rather hear that it doesn't hold up than keep repeating it to people at dinner.
