I'm about a third of the way into a novel set in Cambridge in the early 1880s, built around a fictionalized version of the circle that founded the Society for Psychical Research. My protagonist, a moral philosopher closely modeled on Henry Sidgwick, spends his mornings lecturing on the foundations of ethics and his evenings in darkened rooms, timing thought-transference trials and cross-examining mediums for signs of fraud. A friend who read the draft objected that this makes the character incoherent: surely, she argued, a philosopher famous for rigor, and for candidly admitting an unresolved "dualism of practical reason" at the close of his major work in ethics, would not also spend decades chasing telepathy and spirit photography. Before I revise the manuscript, I want to work out whether her objection actually holds up.

So I'd like you to write me an essay that takes this question head-on: was Sidgwick's real, decades-long involvement in psychical research in genuine tension with his philosophical commitments, or was it, in some important sense, continuous with them? Please engage specifically with the link between that research and the unresolved conflict between egoism and utilitarianism he identifies at the end of The Methods of Ethics — as I understand it, he hoped that empirical evidence of personal survival after death might do the work that a purely rational argument for the ultimate coherence of duty and self-interest could not. I'd also like your take on what it says about him as a thinker that the research never delivered the proof he wanted, and that by his own account late in life he wavered on whether the whole project had been worth the effort. Rather than settling for "it's complicated," please commit to an actual verdict on whether the philosopher and the psychical researcher were, in the end, pursuing a single coherent project or two projects awkwardly sharing one biography — and defend it.
