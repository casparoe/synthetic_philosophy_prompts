In my philosophy elective, I've been reading about how the ancient Stoics handled the sorites ('heap') paradox, and I've been trying to work out how Chrysippus's actual position maps onto the modern debate between epistemicism and supervaluationism about vagueness. Ancient sources (Cicero among them) report that Chrysippus advised the Stoic sage, on reaching the unclear middle stretch of a sorites series, to simply stop responding and fall silent — refusing either to keep assenting to each "one more grain won't matter" premise all the way to an absurd conclusion, or to commit to some specific grain as the exact cutoff. Some commentators read this as an early form of epistemicism in Williamson's sense (there is a real, sharp cutoff; we just cannot know where it lies), while others take it to imply something closer to a supervaluationist denial of bivalence for borderline cases, since the sage apparently cannot even form a clear ("kataleptic") impression to assent to near the boundary. Given what is actually known about Chrysippus's logic, his theory of assent, and Stoic commitments about truth, which of these two modern readings do you think fits him better, and what is the strongest piece of textual or philosophical evidence against your own conclusion?
