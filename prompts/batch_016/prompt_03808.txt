I've been rereading Rawls's 1942 Princeton senior thesis, A Brief Inquiry into the Meaning of Sin and Faith, for a graduate seminar on the theological background of political liberalism, and I keep running into a tension I haven't managed to resolve to my own satisfaction. The thesis is a work of neo-orthodox Protestant theology through and through: its central move is a distinction between "natural" relations -- grounded in desire, appetite, and self-interested calculation -- and properly "personal" ones, and its central diagnosis is that Christian ethics keeps collapsing the second into the first. Rawls treats classical social contract theory as a paradigm case of exactly this collapse. He reads Hobbes's and Locke's political theories, and Adam Smith's picture of a community held together by enlightened self-interest, as false pictures of community, on the grounds that a community which explains its own cohesion in terms of mutual egoism thereby undermines itself. Sin, on his account there (borrowed from Philip Leon), is not self-love as such -- moderate self-love is unobjectionable -- but egotism: a hardening of self-love that seals a person off from genuinely personal relations with others.

Three decades later, in A Theory of Justice, the mature Rawls builds an entire theory of justice around a hypothetical contract: the parties in the original position are modeled as rational and, in his own phrase, "mutually disinterested," deliberating behind a veil of ignorance that strips away all knowledge of their particular ends and social position. And roughly two decades further on, when he turns explicitly to the place of religion in political life, in Political Liberalism and then in "The Idea of Public Reason Revisited," he again reaches for a broadly contractualist apparatus: public reason, the proviso that comprehensive doctrines -- religious or secular -- may be introduced into public political debate as long as properly political reasons are supplied in due course for whatever they are invoked to support, and a hoped-for overlapping consensus among reasonable comprehensive doctrines rather than convergence on any single one of them.

I'd like you to compare these two Rawlses in some detail and then answer a specific question: does the machinery of the original position, together with the later public-reason treatment of religion, actually escape the young Rawls's own indictment of contract theory as a naturalistic, egoism-based false picture of community, or does it just relocate the same structure one level up, with the veil of ignorance doing duty for something like grace and "unreasonableness" inheriting the functional role once assigned to egotism? Please justify your answer by engaging with the specifics of both texts rather than simply asserting continuity or rupture, and tell me which of the parallels you can find strike you as doing genuine philosophical work and which strike you as merely verbal echoes.
