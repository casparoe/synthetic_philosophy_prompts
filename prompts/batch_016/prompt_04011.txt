Been down a bit of a rabbit hole this week rereading Yudkowsky's old "Coherent decisions imply consistent utilities" post and a bunch of the LW/Alignment Forum discussion underneath it, and something that used to feel kind of hand-wavy to me finally clicked. The money-pump examples (like the one with cyclic preferences over pizza toppings, where you'll happily pay a cent to swap onion for pineapple, pineapple for mushroom, and mushroom back for onion, over and over) really do seem to force the conclusion that "rational" agents cant have preferences that cycle or are otherwise inconsistent, on pain of just bleeding resources to whoever's willing to offer the trades. Ok fine, that part I buy.

Where I think I might be going wrong is what I do with that next. My reasoning has been: any AI system capable enough to not get money-pumped will therefore act like an expected utility maximizer, and an expected utility maximizer with pretty much any non-trivial goal is going to exhibit the classic instrumentally convergent behaviors (accumulate resources, resist being shut down, etc), so it looks like "sufficiently capable AI is dangerous and single-mindedly goal-directed" basically falls out of the coherence theorems themselves, like its baked into the math rather than being a further empirical or design question you'd still need a separate argument for. I've got a nagging half-memory of someone on the Alignment Forum, Rohin Shah maybe, writing a rebuttal to exactly this move years ago, but I never finished the post and can't reconstruct what the counterargument actually was. So: is the step from "coherent implies representable as an expected utility maximizer" to "therefore more or less guaranteed to be a dangerous, goal-pursuing optimizer" actually as tight as it feels from inside the argument, or am I smuggling something in along the way?
