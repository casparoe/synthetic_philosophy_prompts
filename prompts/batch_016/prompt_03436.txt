Ok so I've been rereading Yudkowsky's old 2004 CEV writeup for a discussion group I'm part of (we're slowly working through a bunch of the ancient SL4 / Overcoming Bias-era stuff), and the bit that's been stuck in my head isn't the "extrapolate everyone and find where it all lines up" part that everyone always talks about, it's the "Last Judge" clause near the end. IIRC the idea is that even after the whole coherent-extrapolated-volition computation finishes, one single human of good judgement gets shown the output before it's actually enacted and can veto the entire thing — but only if something seems to have gone horribly wrong, not just because the result feels shocking or unfamiliar. Mere future-shock isn't supposed to count. There's also that weirder fail-safe where if the extrapolated volition itself says it doesn't want to be implemented, the dynamic is supposed to shut itself down rather than barrel ahead anyway. I don't think I've ever come across a story that actually sits inside that specific moment, instead of doing the usual "AI wakes up and either kills everyone or saves everyone" thing.

Could you write me a short story set entirely inside that moment, from inside the Last Judge's head, in the stretch of time before they have to give (or withold) the seal of approval? The computation is done. They've been handed something — a document, a vision, a long conversation, whatever medium feels right to you — that's supposed to be what humanity would want if we'd all gotten smarter and more self-aware, and had time to really hash things out with each other. Nobody can point to a flaw in it, it isn't cartoonishly evil, it passes every coherence check anyone can throw at it — but it feels alien to the judge in a way they can't quite pin down, like something wearing humanity's face without being entirely humanity anymore. And they know they're only supposed to veto for "gone horribly wrong," not "gives me the creeps," which is exactly the distinction they can't cleanly draw. It's basically the same problem philosophers run into with "ideal advisor" or full-information theories of well-being — the worry that a sufficiently idealized version of you might drift far enough from you that it's genuinely unclear why its preferences get to override your actual, current, un-idealized ones. I don't want the story to resolve that neatly or tell me whether the judge is right to hesitate, I just want it to sit honestly inside the dilemma. Something like 800-1500 words, first person or close third, your call on everything else.
