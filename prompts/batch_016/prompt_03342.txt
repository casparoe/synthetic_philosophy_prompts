I've spent the past couple of weeks re-reading John Taurek's 1977 paper "Should the Numbers Count?", which came up in a comment thread on the EA Forum, and I've argued myself into a puzzle that a few rationalist friends haven't managed to talk me out of either. Taurek's central case involves a scarce drug that can either save one person, David, who needs the entire dose to survive, or five other people, each of whom needs only a fifth of the dose; there isn't enough to do both. Against the intuitive response that you obviously save the five, Taurek argues that there is no one, not even an impartial observer, for whom the loss of the five lives is worse than the loss of David's life alone, since badness is always somebody's badness, and there is no super-person who bears the "sum" of five deaths. He treats this as showing that interpersonal aggregation is close to a category mistake, and he bites the bullet on the obvious follow-up question: even when the ratio is one against fifty rather than one against five, he still holds that a coin flip, rather than an automatic rescue of the fifty, is the fair procedure, since mere numbers cannot be allowed to matter once aggregation itself has been ruled out.

What I can't work out is why this isn't treated as a live threat to the reasoning underlying most of effective altruism, and to the "shut up and multiply" ethos you see constantly in rationalist writing on cause prioritization. Cost-per-life-saved figures, QALY comparisons, and every argument for funding whichever intervention helps more people rather than fewer are doing exactly the kind of interpersonal summing that Taurek says cannot legitimately be done. Yet people who are clearly familiar with his argument go on comparing cost-effectiveness across causes without apparent hesitation, as though it had never been made. Is there a principled reason Taurek's argument stays confined to his original rescue scenario and doesn't reach ordinary aggregative cost-effectiveness reasoning, or is the community mostly declining to follow an argument it hasn't actually refuted, because the conclusion would be paralyzing? I would like an answer that actually engages with whether, and why, that inference goes through, rather than one that simply reasserts the "numbers count" intuition without addressing Taurek's argument against aggregation on its own terms.
