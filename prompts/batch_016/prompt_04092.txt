When Chemistry Won't Sit Still

For most of the twentieth century, the reduction of chemistry to physics was treated as one of the few unqualified successes of scientific reductionism — the place where philosophers' talk of one theory being "nothing but" another actually cashed out in equations. Quantum mechanics, so the story goes, explains why atoms bond as they do, and a molecule's shape — the tetrahedral carbon, the twist of a helix, the handedness of a sugar — is just what falls out when you solve the Schrödinger equation for the right collection of nuclei and electrons.

There's a wrinkle that rarely survives into the popular version, though, and chemists and philosophers of science have been quietly arguing about it since Friedrich Hund first flagged it in 1927 and R. G. Woolley revived it in a pointed 1978 paper. Taken strictly, the Hamiltonian for an isolated molecule — electrons and nuclei, no outside interference — is symmetric under spatial inversion, and its exact energy eigenstates have to respect that symmetry. For a chiral molecule, the two mirror-image structures chemists draw as distinct objects, its "enantiomers," are not themselves eigenstates of that Hamiltonian. Solved honestly, the ground state comes out as an even superposition of both mirror images — not a molecule with one settled shape, but something closer to a coin balanced on its edge.

Actual chiral molecules don't behave this way, of course; a sample of a chiral sugar keeps its handedness indefinitely. To recover that, quantum chemists invoke the Born–Oppenheimer approximation, which fixes the nuclei at classical positions and thereby breaks the symmetry by fiat. That move is indispensable to every textbook picture of a molecule, but it isn't something the exact theory hands you; it's imported from outside. Fancier modern rescues appeal to decoherence — treating the molecule as an open system whose chirality is "selected" by collisions with its environment — but as Alexander Franklin and Vanessa Seifert have recently argued, this just relocates the difficulty onto the quantum measurement problem: whatever eventually explains why measurements have determinate outcomes will be doing the real work of explaining why molecules have determinate shapes.

None of this licenses talk of some chemical vital force, and I don't think it should. But it suggests that the cleanest available textbook example of intertheoretic reduction survives only by borrowing, unpaid, from an unrelated and still-open debate in the foundations of physics. If even this best case doesn't fit Ernest Nagel's model of reduction by derivation and bridge laws, I'm inclined to think the trouble lies with the model — not only with its usual problem children, biology and psychology.
