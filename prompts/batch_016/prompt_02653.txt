I'm drafting a novel whose protagonist is told, fourteen months after her husband's death, that she has "Prolonged Grief Disorder" — the diagnosis the APA added to the DSM-5-TR in 2022. I described the plot to two friends over dinner, and they ended up arguing about whether the diagnosis does any real work. I'd like help sorting out who had the better of it at each stage.

(1) Ilana, a clinical psychologist, said the diagnosis earns its keep: it picks out a statistically distinct, impairing pattern that predicts worse outcomes than ordinary grief and responds to a specific treatment, so refusing to name it just leaves people undertreated.

(2) Devon, a sociologist who works on Ian Hacking, invoked the looping effect: once "prolonged grief" exists as an official kind, people narrate, monitor, and time their mourning through it — support groups, insurance codes, self-help guides — so the "natural" pattern Ilana cites may be partly produced by the label rather than discovered independently of it.

(3) Ilana replied that looping happens around uncontroversially real conditions too — diabetics reorganize their habits and identity around a diagnosis without that making diabetes any less real — so looping alone can't discredit a category, and here there's also independent evidence, including distinctive neural responses to reminders of the loss.

(4) Devon answered that the analogy fails: blood-glucose physiology was an independent anchor before anyone classified it, whereas grief's twelve-month cutoff was fixed by committee, partly in response to clinicians' and patients' already-shifting sense of what counts as "normal" mourning — so here the loop has nothing independent left to loop around.

Please work through moves (1)–(4) and say who has the better argument at each step.
