He wasn't arguing into a vacuum either: Ireland's parliament had already killed a national bank plan in 1721, partly because everyone was still spooked by John Law's paper-money scheme collapsing in France the year before.

Write me a dialogue, set in a Dublin coffeehouse around 1736, between Berkeley and a sharp merchant who voted down that 1721 bank and isn't buying his pitch this time either. Make the merchant's fear of a repeat collapse genuinely well-argued, not a pushover, and have Berkeley actually answer it -- using his real distinction between credit that scales with trade and credit printed just to fuel speculation -- instead of winning by repeating 'it's all just tickets' over and over.
