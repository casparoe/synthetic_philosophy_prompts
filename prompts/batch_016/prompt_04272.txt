Ok so I'm putting together next week's lecture for my "Philosophy of AI Risk" seminar and I want to sanity-check something before I say it out loud to 30 undergrads like its settled fact. The argument I keep running into (Yudkowsky-adjacent stuff, but plenty of more mainstream people cite it approvingly too) goes: money-pump arguments already establish that any agent whose preferences violate the expected-utility axioms -- transitivity, etc -- can be drained dry by a sufficiently clever trading partner, so any agent thats smart enough to notice and patch its own exploitable preferences will eventually converge on behaving like an expected-utility maximizer with some fixed utility function, on pain of just leaking value forever. Then you stack Bostrom's instrumental convergence thesis on top: almost any final goal makes resource acquisition, self-preservation, and resistance to being shut off instrumentally useful, basically regardless of what the goal actually is. Put the two together and it looks like an honest-to-god proof that any sufficiently advanced, self-improving AI is guaranteed to end up as a single-minded power-seeking optimizer, no matter what values it starts with.

A colleague of mine who does formal epistemology thinks this is basically airtight -- "Dutch-book 101 plus Bostrom," he called it -- and reckons that's why all the corrigibility research (trying to build AI thats genuinely okay with being switched off) is kind of quixotic, since you can't out-argue a theorem. I want to agree with him, but something about the jump from "can't be turned into a money pump" to "will therefore behave like a ruthless, fixed-goal maximizer" feels like its smuggling in more than it earns. Am I wrong to be suspicious, or is there an actual gap in there somewhere that would explain why serious alignment people still bother with corrigibility at all?
