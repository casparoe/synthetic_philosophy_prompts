I'm currently taking a seminar on Nietzsche's late writings, and this week's session is built around Ecce Homo, specifically the chapter "Why I Am So Clever." Coming into it, I expected the usual territory of the revaluation of values and the critique of Christian morality, but roughly a third of the chapter is instead taken up with what reads, at first glance, like an eccentric memoir of Nietzsche's own dietary habits: tirades against German and English cooking, warnings about beer and coffee, precise instructions on how strong to brew tea, and a whole ranking of which climates are hospitable to genius. My first instinct was to file this under "amusing biographical color," but scattered through the chapter are a few sentences that seem to be doing much more philosophical work than that, and I've been struggling to pin down exactly how much weight Nietzsche wants them to carry. Here is the passage I keep returning to (drawn from §1, §2, and §10, with some material cut for length):

"I am much more interested in another question — a question upon which the 'salvation of humanity' depends to a far greater degree than it does upon any piece of theological curiosity: I refer to nutrition. [...] Remain seated as little as possible, put no trust in any thought that is not born in the open, to the accompaniment of free bodily motion — nor in one in which even the muscles do not celebrate a feast. All prejudices take their origin in the intestines. A sedentary life, as I have already said elsewhere, is the real sin against the Holy Spirit. [...] The tempo of the body's functions is closely bound up with the agility or the clumsiness of the spirit's feet; spirit itself is indeed only a form of these organic functions. [...] these trivial matters — diet, locality, climate, and one's mode of recreation, the whole casuistry of self-love — are inconceivably more important than all that which has hitherto been held in high esteem!"

During our discussion section, my TA mentioned in passing that how one reads lines like these actually bears on a live disagreement about just how "naturalist" Nietzsche is. On one reading, he is committed to something like the view that conscious "spirit," including our philosophical and moral convictions, is fundamentally a symptom or byproduct of physiological type — so that getting one's nutrition and climate right isn't a metaphor for philosophical health, it more or less is philosophical health. On another reading, that can't be the whole story, since so much of the rest of Nietzsche's work — the emphasis on self-overcoming, on "becoming what one is," on higher types actively organizing their own drives — seems to require that spirit have some real efficacy over the body, rather than merely riding on top of it as an inert byproduct.

I'd like help thinking through what the passage itself actually commits Nietzsche to, rather than a general summary of his philosophy. Specifically: how strong is the claim that "spirit itself is... only a form of these organic functions" — is it identity, reduction, mere correlation, or something looser — and is that in tension with the fact that the whole chapter is framed as a set of deliberate, almost willed choices (what to eat, where to live, how to recover) that a self is supposed to make on its own behalf? If spirit really were just downstream of physiology in the strong sense, why does Nietzsche present all of this as a discipline to be cultivated rather than a fate to be discovered? And how, if at all, does this connect to his broader complaint against idealism and the philosophical habit of despising the body?
