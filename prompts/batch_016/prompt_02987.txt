Ok, so I fell down a rabbit hole this week while researching a piece on how courts and expert panels actually reach collective verdicts (my editor wants a sidebar on it, which is how I always end up reading way more social choice theory than any sane person should on a Tuesday). The hook is this old chestnut from legal theory, originally Kornhauser and Sager's example I think: a panel of judges votes seperately on two sub-questions — say "was there a valid contract" and "was it breached" — and also on the overall verdict. The weird bit is that a majority can back "yes" on each sub-question, while a majority simultaneously backs "not liable" once you just tally up each judge's individual verdict instead. I gather List and Pettit generalized this "doctrinal paradox" into a whole field called judgment aggregation, complete with its own impossibility theorem.

Every explainer I've read describes that impossibility theorem as basically "Arrow's theorem, but for judgments instead of preferences," which always felt like it was dodging the actual question. Then I found an older paper by Dietrich and List that seems to argue almost the opposite: they build a way of treating ordinary preference rankings as a special case of logically-connected judgments, and then get Arrow's impossibility theorem to fall out as a corollary of the judgment-aggregation result, rather than the other way around. That's a much stronger claim than "these are cousins," and it's exactly the kind of detail that gets flattened in every pop write-up, including some of my own past ones, guiltily.

So here's what I actually want from you: a proper comparison of judgment aggregation (and the discursive dilemma specifically) with classic Arrovian preference aggregation. I care most about (1) what's structurally different about aggregating logically interconnected propositions versus aggregating rankings over options, (2) whether "independence" is doing the same conceptual job in both settings or just wearing the same name, and (3) given the Dietrich-List embedding, whether it's fair to call judgment aggregation the more fundamental result with Arrow as a special case, or whether that's overselling it. Please give me an actual verdict on that last point, not just "it's complicated" — I need something I can responsibly put in front of readers who might know Arrow's theorem and nothing else.
