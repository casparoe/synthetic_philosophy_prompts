A friend of mine—who is finishing a PhD in philosophy of physics—and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about scientific realism, and neither of us left satisfied with where the conversation landed. She is drawn to structural realism, motivated by cases like the shift from Fresnel's elastic-solid ether theory of light to Maxwell's electromagnetic theory: the specific mechanical story about the ether was abandoned, yet the formal relations encoded in Fresnel's equations carried over, in some sense, into the successor theory. The thought is that realist commitment should track mathematical structure itself, rather than whatever story happens to be told about the objects that structure is supposedly a structure "of." I pushed back with an objection I half-remembered from a seminar years ago: if "knowing the structure" ultimately cashes out as the claim that there exists some relation on the domain satisfying such-and-such formal conditions—what I gather gets formalized via the theory's Ramsey sentence—then, for a domain of the right cardinality, that claim is almost guaranteed to be satisfiable no matter what the world is actually like, so the structuralist doesn't seem committed to anything beyond what a committed instrumentalist would already grant. My friend conceded the difficulty (she called it Newman's objection) but insisted that ontic structural realists escape it by refusing to treat relations extensionally, as mere sets of tuples, and invoking instead some thicker, modally loaded notion of structure tied to natural necessity. Neither of us could actually explain why that move helps rather than just relocating the same problem one level up.

Could you reconstruct Newman's objection precisely enough that it is clear exactly why "some structure of the right form exists" collapses toward a near-trivial claim about cardinality, rather than just asserting that it does? And could you then evaluate the ontic structural realist response on its merits: does rejecting an extensional conception of relations in favor of a modal one actually block the trivialization, or does the same worry simply resurface at the level of which modal structures are the "real" ones? I would like to come away with an actual verdict on who had the better of the argument, rather than a neutral summary of both positions.
