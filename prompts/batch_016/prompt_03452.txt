so i've been slowly working through Pascal's Pensées on my own time (just some old public domain translation, no notes or commentary, so i'm probably missing context here) and i got stuck on two bits that are only a few fragments apart but seem to pull in totally opposite directions.

First he's going off about how justice is basically just an accident of where you happen to be born:

"Three degrees of latitude reverse all jurisprudence; a meridian decides the truth... A strange justice that is bounded by a river! Truth on this side of the Pyrenees, error on the other side."

He sounds genuinely disgusted here, like the whole point is that nobody actually has real justice, just local custom wearing a justice costume. But then four fragments later (its literally titled "Justice, might" in my copy) he writes this:

"It is right that what is just should be obeyed; it is necessary that what is strongest should be obeyed. Justice without might is helpless; might without justice is tyrannical... We must then combine justice and might, and for this end make what is just strong, or what is strong just. Justice is subject to dispute; might is easily recognised and is not disputed. So we cannot give might to justice, because might has gainsaid justice, and has declared that it is she herself who is just. And thus being unable to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just."

That second one sounds to me like hes just calmly describing (maybe even accepting?) the exact same swap he was trashing a page earlier, people not having real justice so they just call whatever wins "justice" instead. So is he contradicting himself between these two bits, or is there some difference in what hes actually doing in each passage that im not picking up on? Like is fragment 298 secretly still a critique somehow, or does he genuinely think thats just how it has to work and we shouldn't expect better?
