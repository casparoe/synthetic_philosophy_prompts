Ok, this is going to sound like a weird combo of topics but bear with me. I've been doing governance work for a DAO for about a year now (basically a crypto collective run by smart contracts instead of normal bylaws), and we just went through a massive internal fight over whether to add an emergency multisig override to our treasury contract or keep it fully immutable, the way it was originally deployed. The "code is law" faction kept invoking the 2016 DAO hack and the whole Ethereum/Ethereum Classic split as their origin myth: once the code is live and the community has voted, nobody gets to step in and reverse the outcome later, even to undo an exploit, because the entire point of the system is that the rules bind future-us just as much as anyone else.

That fight sent me down a rabbit hole into precommitment theory. A friend doing a poli-sci PhD told me the actual term of art for this is a "Ulysses contract" (like tying yourself to the mast), and pointed me to a Jon Elster book that treats constitutions themselves as this kind of self-binding device. Which is how I ended up reading Jefferson's 1789 letter to Madison, where he literally uses a French naturalists mortality tables to calculate that a "generation" lasts about 19 years, and argues that no generation, and therefore no law or constitution, has any right to bind the next one past that point - "the earth belongs to the living," basically, full stop. Madison wrote back pushing back pretty hard on this, something about stability and continuity being valuable in it's own right, but Jefferson never really seemed to concede the point.

So here's my actual question: if Jefferson somehow saw a modern DAO, where the "constitution" is a piece of code that quite literally cannot be revised except by convincing the entire network to hard-fork, what do you think he'd say about it? Is that just a more extreme, more literal version of the dead-hand problem he was already worried about, only worse because there's no legislature or court around to ignore or reinterpret it once it produces something unjust? Or does the fact that people opt into a DAO voluntarily, by buying tokens, rather than being born into it, change his objection in some real way? I'd genuinely like to know which of his specific arguments you think actually carries over to a system like this, and which ones don't.
