A close friend of mine, who is finishing a residency in critical care, and I spent most of a dinner last week arguing about whether "brain death" is really death, or just a useful fiction we've agreed to maintain because organ transplantation needs a bright line. She kept coming back to the Jahi McMath case — the Oakland teenager declared brain dead in 2013 after a tonsillectomy, whose family moved her to New Jersey (the one state with a religious exemption) and kept her on a ventilator for another four and a half years, during which she went through puberty — as proof that whole-brain criteria don't track anything metaphysically serious about when an organism dies. I countered that hard cases make bad theory and that isolated anecdotes shouldn't outweigh the medical consensus, but by the time we got to dessert I'd more or less conceded that her challenge was sharper than I wanted to admit, and I've been turning it over since.

What I've since drafted, in note form, as my actual view is roughly this: I think the search for a purely biological rationale for whole-brain death is a lost cause. The original 1981 rationale (the brain confers "integrative unity" on the body) doesn't survive contact with Alan Shewmon's case series on chronic survival after whole-brain destruction, and the President's Council on Bioethics' 2008 fallback — redefining life around the "fundamental work" of spontaneous breathing rather than integration — strikes me as a rearguard action that quietly gives up the game. But I don't want to follow Truog and Miller all the way to jettisoning the dead-donor rule and grounding organ procurement in bare consent, because that seems to dodge the metaphysical question rather than answer it. My tentative landing spot is that whole-brain death should be openly acknowledged as a higher-brain, consciousness-based criterion in disguise — closer to Veatch and McMahan's view that what has ended is the person, not the organism — with the law and clinical practice adjusted to say so honestly.

Before I try to turn this into something more presentable, I'd like your comments. In particular: does the position I've landed on actually hang together, or have I just backed into the higher-brain view while dressing it up as a compromise? Higher-brain criteria are usually thought to founder on persistent vegetative state and anencephaly, where almost nobody is willing to say the patient is already dead even though the "capacity for consciousness" test is failed just as thoroughly as it is in whole-brain death — am I under-selling how serious that problem is for my view? And is there anything in the more recent, stalled attempt by the Uniform Law Commission to revise the Uniform Determination of Death Act that should be pushing me toward a different conclusion? I'd rather have the weak points found now than discover them after I've shown this to my friend.
