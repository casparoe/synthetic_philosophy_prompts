I've been working my way through John Maynard Keynes on my own time (I have no formal background in economics or philosophy, just a lot of curiosity), and I got stuck on a specific part of his short 1930 essay "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren." Most of it is easy enough to follow: he predicts that within a hundred years, compound growth will have basically solved the "economic problem," and that the real difficulty after that will be psychological rather than material — learning how to actually enjoy abundance and leisure instead of just working. But partway through he introduces this idea of "purposiveness," which he defines, roughly, as caring more about the remote future consequences of what we do than about the doing of it or its immediate effects, and he illustrates it with a strange little image, writing that a person like this "does not love his cat, but his cat's kittens," and not even them, really, but their kittens, and so on forever. He seems to think most of us are like this to some degree, and that it's a serious obstacle to actually living well once scarcity stops forcing our hand.

What I can't quite work out is how this idea fits into the rest of his thinking about the good life. I came across, in some further reading about his life, that as a young man Keynes belonged to the Bloomsbury group and was influenced by the philosopher G. E. Moore, who apparently believed that the best things in life were things like love and the contemplation of beauty, valued for their own sake in the moment rather than as steps toward something further off. If that really was Keynes's own standard for a good life, it would make sense that "purposiveness" — this habit of always pushing enjoyment further into the future — is exactly the psychological obstacle he thinks stands between people and that good life, even once money is no longer scarce. But I don't know if I'm overreading the connection, or whether that's actually a fair way to read the essay. Could you help me work through what job the idea of purposiveness is doing in Keynes's argument, how solid the link to his earlier, Moore-influenced views on what makes life worth living really is, and whether, in your view, his diagnosis of why abundance alone wouldn't make people happy still seems right?
