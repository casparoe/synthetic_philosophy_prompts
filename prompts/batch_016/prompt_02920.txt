I've spent years reading rationalist blogs and forums, and one habit of mind that's stuck with me from that world is the "dissolve the question" move: the idea that many disputes that look deep are really just two people using a word differently, and that the fix is to taboo the term and see whether any disagreement survives the ban. A few of us who met through a LessWrong-adjacent Discord eventually started a small reading group working through the more technical philosophical literature on the same territory — Hirsch, Sider, and Chalmers on "verbal disputes" and metaontology — partly to see whether the blog-post version of the idea holds up against the professional one. Last week two members, Callum and Sasha, got into an extended argument that I think illustrates the subtleties better than any of the readings did, and I'd like help sorting out who was right about what.

The trigger was mundane: Callum mentioned over dinner that he'd "had the same commuter bike for eight years," then admitted, when pressed, that he'd replaced the frame after a crash, the wheels twice, and the entire drivetrain at various points since buying it, so that, depending how you count, none of the original parts remain. (1) Sasha said this is a textbook case of what Hirsch calls a merely verbal dispute: whether it's "the same bike" depends on whether "same" gets read in a legal/continuity-of-ownership sense or a same-matter sense, and once both readings are made explicit, Callum's claim comes out true on one and false on the other, so there's no remaining question of who's right, only of which sense of "same" is in play. (2) Callum objected that this can't be the whole story, because Hirsch's actual test isn't "can each reading be made to come out true" — that's cheap, and would make every dispute verbal — it requires that both readings be compatible with a single, antecedently existing concept of identity-over-time that ordinary speakers already share, and it's a further, substantive question whether ordinary usage really contains such a concept here rather than favoring one reading over the other. (3) Sasha replied that this objection backfires: the reason gradual-replacement cases are a standing philosophical puzzle at all is that ordinary usage is vague or simply silent about them, so there is no shared determinate concept for either reading to be getting right or wrong — the disagreement is just two legitimate ways of sharpening an antecedently vague term, which is exactly what a verbal dispute looks like. (4) Callum's comeback was that showing the folk concept is vague only shows that ordinary language doesn't settle the question; it doesn't show there's no fact of the matter at a deeper level, and he invoked something he attributed to Sider — the idea that reality itself might have a "joint" here, so that one way of sharpening the concept could be a strictly better description of what's actually going on, independent of anyone's willingness to stipulate.

Neither of them budged, and I honestly can't tell how the exchange should be scored. Could you go through moves (1) through (4) individually and say who has the better of it at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner, and flag anything you think both of them are taking for granted that a more careful metaontologist would push back on? I'd also be curious whether the rationalist "taboo your words" heuristic is really doing the same work here as the Hirsch/Sider apparatus it's being tested against, or whether it's a cruder tool that just happens to get the easy cases right.
