I spent nearly forty years as an experimental physicist before retiring a few years back, and it's really only in the last year or two that I've sat down and read philosophy for its own sake -- feels like starting a second career backwards, but here we are. Lately I've been on a bit of a Kurt Godel kick. I already knew the famous stuff: incompleteness, the ontological proof of God's existence that he was oddly cautious about ever publishing, and the fact that he and Einstein used to walk home together from the Institute most days, with Einstein reportedly saying that was basically the main reason he still bothered coming in to work. But I recently came across a piece about a set of letters Godel wrote to his elderly mother Marianne in 1961, after she'd apparently asked him point blank whether he thought they'd see each other again after death. His answer amounts to this careful, almost logicians proof: the world is demonstrably rational and orderly (he points to the regularity science keeps turning up as evidence of this), and a rational, meaningful world that produces this much unresolved human striving and suffering without any further chapter would kind of undercut its own rationality -- so there has to be some further state where things get resolved.

What's been rattling around in my head is how that sits next to his other, better known philosophical view: that because of incompleteness, either the human mind isn't equivalent to any Turing machine, or there exist mathematical propositions that are absolutely undecidable -- and that Godel himself apparently leaned hard toward the "mind isn't a machine" horn of that disjunction, on top of being a pretty committed mathematical Platonist. So here's my actual question, and I'd like you to really speculate rather then just hand me back the historical facts I just gave you: if Godel somehow got to see whole-brain emulation and "mind uploading" explained to him today -- the idea that you scan a brain and run the resulting computational model on a machine so the person survives their body's death -- do you think he'd take that as a mechanized, science-flavoured route to the same Wiedersehen he promised his mother? Or would his own anti-mechanist and Platonist commitments make him think an uploaded copy obviously isn't a continuation of the person in whatever sense he actually meant? I put this to an old colleague from my lab and we just went around in circles, so I'd genuinely like a proper reasoned take instead of a "we can't ever know" shrug.
