I'm a backend engineer, and for the last couple of months I've been working through E. T. Jaynes' *Probability Theory: The Logic of Science* on evenings and weekends, mainly to get a better sense of where the priors in some of the Bayesian models at work actually come from. The chapter on ignorance priors and transformation groups covers Bertrand's chord paradox: inscribe an equilateral triangle in a circle, pick a chord "at random," and ask for the probability that it's longer than a side of the triangle. Depending on whether you generate the chord from two random endpoints on the circumference, a random point along a fixed radius, or a random midpoint inside the disk, you get 1/3, 1/2, or 1/4 respectively, and each derivation looks locally impeccable. Jaynes' resolution is that the problem statement never fixes the circle's size or position, so any legitimate answer has to come out the same under rescaling and translation; when he turns that requirement into equations for the distribution directly, rather than just using it to screen the three candidate answers after the fact, only the "random point on a radius" method survives. He presents this as a genuine rescue of the principle of indifference: "a chord drawn at random" was never as ambiguous as Bertrand's three answers made it look, once you're careful about which symmetries the problem actually has.

I mentioned this to a friend from college who went on to do a philosophy MA, and she was unconvinced in a way I haven't been able to answer on my own. Her point was that turning "invariant under translation and rescaling" into an actual distribution still requires a physical story about how the chord gets generated. Jaynes' own procedure is straws thrown down from far away and extended into chords, which is translation- and scale-invariant and yields 1/2 — but a dart thrown at the disk, with the chord defined by taking the landing point as its midpoint, is just as clearly translation-, rotation-, and scale-invariant, and it reproduces the 1/4 answer instead. So two generating procedures can each fully satisfy Jaynes' invariance requirements and still disagree, which suggests that "impose the symmetries of the problem" was never the procedure-free instruction it was advertised as: it already presupposed a choice of generating procedure (straws, not darts), and that choice looks exactly as unforced by the bare problem statement as the original choice between Bertrand's three parameterizations.

I'd like you to make my friend's case as rigorously as you can. Specifically, please argue that Jaynes' transformation-group solution does not actually rescue the principle of indifference from Bertrand's paradox, but merely relocates the arbitrary choice from "which partition of outcomes is equally likely" to "which random-generating procedure is canonical" — a choice no more forced by the problem statement than the first one was. Please engage with the strongest version of Jaynes' reply (e.g., that the straw-throwing setup, not the dart, is the one actually picked out by ordinary language about a chord "laid down at random" on a fixed circle, so it isn't an arbitrary add-on at all) rather than a strawman, and walk through enough of the actual geometry that someone could see exactly where the extra, unforced choice enters the derivation. End with your own honest sense of how damaging this is to Jaynes' broader project of grounding "ignorance" priors in invariance: a fatal flaw, or a fixable complication?
