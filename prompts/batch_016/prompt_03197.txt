So I fell down a bit of a rabbit hole this week and could use a hand climbing back out. I'm working on a piece about all the internet arguing over whether public figures "get credit" for charity when it's transparently done for PR, and a philosopher I interviewed pointed me toward this whole sub-literature on "moral worth" that's way more granular than the Kant 101 version I half-remember from undergrad (the shopkeeper who's only honest because honesty's good for business, etc). Apparently there's a real fight among people who are all broadly sympathetic to Kant about whether you actually have to be thinking "this is my duty" while you act. Barbara Herman's move, if I'm getting this right, is that the duty-motive doesn't have to be doing the driving in the moment — it can sit in the background as a kind of "limiting condition" that would kick in and stop you if your other motives ever pointed somewhere wrong, so the guy who saves his drowning wife purely out of love still gets full moral credit. Then there's a newer view, I think from Julia Markovits, called the "coincident reasons thesis," which just says forget the duty machinery altogether — all that matters is whether the reasons that actually moved you happen to be the same reasons that make the act right.

Both camps seem to lob the same two objections at everybody else — Bernard Williams's "one thought too many" bit about the drowning wife, and Michael Smith's "moral fetishism" charge — and honestly I cant tell if Herman and Markovits have basically converged on the same idea from two directions, or if theres a real substantive daylight between them that would actually show up differently in some case. Could you write me a dialogue between a Herman-style defender of "acting from duty as a limiting condition" and a Markovits-style defender of "coincident reasons," where they actually engage each other's arguments rather than just restating their own? I'd love it if somewhere in there they landed on (or at least argued over) a concrete test case — maybe something involving a love or loyalty so unconditional that it would carry the person into doing something impermissible in a slightly different version of the same case — that would make the two views actually cash out differently on whether the original act had moral worth. And please give both sides a fair fight; I don't want either one to be the obvious loser.
