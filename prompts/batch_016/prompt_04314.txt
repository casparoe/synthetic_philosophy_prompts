I'm a PhD student in philosophy working mainly on decision theory, but a conversation with two friends outside the department has been nagging at me for weeks, and I'd value an outside perspective. My friend Priya is an ethical vegetarian: she thinks eating factory-farmed meat is a serious, ongoing wrong, full stop, but she buys her clothes from whichever fast-fashion retailer is cheapest and has never thought twice about it. My other friend Sam is the mirror image: he has spent the last few years volunteering with a garment-workers' rights group and considers buying from exploitative fast-fashion supply chains a genuine moral failure, but he eats meat at every meal without a flicker of guilt.

At dinner, half as a joke at first, they talked their way into an actual arrangement: Sam would go vegetarian for a year if Priya switched entirely to secondhand or certified-ethical clothing for the same period. The important thing is that neither of them was persuaded by the other's reasoning — Sam still thinks there's nothing wrong with eating meat, and Priya still sees nothing wrong with her shopping habits. Each of them would simply be changing a behavior that only the other person objects to, in exchange for the other person doing the same.

I can't decide what to make of this. Part of me thinks it's a neat way for two people who will never agree to nonetheless each end up acting better by their own lights than they currently do — surely that counts as progress, maybe even something to be encouraged more generally? But another part of me suspects something is off: it feels a little like each of them is being paid to behave, and I'm not sure whatever is good about going vegetarian or giving up fast fashion survives being done as a bargaining chip rather than out of conviction. I also don't know how Priya and Sam could even settle on fair terms — why a year-for-a-year swap rather than two years of one for six months of the other, say? Is there a principled way to think about whether arrangements like this are something to welcome rather than be suspicious of, and if so, how two people with no shared moral yardstick could work out what would actually make the deal a fair one?
