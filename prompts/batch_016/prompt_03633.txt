For a seminar I'm taking on nineteenth-century American political and constitutional thought, we spent this week's session on the rupture between William Lloyd Garrison and Frederick Douglass over how to read the U.S. Constitution — a dispute that, I was surprised to learn, turned on genuine interpretive disagreement rather than merely on how strongly each man opposed slavery (which neither doubted). Garrison held that the Constitution was, as he famously put it, "a covenant with death, and an agreement with hell," pointing to the three-fifths clause, the fugitive-slave clause, and Article I's twenty-year shield for the slave trade as proof that the document was irredeemably proslavery, so that voting or holding office under it only implicated the abolitionist further in the crime it protected. Douglass, who had defended exactly that Garrisonian position for roughly a decade, stood up at the 1851 American Anti-Slavery Society meeting in Syracuse and announced that he had changed his mind: having studied Lysander Spooner and Gerrit Smith in the years leading up to that meeting, he now argued that since the document never once uses the words "slave" or "slavery," its ambiguous clauses ought to be construed, as with any legal text, in light of the purposes it announces for itself in the Preamble, rather than in light of what everyone privately understood the Philadelphia delegates to have been willing to tolerate.

I'd like to work through the strongest form of both sides of this argument, so could you write me a dialogue between the two men set immediately after that Syracuse meeting, with Garrison — feeling blindsided by his former protégé's public defection — following Douglass out to demand an explanation before word spreads any further? I'd like a real back-and-forth of somewhere around ten to twelve exchanges, in which each man is made to answer the other's actual best point rather than simply restate his own position: Garrison should press Douglass on what licenses reading a founding legal text against the known intentions and historical compromises of the men who wrote it, and on what is practically gained by pretending the document is other than what it has always been enforced as; Douglass should press Garrison on why an odious private intention, nowhere stated in the text, should be allowed to override the actual words the framers chose — and conspicuously avoided — putting to paper, and on what follows practically from declaring the country's founding law permanently beyond redemption. Please don't let either man win too easily; both positions had serious, sophisticated adherents within the abolitionist movement itself, and I'd like the dialogue to make that clear.
