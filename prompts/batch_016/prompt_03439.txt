A close friend of mine, who's a fairly serious Christian, and I got into a long argument last week over dinner about the doctrine of penal substitutionary atonement: the idea that Christ's suffering discharged the punishment that humanity's sins had otherwise earned. I raised the standard objection that punishing an innocent party in someone else's place seems unjust no matter how willing that party is. My friend countered with a short essay by David Lewis called "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?", arguing that Lewis actually exposes the rest of us as hypocrites: we clearly think it's fine for a friend to pay someone else's fine, a fine is just as much a punishment as a prison sentence, so anyone who tolerates that but balks at the Atonement doesn't have a principled objection at all. I pushed back that Lewis explicitly raises and rejects that very move -- something about fines being different from mere "fees," and about why the fact that we can't practically stop people from paying each other's fines doesn't actually let us off the hook -- but neither of us could reconstruct the argument precisely enough to settle who was right, and the conversation eventually moved on without a resolution.

Could you reconstruct Lewis's actual argument for me in more detail than either of us managed at the table? I'd specifically like to understand: (1) why he thinks the everyday metaphor of a "debt of punishment" is disanalogous to a literal debt in a way that should make substitution look senseless to begin with; (2) why he doesn't accept either of the two obvious ways of explaining away the fines case -- that serious fines aren't "really" punishment, or that we merely tolerate substitution there because preventing it would be impractical; and (3) what he actually concludes follows from all this, since my recollection is that his final position is considerably more ambivalent than either "the objection to penal substitution is refuted" or "penal substitution is simply incoherent." I'd rather work from the real shape of his argument than from either of our half-remembered versions of it.
