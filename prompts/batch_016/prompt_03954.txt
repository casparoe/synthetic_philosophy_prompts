For my school's Ethics Bowl team, I've been working through Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics* (Book II, Chapter 4, in the W. D. Ross translation) as background reading on virtue ethics, and I keep getting stuck on this passage:

"Again, the case of the arts and that of the virtues are not similar; for the products of the arts have their goodness in themselves, so that it is enough that they should have a certain character, but if the acts that are in accordance with the virtues have themselves a certain character it does not follow that they are done justly or temperately. The agent also must be in a certain condition when he does them; in the first place he must have knowledge, secondly he must choose the acts, and choose them for their own sakes, and thirdly his action must proceed from a firm and unchangeable character. These are not reckoned in as conditions of the possession of the arts, except the bare knowledge; but as a condition of the possession of the virtues knowledge has little or no weight, while the other conditions count not for a little but for everything, i.e. the very conditions which result from often doing just and temperate acts."

I'm having trouble with two aspects of this in particular. First, Aristotle seems to say that the "firm and unchangeable character" required for virtuous action is itself produced only "from often doing just and temperate acts" — doesn't that make the account circular, since the character needed to act virtuously would have to already be in place before it could be built? Second, we just covered the situationist critique in AP Psychology (the obedience and bystander-intervention studies), and "firm and unchangeable character" sounds like precisely the kind of cross-situational consistency those experiments are used to challenge. Could you help me work out what Aristotle is actually committed to in this passage, and whether there's a reading of "firm and unchangeable" that wouldn't be undermined by that kind of evidence?
