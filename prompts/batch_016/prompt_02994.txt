I've been working through Aristotle's Physics on my own time – I have no formal training in philosophy – and I've gotten stuck on a passage in Book II where he raises an objection to the idea that nature does anything "for the sake of" something, before apparently going on to answer it. Here is the passage, in the old Hardie and Gaye translation:

"A difficulty presents itself: why should not nature work, not for the sake of something, nor because it is better so, but just as the sky rains, not in order to make the corn grow, but of necessity? What is drawn up must cool, and what has been cooled must become water and descend, the result of this being that the corn grows. Similarly if a man's crop is spoiled on the threshing-floor, the rain did not fall for the sake of this—in order that the crop might be spoiled—but that result just followed. Why then should it not be the same with the parts in nature, e.g. that our teeth should come up of necessity—the front teeth sharp, fitted for tearing, the molars broad and useful for grinding down the food—since they did not arise for this end, but it was merely a coincident result; and so with all other parts in which we suppose that there is purpose? Wherever then all the parts came about just what they would have been if they had come to be for an end, such things survived, being organized spontaneously in a fitting way; whereas those which grew otherwise perished and continue to perish, as Empedocles says his 'man-faced ox-progeny' did."

Taken by itself, this sounds like Aristotle is proposing something close to natural selection, and I've seen it quoted online as evidence that he "almost" beat Darwin to the idea. But I get the impression that he is actually setting this position up so that he can reject it. Could you help me work out, from the structure of his argument, whether he means this as his own view or as an opponent's view he is about to knock down, and what exactly his grounds are either way? I would also like to know how his eventual answer relates to a modern dispute I've come across: whether the "function" of a body part should be defined by what it was historically selected to do, or simply by whatever causal contribution it currently makes within the organism, independent of its history. Does Aristotle's resolution of the teeth-and-rain puzzle line up with either of those two modern positions, or is it really doing something else entirely?
