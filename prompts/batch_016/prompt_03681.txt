I have no formal training in philosophy or linguistics, but I've been turning something over in my head for a while. Put a slur inside a conditional or under negation, and its ugliness does not switch off the way ordinary content does when embedded like that — it comes through regardless of the sentence's logical shape. That suggests the derogatory force is not really part of what the sentence asserts, but a separate layer riding along with the word no matter the context. Yet members of the targeted group can sometimes use that same word on one another without it landing as an insult at all, which points the other way: toward the force depending on who is speaking, rather than being a fixed property the word drags along wherever it goes.

I would like to see these two intuitions fought out properly, in real back-and-forth rather than two monologues. Could you write a dialogue between two philosophers of language on opposite sides of this question: one who holds that a slur's derogatory content is literally part of its truth-conditional meaning, and one who holds that it is a separate, non-asserted layer attached to a neutral descriptive core. Have each of them account for both the embedding facts and the in-group cases from inside their own framework, press on whatever point in the other's account strains hardest, and see whether they end up disagreeing about the actual facts of meaning or just about where to draw the line between semantics and pragmatics.
