I work as a backend software engineer at a startup that's been bolting LLM agents onto our product, and a coworker on our "trust and safety" side recently sent around Alignment Research Center's 2021 report on Eliciting Latent Knowledge (ELK) after a discussion about whether a model could ever learn to tell us what it actually "believes" rather than just what we want to hear. I don't have any formal background in philosophy or ML theory, but the report hooked me because of its format: every proposed fix gets a concrete counterexample, and every counterexample gets a new attempted fix, back and forth, dozens of rounds. After spending a couple of weekends going through it and the follow-up prize-competition writeup, I've ended up arguing with that same coworker over lunch about which of these fixes actually represent progress versus which just relocate the problem somewhere less visible. I'd like a more considered outside opinion to settle the argument (or at least sharpen it).

For context, the setup in all these strategies is the same: you're training a "reporter" attached to a predictive model to answer questions honestly, and the worry is that instead of learning to be a direct translator (reporting what the predictor's own internal model implies), it learns to be a human simulator (reporting whatever a human watching the sensors would naively guess), since the two are indistinguishable on every case you can check by hand. Here are seven of the proposed training strategies meant to pull those two apart:

1. Complexity penalty: favor the reporter with the shortest description length, on the assumption that direct translation is simpler than reconstructing a whole human belief process.
2. Compute penalty: favor the reporter that needs the least extra computation beyond the predictor's own forward pass, on the assumption that simulating a human requires running a second model.
3. Downstream-node penalty: penalize reporters whose answers depend only on the latest, most human-visible variables in the predictor's model, rather than on earlier, harder-to-observe state.
4. Cross-question consistency: penalize reporters that answer inconsistently on pairs of inputs that are nearly identical, on the assumption that a simulator will only slip up exactly when tampering is too subtle for a human to notice.
5. Causal-intervention probing: require the reporter to answer via hypothetical edits to the predictor's internal state and a rerun of its computation, on the assumption that only a genuine translator tracks what such edits actually do downstream.
6. Human-inference subroutine: give the reporter access to a separately trained "what would a human conclude" module it can call and reuse, on the assumption that a direct translator can exploit that shortcut for a computational discount while a simulator gains nothing extra from it.
7. Auxiliary-usefulness training: reward the reporter for producing answers that help a separate downstream model succeed at some unrelated task, on the assumption that a simulator's answers are less useful for tasks that don't just track human belief.

Taking into account the known failure modes for each of these (and any further ones you can think of yourself), how would you tier these seven strategies by how much genuine progress they represent toward a worst-case solution to ELK, as opposed to being fixes that just get taken apart in the next round by a sufficiently determined critic?
