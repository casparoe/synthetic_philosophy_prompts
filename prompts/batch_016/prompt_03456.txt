Quick one for you — I've been down a Hart-Devlin rabbit hole for a piece on internet speech rules and it's kind of messing with my head. Devlins whole case for legislating "private" immorality rested on that disintegration thesis of his: shared morality as the glue holding society together, so much so that he flat-out compares tolerating enough moral deviance to treason. His test for when the law should actually step in was basically vibes-based — he said outright that "no society can do without intolerance, indignation, and disgust," and that if ordinary people feel real disgust about something, that's evidence the bounds of tolerance are being reached. Hart's comeback was basically "where's your evidence that toleration actually causes disintegration," plus the point that moral consensus shifts all the time without society falling apart.

So here's what I can't stop chewing on: the UK's Online Safety Act breifly had a "legal but harmful" category — stuff nobody could be prosecuted for, but platforms were still supposed to suppress it because it was deemed corrosive to society — before it got dropped in 2022 after critics said it was basically the government legislating against being offended. If you plopped Devlin and Hart down in front of that whole saga, what do you think each would actually say, using their real arguments rather than just "conservative bad, liberal good"? Like, does Devlin's disgust-test even function on something as diffuse and mediated as online content, and does Hart's "no evidence of disintegration" line still hold up now that we've had decades of societies absorbing huge moral shifts without collapsing?
