I am a philosophy PhD student working on the metaphysics of causation, and I would like your help thinking through a tension in the causation-by-omission literature that I have not seen satisfactorily resolved. Counterfactual/dependence theories seem committed to holding that a lifeguard's failure to jump in caused a swimmer's death, yet the very same counterfactual test seems to make anyone else who could have intervened -- a distracted bystander, a stranger walking by -- equally a cause, since the drowning would equally have been prevented had they acted. Physical-process theories such as Dowe's avoid this over-generation, but only by relegating omissions to mere 'quasi-causation,' which sits awkwardly with the way both ordinary judgment and the common-law tradition analyzed by Hart and Honoré treat certain failures to act as genuine causes. Please explain why this dilemma resists easy resolution, and assess how promising you find the main proposed escapes -- for instance, building normative or statistical defaults into the causal relation itself, restricting causation to a contextually selected contrast class, or simply decoupling the metaphysics of causation from questions of moral and legal responsibility.
