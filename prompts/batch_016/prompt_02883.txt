My publisher wants me to add a short content warning before the climactic chapter of my new novel, which depicts a sustained, graphic act of violence. Their reasoning is straightforward: out of the several hundred thousand people who will eventually read the book, maybe a handful carry a trauma history serious enough that encountering the scene unprepared could be genuinely damaging, while the warning itself costs almost nothing—a moment's interruption and a slight dulling of the scene's impact for everyone else. Framed that way, the decision looks obvious, but something about the framing itself unsettles me. It seems wrong to think that enough trivial, momentary costs, spread across enough readers, could ever add up to something worse than serious, lasting harm to one or two people—yet it also seems wrong to think that the severe harm must simply always win, no matter how the numbers stack up. Assume the psychological facts are exactly as I've described, so we aren't debating whether content warnings actually work. Is there a principled way to weigh a very large number of small costs against a very small number of severe ones, or am I wrong to think they belong on the same scale at all?
