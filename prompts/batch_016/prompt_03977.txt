I'm a philosophy PhD student writing a dissertation chapter that sets Leibniz's natural law theory against Pufendorf's. I've gotten stuck on what looks like a genuine tension internal to Leibniz's mature definition of justice as "caritas sapientis," the charity of the wise. In the "Meditation on the Common Concept of Justice," Leibniz opens by posing a version of the Euthyphro question -- whether something is just because a supreme will decrees it, or whether even a supreme will has to answer to an independent standard of justice -- and comes down firmly on the rationalist side, treating justice as an eternal truth on a par with a theorem in geometry, something even God's will must conform to rather than create. That much is a fairly standard anti-voluntarist move against Pufendorf and Hobbes. But elsewhere, in the "Codex Iuris Gentium" and the short piece "Felicity," Leibniz grounds all three of his "degrees of right" (strict right, equity, and piety) in the agent's own happiness, and defines wisdom itself as nothing but the science of one's own lasting pleasure.

What I can't work out, even after going through the relevant sections of Patrick Riley's "Leibniz's Universal Jurisprudence," is how "charity" -- glossed as taking disinterested pleasure in the happiness and perfection of other minds, for its own sake -- is supposed to survive being derived from a wisdom that is, by Leibniz's own definition, oriented toward the agent's own felicity. Leibniz explicitly rejects Hobbes's picture on which justice is simply whatever an absolute power decrees to be good for the whole; but if a rational agent's own pleasure in perceiving cosmic harmony is ultimately what explains why she is charitable at all, couldn't a sufficiently patient and clear-sighted egoist accept every clause of Leibniz's doctrine and just redescribe "universal benevolence" as a far-sighted strategy for maximizing that same pleasure? My advisor suspects the tension dissolves once the theodicy -- the claim that this is the best of all possible worlds -- is properly factored in, but I haven't been able to reconstruct why that would follow. I'd appreciate a close, patient walk-through of what, if anything, in Leibniz's metaphysics of perfection and harmony is actually supposed to block the egoistic redescription, rather than Leibniz simply asserting by fiat that rational self-love and disinterested love of others converge once wisdom is complete.
