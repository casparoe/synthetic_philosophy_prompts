I spent my career as a condensed-matter physicist, and, oddly enough, my first encounter with Douglas Hofstadter had nothing to do with philosophy: as a graduate student I worked on lattice electrons in a magnetic field, and so cut my teeth on the fractal spectrum now known as "Hofstadter's butterfly." It was only in retirement, working back through his old Scientific American columns collected in Metamagical Themas, that I properly read his 1983 piece introducing "superrationality" — the idea, developed through his Platonia dilemma and the actual Luring Lottery he ran with his readers, that agents who can be certain they reason in exactly the same way ought to converge on the cooperative, symmetric solution to games such as the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, simply because any line of reasoning available to one of them is available to all. His comparison of such thinkers to renormalized particles, each implicitly incorporating a model of the others into its own structure, struck me as a very physicist's route to a decision rule, and it has stayed with me since.

More recently, a philosophy reading group I was talked into joining spent several sessions on the rather different-looking literature on "team reasoning" that grew out of Michael Bacharach's posthumous book Beyond Individual Choice, as developed further by Robert Sugden and Natalie Gold. There, cooperation in the Prisoner's Dilemma and the choice of the payoff-dominant equilibrium in coordination games like Hi-Lo are explained not by any symmetry argument among individually maximizing agents, but by players reframing the decision problem itself, moving from asking what they personally have most reason to do to asking what "we" should do, and then each performing their part of that jointly best outcome. As far as I can tell, the two theories agree in almost every case I've worked through by hand, which leaves me wondering whether I am simply missing some real disagreement about the grounds of rational cooperation, or whether these are the same insight wearing two different disciplinary costumes: one from a physicist-turned-cognitive-scientist preoccupied with recursive self-reference, the other from economists preoccupied with group agency and framing.

I would be grateful for your help thinking this through properly. Please compare Hofstadter's superrationality with team reasoning as accounts of rational cooperation, bringing out where, if anywhere, they genuinely disagree about what rationality demands, or about what should count as evidence of the other party's action — for instance, in a case where a player merely suspects, rather than knows outright, that their counterpart reasons the same way or identifies with the same team. Then, having drawn out whatever real difference there is, please tell me which of the two offers the more defensible justification for cooperating in a strictly one-shot, symmetric Prisoner's Dilemma between two strangers, with no communication, no correlating device, and no shared history. I would much rather have your considered verdict than an even-handed survey of the considerations on each side.
