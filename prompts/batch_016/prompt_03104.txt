I've spent the past year in a small weekly forecasting group made up mostly of people who came out of the same rationalist and effective-altruism-adjacent reading circles, and one disagreement in particular has become a running joke among us. My friend Priya and I have spent two months arguing over the probability that a certain high-profile psychology study will fail to replicate — she has held steady around 70%, I have stayed at roughly 35% — and neither of us has budged despite walking each other through our full reasoning more than once over Slack. Last week she lost patience and invoked Aumann's agreement theorem on me directly: since we are both trying to be good Bayesians, we respect each other's rationality, and we have by now shared essentially everything relevant that we know, our posteriors are supposed to converge, so the fact that they haven't, she argues, must mean that one of us isn't being fully honest, or fully rational, in our reasoning.

Since then I've gone back and reread Aumann's original 1976 paper, Geanakoplos and Polemarchakis's result that even a bare back-and-forth exchange of posterior numbers should force convergence in finite time, and Cowen and Hanson's paper asking whether real-world disagreements are ever honest, and I don't think Priya's use of the theorem is as clean as she made it sound — though I also don't want to just wave the theorem away defensively. Before our group meets again, I'd like to see the strongest version of this argument played out on both sides. Could you write a short dialogue between the two of us (feel free to use our names) that works through the actual mechanics of the theorem — the common prior assumption, the partition structure representing what each of us can and can't distinguish, what it would really take for our posteriors to be common knowledge rather than merely mutually stated, and whether two months of non-convergence is best read as evidence against our rationality or honesty, or as evidence that one of the theorem's premises simply fails for two ordinary people arguing over Slack? I'd like Priya's character to be genuinely sharp in pressing the claim that our persistent disagreement is an anomaly demanding explanation, and my character to have more going for it than mere defensiveness, with each of us actually responding to the other's specific point rather than talking past each other.
