After thirty-two years running a precision-metrology lab -- mostly high-resolution spectroscopy and atomic frequency standards -- I retired two years ago and joined a small reading group of former academics that works through one classic philosophical text each term. This term we are on Timothy Williamson's Knowledge and Its Limits, and I confess that chapters four and five have kept me awake longer than anything from my working life. Williamson argues that no interesting condition is "luminous" in his technical sense: it is never true that whenever a condition obtains, the subject is thereby in a position to know that it obtains. He builds the case around feeling cold. Imagine someone freezing at dawn who warms up so gradually that no change is perceptible from one millisecond to the next, yet who is plainly hot by noon. Williamson argues, via a safety requirement on knowledge (a known belief could not easily have been false, given how similar neighbouring instants are), that if this person knows, at some instant, that they feel cold, they must still feel cold at the very next instant. Now suppose, for reductio, that feeling cold is luminous after all, so that whenever one feels cold one is thereby in a position to know it. Chaining that supposition together with the safety-based claim across the whole morning forces the conclusion that, since the person was certainly cold at dawn, they must still be cold at noon -- which is false. Something has to give, and Williamson's verdict is that it is the luminosity of feeling cold, not the safety principle, that collapses. He then runs the same style of reasoning directly on the condition of knowing that p, which threatens the KK principle -- that knowing entails being in a position to know that one knows -- for everything except a handful of trivial cases.

What strikes me, as someone who spent decades reporting measured values to a stated number of significant figures while knowing perfectly well that a finer instrument could always prove me wrong at the next decimal place, is how closely Williamson's "margin for error" resembles ordinary measurement uncertainty, and I cannot decide whether that resemblance vindicates his argument or exposes it as proving too much. He gives an independent version of the argument for numerical estimates -- judging a tree's height or a crowd's size -- where margin-for-error principles, combined with a closure principle, conflict with KK directly, without relying on the disputed safety-based defence of the feeling-cold case. Taken to its limit, this style of reasoning seems to threaten the idea that we can ever strictly know the precise truth of an empirical claim rather than some coarser approximation of it, which feels like too strong a conclusion to swallow whole. I gather the responses on offer include denying the safety principle outright (there are lottery-style cases where someone seems to know something despite a very similar nearby case in which the very same method would have gone wrong), defending the crucial claim about neighbouring instants on grounds that do not depend on safety at all, and observing that even if sensations like feeling cold are "self-presenting" in a way that rescues their luminosity, knowing that p obviously is not, so the argument can simply be rerun on knowledge itself while the independent tree-and-crowd version stands untouched regardless.

Could you write me an essay that works through this dispute properly? Please set out the strongest form of Williamson's case against luminosity and against the KK principle, engage seriously with the best replies (including what you make of the self-presentation move, the various attacks on the safety principle, and the costly fallback options of simply accepting scepticism about empirical knowledge or an implausibly generous view on which any true belief counts as knowledge), and then tell me plainly where you land. I want an actual verdict, not a survey that carefully avoids taking sides -- something in the spirit of "the argument defeats KK in general but leaves a restricted, non-graded version of it standing" or "the argument fails because premise such-and-such does not hold" is exactly the kind of answer I am after.
