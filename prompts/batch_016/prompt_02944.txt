This summer I've been reading through some of W. V. O. Quine's papers for an independent project that grew out of my IB Theory of Knowledge class, where we spent a few weeks on the fact/value distinction. Quine is mostly known for his work in logic and epistemology, and both my teacher and most of what I could find online treat him as a philosopher who basically stayed out of ethics. But I tracked down a short, strange 1978 piece of his called "On the Nature of Moral Values" — which appeared that year both in the journal Critical Inquiry and in a festschrift for William Frankena, Charles Stevenson, and Richard Brandt, and was later folded into his essay collection Theories and Things — where he actually does take the subject on directly. He opens with a scenario about a dog that, for some unclear reason, ignores a turnip lying nearby, and uses it to argue that all behavior is a tangle of belief and desire. From there he argues that ethics stands on much shakier methodological ground than science does, because moral disagreements have nothing playing the role that observation sentences play in science: no shared trigger that makes everyone assent the instant they're confronted with it, the way a rabbit darting across a field makes everyone standing there agree that a rabbit just ran by. Still, he stops short of dismissing moral discourse as meaningless, and the handful of secondary sources I've found seem genuinely divided on whether the essay leaves Quine as a kind of noncognitivist, a naturalist who thought ethics could eventually be brought into the scientific fold like everything else, or something in between.

Here is what I can't work out on my own, and my teacher wasn't much help either. Quine, together with Hilary Putnam, is also responsible for the indispensability argument: the claim that we ought to believe in things like numbers and sets because they are indispensable to our best scientific theories. More than a decade after Quine's death, the philosopher David Enoch built a version of that very same argument for moral realism, claiming that moral facts are just as indispensable to practical deliberation as mathematical facts are to physics, even if they don't do the same causal-explanatory work. Quine never got the chance to respond to Enoch directly, and I haven't come across anyone who has seriously tried to reconstruct how he would have. So: reasoning only from commitments Quine actually held — his naturalism, his holism, his insistence on observation as the anchor of any respectable ontology, and his own doubts about how methodologically secure ethics could ever be — would he have accepted Enoch's move as a legitimate extension of his own argument, or would he have had principled grounds for blocking it? Feel free to note considerations that cut in each direction, but please don't leave the question hanging: reconstruct the reasoning Quine most plausibly would have used, and end with a clear, defended verdict.
