An old friend of mine from graduate school, who left academic philosophy some years ago to train as a clinical psychologist, called me recently about a case she has not been able to shake, and it has lodged itself in my thinking as well. One of her patients, a woman in her forties with a long history of bipolar I disorder, worked with her care team during a period of stability to draft an unusually detailed crisis plan. The plan specifies the exact early warning signs of an oncoming manic episode — markedly reduced sleep, sudden extravagant spending, a cluster of grandiose projects — and instructs the team, once those signs appear, to hospitalize her and administer medication even over her objections at the time. She was insistent that the team disregard anything she said to the contrary once an episode was under way, on the grounds that her own judgment during mania cannot be trusted. She has invoked the plan twice before, and on both occasions, once she had recovered, she thanked the team and confirmed that they had done exactly what she wanted.

Ten days ago the same warning signs reappeared, and when the team moved to act on the plan, they found a patient who was manic by every clinical measure yet unusually lucid and articulate, and who argued at some length that this time was different: that she felt clearer-headed than she had in a year, that the document in her file did not speak for the person now sitting in front of them, and that enforcing it anyway meant letting an earlier, more cautious version of herself dictate to the person she actually is. My friend did not call me for clinical or legal guidance, which she has in abundance; she wanted to know who was actually right, and I found that I did not have a ready answer either.

It seems too quick to say the earlier self simply outranks the later one merely because it went first and took precautions, yet it also seems odd to treat the plan as refuted by an objection it was explicitly designed to survive, since a safeguard that collapses the moment it is tested was never much of a safeguard to begin with. I keep suspecting that this case is a small, personal instance of a much older puzzle about whether, and on what grounds, one stage of a person's life can bind a later stage against its explicit wishes, though I have not worked out how far that suspicion should be trusted. I would value your view: whose judgment should have carried the day in that room, and is there a principled basis for an answer, or does it ultimately come down to an unprincipled judgment call about her state of mind at that moment?
