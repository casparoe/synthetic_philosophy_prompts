At Thanksgiving dinner this year, my cousin Ravi -- who has skipped nearly every family holiday gathering over the past five years, always citing unavoidable work travel -- told our cousin Elena, in front of the whole table, that her decision to spend the holiday with her boyfriend's family instead showed she didn't take the family seriously. This set off a running argument between two of my aunts, Meera and Claudia, that lasted well into the evening, and I've been turning it over ever since without managing to settle it myself.

(1) Meera said Ravi had no business saying any of this to Elena: whatever the merits of the point, someone who has missed more holidays than Elena ever has forfeits any standing to lecture her about it. (2) Claudia replied that this only shows Ravi is inconsistent, not that he's wrong -- if it's true that skipping Thanksgiving for a partner's family is a bad sign, pointing out Ravi's own record doesn't touch that claim, it just changes the subject from Elena's choice to his character. (3) Meera answered that she wasn't disputing the truth of what he'd said, only his right to be the one who says it to Elena's face -- the way you'd resent being lectured about fidelity by someone you knew to be serially unfaithful, even if every word of the lecture happened to be correct. (4) Claudia pushed back that this "standing" talk was doing suspiciously little work: if Ravi had said exactly the same thing gently and in private the next morning, instead of pointedly in front of the whole table, would Meera still say he lacked standing to say it -- or is the real objection about tact, merely dressed up in the language of hypocrisy? (5) Meera said it wasn't about tone: someone who holds other people to a standard he exempts himself from is claiming an authority over them that he hasn't earned, however nicely he puts it, and it's that unearned authority that's actually galling, not the delivery. (6) Claudia closed by pointing out that we don't actually know the two cases are comparable -- Ravi's absences were for work he says he couldn't refuse, while Elena's was a preference she could easily have overridden -- so calling him a hypocrite assumes an equivalence between the two situations that hasn't been established, and without genuinely like cases there's no hypocrisy, and hence no loss of standing, to speak of.

Could you go through this move by move, saying who has the better of the argument at each numbered point and why, and flag anything you think both Meera and Claudia are missing? Please close with an overall verdict on whether Ravi's apparent hypocrisy actually undermined his standing to say what he said to Elena.
