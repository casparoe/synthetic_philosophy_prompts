This term I've been teaching a graduate seminar on the philosophical foundations of probability, and a session on Chapter IV of Keynes's Treatise on Probability turned into an argument among the students that I still haven't resolved to my own satisfaction. Keynes devotes much of that chapter to showing that the Principle of Indifference collapses once it is applied to continuous quantities, and the most famous of his examples, borrowed from Bertrand, is the problem of a chord drawn "at random" in a circle and the probability that it is longer than the side of the equilateral triangle inscribed in that circle. He lays out the same three constructions that later became famous — fix one endpoint and randomize the chord's direction; fix the direction and randomize the chord's distance from the centre; randomize the chord's midpoint over the area of the disc — gets a different answer from each, and then, much later in the chapter, diagnoses the trouble: each construction implicitly treats the chord as the limit of a different shrinking area, "a triangle, the length of the base of which tends to zero" in the first case, a thin parallelogram in the second, and "a central section of undefined shape" in the third. Nothing in the bare instruction "choose a chord at random," he concludes, tells us which of these shapes we are entitled to shrink to the limit, so the question as posed has no determinate answer — and this, for Keynes, is symptomatic of a general defect in applying the Principle to any magnitude that cannot be resolved into what he calls "indivisible" alternatives.

Some fifty years after Keynes wrote this, E. T. Jaynes proposed a very different diagnosis of the very same problem in his 1973 paper "The Well-Posed Problem." Jaynes, too, held that the problem as ordinarily stated is underdetermined, but he argued that once you specify an actual physical procedure for producing the chord and then require the resulting probability distribution to be invariant under whatever translations, rotations, and rescalings that procedure leaves unfixed, a unique answer is forced on you — one that happens to coincide with Keynes's second construction, giving a probability of one half. Jaynes drew a general moral from this: appeals to indifference are not hopeless for continuous magnitudes, provided the requirement of symmetry is imposed on the right object, namely the transformation group implicit in the physical set-up, rather than on the space of outcomes directly.

I would like you to reconstruct, arguing strictly from the resources of Keynes's own chapter, how he would most plausibly have judged Jaynes's proposal. Does the demand for invariance under a transformation group supply exactly the kind of principled, evidence-based way of "proceeding to the limit" whose absence Keynes thought was fatal to the Principle in cases like this — so that Jaynes essentially vindicates the restricted, non-arbitrary version of indifference reasoning that Keynes himself was groping toward? Or would Keynes have insisted that the choice of which transformation group to privilege is just as unforced by the evidence as the choice among his own three constructions, so that Jaynes has only relocated the arbitrariness rather than removed it? Please commit to one of these two verdicts, or to some precise intermediate position, rather than simply cataloguing considerations on both sides, and defend your answer by engaging closely with the actual structure of Keynes's reasoning.
