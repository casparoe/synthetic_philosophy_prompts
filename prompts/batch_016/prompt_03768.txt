My friend Theo and I did the whole Flanders Fields battlefield day-trip last month -- Tyne Cot in the morning, then the German cemetery at Langemark right after -- and walking those rows of headstones from both sides that same afternoon, we got into this huge arguement about whether some 19 year old German soldier buried at Langemark and some British private buried twenty minutes away at Tyne Cot actually had equal moral standing to try and kill each other. Theo's basically a Walzer guy about it: soldiers are soldiers, once you put the uniform on you get the same rights and immunities as anyone else in uniform regardless of which government sent you (the "moral equality of combatants" thing), and jus in bello is supposed to stay independent of whether your side even had a just cause in the first place. I've been reading some McMahan lately and kept pushing the revisionist line back at him -- that being liable to get killed has to track actual moral responsibility for posing an unjust threat, so the German kid fighting an aggressive war just doesn't have the same standing as the British kid defending against it, full stop, even if the German kid is personally 100% blameless/excused because nobody in his village had access to a free press.

Where I think I lost the thread was when Theo brought up the epistemic argument -- that you can't expect an ordinary 19 year old to correctly adjudicate jus ad bellum from inside a trench, so punishing/blaming one side and not the other is both unfair and completely unworkable in practice, and anyway even McMahan apparently thinks the actual laws of war should go on treating both sides symmetrically for practical reasons regardless. All I had in the moment was "being excused isn't the same as being justified," which felt weak, and I've been chewing on it since.
