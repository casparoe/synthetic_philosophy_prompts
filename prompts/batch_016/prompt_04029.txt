I'm plotting a scene for a historical novel in which Leibniz, late in his correspondence with Clarke, is somehow confronted with an argument from long after his death: the 'hole argument' against spacetime substantivalism, which Einstein stumbled into in 1913 while wrestling with general covariance and which Earman and Norton later sharpened into a direct case against treating spacetime points as substances. In brief: because Einstein's field equations tolerate any smooth relabeling of the manifold (a diffeomorphism), one can leave the world untouched outside some small region and relabel it differently within, producing two solutions that agree on every observable and on all the data outside that region, yet disagree about which point ends up with which properties inside it — so even a complete specification of matter and geometry fails to fix a unique outcome. I want the scene to be philosophically earned rather than a costume for modern talking points, so I need to know what Leibniz would actually make of this, relying only on the equipment he had already turned on Clarke within that same exchange: the shift argument (reflect the universe east-to-west, or have God create it all a few years sooner; either way, by the principle of sufficient reason and the identity of indiscernibles, nothing has truly changed), deployed against a Clarke who himself leaned on Newton's rotating bucket for absolute space. Would Leibniz recognize the hole argument as his own reasoning simply rediscovered in a new physics, or would he notice that a smooth relabeling confined to a single region is doing something importantly different from his own rigid, global shifts — and that the hole argument's real sting is a failure of determinism rather than mere indiscernibility, which is not obviously the same complaint? Please reconstruct his likely reasoning rather than simply asserting a verdict.
