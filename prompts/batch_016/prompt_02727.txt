so i keep coming back to this question: why do promises actually bind, morally speaking -- not just the fact that keeping them is usually good policy, but why breaking one is a distinctive wrong on top of whatever harm it causes. Hume's answer is that its all convention, basically like the rules of baseball, where the obligation comes from everyone being invested in a mutually useful practice (he has this great line about how the naive picture -- where you just will a new obligation into existence by saying the words -- is barely more intelligible than transubstantiation). Rawls runs a similar-ish line later, tied to fairness instead of sentiment.

here's what bugs me though: take two strangers, totally alone, no witnesses, no chance of reputational fallout, no way of reinforcing any ongoing practice -- say someone dying on a desert island extracts a promise from the only other survivor, who will never tell a soul and will never see another person again either. the promise still seems binding to basically everyones intuitions, which seems like a real problem for any theory that locates the obligation in a social practice or in the expectations/trust generated in the promisee, since neither one is doing any work in this case.

can you give me a gears-level walkthrough of how the major theories of promising (Hume/Rawls-style conventionalism, Scanlon-ish expectation views, and whatever else is relevant) actually handle a case like this? and be honest about whether any of them really succeed, or whether we're stuck admitting the obligation comes from something more primitive that convention and expectation just can't capture.
