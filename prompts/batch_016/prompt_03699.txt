Six weeks ago I started taking semaglutide for weight management. It has worked better than years of willpower ever did, but I've felt oddly uneasy about it, as though I bought my way out of a test of character rather than passed it. This sent me back to the "Why I Am So Clever" chapter of Nietzsche's Ecce Homo, where he insists that nutrition, climate, and recreation must be governed by cultivated instinct and "taste" rather than by convention or indifference to one's own constitution, and where he is unusually candid about experimenting on his own body, including dosing himself with chloral hydrate and other drugs of his day for his migraines and insomnia. He obviously never imagined a drug that mutes appetite directly at the level of the brain rather than through diet or discipline. Based specifically on his actual views on nutrition, instinct, and self-experimentation, rather than his philosophy in general, how do you think he would judge a drug like this: one more legitimate tool of self-overcoming, continuous with the remedies he freely used on himself, or — since appetite sits closer to the will than pain or sleep do — exactly the kind of chemical comfort-seeking he mocked in his account of the "last man"? I'm curious which strands of his thought actually pull harder here, and why.
