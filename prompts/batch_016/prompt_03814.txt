I work as a software engineer and have no formal training in philosophy, but a colleague with a philosophy background recently got me interested in Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument, a challenge Kim raises against non-reductive physicalism about the mind. As I understand it, the worry is that if mental properties are merely realized by physical properties rather than identical to them, and every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause, then any mental property that seems to cause a physical event ends up preempted by its physical realizer, leaving it with no independent causal work to do. This struck a chord with debates I keep having at work about whether it even makes sense to say that a high-level abstraction, such as a function call or a learned representation inside a neural network, "causes" anything over and above the transistor-level physics that implements it.

While reading around the topic, I came across the outlines of Leibniz's monadology and his doctrine of pre-established harmony: monads are causally "windowless" and never interact with one another, and the apparent correspondence between mental and bodily states is instead explained by God having synchronized each substance's independent, self-generated development from the moment of creation. It struck me that Leibniz had already denied any real causal interaction between mind and body, for reasons entirely his own, roughly three centuries before Kim's argument was ever formulated.

What I'd like from you is a considered reconstruction of how Leibniz would most plausibly respond if the causal exclusion argument were somehow put to him directly. Would he treat it as belated vindication of pre-established harmony, on the grounds that he never believed in mind-body causal interaction to begin with? Would engaging with it instead expose some tension in his own system, for instance around his treatment of bodies as well-founded phenomena grounded in monadic activity, or around whatever he would say about the causal closure of the physical as Kim understands it? Or would he simply reject one of the argument's premises on independent metaphysical grounds? Please go beyond a one-line answer, and note explicitly any point at which you think the analogy between pre-established harmony and the modern exclusion problem breaks down.
