I write historical fiction, and I recently sent a chapter to two friends who know more medieval political theory than I do. In it, a lord assassinates the duke he served, calls him a tyrant, and defends the killing afterward in a speech invoking Aquinas. My friends ended up arguing about whether the speech holds up, and I'd like a third opinion on their exchange, not just on who won.

Mara said the speech misuses Aquinas: the duke inherited his title lawfully and turned tyrannical only in how he ruled, and Aquinas restricts killing such a ruler to the community acting through its proper public authority, not to any private person's own judgment, however wronged.

Dev objected that the lord isn't "any private person": he is one of the few peers whose consent the duchy's charter requires for new taxes, so he belongs to the institution that traditionally checks the duke.

Mara replied that Aquinas's worry is precisely that people judge badly in their own cause, and letting one councilor's rank substitute for a collective act is exactly what he warns against — it's how usurpation gets relabeled liberation.

Dev came back that this makes the doctrine self-defeating here: the duke stopped summoning the council years ago and had two of its members quietly killed, so no body capable of "public authority" still exists. If resistance is only licensed through institutions a thorough tyrant can dissolve, doesn't the doctrine guarantee that the most ruthless tyrants are the safest?

Could you go through the exchange move by move, saying who has the stronger reading of Aquinas at each step, and close with what, if anything, both of them are missing?
