For most of my working life I was a field geologist, spending long seasons reconstructing the environmental history of ancient sedimentary basins from whatever scraps of evidence had managed to survive erosion, metamorphism, and the sheer passage of time. That work left me with a settled, almost professional intuition about the gulf between what is true of the world and what can actually be known about it: countless perfectly determinate facts about the deep past (the salinity of some particular Devonian lagoon on some particular afternoon, say) are simply gone, the relevant evidence destroyed long before anyone existed who might have recorded it.

Since retiring I have taken up philosophy in earnest with a small reading group of former colleagues, and last month one of them introduced me to Fitch's paradox of knowability, which I initially assumed would turn out to be a fancier restatement of my own geological intuition. I now gather it is something stranger and more general than that: Fitch's 1963 proof shows that the mere existence of any unknown truth whatsoever, however mundane and however easily it might in principle be discovered tomorrow, suffices, together with the seemingly modest premise that all truths are knowable by someone at some time, to derive the absurd conclusion that every truth is in fact already known by someone. My eroded lagoons are not required for the argument to bite; an uncounted pebble would do just as well.

What I would like from you is a worked example that brings this home using a case from live, present-day science rather than deep time or a logician's bare placeholder variable. Please choose some specific proposition that is very probably true right now, is entirely mundane and knowable in principle by existing methods, yet is in fact known by no one at present (kindly avoid deep-time examples along the lines of my own eroded lagoons, and please also avoid anything that trades on being unknowable even in principle, since that, as I understand it, is precisely not what does the logical work in Fitch's argument). Then walk me through how substituting that proposition, conjoined with the claim that nobody currently knows it, into the knowability principle forces the contradiction at the heart of Fitch's proof, in enough detail that I could reconstruct the key steps myself afterward. Finally, I would be curious which of the standard responses to the paradox strikes you as most defensible: restricting the knowability principle to a syntactically well-behaved class of statements, as Tennant and Dummett separately propose, revising the underlying logic along intuitionistic or paraconsistent lines, or simply treating the whole result as a refutation of any view, from logical positivism to semantic anti-realism, that wishes to equate truth with potential verifiability.
