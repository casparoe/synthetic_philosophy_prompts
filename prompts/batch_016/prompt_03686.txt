I don't have any formal training in philosophy or economics, but over the past year or so I've spent a lot of my spare time reading about rationality and decision theory on various blogs and forums, and one argument that has been nagging at me is the "money pump" case for why a person's preferences must be transitive. The basic version is simple enough: suppose I prefer my vintage amp to my guitar, my guitar to my box of pedals, and yet, going around the circle, my pedals to my amp. A patient trader can then walk me around that loop, taking a small fee from me at each step, until I've paid three separate fees and ended up holding the exact same amp I started with. That looks like a clean proof that something has gone wrong with my preferences.

What's been bothering me is a reply I stumbled on, which people apparently discuss under headings like "resolute choice" and "diachronic rationality" (I hope I have those terms roughly right), which claims I can dodge the whole trap just by planning out the entire sequence of trades ahead of time and then simply refusing to make the final trade back to the amp, even though, considered on its own, I would still say I prefer the amp to whatever I'd be left holding instead. Do that, and I only pay two fees instead of three, and I am never actually fleeced. I brought this up with a friend of mine who plays a lot of poker and likes to think in terms of game theory, and he insisted that this "solves" the puzzle and shows that having cyclical preferences isn't really irrational, just dangerous if you're careless about it. It struck me, though, as more of a sleight of hand: to "stick to the plan," I have to be willing to turn down the very item I claim, at that moment, to like best, which seems like its own kind of inconsistency, just relocated to a later point in time, and it isn't obvious to me why avoiding a predictable fleecing should count as the same thing as being rational.

I'd like you to actually build the case for my side of that argument, rather than just gesture at it the way I did with my friend, and to engage with the strongest version of his view rather than a weak one. Please argue, as rigorously as you can, that this "resolute choice" escape does not show that cyclical preferences can be rational after all: that at most it shows a cyclical agent can avoid being predictably exploited over time, which is a much thinner and less interesting achievement than showing that the underlying preferences are rationally permissible, and that on close inspection the strategy either smuggles in a hidden, perfectly transitive ranking of entire trading plans to do the real work, or requires the agent to act against their own stated preference in a way that is no more defensible than the original cycle it was meant to fix.
