My mother is in home hospice for terminal cancer, and last week her palliative care physician raised her morphine dose to a level she admitted would probably hasten death, justifying the decision by invoking the doctrine of double effect: since the intention is to relieve pain rather than to kill, this is supposedly quite different from euthanasia. Trying to understand the principle she was leaning on, I read Aquinas's original conditions and then Warren Quinn's reformulation of double effect in terms of "direct" versus "indirect" agency — the distinction commonly illustrated by the terror bomber and strategic bomber, who each foresee civilian deaths though only one intends them. But I got stuck on the "closeness problem": depending on how finely we individuate the "intended" act, the same event seems to fall on either side of the line. Describe the physician's act as "binding opioid receptors to blunt pain signaling," and the resulting respiratory depression looks like a mere foreseen side effect; describe it as "suppressing central nervous system activity generally," and the slowed breathing looks like part of the very means by which the pain relief is achieved. Could you explain why this kind of redescription worry is taken to be so damaging to the doctrine of double effect, and assess whether Quinn's direct/indirect agency distinction genuinely blocks it, or merely relocates the same problem one level down?
