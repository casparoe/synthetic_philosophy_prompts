I'm the captain of my school's Ethics Bowl team, and this question came up in a roundabout way while we were prepping a case about a whistleblower who reported her employer partly because she was afraid of being implicated later if she stayed silent. Our coach used it to bring up Kant's example of the shopkeeper from the Groundwork: a shopkeeper who never overcharges his customers, but purely because a reputation for honesty is good for business. Kant says that even though the shopkeeper does the right thing, the act has no real moral worth, since it isn't done "from duty." That struck me as basically correct until our coach mentioned Michael Smith's "moral fetishism" objection, which says that requiring people to be motivated by the bare thought "this is my duty," rather than by the concrete features that make an act right (someone's suffering, a friend's trust), makes morally good agents sound weirdly self-absorbed with their own rightness instead of genuinely caring about others. Then I found a paper by Nomy Arpaly that uses Huckleberry Finn as a counterexample from the other direction: in Chapter 16, when slave hunters come looking for runaways, Huck can't bring himself to hand Jim over, even though he sincerely believes, because of how he was raised, that helping Jim escape amounts to stealing him from his "rightful owner," and he feels crushed with guilt about it. Arpaly argues that Huck's act still has full moral worth -- maybe more than an act done from correct principle would -- because he's actually responding to Jim as a person, whatever he consciously believes about his duty.

Could you write me a dialogue between two philosophers hashing this out? One should be a committed Kantian who holds that moral worth requires consciously acting from a recognition of duty, and who is willing to bite the bullet on hard cases -- including, if it comes to that, Huck Finn -- rather than cave to the fetishism charge; I want this character to have a genuine reply to that objection, not just concede it. The other should be a critic who thinks moral worth consists in being moved by whatever features actually make an act right, whether or not the agent frames this to herself in moral terms at all; this character should have to answer the natural worry that her view makes moral worth cheap or accidental, since it seems to imply that even someone with generally bad values could rack up moral worth just by getting lucky in one particular case. Please have them respond directly to each other's actual last move rather than delivering parallel monologues, keep both sides equally sharp, and don't force a tidy resolution at the end -- I'd rather see exactly where the disagreement gets stuck. Somewhere around a page or two would be perfect.
