I'm writing my IB Extended Essay on rational choice theory and have gotten stuck on how philosophers respond to "money pump" arguments against agents with cyclic preferences (say, A over B, B over C, and C over A). Two strategies are supposed to let such an agent avoid exploitation without ever abandoning the cyclic preferences: "sophisticated choice," which uses backward induction to foresee and refuse any losing sequence of trades, and "resolute choice," which commits up front to a full plan of trades and sticks to it even when deviating would, at some later point, locally seem better. Please argue that resolute choice, not sophisticated choice, offers the more defensible account of diachronic rationality — one on which an earlier commitment can genuinely bind an agent's later choices, rather than everything reducing to what she would prefer moment by moment.
