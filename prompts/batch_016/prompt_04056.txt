In my school's political philosophy elective — it's a small, discussion-based class, so tangents like this tend to take over — we just wrapped up a unit pairing Kant's "Perpetual Peace" with the section of Hegel's Philosophy of Right on sovereignty and war (roughly §321 through §340, including the Remarks and the Zusätze compiled from his students' lecture notes). For discussion, our teacher asked us to work out whether Hegel's treatment of war amounts to more than the claim that wars will simply keep happening. Two people in my group, Marcus and Elena, kept arguing about this well after the teacher tried to move everyone on to Kant, and since I sit between them and watched the whole thing unfold, I've been trying to work out ever since who actually had the better of it.

(1) Marcus opened with the Remark's comparison of a lasting peace to a calm sea that would eventually turn foul, with war playing the part of the wind that keeps it healthy, plus the Addition's claim that under prolonged peace, people's habits calcify into a rigid separateness that Hegel treats as tantamount to death for the community. Marcus argued this isn't just a prediction that war will recur; it's Hegel telling us that a genuinely warless world would be bad for a people's ethical health, which makes this a real, if qualified, endorsement of war's value rather than mere resignation to it.

(2) Elena answered that Marcus was running together two different claims: the point, made just before the war passage, that a citizen's rights and property are shown to be non-absolute, merely "ideal" moments within the larger ethical whole once the state calls on citizens to risk them — and the further claim that war is therefore something to be positively sought. She noted that Hegel immediately flags this "ethical health" reasoning as merely philosophical, adding that any actual war would still need a separate justification he hasn't given; that the decision to fight a given war is left to the monarch's practical judgment about the state's welfare and circumstances, rather than derived from the stagnation argument; and that Hegel insists war be conducted within real limits — never touching domestic institutions or ordinary family and private life — while treating any particular war as something whose point is ultimately to end, not to continue. None of that, she said, sounds like praise of war as such.

(3) Marcus granted the point about limits and about who decides on any given war, but said that only shows Hegel wants war fought in a civilized way once it starts, not that he's neutral about whether it keeps recurring. He came back to Elena's own reading: if a state's individuality requires something to be negated against, so that any league of states will sooner or later call a rival, and so an enemy, into being, that's a conceptual claim ruling out permanent peace altogether, not merely a prediction that it won't last — and treating its absence as a kind of civic death is doing more than describing.

(4) Elena replied that ruling out permanent peace as impossible, and regarding war as good, are still two separable claims; you can find exactly that combination in realist international-relations theory today, among people who treat great-power conflict as structurally bound to recur without thinking any given war, or war as such, is good. She agreed Hegel treats a fully warless world as a kind of stagnation, but said the open question is whether "avoiding stagnation" functions as a genuine reason for Hegel, on a par with, say, self-defense, or is doing a narrower, explanatory job — accounting for why Kantian peace projects are naive without telling anyone to pursue war in order to stave off decline. She admitted she wasn't sure the primary text settles this by itself.

Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who has the better of the argument at each of the four points, rather than just picking an overall winner? I'd also be curious what, if anything, you think both of them are still missing about Hegel's actual position that might help settle this.
