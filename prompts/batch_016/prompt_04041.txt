I'm a PhD student in behavioral economics writing the historical-background chapter of my dissertation on present bias, and I've gotten stuck on a passage from Adam Smith's *Theory of Moral Sentiments* (Part IV, Chapter II) that seems to be doing more philosophical work than it usually gets credit for in that literature:

"That self-command, in the same manner, by which we restrain our present appetites, in order to gratify them more fully upon another occasion, is approved of, as much under the aspect of propriety, as under that of utility. When we act in this manner, the sentiments which influence our conduct seem exactly to coincide with those of the spectator. The spectator does not feel the solicitations of our present appetites. To him the pleasure which we are to enjoy a week hence, or a year hence, is just as interesting as that which we are to enjoy this moment. When for the sake of the present, therefore, we sacrifice the future, our conduct appears to him absurd and extravagant in the highest degree, and he cannot enter into the principles which influence it. On the contrary, when we abstain from present pleasure, in order to secure greater pleasure to come, when we act as if the remote object interested us as much as that which immediately presses upon the senses, as our affections exactly correspond with his own, he cannot fail to approve of our behaviour [...] It is the consciousness of this merited approbation and esteem which is alone capable of supporting the agent in this tenour of conduct. The pleasure which we are to enjoy ten years hence interests us so little in comparison with that which we may enjoy to-day, the passion which the first excites, is naturally so weak in comparison with that violent emotion which the second is apt to give occasion to, that the one could never be any balance to the other, unless it was supported by the sense of propriety, by the consciousness that we merited the esteem and approbation of every body, by acting in the one way, and that we became the proper objects of their contempt and derision by behaving in the other."

What I can't settle is whether Smith thinks the pull of the present is a mistake about value or a correctly perceived but simply weaker motive. On one reading, the spectator's indifference between "a week hence" and "this moment" fixes the correct valuation, and someone who discounts the future is misjudging it, rather as one might misjudge the size of a distant object. On the other reading, Smith is granting that we see the future pleasure for what it is and are simply moved by the present one more strongly regardless, which would explain why he thinks self-command requires an entirely separate motive, the desire for the spectator's esteem, rather than any correction of belief. These strike me as two quite different diagnoses of weakness of will: one where the whole problem dissolves into an error of judgment, and one where there is a genuine gap between judging and being moved. Which reading does the passage actually support, and does Smith give us grounds for deciding between them, or is he simply not distinguishing the two?
