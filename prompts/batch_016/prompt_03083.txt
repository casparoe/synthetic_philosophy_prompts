Debates about whether LLMs "really" understand language tend to cycle through the same set pieces: the Chinese Room, the grounding problem, emergent world models like Othello-GPT. I'd rather see the question worked through a more specific piece of philosophical machinery: Ludwig Wittgenstein's treatment of rule-following in the Philosophical Investigations, developed decades before neural networks existed but bearing on this question more directly than people usually notice.

Two threads matter. First, the rule-following paradox (PI §201): no finite stretch of past behavior fixes which rule is being extrapolated, since indefinitely many rules agree on all past cases and diverge only later (Kripke's "quus" is the standard sharpening). Wittgenstein had already made much the same point rejecting the image of a machine whose construction "contains" its future movements in advance (§§193–195); his own way out of the paradox locates correctness not in some further fact about the follower's insides or construction, but in a standing practice of use and correction. Second, the private language argument pushes further, insisting that any such practice must be public rather than private: meaning requires participation in a shared "form of life," not mere internal consistency over time.

Working from these two threads specifically, reconstruct the position Wittgenstein would most plausibly take on whether a large language model — a mechanism trained on a finite corpus that grows unreliable off-distribution — can be credited with following a rule, or understanding a word, at all. Does training on, and correction against, an enormous body of human practice give it a foothold in the practice-based resolution, or does the absence of genuine, mutual participation in a form of life disqualify it regardless of fluency? Commit to one reading rather than leaving the matter open.
