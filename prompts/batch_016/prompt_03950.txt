A few years ago I came across some news coverage about logicians who had used an automated theorem prover to check that Gödel's proof of God's existence was logically valid, which got reported in some outlets as something close to "a computer proved God exists." I don't have any formal background in logic or philosophy, but the story stayed with me, and a while back I decided to stop reading summaries of summaries and actually track down what the argument itself says. What I found, in the form generally attributed to Dana Scott's write-up of Gödel's own notes, is a short list of definitions, axioms and theorems. I've copied it out below in the same order I found it, doing my best to reproduce the logical notation by hand since I couldn't copy it directly from the PDF I was reading:

A1. Either a property or its negation is positive, but not both: ∀φ [P(¬φ) ↔ ¬P(φ)]
A2. A property necessarily implied by a positive property is positive: ∀φ∀ψ [(P(φ) ∧ ∀x(φ(x) → ψ(x))) → P(ψ)]
T1. Positive properties are possibly exemplified: ∀φ [P(φ) → ◊∃x φ(x)]
D1. A God-like being possesses all positive properties: G(x) ↔ ∀φ [P(φ) → φ(x)]
A3. The property of being God-like is positive: P(G)
C. Possibly, God exists: ◊∃x G(x)
A4. Positive properties are necessarily positive: ∀φ [P(φ) → □P(φ)]
D2. An essence of an individual is a property possessed by it and necessarily implying any of its properties: φ ess. x ↔ φ(x) ∧ ∀ψ [ψ(x) → □∀y(φ(y) → ψ(y))]
T2. Being God-like is an essence of any God-like being: ∀x [G(x) → G ess. x]
D3. Necessary existence of an individual is the necessary exemplification of all its essences: NE(x) ↔ ∀φ [φ ess. x → □∃y φ(y)]
A5. Necessary existence is a positive property: P(NE)
T3. Necessarily, God exists: □∃x G(x)

I can follow each of these lines on its own, and I understand the basic idea that being "God-like" just means having every positive property. What I cannot do, even after reading it many times, is reconstruct in my head how the argument actually gets from C, which only says a God-like being is possible, to T3, which says one necessarily exists. It's clear that D2, D3, A4 and A5 must be doing that work, since everything up through C only ever established mere possibility, but I can't see why "essence," defined the way it is in D2, is the right tool for bridging possibility and necessity, or why D3 defines necessary existence in that roundabout way instead of simply saying "exists in every possible world." I would be grateful if you could walk me through, step by step and in plain language, exactly how A4, D2, D3 and A5 work together to force that final jump from C to T3. I'd also be curious to hear, once that mechanism is clear, why some commentators apparently think this same machinery has the side effect of collapsing the distinction between what merely happens to be true and what is necessarily true, for propositions generally and not just for God's existence, and whether that is generally regarded as a fatal problem for the argument or as a cost its defenders are willing to accept.
