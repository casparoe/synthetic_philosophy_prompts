I'm prepping a lecture for my Greek ethics seminar on Plato's moral psychology, and I went down a bit of a rabbit hole last night re-reading Book 1 of the Laws (the part before the Athenian Stranger and his companions even get to any actual legislation). I usually build the whole unit around the Republic's picture of reason keeping the appetites in line, but this passage is throwing a wrench in that tidy narrative and I cant quite work out the argument to my own satisfaction. Here's the bit, in Jowett's translation (I've trimmed some of the Socratic back-and-forth and marked the cuts in brackets — everything else is verbatim):

"Suppose that some God had given a fear-potion to men, and that the more a man drank of this the more he regarded himself at every draught as a child of misfortune, and that he feared everything happening or about to happen to him; and that at last the most courageous of men utterly lost his presence of mind for a time, and only came to himself again when he had slept off the influence of the draught. [Cleinias admits no such potion actually exists, but agrees it would make a wonderfully cheap, low-risk test of courage if it did; the Athenian then asks whether there's a real potion that tests the opposite failing — overconfidence and reckless boasting — and Cleinias volunteers wine.] Is not the effect of [wine] quite the opposite of the effect of the other? When a man drinks wine he begins to be better pleased with himself, and the more he drinks the more he is filled full of brave hopes, and conceit of his power, and at last the string of his tongue is loosened, and fancying himself wise, he is brimming over with lawlessness, and has no more fear or respect, and is ready to do or say anything. [A little more back and forth, in which the Athenian argues that just as courage has to be trained by exposure to fear, its opposite virtue has to be trained by exposure to whatever makes us reckless.] Are not the moments in which we are apt to be bold and shameless such as these?—when we are under the influence of anger, love, pride, ignorance, avarice, cowardice? or when wealth, beauty, strength, and all the intoxicating workings of pleasure madden us? What is better adapted than the festive use of wine, in the first place to test, and in the second place to train the character of a man, if care be taken in the use of it? What is there cheaper, or more innocent? For do but consider which is the greater risk:—Would you rather test a man of a morose and savage nature, which is the source of ten thousand acts of injustice, by making bargains with him at a risk to yourself, or by having him as a companion at the festival of Dionysus? Or would you, if you wanted to apply a touchstone to a man who is prone to love, entrust your wife, or your sons, or daughters to him, perilling your dearest interests in order to have a view of the condition of his soul?"

So if I'm reading this right, the actual proposal is that cities should run supervised drinking parties precisely because getting people drunk strips away shame and lets a ruler see what someone is "really" like underneath — a cheap, survivable version of testing a soldiers courage under fire. I mentioned this to a colleague over lunch and she just said "yeah, that's Plato for you" and moved on, which, thanks, very helpful. What I'm stuck on: why does he need the imaginary fear-potion at all instead of just talking about wine from the start — is it doing real philosophical work, or is it mostly rhetorical throat-clearing? Is the "touchstone" idea a serious epistemic point about how self-control can only be verified under real temptation (which would make it oddly continuous with, not opposed to, the Republic's concerns), or is it basically an after-the-fact justification for a practice he was already committed to defending against the Spartans and Cretans? And that line about handing your wife or kids over to a lecherous man to "test" him — is there any way to read that as making sense within his ethics, or is it just a bad argument dressed up in nice prose? Would love your take, I keep going in circles on this one.
