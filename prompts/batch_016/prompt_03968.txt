This term I'm taking a seminar on formal epistemology, and this week's readings centered on Bas van Fraassen's well-known "Judy Benjamin problem," from his 1981 paper "A Problem for Relative Information Minimizers in Probability Kinematics." As a reminder of the setup: Judy Benjamin has parachuted into a region divided into Red and Blue territory, each of which is further split into a Headquarters area and a Second Company area, so that she starts out assigning equal (1/4) credence to each of the four resulting cells. Command then radios her the conditional information that "if you are in Red territory, the odds are 3:1 that you are in the Headquarters area" — i.e., her conditional credence in Headquarters given Red should become 0.75 — and the puzzle is exactly how she should revise her full distribution over all four cells in light of this, since neither plain Bayesian conditionalization nor ordinary Jeffrey conditionalization straightforwardly tells us what to do with evidence that comes in this conditional form.

In discussion, our professor walked us through four broad reactions to this puzzle that one finds in the literature. First, there is van Fraassen's own proposal, which updates by minimizing relative information (KL-divergence) between the old and new distributions subject to the stated constraint — call this the relative-entropy solution — which has the notoriously counterintuitive consequence that Judy's unconditional credence in Red territory itself must shift, even though the news seems to bear only on how Red territory is internally divided. Second, there is the response associated with Grove and Halpern — call this the fixed-credence solution — who argue that rationality actually requires Judy's unconditional credence in Red to stay exactly fixed at one-half, contrary to van Fraassen's own answer. Third, there is the later resolution offered by Douven and Romeijn — call this the tailored-Jeffrey solution — who agree that credence in Red should stay fixed but reach that verdict via a specially tailored instance of Jeffrey conditionalization built on a different, independently motivated distance function, rather than by simply stipulating invariance. And fourth, there is the more deflationary reaction found in some surveys of this debate — call this the no-solution view — which treats the whole case as showing that no update rule confined to orthodox Bayesian or Jeffrey conditionalization can adequately handle this sort of conditional evidence, so that something has to give in the standard probabilistic picture of learning.
