In a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of science, we have spent the last few weeks contrasting Humean and anti-Humean accounts of laws of nature: David Lewis's best-system analysis, on which a law is simply a theorem of whichever true deductive systematization of the world's particular, non-modal facts best combines simplicity and strength, against necessitarian views such as Armstrong's, on which a law consists in a distinct, contingent relation of natural necessitation holding between universals, and dispositional-essentialist views such as Bird's, on which the relevant necessity is already built into the essential causal powers of properties themselves. Since the whole dispute is named after Hume, a few of us went back to the passage where he actually tries to define "cause," in Section VII, Part II of the first Enquiry, and found it more puzzling than any textbook summary had prepared us for. Here is the passage:

"Similar objects are always conjoined with similar. Of this we have experience. Suitably to this experience, therefore, we may define a cause to be an object, followed by another, and where all the objects, similar to the first, are followed by objects similar to the second. Or in other words, where, if the first object had not been, the second never had existed. The appearance of a cause always conveys the mind, by a customary transition, to the idea of the effect. Of this also we have experience. We may, therefore, suitably to this experience, form another definition of cause; and call it, an object followed by another, and whose appearance always conveys the thought to that other."

Hume introduces the counterfactual clause as though it were merely "in other words" a restatement of the initial regularity claim, then goes on to offer a third, explicitly psychological formulation in terms of the mind's customary transition, without ever remarking that the three might come apart. Could you help me work out whether they really are equivalent, and if not, what sort of case would separate them? I would also like your assessment of how this bears on the contemporary debate over laws of nature: can a Humean like Lewis legitimately treat only the regularity formulation as ancestral to the best-system account, setting the counterfactual and psychological formulations aside as inessential, or does Hume's own felt need for more than one definition already give the anti-Humean grounds for saying that bare regularity was never sufficient, even in the passage credited with inaugurating the regularity tradition?
