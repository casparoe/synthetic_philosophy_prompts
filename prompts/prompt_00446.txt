I spent my career as an experimental chemist, and only in retirement have I had the time to sit down properly with the philosophy of physics literature—Sean Carroll, David Albert, Tim Maudlin, that sort of thing. Lately I've been going back and forth with an old graduate school colleague about the various interpretations of quantum mechanics, and we've hit a genuine snag concerning Bohmian mechanics. As I understood it from my undergraduate coursework decades ago, Bell's theorem—confirmed experimentally by Aspect and refined by many others since—closed the door on hidden variable theories once and for all; it's always presented as the definitive rebuttal to Einstein's hope that quantum mechanics was merely an incomplete description of a deeper, deterministic reality. And yet I keep running into serious physicists and philosophers who treat de Broglie-Bohm theory as a live contender, complete with a "pilot wave" that guides particles along perfectly definite trajectories at all times, hidden variables and all. If Bell's theorem really did rule out exactly this kind of hidden-variable picture, why does Bohmian mechanics still get taken seriously in these discussions? Is this simply a case of philosophers lagging behind the physics, or am I misremembering what Bell's theorem actually forecloses?
