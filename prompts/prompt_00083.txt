I've been working through a seminar paper on updateless decision theory, and I keep getting stuck on a move that most defenses of UDT seem to make without much justification. The standard pitch is that updatelessness lets you do better "ex ante" by avoiding the dynamic inconsistency that plagues CDT and EDT in cases like counterfactual mugging or Parfit's hitchhiker. But this is normally framed as a purely instrumental victory — you commit to the policy that has the best expected value before you know which branch of the decision tree you'll end up in, and you stick to it even when, updated on your current information, it looks like a bad deal. My worry is that this seems to quietly presuppose that the ex ante perspective has some kind of privileged rational or even moral authority over the perspective of your actual, updated epistemic state — and I don't see why we should grant that.

So here's what I'd like you to argue: make the strongest case you can that agents should follow through on updateless commitments even in the specific moment where, given everything they currently believe, the updated calculation says defection or deviation is locally optimal. I don't just want the standard appeal to "but you get more utility on average across the ensemble of possible worlds" — I've heard that one from my study group already, and it feels like it's helping itself to exactly the frame that's in question, since the agent making the decision only ever lives in one of those worlds. I want an argument that actually engages with why the updated, in-the-moment self ought to regard the prior commitment as authoritative, rather than treating updatelessness as a kind of externally imposed precommitment device that the in-the-moment agent has no real reason to honor once the relevant information has already come in.
