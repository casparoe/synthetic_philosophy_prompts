I'm a novelist currently drafting a family drama, and a real situation involving my brother-in-law has been nagging at me in a way that I suspect could sharpen the ethical spine of the book. Two years ago, he and his business partner left the same holiday party after drinking roughly the same amount, both convinced they were fine to drive. My brother-in-law made it home without incident. His partner ran a red light on a quiet street, and through sheer chance a jogger happened to be crossing at that exact moment; she was struck and later died from her injuries. The partner was charged, lost his medical license, and is now spoken of, even by old friends, as someone who killed a person. My brother-in-law faced no consequences at all and still refers to that night as "a scare" rather than anything more serious, even though, as far as I can tell, he made exactly the same reckless choice.

He is, by every other measure, a thoughtful and careful man now, and yet I can't shake the feeling that the enormous gap in how these two are judged has less to do with what they actually did than with what happened to occur afterward. Were they equally culpable the moment they got into their cars, or does the outcome genuinely change the moral weight of the decision itself? I'd like a clear-headed answer to this, partly to settle how I think about my brother-in-law, and partly because I want the ethical stakes in my novel's parallel storyline to hold up to real scrutiny rather than just feel dramatically convenient.
