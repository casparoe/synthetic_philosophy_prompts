I've been working through David Lewis's "Convention" for a graduate seminar, and I keep getting stuck on how his account of convention as a solution to recurring coordination problems would map onto phenomena he never anticipated. Lewis built his framework on cases like driving on the same side of the road or agreeing on meeting points—situations with clear common knowledge, stable populations, and repeated face-to-face interaction. But so much of contemporary norm-formation happens through algorithmically mediated, large-scale, often anonymous interaction: think of how linguistic conventions mutate on social media, or how norms around privacy, dataset consent, or even acceptable AI-generated content are forming right now in real time, without the kind of tight-knit community or iterated common knowledge Lewis's model seems to presuppose.

Given his emphasis on common knowledge as the glue that holds conventions together, and his insistence that conventions are contingent, self-perpetuating solutions to coordination problems, what do you think Lewis would say about whether internet-scale norms—like conventions around emoji usage, meme formats, or platform-specific etiquette—actually count as conventions in his technical sense? Would he bite the bullet and say common knowledge can be approximated well enough through platform affordances and visible aggregate behavior (like counts, upvotes, or trending signals), or would he think these cases reveal a real limitation in his theory, requiring something like weaker or "thinner" notions of mutual expectation to do the work that common knowledge once did?
