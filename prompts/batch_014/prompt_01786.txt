A couple of winters ago a bad viral infection left me with lasting parosmia: my sense of smell came back badly scrambled, and with it most of my sense of taste. Before that I had spent years running an amateur wine-tasting group and thought of myself as having a reasonably trained palate; now coffee often tastes faintly of burnt plastic, and I can no longer trust myself to tell whether a sauce is genuinely well balanced or just registering as "edible" to whatever is left of my senses. The strange consequence is how much I now depend on other people's word for it - my partner tells me the risotto needs salt, a sommelier friend tells me a bottle has turned - and I simply have to take their testimony on trust, since I no longer have any independent way of checking.

My partner, who is finishing a doctorate with a minor field in aesthetics, sent me Hume's 1757 essay "Of the Standard of Taste" as the classic treatment of this kind of situation, and one stretch of it has been nagging at me ever since. Early on, Hume is bluntly deflationary about the whole idea of getting beauty "right": "Beauty is no quality in things themselves: It exists merely in the mind which contemplates them; and each mind perceives a different beauty." A few pages later, though, he wants to disqualify certain verdicts as simply defective: "A man in a fever would not insist on his palate as able to decide concerning flavours; nor would one, affected with the jaundice, pretend to give a verdict with regard to colours. In each creature, there is a sound and a defective state; and the former alone can be supposed to afford us a true standard of taste and sentiment," and he adds that this is "in like manner as the appearance of objects in day-light, to the eye of a man in health, is denominated their true and real colour, even while colour is allowed to be merely a phantasm of the senses." Shortly after that, to define the "delicacy" that separates good judges from bad ones, he retells the story from Don Quixote of Sancho Panza's two kinsmen, called on to judge a hogshead of wine: one "tastes it; considers it; and after mature reflection pronounces the wine to be good, were it not for a small taste of leather, which he perceived in it," while the other "gives also his verdict in favour of the wine; but with the reserve of a taste of iron." Both are mocked for their fussiness - "But who laughed in the end? On emptying the hogshead, there was found at the bottom, an old key with a leathern thong tied to it." Hume then says that arriving at general rules of criticism is "like finding the key with the leathern thong; which justified the verdict of Sancho's kinsmen, and confounded those pretended judges who had condemned them."

What I can't work out is whether Hume is entitled to run both moves at once. If beauty and flavour really are, as he says, "merely a phantasm of the senses," with no fact of the matter beyond individual sentiment, then talk of a "sound" versus "defective" organ, of a "true and real" colour, or of a buried key that retroactively proves one taster right and another wrong, all seem to smuggle back in exactly the kind of mind-independent aesthetic fact he opened by denying. Is there a way of reading this passage on which the fever-and-jaundice language, and the key in the hogshead, are genuinely compatible with his official subjectivism - rather than Hume just quietly changing the subject from "what beauty is" to "who gets socially recognized as a good judge of it"? And since my own palate is now, by Hume's own lights, pretty squarely in the "defective" camp, I'd be curious what, if anything, his argument actually entitles me to say about why I should keep deferring to other people's aesthetic testimony about flavour, rather than that deference just being convenient.
