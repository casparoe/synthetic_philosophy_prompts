For the last couple of years I've been reading philosophy for fun in retirement—I spent my career as an experimental particle physicist—and mostly this happens through a running email argument with an old postdoc of mine who's now faculty somewhere in Germany. Lately we've been going back and forth about interpretations of probability, and I think I've finally got a clean argument that settles the matter, but she won't budge, so let me run it by you.

In my field you don't get to call something a discovery unless you clear five sigma, and that whole apparatus—p-values, confidence intervals, all of it—is frequentist to the core: probability just means long-run relative frequency, full stop. And underneath the statistics, the actual physics of things like radioactive decay involves genuine objective propensities: a given nucleus has a fixed probability per unit time of decaying whether or not anybody is around to have an opinion about it. So between frequencies and propensities, physics seems to hand us probability as a plain objective fact about the world, no different in kind from mass or charge.

Given that, I dont really see what work the "subjectivist" or Bayesian interpretation is supposed to be doing when it defines probability as a degree of belief. If my credence and my postdoc's credence about the same hypothesis can differ and we can both still count as perfectly rational, doesn't that just show "subjective probability" isn't a rival theory of the same thing at all, its a softer, different subject that borrowed the name and the calculus for decoration? So why do philosophers still treat it as a serious contender alongside frequentism and propensity theories, instead of just a category mistake dressed up in equations?
