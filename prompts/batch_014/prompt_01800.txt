I've been reading up on the Hart-Fuller debate for my jurisprudence seminar, specifically the grudge-informer case -- the wife who reported her husband to the Nazis for badmouthing Hitler, and then got prosecuted herself after the war for it. From what I can tell, Fuller's whole "inner morality of law" thing is basically just Aquinas's old idea that a sufficiently unjust law isn't really law at all, dressed up in new terminology -- that's supposedly how he gets to say the wartime statute criminalizing her husband's remarks was never valid law in the first place, because its content was too morally monstrous to count as real law. Meanwhile Hart, as the positivist in this story, is stuck saying the statute WAS valid law no matter how vile it was, which means his side doesn't really have a principled way to condemn what happened beyond just shrugging and going "well, it was legal at the time, nothing to be done."

Given that, i'm having trouble seeing why Fuller's move gets treated as such a big original contribution to legal philosophy instead of just Aquinas with extra steps -- my professor keeps going on about "the eight principles of legality" like they're doing serious independent work, but if the bottom line is still "immoral enough = not law," i don't see whats actually new there. And on the flip side, if positivism's best answer to an actual functioning legal system being used to legitimize atrocities is "yep, still valid law," why does anyone still treat Hart's side of this as a serious, live position instead of something the grudge-informer case basically refuted?
