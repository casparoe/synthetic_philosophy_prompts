I've been putting together a reading pack for my grad seminar on decision theory and AI, and this week's rabbit hole has been "surrogate goals" as a defense against acausal threats -- the idea (I think it traces back to Tobias Baumann, with Oesterheld and Conitzer generalizing it later) that an agent facing possible extortion could deliberately bolt on some goal it doesn't otherwise care about, so a threatener redirects their threat onto that decoy instead of whatever the agent actually values. This is all in the context of agents -- future AI systems, say -- that can threaten each other "acausally," i.e. with no causal channel between them at all, just by simulating or predicting one another accurately enough.

Two of my students got into a real argument about this after class and I can't stop thinking about it. One of them thinks surrogate goals basically work and are an underrated tool for defusing conflict between systems that can predict each other; the other thinks the trick can't survive contact with a sufficiently sophisticated threatener -- either the threatener isn't fooled and just threatens the real goal anyway, or, if the agent really would pay out to protect the "fake" goal, its not obvious that goal is fake anymore, so you've arguably just relocated the vulnerability rather than removed it (he keeps invoking Yudkowsky's point about reflective inconsistency here, though I'm not 100% sure he's using it right).
