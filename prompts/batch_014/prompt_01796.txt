For my IB Extended Essay in philosophy, I've been working through F. H. Bradley's discussion of relations in Appearance and Reality (1893), and there's a paragraph I keep re-reading without feeling like I've actually grasped the logic of it. It's from Book I, Chapter III, "Relation and Quality," where he's arguing that relations are just as unintelligible as the qualities they're supposed to connect:

"But how the relation can stand to the qualities is, on the other side, unintelligible. If it is nothing to the qualities, then they are not related at all; and, if so, as we saw, they have ceased to be qualities, and their relation is a nonentity. But if it is to be something to them, then clearly we now shall require a new connecting relation. For the relation hardly can be the mere adjective of one or both of its terms; or, at least, as such it seems indefensible. And, being something itself, if it does not itself bear a relation to the terms, in what intelligible way will it succeed in being anything to them? But here again we are hurried off into the eddy of a hopeless process, since we are forced to go on finding new relations without end. The links are united by a link, and this bond of union is a link which also has two ends; and these require each a fresh link to connect them with the old."

What I can't work out is why Bradley thinks a relation has to itself stand in a further relation to its terms just in order to relate them at all. The obvious reply seems to be: a relation just relates its terms directly, full stop, without needing to be related to them in some further sense -- otherwise you'd need a further relation to relate *that* relation to its terms, and so on forever, which is absurd, so something must just relate without being related. Bradley seems to rule this reply out rather than argue against it. Is there an actual argument buried in the passage for why "being something to the terms" requires "bearing a relation to the terms," or is he just helping himself to that step?

Separately, I keep seeing this passage cited as the source of the "unity of the proposition" problem that Russell wrestled with -- the question of why "Socrates is wise" is a genuine claim and not just a list containing Socrates, wisdom, and instantiation. Is that really the same problem as Bradley's, or only a cousin of it that happens to look similar on the surface?
