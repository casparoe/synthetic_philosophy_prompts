I've been reading LessWrong and its predecessor Overcoming Bias for close to fifteen years now, and one piece I still find myself returning to is Yudkowsky's 2008 post "Taboo Your Words," from the "A Human's Guide to Words" sequence. His fix for a stalled argument over some contested term is to ban the word itself and make both sides redescribe their actual anticipated experience without it: if the disagreement survives translation, it was substantive, and if it evaporates, it was merely verbal. I had always filed this away as a fairly distinctive rationalist contribution, until a friend with formal philosophy training pointed out, somewhat deflatingly, that David Chalmers had proposed what looks like essentially the same procedure in his 2011 paper "Verbal Disputes," under the label "the method of elimination," and that the whole line of thought traces back to Carnap's distinction between internal and external questions and his principle of tolerance about choice of linguistic framework.

Could you give me a real comparison of the two, rather than just a note that "they're basically the same idea"? Concretely: do tabooing and the method of elimination actually license the same verdicts once you get into the details, or does Chalmers build in safeguards — about what counts as a genuinely residual, non-verbal disagreement, and about disputes that resist elimination altogether — that Yudkowsky's more casual version doesn't bother with? And is there a sense in which Yudkowsky, despite writing three years earlier, ends up the more thoroughgoing Carnapian of the two, given how readily the Sequences treat contested philosophical questions (free will and personal identity, say) as dissolvable once you taboo the right words, whereas Chalmers — the author of the paper on verbal disputes — has always resisted giving his own hard problem of consciousness the same treatment? I'd rather you engage with what these three actually committed themselves to than just gesture at the family resemblance between them.
