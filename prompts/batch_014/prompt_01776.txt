I'm a retired physicist who has been reading philosophy fairly unsystematically since retiring, and Douglas Hofstadter has been a recurring touchstone. Rereading his old "Metamagical Themas" columns recently, I was struck again by his notion of "superrationality": agents who know they reason exactly alike should cooperate in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma purely because of that symmetry, with no causal interaction between them at all. Some effective-altruism-adjacent reading then led me to an odder-sounding descendant of this idea, "evidential cooperation in large worlds" (ECL), originally called "multiverse-wide superrationality": if the universe or multiverse is sufficiently large, it contains countless causally unreachable agents whose values may differ enormously from our own, and we should still act cooperatively toward them, since our choices remain evidence about theirs. Please compare the two ideas: is ECL simply superrationality generalized to an enormous universe of dissimilar agents, or does it depend on further assumptions -- about decision theory, cosmology, or the sort of correlation that could actually license cooperation -- that Hofstadter's original argument never needed? And of the two, which do you think gives us the stronger reason to actually change our behavior?
