Ok, a bit of an odd one today. I spent about 35 years as an experimental physicist and I'm still pretty new to philosophy, but I finally sat down and read Turing's actual 1950 paper instead of just the pop-science version of the "Turing test" everyone quotes. Buried in section 6, as the very last of his listed objections, is the "Argument from Extrasensory Perception." Turing says flat out that these phenomena "seem to deny all our usual scientific ideas," that the statistical evidence for telepathy is "overwhelming," and that the resulting threat to the idea that our bodies move by ordinary physical law is "quite a strong" argument in its own right. Working through a card-guessing version of the imitation game, he even admits a machine's own random-number generator could get skewed by the interrogator's psychokinesis, so above-chance guessing wouldnt reliably tell you which contestant is human. His fix for the whole mess is just to put both contestants in a "telepathy-proof room."

That struck me, as an old experimentalist, as a control that can't possibly do the job its supposed to do. Can you argue the case that if ESP is real in the way Turing describes it here - something that already defies ordinary physical law and reaches right into a machine's own circuitry - then no physical room could ever shield against it, so his fix fails by his own logic, and the imitation game is left with no principled way to rule ESP out at all? I want a genuine developed argument for that, not just a summary of the section.
