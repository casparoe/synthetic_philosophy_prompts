I'm working on a longread about the intellectual roots of "morals legislation" debates -- should the law punish something just because most people find it immoral, even when there's no identifiable victim -- and for background I went down a total rabbit hole re-reading the Hart vs. Devlin exchange from the late 50s/early 60s (started with Devlin's 1959 Maccabaean lecture, then Hart's replies). Every pop-science-of-law summary flattens this into a one-line morality tale, liberal philosopher vs. conservative judge, pick your favorite, which loses what's actually a pretty tight four-move arguement. I want to lay out the moves as I've reconstructed them and get a real referee's take, not a verdict on who "won" in hindsight.

Here goes. (1) Devlin: a society is held together by a shared morality the same way it's held together by its government, so the law is entitled to defend that shared morality exactly as it defends the state against treason -- neither treason nor private immorality needs an identifiable victim to be dangerous, since both threaten the fabric a society is organized around. The test for when a private practice crosses that line isn't a judge's or a philosopher's opinion, it's whether the "man in the jury box" -- an ordinary person, after calm reflection -- reacts with real intolerance, indignation and disgust, not mere distaste. (2) Hart: that argument smuggles in an empirical claim it never earns -- Devlin never shows that tolerating, say, private homosexual acts between consenting adults actually erodes social cohesion, he just asserts it (this move later got the label "the disintegration thesis"). Worse, if "society's morality" just means whatever the majority happens to feel right now, then any moral change at all counts as a bit of disintegration, which trivializes the whole argument before it even gets going. (3) Devlin: already blocked that one -- I never claimed every deviation threatens society, any more than every subversive act threatens the state; some moral rules are peripheral and can shift freely, while others are close to the core a society is actually organized around, and the jury box is how you find out which is which, without waiting around for philosophers to agree on ultimate moral truths, a luxury no legislator actually has. (4) Hart: but that's the problem, not a solution to it -- that same sincere, near-unanimous public revulsion has, at other times, attached itself to interracial and interfaith marriage, to religious minorities, to plenty of things we'd now call bigotry rather than moral insight. What you're calling the jury's verdict is what I'd call "positive morality" -- whatever a society happens to feel at a given moment -- and that's just not the same thing as "critical morality," a reasoned standard for judging whether those feelings deserve to be acted on. A jury box can only ever report the former.

Could you referee moves 1 through 4 individually, rather than just declaring an overall winner? Then tell me in prose if there's some deeper point neither of them ever actually lands a hit on. Finally, on its own line at the very end, give your overall verdict in exactly this format: \answer{Hart} or \answer{Devlin} -- whichever of them comes out ahead once you weigh all four moves together.
