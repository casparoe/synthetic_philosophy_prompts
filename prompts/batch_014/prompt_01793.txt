I don't have any formal education in philosophy, but a philosophy podcast I listen to on my commute spent an episode on "the problem of induction," and I couldn't let it go, so I've spent the last few weeks working through David Hume's An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding on my own, using the free Gutenberg text. I got stuck on a passage in Section IV, Part II, right where he's building toward the claim that it's custom, not reasoning, that makes us expect the future to resemble the past. Here it is, copied out exactly as it appears:

"In reality, all arguments from experience are founded on the similarity which we discover among natural objects, and by which we are induced to expect effects similar to those which we have found to follow from such objects. And though none but a fool or madman will ever pretend to dispute the authority of experience, or to reject that great guide of human life, it may surely be allowed a philosopher to have so much curiosity at least as to examine the principle of human nature, which gives this mighty authority to experience, and makes us draw advantage from that similarity which nature has placed among different objects. From causes which appear similar we expect similar effects. This is the sum of all our experimental conclusions. Now it seems evident that, if this conclusion were formed by reason, it would be as perfect at first, and upon one instance, as after ever so long a course of experience. But the case is far otherwise. Nothing so like as eggs; yet no one, on account of this appearing similarity, expects the same taste and relish in all of them. It is only after a long course of uniform experiments in any kind, that we attain a firm reliance and security with regard to a particular event. Now where is that process of reasoning which, from one instance, draws a conclusion, so different from that which it infers from a hundred instances that are nowise different from that single one? This question I propose as much for the sake of information, as with an intention of raising difficulties. I cannot find, I cannot imagine any such reasoning."

What I can't pin down is the sentence about "a hundred instances that are nowise different from that single one." My first reading was that Hume finds it obvious that one instance and a hundred instances should lead to different conclusions, and he's simply asking what argument entitles us to move from the one to the many. But the more I sit with it, the more I suspect he means almost the reverse: that reasoning in the strict sense has no business treating a hundred repetitions as any different from a single case, so the extra confidence we clearly do feel after a hundred repetitions must be coming from something other than reasoning. Could you walk me through which of these two readings is actually right, or tell me if I'm missing a third option, and explain how the "eggs" sentence just before it is supposed to be doing the work of backing that reading up?

There's a second thing I'd like your help with, which is how this passage relates to Nelson Goodman's "grue" puzzle, something I've also read about but only in secondhand descriptions. As I understand it, "grue" is defined so that it applies to anything examined before some future date just in case it's green, and to anything examined after that date just in case it's blue, and all our current evidence supposedly confirms "all emeralds are grue" exactly as well as it confirms "all emeralds are green." Hume's passage above leans a great deal on the word "similarity" as the thing that custom fastens onto in carrying us from past cases to future ones, and it seems to me that grue is really just an attack on that word, showing that what counts as a "similar" object can be redefined to fit any data you like, which would undercut the very idea Hume is taking for granted here. Is that actually a fair way to connect the two, or would someone who knows this material say Goodman's puzzle is really a separate problem from Hume's, and that I'm forcing them together where they don't belong?
