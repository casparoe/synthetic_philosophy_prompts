A discussion from this term's graduate seminar on decision theory has unexpectedly followed me into my personal life, and I would appreciate an outside opinion on it. Two former doctoral students of mine — close friends, and now several years into the philosophy job market together — made an informal pact early in their PhDs: since so much of who lands a permanent position seems to depend on luck (which subfields happen to be hiring in a given year, which committee happens to like which writing sample) rather than on the quality of anyone's work, they agreed that if either of them landed a permanent position while the other was still without one, the successful one would quietly share a portion of their income with the other for a few years. Nothing was ever written down; the arrangement has survived only because both still regard it as fair, and it was struck at a time when neither had any real evidence about which of them the market was likely to favor.

One of them has recently been offered, at no cost, participation in a research project that would use placement records, letter-writer language, and subfield hiring trends to give her a personalized, reasonably reliable forecast of her odds of landing a permanent position in the coming hiring cycle. She is inclined to request it, reasoning that since it costs her nothing, looking at it cannot possibly leave her worse off than not looking: at worst, she says, she can simply disregard it. Her friend has asked her not to. He concedes that her reasoning about the forecast, considered by itself, sounds correct, but insists that something important about their arrangement would nonetheless be put at risk if she went ahead, even though he cannot articulate exactly what.

My own sympathies are with him, but I would like to know whether that instinct can actually be made rigorous, or whether it is really his friend who has the better of the argument. If there is a genuine risk here, I would especially value an account of exactly how it can be squared with the fact that, taken on its own, her reasoning about the forecast does seem sound, along with whatever practical advice follows from that for the two of them — whether it concerns what to do before she requests the forecast, or how to proceed afterward.
