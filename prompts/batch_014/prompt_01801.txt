So a few of us from work went to see this exhibit thing a while back that I still can't stop thinking about. There's no object at all in it -- the whole "artwork" is just museum staff (the artist calls them "interpreters," not actors) who walk up to random visitors and strike up an actual conversation with you, sometimes it feels scripted, sometimes it just turns into a real chat. Guards stop you if you even pull out your phone. And apparently when a museum buys the piece there's no contract either -- it's a verbal agreement done in front of a notary, nothing written down, nothing photographed, ever, because (per the artist, I think his name's Tino Sehgal or something) writing it down would turn it into a "product." MoMA and the Guggenheim have still payed real money for these, tens of thousands of dollars at least.

Two people who came with us have not let this go since. Jake says it doesn't count as art -- it's just an elaborate customer-service interaction that a museum decided to call art, and to him "the museum called it art" is basically like closing a bug ticket as "working as intended," it doesn't actually explain anything, it just ends the conversation. Mei said by that logic a play or a symphony isn't art either, since the performance itself is just as temporary and only a script or score survives -- and here the refusal to leave any record isn't a cop-out, it's the actual point, a comment on how we flatten every experience into something photographable and resellable. Jake came back with: sure, but they DO resell it, for real money, so the "resisting commodification" thing is just a good story they tell -- they want credit for being above the market while being extremely, extremely in the market. Mei said not writing something down isn't the same as not selling it, plenty of stuff nobody doubts is art (site-specific installs, whatever) also gets bought and sold as contracts or rights instead of objects, so this isn't some special hypocrisy, it's just normal plumbing for how non-object art gets sold.

Nobody backed down and it got weirdly heated for what was supposed to be a chill dinner. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's actualy right at each step (not just who "wins" overall), and flag anything you think both of them are missing? Is there a real, principled line buried in here about what makes something art, or does it genuinely dissolve into "whatever the artworld decides" once you push on it this hard?
