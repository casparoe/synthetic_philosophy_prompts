I'm a novelist assembling a small collection of short fiction, and for the last few months I've been stuck on a puzzle a friend who teaches aesthetics keeps sending me articles about: the so-called paradox of fiction, first posed by Colin Radford in his essay on why we can be moved by the fate of Anna Karenina. The puzzle, roughly, is that emotions like pity, dread, or grief seem to require believing that something bad is actually happening to someone, yet no competent reader ever forgets that Anna doesn't exist and nothing has actually happened to her — which by ordinary standards should make our tears about as sensible as screaming at a haunted house because we've momentarily forgotten it's built from plywood. I've read the usual responses: Kendall Walton's idea that we only feel "quasi-emotions" as part of a licensed game of make-believe, and Noël Carroll's competing thought theory, on which merely entertaining an unasserted thought about a character's fate is enough to produce a perfectly real emotion, no belief or pretense required. Neither has ever quite dissolved, for me, the strangeness of what happens when I find myself undone by a scene I invented myself only that morning.

What I'd like from you is a short story, somewhere in the 800–1,500 word range, that stages this puzzle from the inside rather than explaining it. I'm picturing something like an actor in the closing week of a regional production of a tragedy, who notices that her grief for the character she plays has quietly started to outlast the curtain call and bleed into her ordinary life — though you should feel entirely free to invent your own vehicle instead (a novel-within-the-novel, a bedtime story, an old home movie), so long as some character's emotional response to a fiction becomes strange or excessive in a way that leans on either the make-believe view or the thought theory, without the story ever naming either one directly. I'd rather you left the underlying puzzle unresolved at the end than have it snap into a tidy moral, since it's that unresolved itch, more than any answer, that I'm hoping the piece will capture.
