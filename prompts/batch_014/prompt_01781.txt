In Chapter VI ('The Ideal') of Principia Ethica, G. E. Moore treats both the aesthetic appreciation of beauty and personal affection (his term for the love we feel for friends and loved ones) as organic unities combining cognition and appropriate emotion, and he concludes that personal affection is far more valuable than the mere love of beauty. Yet he also concedes that admiration of someone's mental qualities alone, apart from their bodily expression, may be worth no more—perhaps even less—than the mere appreciation of physical beauty. Compare Moore's analysis of these two goods, explaining what role the principle of organic unities plays in addressing this apparent tension. Does his overall argument succeed in showing that personal affection deserves to rank above aesthetic appreciation, or does the concession about mental qualities undermine that ranking?
