As a retired experimental physicist who only took up philosophy in earnest after leaving the laboratory, I confess a particular fondness for Jeremy Bentham, if only because his felicific calculus -- his attempt to tally pleasures and pains along dimensions like intensity, duration, and certainty -- reads like an early, if hopeless, effort to give ethics the rigor of a measurement science. A small reading group I belong to recently worked through one of his unpublished manuscripts, an essay titled "Offences Against One's Self," composed around 1785 but withheld by Bentham himself and left in manuscript for nearly a century and a half. In it, he applies this same utilitarian reasoning to what the legal and philosophical writing of his day called "paederasty" (consensual sexual acts between men), concluding that since such acts yield pleasure to the parties involved and pain to no one else, the utility principle gives the legislator no ground whatsoever for punishing them. He goes on to argue that the horror his contemporaries felt toward such acts stemmed not from any genuine weighing of pleasure against pain, but from what he elsewhere calls the principle of asceticism compounded with sheer antipathy: prejudice dressed up as morality.

What struck several of us in the group was how closely this anticipates John Stuart Mill's harm principle from On Liberty, published some seventy years later, and yet how differently the two men seem to arrive at what looks like the same conclusion. Bentham reasons directly and case by case, from an aggregate reckoning of pleasures and pains; Mill instead lays down a standing rule carving out a protected sphere of self-regarding conduct, and when pressed on its utilitarian credentials, appeals to "utility in the largest sense, grounded on the permanent interests of man as a progressive being" rather than to any specific hedonic tally. I would like you to compare these two arguments for leaving consensual sexual conduct unpunished in some depth: is Mill's principle, at bottom, just a more cautious, rule-governed repackaging of Bentham's reasoning -- a way of securing the same result while insulating it from case-by-case recalculation -- or does it rest on a genuinely different conception of the relationship between individual liberty and the general welfare? And of the two, whose argument would you judge more secure against a fellow utilitarian who insists that, in some particular case, the sums might actually favor prohibition? Please justify your assessment.
