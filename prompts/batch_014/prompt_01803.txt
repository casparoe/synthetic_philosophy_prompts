For my history-of-science elective, I'm writing an independent paper on Otto Neurath, and a footnote about Isotype in a data-visualization article sent me further down the rabbit hole than my teacher probably expected. Rather than repeat the standard "Neurath invented picture-based statistics for Red Vienna" summary, I've been trying to connect Isotype's design rules to his epistemology. Below is the core of my draft (I cut the biographical background on the Social and Economic Museum of Vienna to keep this short). I'd appreciate feedback before I write the rest.

Isotype is usually filed under design history: standardized pictograms Neurath developed with the artist Gerd Arntz and the "transformer" Marie Reidemeister to teach statistics to people with little formal schooling. That's accurate, but it undersells Isotype by treating it as a communication technique that a philosopher happened to produce on the side. I want to argue instead that Isotype's specific rules are best read as the protocol-sentence debate carried out in a different medium.

By 1932 Neurath had broken with Carnap over what a "protocol sentence" — the basic report from which empirical science is supposedly built up — should look like. Carnap's early protocol sentences recorded immediate private experience; Neurath insisted they had to be physicalist and public from the start, since otherwise no scientist could check another's basic reports against their own. This is also why Neurath preferred the image of sailors rebuilding their ship at sea, plank by plank, to any picture of a foundation: there is no dry dock of pure, incorrigible experience to retreat to.

Now look at Isotype's actual rules, as Neurath laid them out in his 1936 International Picture Language. Most famously, a bigger quantity is never shown by enlarging a symbol, only by repeating it — ten cows are ten identical, same-sized cow-icons, not one large cow. Every icon has one fixed, conventionally assigned meaning; there is deliberately no perspective and no individual artistic style, since those are exactly what let a picture be read subjectively.

These read to me as the same commitments as the protocol-sentence view, moved into the visual register: publicity over privacy, convention over resemblance, intersubjective checkability over "impression." Neurath's own slogan, "Words divide, pictures unite," is usually read as being about crossing language barriers. I think it's also a compressed physicalist claim: ordinary words carry private, culturally loaded connotations that make intersubjective checking harder, while a rule-governed picture-sign, like a protocol sentence, means the same public thing to anyone who has learned the convention.

This also puts Neurath at odds with something closer to home in the Circle. Wittgenstein's early picture theory of language — a proposition is a picture of a fact — was something Neurath and Carnap had themselves enthusiastically taken up, yet the Tractatus treats picturing itself as something that can only be shown, and ends by counseling silence about whatever cannot be pictured this way. Neurath, uncomfortable with that mystical remainder, spent the next decade building an actual, literal picture-language meant to leave nothing essential unpictured: if a fact can be known at all, Isotype assumes it can be drawn by rules anyone can learn and check.

The obvious objection is that I'm retrofitting a tidy philosophical story onto what might just be practical design choices — repetition may simply be easier to count accurately than resizing, quite apart from any stand against Carnap or Wittgenstein. Neurath was also, first and foremost, an adult educator trying to reach people the graphs and prose of professional statisticians left out, which is a humbler motive than "refuting the picture theory of language."

I'd like comments on three things especially: first, whether the parallel between the protocol-sentence debate and Isotype's design rules is a genuine structural connection or just two things that happened to share a decade and a milieu; second, whether it's fair to frame Neurath's project as an implicit rejection of the silence that closes the Tractatus, given how much the Circle's early physicalism owed to Wittgenstein's picture theory in the first place; and third, whether I'm too quick to prefer the philosophical story over the more mundane, practical explanations for Isotype's design.
