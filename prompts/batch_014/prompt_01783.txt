I have no formal training in philosophy, but for the past couple of years I've been working through Spinoza's Ethics on my own, slowly, with a lot of secondary reading to help me along, and the part of his system that has stuck with me most is how far he pushes the denial of contingency. He says flatly, in Part I, that there is nothing contingent in nature and that everything follows from the necessity of the divine nature exactly as it follows from the nature of a triangle that its three angles equal two right angles. He doesn't spare human beings from this either: in his letter to Schuller (usually numbered Letter 58), he compares our sense of free will to a stone in flight that, if it could think, would feel itself to be freely choosing to keep moving, simply because it's aware of its own striving but not of the external causes behind it. His own notion of "freedom" survives this only because he redefines it completely, as acting from the necessity of one's own nature rather than being pushed around by external causes, which as far as I can tell makes him one of the earliest and strangest entrants in the whole compatibilism-versus-incompatibilism argument: he keeps the word "free," but only by building a system with zero contingency anywhere, at any level.

What I keep wondering about is what someone that committed to necessity would do with the Conway-Kochen "free will theorem" from 2006, which I stumbled on by way of a footnote in a physics book. As I understand it, the theorem shows that, given a few fairly modest assumptions from quantum mechanics and special relativity, if an experimenter's choice of what to measure is not itself a function of everything that has happened in the universe up to that point, then the particle's response to the measurement can't be a function of that history either. Physicists mostly seem to read this as a serious problem for any deterministic, relativistic, local account of nature, unless one is willing to accept "superdeterminism," meaning that the experimenter's supposedly free choice of setting was itself fixed in advance by the very same causal history that produced the particle, so that the appearance of two independent "free" choices is itself an illusion, not unlike Spinoza's stone.

Given all this, how do you think Spinoza would react if the theorem were somehow explained to him? Would he simply embrace the superdeterministic reading, on the grounds that he already denies humans any real independence from prior causes, and if so, does that actually leave his system intact, or does he still owe an account of why nature would go to such lengths to look random at the level of particles while remaining necessary underneath? Would his monism, the idea that finite things are only modes of one substance rather than separately existing atoms, make him more willing to give up locality than to give up necessity? And more generally, was his whole compatibilist move of redefining freedom as self-determination actually parasitic on the assumption that nature is necessary through and through, so that a universe with genuine, ground-floor indeterminism would force him to rebuild his account of freedom from scratch rather than merely adjust it at the edges? I'd like you to really work through the internal logic of his system here rather than give me a general "Spinoza was a strict determinist" answer.
