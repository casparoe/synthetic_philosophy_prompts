Last night a friend and I spent hours arguing over a fairly technical point, both of us convinced we were reasoning in good faith from similar premises, yet we never converged. It left me thinking about Aumann's agreement theorem, which shows that Bayesian agents who share a common prior cannot rationally "agree to disagree" once they've exchanged their posterior beliefs. Leibniz died centuries before this result was proven, but his dream of a calculus ratiocinator that would let disputants settle disagreements by calculation seems to anticipate exactly this kind of theorem. How do you think Leibniz would have reacted to Aumann's proof? Would he have taken it as vindication of his rationalist project, and how might he have accounted for the fact that people who seem to be reasoning honestly so often fail to converge?
