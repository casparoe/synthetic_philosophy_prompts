I'm a backend engineer who does a fair amount of on-call work, and something from an incident postmortem a few weeks ago has been nagging at me ever since, so I'm hoping you can help me reason through it more carefully than I've managed on my own. We'd had an outage, and going into the investigation there were four equally plausible root causes on the table: a network problem, a disk problem, a memory leak, and a race condition. The first two are infrastructure-side causes, the last two are application-side causes, but at that early stage nothing pointed toward any one of the four over the others, so I treated them as 25% each. Partway through, a teammate who had only been looking at infrastructure-side metrics told me, roughly: "I have no idea whether this is even an infra problem in the first place, and I haven't looked at the application side at all, but if it does turn out to be infra, the error logs make the network about three times more suspicious than the disk."

I wanted to fold that tip into our four probabilities properly rather than eyeballing it. My first instinct was that this should just be an application of Bayes' rule, but Bayes' rule wants a hypothesis and a likelihood, and what I'd actually been handed was a comparative claim relating two of my four hypotheses to each other while saying nothing about the other two, or about infrastructure-versus-application overall, so I wasn't sure how it was even supposed to plug in. In the end I wrote a small script to find the distribution over all four causes that respected the new three-to-one ratio while otherwise adding as little extra information as possible — the same maximum-entropy trick I've used before for some NLP work. I expected it to simply rescale the infrastructure pair and leave the 50/50 infra-versus-application split, and the 50/50 memory-leak-versus-race-condition split, exactly where they started. Instead, it returned something like network 35%, disk 11.7%, memory leak 26.6%, and race condition 26.6%: the two application causes stayed tied with each other, as I would have guessed, but the application category as a whole became more likely overall, rising from 50% to roughly 53%, apparently as a side effect of a tip that, as far as I can tell, said nothing whatsoever about application-versus-infrastructure.

I ran this by a friend who is doing a statistics PhD, half-expecting her to spot an obvious bug in my script, but she ended up just as puzzled as I was. So I'd like your help: is the script actually doing the mathematically correct thing here, and if so, why should learning something about the internal breakdown of one category rationally change my confidence in an entirely different category that the tip never touched? And if the script isn't correct, what would the principled way of incorporating a tip like this actually look like, and how would it differ from what I did?
