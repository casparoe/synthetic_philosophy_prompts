I'm a software engineer with no background in philosophy, but I've been slowly working through the Zhuangzi in an old public-domain translation (James Legge, 1891) as part of some casual reading on how different traditions argue that people shouldn't fear death. I got stuck on a passage from the chapter titled "The Great and Most Honoured Master." One of four friends, Dze-lâi, is dying, and another, Dze-lî, comes to see him and leans against the doorway to talk with him while his family weeps around the bed:

Before long Dze-lâi fell ill, and lay gasping at the point of death, while his wife and children stood around him wailing. Dze-lî went to ask for him, and said to them, "Hush! Get out of the way! Do not disturb him as he is passing through his change." Then, leaning against the door, he said (to the dying man), "Great indeed is the Creator! What will He now make you to become? Where will He take you to? Will He make you the liver of a rat, or the arm of an insect?"

Dze-lâi replied, "Wherever a parent tells a son to go, east, west, south, or north, he simply follows the command. The Yin and Yang are more to a man than his parents are. If they are hastening my death, and I do not quietly submit to them, I shall be obstinate and rebellious. There is the great Mass (of nature);—I find the support of my body in it; my life is spent in toil on it; my old age seeks ease on it; at death I find rest on it:—what has made my life a good will make my death also a good.

"Here now is a great founder, casting his metal. If the metal were to leap up (in the pot), and say, 'I must be made into a (sword like the) Mo-yeh,' the great founder would be sure to regard it as uncanny. So, again, when a form is being fashioned in the mould of the womb, if it were to say, 'I must become a man; I must become a man,' the Creator would be sure to regard it as uncanny. When we once understand that heaven and earth are a great melting-pot, and the Creator a great founder, where can we have to go to that shall not be right for us? We are born as from a quiet sleep, and we die to a calm awaking."

That's the whole passage. Two things about the second half trip me up.

First, the smith-and-metal comparison seems to say it would be perverse for the metal to insist on becoming one particular sword, and equally perverse for a person to insist on remaining human rather than becoming whatever comes next. But that logic seems to prove more than "don't fear death": it would be just as presumptuous, on this reasoning, for the metal to want any shape at all, or for a person to want to get good at their work, or to fight off illness, rather than being indifferent to every outcome. Is this really an argument about death specifically, or a general argument against wanting anything, that just happens to get aimed at death here?

Second, the comfort on offer seems to be that some underlying "stuff" — the great Mass, the metal, the melting-pot — persists and gets reshaped. But what people fear about dying is losing this particular run of experience, not whether matter continues in some form. "Your material gets reused" doesn't obviously answer "you are about to stop existing" — unless Zhuangzi means to deny there's a "you" of the relevant kind in the first place.

Am I missing something, or is there a real gap here? I'd like a genuine reconstruction of the argument, not the "Daoists are at peace with death" summary I keep running into.
