I'm a third-year philosophy PhD student currently drafting a dissertation chapter on the ethics of blame. Something has been nagging at me since our department's reading group worked through G.A. Cohen's "Casting the First Stone" alongside the more recent literature on hypocrisy and the "standing to blame" — Wallace's equal-standing account, Fritz and Miller's rival view, and the various replies to both. Almost everyone in the group accepted without much argument that a hypocrite's condemnation of, say, tax evasion or infidelity can be legitimately deflected just by pointing out that the condemner does the very thing they're condemning.

What I can't shake is that this looks like a direct collision with something I learned in my very first critical thinking course: that "tu quoque" — rebutting a criticism by pointing out that the critic is guilty of the very same fault — is a textbook informal fallacy, precisely because the critic's own conduct has no bearing on whether their claim is true. If dismissing a critic on grounds of hypocrisy is fallacious reasoning when an intro logic textbook says so, I don't see how the same move becomes respectable philosophy just because the standing-to-blame literature dresses it up in the vocabulary of "authority," "equal standing," and "silencing." At best, it looks like two literatures are simply talking past one another; at worst, it looks like an entire subfield of ethics has spent two decades rehabilitating a fallacy that any critical thinking course already teaches undergraduates to reject. Before I raise this in front of my advisor and embarrass myself, could you help me work out whether there is a genuine inconsistency here, or whether I'm missing some distinction that would let both bodies of literature be correct at once?
