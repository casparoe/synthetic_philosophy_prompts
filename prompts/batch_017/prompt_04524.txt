I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm currently working on a feature about the long-running friction between physicists and philosophers over the nature of time. In reporting it, I ran into an argument I'd like some help thinking through. The physicist I interviewed was quite insistent that special relativity has essentially settled the old metaphysical dispute between presentism (the view that only what exists right now is real) and eternalism (the view that past, present, and future events are all equally real, laid out together on a four-dimensional "block universe" picture). Her reasoning ran roughly as follows: relativity tells us that simultaneity is relative to an observer's frame of motion, so two distant events that count as happening "at the same time" for one observer will not be simultaneous for another observer moving relative to the first, with no experiment able to single out either frame as the uniquely correct one. If there is no fact of the matter, independent of an arbitrary choice of frame, about which distant events are happening "now," she argued, then the presentist's claim that only "now" is real has no stable content left to latch onto; the very idea of a single, universe-wide present moment simply falls out of the physics. She closed by invoking Einstein's own remark, from the condolence letter he wrote to the family of his lifelong friend Michele Besso, to the effect that the boundary separating past, present, and future is little more than a stubborn illusion.

Before writing this up, I went back to check the claim against the philosophical literature, and I was surprised to find presentism still being defended in careful, technically literate work by philosophers who are quite well versed in relativity, rather than treated as a position that has simply been refuted and abandoned. That's left me unsure how to frame the state of the debate for readers. If the relativity of simultaneity really does rule out any observer-independent global "now," as my source described, what exactly is left for a serious presentist to say in response that doesn't amount to quietly rejecting well-confirmed physics? Put differently, is there a specific point in the argument I've just reconstructed where the physics stops dictating the answer and a further, more contestable philosophical premise quietly takes over?
