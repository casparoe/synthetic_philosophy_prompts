In machine learning, I've always taken for granted that the Brier score and log-loss are "proper scoring rules": a model has nothing to gain, in expectation, by reporting a probability other than its actual estimate. I recently learned that the formal epistemologist James M. Joyce leans on essentially this same fact to argue for probabilism, the thesis that rational credences must obey the axioms of probability. His accuracy-dominance argument holds that any credence function violating those axioms is guaranteed to be less accurate, in every possible world, than some credence function that does satisfy them -- provided inaccuracy is measured by a "legitimate" rule, which is usually cashed out as one that is additive, continuous, and strictly proper.

What's bothering me is that propriety is itself defined in terms of probabilistic expectation: a rule counts as proper exactly when reporting your true probability estimate maximizes your expected score computed under that same probability. So restricting "legitimate" inaccuracy measures to proper ones already seems to assume that probabilistic expectation is the right currency for rational choice among credences -- which looks uncomfortably close to presupposing probabilism rather than deriving it from accuracy considerations alone.

Write an essay assessing whether this circularity charge defeats the accuracy-dominance argument, or whether the restriction to proper scoring rules can be independently motivated without begging the question. Weigh the strongest considerations on both sides and close with a definite verdict rather than a hedge.
