ok so I've been falling down a rabbit hole (pun 100% intended) for a piece on philosophy of physics. Einstein's old 'hole argument' -- which Earman and Norton dusted off in the 80s -- says that if spacetime points are real substantival things in their own right, general relativity saddles you with a totally unphysical indeterminism: you can build two different metric fields on the same manifold, related by a diffeomorphism thats the identity everywhere outside some 'hole,' and both count as legitimate solutions given the exact same data outside it. Every writeup I've read mentions in passing that this is basically Leibniz's old shift argument against Newton and Clarke (why would God bother placing the material universe HERE rather than five feet to the left, or with east and west swapped) just reloaded for spacetime points instead of whole universes. So here's my actual question: if you walked Leibniz through the full modern version -- diffeomorphism invariance, "Leibniz equivalence," and especially the "sophisticated substantivalist" escape hatch where people keep the points but deny diffeomorphic models are genuinely distinct possibilities -- would he take it as total vindication, or would his other commitments (PSR, identity of indiscernibles, his views on necessity and contingency) make him uneasy with parts of the modern setup or its rescue attempts? Give me your best reconstruction of his actual verdict, reasoned through his own principles, not just 'well, Leibniz liked relations, so yay relationalism.'
