I'm assembling a small collection of linked short stories, each of which is meant to dramatize a canonical philosophical argument entirely through plot and character, without ever naming the philosopher or the debate on the page. A friend in my workshop group keeps flagging the stories where I've let an idea sit decoratively in the background instead of actually driving the plot, and the piece I'm stuck on now is supposed to work through Hume's argument against believing in miracle testimony, from Part I and Part II of Section X ("Of Miracles") of the Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding.

What I want the story to earn isn't the flattened version people usually reach for ("Hume thought miracles can't happen"), but the actual mechanics of his case: that any report of a miracle pits one "proof" — the uniform experience underlying a law of nature — against another "proof" — a witness's word — and that a careful observer has to weigh these against each other rather than simply crediting whichever arrives with more conviction attached. I'd also like the story to metabolize, without stating outright, a couple of the further considerations Hume raises to tip that balance: the sheer pleasure people take in being the bearer of something astonishing, quite apart from whether they believe it themselves, and the way such reports cluster most thickly wherever there is the least appetite or capacity to check them. His own summary of the upshot — that no testimony can establish a miracle "unless its falsehood would be more miraculous than the fact it endeavours to establish" — is the note I'd like the plot to land on obliquely, rather than anyone actually saying it.

Could you write this story for me, somewhere in the range of 1,200 to 1,800 words? I'd like a protagonist whose ordinary work already requires weighing other people's word against the evidence — an insurance adjuster, a small-town coroner, a journalist on the religion beat, or another occupation you think fits better — who is confronted, by someone they would otherwise trust without a second thought, with a first-hand account of something that should not be possible. I'd rather the story not tip its hand about whether the event "really" happened, and I'd like it to steer clear of the shapes this kind of premise usually falls into (the tidy debunking, the deathbed conversion, the courtroom cross-examination). What I'm after is a piece where the pull of the testimony and the pull of everything the protagonist already knows about how the world behaves both stay genuinely alive right up to the last line.
