I'm the secretary of my high school's philosophy club, and for the last few meetings we've been working through the "quus" argument from the second chapter of Saul Kripke's book on Wittgenstein and rule-following (our faculty advisor typed up a packet of the key pages, since none of us could get hold of the whole book). After last week's session officially ended, two of our members, Elena and Marcus, kept arguing about it long after everyone else had moved on, and since I was taking notes anyway, I wrote down their exchange almost in full. I can't tell who actually had the better of it at each stage, so I'd like your help sorting through the exchange properly rather than just picking an overall winner.

Marcus opened by claiming that the puzzle is really a non-problem, since there is an obvious fact that fixes what I mean by "+": I am disposed, when asked "68 + 57" for the very first time, to answer "125" rather than "5," which is what the deviant "quus" function -- defined to agree with addition for numbers below 57 but to return 5 for anything larger -- would assign instead, and that disposition is an ordinary fact about my psychology, so it can simply be the fact that makes it true that I mean addition rather than quaddition. Elena answered that this can't be the whole story, for two separate reasons: first, my actual dispositions are finite and error-prone -- I might slip on a digit, get distracted, or form no disposition whatsoever about sums involving numbers too large for me to ever compute, yet there is still supposed to be a fact about what "+" yields even in those cases; and second, even where a disposition is present and accurate, it only tells us what I would say, whereas meaning is normative and is supposed to tell me what I should say, so no purely descriptive fact about my psychology can by itself generate that "should." Marcus then tried to repair the theory by idealizing it away from these problems: strip out fatigue, memory limits, and slips of attention, and say that I mean addition because, under those ideal conditions, I would be disposed to answer with the sum rather than the quus-value for literally any pair of numbers whatsoever. Elena's response was that idealizing does not rescue the view, because "ideal conditions" can only be spelled out in one of two ways: either the description of the ideal case already builds in "answers with the sum, not the quus-value," in which case the very meaning-fact we needed to explain has just been smuggled into the setup, or the ideal conditions are spelled out independently (more memory, more time, a calmer state of mind, and so forth), in which case nothing has been said yet about why a being satisfying that independent description would track addition specifically rather than any of the infinitely many functions that agree with addition on every case a human being could ever actually be tested on.

Could you go through this move by move and say who comes out ahead at each stage, including whether Elena's objection to the idealization move actually succeeds, or whether Marcus has some further reply available that neither of them raised? I would also be curious whether there is some important consideration about rule-following that both of them are overlooking entirely. Please work through the exchange step by step before giving any overall summary, and only offer that summary at the very end.
