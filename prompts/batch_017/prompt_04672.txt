I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and in preparing the anti-mechanism section of my comprehensive exams I keep finding that discussions of the Lucas-Penrose argument stall at the easy objection ('how do you know you're consistent?') rather than engaging the more careful version Penrose gives in Shadows of the Mind: suppose my reasoning is captured by some formal system F; if I know I am sound, then I know F is sound, hence so is F+ (F together with the premise that I am F); but then I can see the truth of F+'s own Gödel sentence, which F+ itself cannot produce -- a contradiction, so no F captures me.

Write a dialogue between two philosophers: Nadia, who defends this argument, and Owen, a computationalist skeptical of it. Have Owen zero in on the premise doing the real work -- that I can know I am sound, not merely consistent -- and have Nadia answer with Penrose's fallback distinction between ordinary, correctable mistakes and claims a mathematician would regard as 'unassailably' true. Then have Owen press her with a concrete case: Kempe's 1879 proof of the four-color theorem stood unchallenged for eleven years before Heawood located the flaw in 1890. Give Nadia a substantive reply rather than a concession, and let the exchange close with each side having landed a genuine point on the other, without forcing a resolution.
