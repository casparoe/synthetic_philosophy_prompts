I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on a dissertation chapter on expressivist semantics, and I keep running into a wrinkle in the Frege–Geach problem that most introductory treatments never get to. They typically stop once they've shown how an attitude-based semantics along Blackburn's or Gibbard's lines can validate the moral modus ponens, but the harder test case, I think, is negation.

Take a normative belief-ascription like "Priya believes that firms are obligated to disclose conflicts of interest," and consider three different ways of denying it: (1) "Priya believes that firms are obligated not to disclose conflicts of interest," (2) "Priya believes it is not the case that firms are obligated to disclose conflicts of interest," and (3) "Priya does not believe that firms are obligated to disclose conflicts of interest." These are not equivalent: (1) attributes a positive, contrary belief; (2) attributes a positive belief that no such requirement exists, leaving open whether disclosure is merely optional; (3) only denies that Priya holds the original belief, and is compatible with her having no view on the matter at all. If an expressivist analyzes "believes P is obligatory" as something like "hoorays P" or "has accepted a norm requiring P," it's not obvious that a semantics built from a handful of primitive attitudes can keep all three readings apart without collapsing two of them together or adding further primitive attitudes on an ad hoc basis. (This is essentially a sharpened version of what's sometimes called the negation problem for expressivism.)

Could you write a dialogue between a committed expressivist and a sharp critic (who need not be a moral realist, just skeptical that attitude-semantics can pull this off) working through whether the three-way distinction can be preserved? I'd like the expressivist to try at least one technically specific fix rather than simply restating the general program, and the critic to press a concrete case where that fix either collapses two of the readings or is bought at too high a cost. Please don't let either side win too easily, and let the dialogue end wherever the arguments honestly lead, even if that's a stand-off.
