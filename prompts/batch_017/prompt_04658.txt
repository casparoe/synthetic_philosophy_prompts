Last week I sat through a long incident postmortem at the software company where I work, trying to help figure out why a known vulnerability had sat unpatched for eight months before it was finally exploited. By the end, at least six different people could point to a moment where, had they simply done the thing they didn't do — filed an escalation, run an audit, read an alert, reprioritized a backlog item — the breach, as far as anyone could tell, would not have happened. Our "blameless" postmortem culture insists we shouldn't fixate on any single one of these omissions as "the" cause of the incident, but I couldn't shake the question of what would even justify treating one of those six failures-to-act as more responsible than the others, given that each one seems to satisfy exactly the same test for being a cause.

That question sent me down a rabbit hole into the metaphysics of causation by omission over the weekend. I learned that theories of causation built on counterfactual dependence apparently run into what's called the problem of profligate omissions: if a colleague's failure to escalate counts as a cause of the breach because the breach wouldn't have happened if she had escalated, then the same is true of everyone else on the planet who also failed to escalate it, which seems absurd, yet we obviously don't want to say there's no such thing as causation by omission at all. I gather at least one philosopher (Sarah McGrath, if I'm remembering the name right) treats this as a genuine dilemma rather than a puzzle with a clean fix, and that popular attempted solutions try to smuggle in some notion of "default" or "expected" behavior to pick out the omission that really matters — which makes me suspicious that causation is doing no real work here and "responsibility" is just being relabeled as "cause."

Nearly everything I've found illustrates all this with the same well-worn example of a neighbor who forgets to water someone's houseplant, which hasn't helped me reason about my actual situation at all. Could you construct a different, fully worked-out example — it doesn't have to involve software, and ideally shouldn't involve plants either — that brings the profligate-omissions problem into sharp focus, and then talk me through how a normative or "default/deviation" style solution would try to single out the one omission that intuitively deserves to count as the cause? I'd especially appreciate your honest assessment of whether that kind of solution actually resolves the underlying metaphysical puzzle or simply relocates it, since I'd like to know whether there's a principled answer to be had here before our team's next retrospective, or whether the philosophy suggests our instinct to resist "root-causing" this kind of failure is the more defensible position.
