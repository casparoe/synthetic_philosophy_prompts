This term I am taking a seminar on Victorian moral philosophy, and this week's session covered a side of Henry Sidgwick that nothing in our reading of The Methods of Ethics had prepared me for. Sidgwick is well known among ethicists for what he called the "dualism of practical reason": his worry that the rational claims of self-interest and of impartial benevolence might simply fail to converge, so that, as he put it near the end of the Methods, "the Cosmos of Duty is thus really reduced to a Chaos." What I had not known is that this same man spent nearly two decades, from the founding of the Society for Psychical Research in 1882 until his death in 1900, as its guiding figure and repeat president, sitting through séances, testing children who claimed telepathic powers, and cross-examining mediums almost as a second career. Our professor's point was that this was not a hobby kept apart from the ethics: Sidgwick apparently suspected that only some guarantee of personal survival after death could close the gap his own moral philosophy had opened, which is presumably why he needed the psychical research to succeed even as he brought a famously merciless skepticism to it. It was Sidgwick himself, together with his wife Eleanor and their colleague Edmund Gurney, who caught the young "telepathic" Creery sisters signaling to one another, and who then made the fraud public themselves. By the end of his life, having given decades to the search, he is said to have judged the whole endeavor largely wasted effort, while still allowing that a handful of cases remained genuinely unexplained.

I cannot stop thinking about what it would be like to be that particular kind of investigator: someone who has to be debunker and hopeful believer at once, in the very same room, because the debunking is undertaken in service of a hope that something genuine might survive it. For a short-fiction assignment in a writing course I am also taking, I would like to try to dramatize exactly that bind rather than write a conventional ghost story. Could you write me a short story, a page or two long, set over the course of a single evening's sitting with a medium, whose investigator is a philosopher clearly cut from Sidgwick's mold (you are welcome to use his name and circumstances loosely, or invent a stand-in if that gives you more room to work with)? I would like the real tension to run through the investigator's own rigor rather than through any actual supernatural event on the page, and I would like the ending to leave open, much as it apparently stayed open for Sidgwick himself, whether anything was ever really found.
