A few years ago I came across one of those "strange but true" news items about a handful of red heifers being flown from a ranch in Texas to Israel, apparently so that an organization called the Temple Institute could use them in a purification ritual tied to a future rebuilt Third Temple. I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or religious studies, so I didn't think much of it at the time, until a friend who has gotten involved in religious Zionist circles recently explained that the Temple Institute has also been training kohanim in the sacrificial service and has already reconstructed dozens of the priestly garments and sacred vessels, all in active preparation for resuming literal animal sacrifice the moment it becomes politically possible. That conversation sent me down a rabbit hole into the traditional sources on sacrifice, where I ran into what seems to be a genuinely famous, and apparently still unresolved, tension in Maimonides.

In the Guide of the Perplexed, especially III:32 (which builds on his long excursus on the ancient star-worshipping "Sabians" in the surrounding chapters), Maimonides argues that God commanded animal sacrifice only because the Israelites, fresh out of Egypt, were psychologically incapable of abandoning the sacrificial worship they already knew; rather than banning it outright, God redirected it toward Himself, the way one weans a nursing animal off milk gradually rather than all at once. Taken at face value, this makes sacrifice sound like a historically contingent accommodation to primitive habit, with no value of its own — one rung on a developmental ladder whose higher rungs are prayer and, eventually, silent intellectual apprehension of God. Yet in the Mishneh Torah (Hilchot Melachim 11:1), Maimonides states as settled law, with no hedging at all, that the Messiah will rebuild the Temple and restore the sacrificial order exactly as it was, and adds that anyone who denies this or fails to await it has thereby denied the Torah and Moses himself — and he goes on to devote several of the code's fourteen books to the minutiae of Temple and sacrificial law. Nahmanides attacked the Guide's account in sharp terms, and later readers (I gather even R. Joseph Soloveitchik sided with Nahmanides on this point) have never really agreed on whether the Guide or the Mishneh Torah expresses Maimonides' considered view, or whether he simply never reconciled the two.

Given that background, I'd like your best reconstruction of how Maimonides himself would likely react to something like today's Temple Institute: a well-organized, well-funded effort that treats the literal, physical resumption of animal sacrifice not as a remote messianic hope to be passively awaited, but as an urgent practical project to be actively hastened through lobbying, fundraising, livestock breeding, and priestly training. Would the logic of his own concession theory — on which humanity, or at least the Jewish people, has already been successfully weaned past the developmental stage where sacrifice was pedagogically necessary — push him toward real discomfort with, or even opposition to, this kind of activism? Or can you construct a way for him to consistently welcome it? I'd also be curious whether, in your view, Maimonides has any genuine internal resources for explaining why sacrifice specifically, rather than something "higher" in his own hierarchy of worship, is what returns in his idealized messianic future, or whether this is simply a loose thread he left untied.
