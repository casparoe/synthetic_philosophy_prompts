I cover physics and its philosophical fallout for a science magazine, and I'm drafting a long-form piece pegged to the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics, which recognized Alain Aspect, John Clauser, and Anton Zeilinger for their experimental work on Bell inequalities. My editor wants an opening section of a few hundred words that gives general readers the standard account of what Bell's theorem shows, before the piece goes on to complicate that picture. I drafted the passage below, but a physicist friend warned me at dinner that the "you must give up either locality or realism" framing I've used is exactly the sort of thing that makes people who work on the foundations of quantum mechanics wince, and that I've probably flattened a much thornier argument. She's traveling and unreachable before my deadline, so I'm hoping you can play that role instead.

Here is the passage:

"In 1964, John Stewart Bell showed that any theory satisfying two seemingly modest assumptions — locality, meaning no instantaneous influences between distant events, and realism, meaning that a particle has definite properties whether or not anyone measures it — must obey a statistical constraint now called a Bell inequality. Quantum mechanics predicts that entangled particles will violate that inequality, and when physicists finally closed the loopholes in 2015 using detector settings determined in real time by starlight, entangled photons violated it exactly as the theory said they would. Since giving up realism amounts to accepting something quantum mechanics already implies — that a particle has no definite state until it's measured — most physicists have concluded that locality is the assumption that has to go: at bottom, the universe lets entangled particles influence one another instantaneously, no matter how far apart they are."

Could you go through this with me? Third, I'd like a paragraph I can adapt for the piece that distinguishes what Bell means by "locality" from the weaker (and still true) claim that entanglement cannot be used to send a signal faster than light, since I suspect that distinction is doing a lot of work that my passage above glosses over. Finally, if you're willing to go further: how would you situate Bohmian mechanics, many-worlds, and superdeterminism specifically with respect to locality — do any of them genuinely preserve it, or do they each just move the cost somewhere else?
