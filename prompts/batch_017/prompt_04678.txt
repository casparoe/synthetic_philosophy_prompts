So I'm putting together a collection of short stories that each grow out of a specific case or thought experiment from philosophy of mind, and the one I'm stuck on right now is about split-brain patients. My writing group is sick of hearing about it at this point, but I can't let it go: people who had their corpus callosum cut as a last-resort epilepsy treatment, so that the two hemispheres literally can't pass information to each other anymore.

I went down a rabbit hole a couple months back reading about the old Sperry and Gazzaniga experiments — flashing a word or picture to just one half of someone's visual field and watching the hand on the opposite side write out or point to an answer that the talking half of the person swears up and down it never saw anything. There's the well-known bit about a patient's left hand buttoning a shirt while the right hand undoes the buttons right behind it, and I even came across a (maybe apocryphal, I honestly don't know) account of a man whose left hand grabbed his wife by the throat while his right hand had to wrestle it off her. What I actually want out of a story like this isn't the body horror angle though, its more about the same thing that got Nagel writing his brain bisection paper and got Parfit going on personal identity: once the wire's cut, is there one person left in there, two, or does the question itself just stop making sense?

Could you write the story for me? Something like 1500-2000 words, following a woman a few weeks out from the surgery who starts noticing her left hand doing small things she, the talking "she," didn't decide on — leaving notes, reaching for things before she's chosen to, whatever feels right — and who has to figure out whether to be frightened of that, grieve it, or treat it as company. Use the experiments I mentioned loosely, more as texture than as a script to follow, and please don't let it resolve into a neat "it was two people all along" or "nope, still just one" ending. I'd rather the uncertainty stayed load-bearing all the way through than get tidied up in the last paragraph.
