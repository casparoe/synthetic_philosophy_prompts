Had a long argument with my friend last night about whether stuff like day-trading or being a landlord counts as "unnatural" wealth-getting in Aristotle's sense, vs. a farmer selling off surplus wheat, which she insists he'd be totally fine with. We ended up digging up the actual passage in the Politics to settle it:

"Of everything which we possess there are two uses: both belong to the thing as such, but not in the same manner, for one is the proper, and the other the improper or secondary use of it. For example, a shoe is used for wear, and is used for exchange; both are uses of the shoe. He who gives a shoe in exchange for money or food to him who wants one, does indeed use the shoe as a shoe, but this is not its proper or primary purpose, for a shoe is not made to be an object of barter."

Aristotle then says barter that arises because some households have "too little" of one thing and "too much" of another is natural and blameless, and its only the later, money-based trade for profit that he actually condemns as unnatural. But if using a shoe to get money counts as an "improper" use of the shoe, isn't a farmer handing over surplus wheat for coin also using the wheat improperly, in exactly the same sense? My friend thinks there's obviously some other distinction doing the real work here and that I'm just being dense about it, but neither of us could actually spell out what it is. Can you actually explain what's doing the real work in separating "natural" from "unnatural" exchange for him, and whether the shoe/proper-use argument holds up once you notice this, or if Aristotle's just being inconsistent?
