So for my phil of logic seminar this term, this week's unit was on intuitionism, and the prof assigned the SEP entry on intuitionistic logic as background reading (I actually read the proof theory section for once instead of skimming straight to the philosophical upshot). The thing that's stuck with me is Glivenko's theorem: for propositional logic, A is classically provable iff ¬¬A is provable intuitionistically. But apparently that clean result doesnt survive the jump to predicate logic — you either have to bolt the "double negation shift" schema onto intuitionistic predicate logic, or use the full Gödel–Gentzen negative translation, which doesn't just slap two negations in front of everything but actually rewrites disjunction as a negated conjunction and the existential quantifier as a negated universal (so "there's an x such that A(x)" becomes something like "it's not the case that every x fails to satisfy A," rather than the plain double-negated original). Either way you land on classical and intuitionistic logic — and PA and HA — being equiconsistent (hopefully I'm not garbling this too badly).

In discussion section our TA connected this back to Quine's old line about deviant logicians "changing the subject": the idea that when the intuitionist rejects excluded middle, they're not actually disagreeing with the classicist, they just mean something different by the connectives, so there's no real dispute, just a difference in vocabulary. She floated the idea that Glivenko's theorem and the negative translation are basically the technical cash-value of that meaning-variance idea — everything the classicist can prove has a recoverable cousin on the intuitionistic side, so whats really left to fight about?

I want to see that argument actually made properly, as strong as it can be built: argue that Glivenko's theorem plus the negative translation support Quine's meaning-variance verdict, i.e. that the classical/intuitionist disagreement is merely notational/verbal rather than a real dispute over which inferences are good ones. But don't just breeze past the obvious hole here — the negative translation works by getting rid of ∨ and ∃ in favor of ¬, ∧, and ∀, so what gets "recovered" on the intuitionistic side for an existence claim is nowhere near a witness-producing proof, which was supposedly the whole point of caring about intuitionistic existence claims in the first place. So either show how the meaning-variance argument survives that complaint, or build the complaint into the argument itself — I don't want a version that just pretends ∨ and ∃ aren't an issue.
