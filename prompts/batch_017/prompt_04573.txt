I recently moderated a recorded conversation between a political theorist and a historian of the Cold War for a piece I'm writing on how officials and commentators talk about "misinformation." Off the record afterward, the two of them — whom I'll leave unnamed, since the exchange wasn't on tape — fell into a disagreement I haven't been able to settle myself, and I'd like a more rigorous outside opinion.

The theorist argued that Hannah Arendt's writing on lying in politics gives us a principled way to rank political falsehoods by severity. On her reading, a politician's calculated, self-serving lie about one embarrassing fact is categorically less dangerous than the mass-mediated "defactualization" Arendt diagnosed in her essay on the Pentagon Papers: a wholesale substitution of a manufactured story for the factual record, sustained by officials who eventually deceive themselves.

The historian objected that "self-deception" can't be the real dividing line, since Arendt herself treats it as too mild a diagnosis of what went wrong with the Vietnam-era policymakers: they weren't fooling themselves about facts so much as they had stopped asking factual questions at all, replacing judgment about events with calculations about which model fit the numbers. If even Arendt keeps reaching for a more extreme description, he argued, that suggests a continuum of increasing detachment from reality rather than two clean categories.

She answered that this concedes her real point rather than undermining it: the shift from weighing evidence to optimizing a model is exactly the qualitative break she meant. A fabricated story, however elaborate, stays hostage to facts that could surface and unravel it; a self-contained model, answerable only to what Arendt calls rational or statistical truth, is not. That, she said, is a difference in kind, not degree.

His last word was that even a genuine difference in kind at the level of an official's own psychology is useless to a contemporary observer, since a strategic lie and a model-driven fabrication sound identical from the outside; only historians working from documents years later, as with the Pentagon Papers themselves, can tell which one they were actually facing.

Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better argument at each stage, not just overall? I'd also like to know what, if anything, both of them are missing about how Arendt's distinction should actually guide how we judge political speech in real time, rather than in hindsight.
