I'm a novelist working on a novel-in-stories about the community of émigré scientists at Princeton's Institute for Advanced Study in the last years before Einstein's death, and I've reached the chapter I've been putting off: an imagined late-afternoon walk home from Fuld Hall between Einstein and Kurt Gödel, who were by then so inseparable that Oskar Morgenstern recalled Einstein confessing his own work scarcely mattered to him anymore, and that he still came to the Institute chiefly for the privilege of walking home with Gödel. I want the conversation on this particular walk to turn to whether anything of a person survives death.

I've been reading through Gödel's 1961 letters to his mother, Marianne, in which he sets out, with real argumentative care, why he thought personal existence must continue: the world, he insists, is demonstrably rational and ordered — this is what makes natural science possible at all — so it would be simply irrational for a being with a human's scope for intellectual and moral development to be cut off after actualizing some negligible fraction of that potential. He ties this to his independent conviction that materialism is false, a conviction he thought was reinforced, if not strictly entailed, by the either/or of his own incompleteness results: either the mind cannot be captured by any finite formal system, or mathematical objects exist mind-independently and are merely being discovered by us — and either horn, he thought, is fatal to a materialist picture of the world. Einstein, for his part, is on record over decades denying any belief in personal immortality, and describing his own faith as a Spinozist reverence for the impersonal, lawful order of the universe, not the kind of order that owes anyone a continued existence.

Could you write this as a dialogue between the two of them — mostly talk, minimal stage direction, long enough for the argument to actually develop rather than being gestured at? Neither man should serve as a mere mouthpiece for a position he simply asserts: Einstein should press on the real pressure points in Gödel's reasoning (why should a rationally ordered whole care about any individual's unfinished business, and why must "rationally ordered" entail "personally continuous" rather than something more impersonal?), and Gödel should have genuine resources to draw on in reply, including whatever he can make of his own incompleteness argument in this context, rather than simply restating his premises. I'd rather the scene end with both men a little unsettled by the other than with either side winning outright — and it should still read like two old friends talking on a walk, not like a staged debate.
