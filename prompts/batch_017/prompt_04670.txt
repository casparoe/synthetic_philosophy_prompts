Kurt Gödel's incompleteness theorems are famous; the argument he gave his mother, Marianne, for believing in an afterlife is not. Below is my reconstruction of that argument from his 1961 letters to her, together with a comparison of my own that I am not sure survives scrutiny. I would like comments on both.

The argument, as I read it:

(1) The world is rationally organized: nothing in it is arbitrary or pointless. Gödel takes the very possibility of natural science to confirm this, and treats it as extending Leibniz's principle of sufficient reason from causes to meaning, so that everything having a cause is matched by everything having a point. As he wrote to her, "if the world is rationally organised and has meaning, then it must be the case."

(2) Human life, taken on its own, sits oddly with this picture: a single lifetime gives a person only the barest fraction of the intellectual and personal development of which they are capable. Gödel's own image is of someone who lays the foundation for a house and then simply walks off the job, never to return.

(3) But a world that is rationally organized in the sense of (1) could not really contain this kind of waste of exactly the potential it took the trouble to create.

(4) So the potential described in (2) must be realized somewhere else — each of us persists, in some further state, beyond the death of the body.

What strikes me as most distinctive about the argument is that it does not deny how unfinished and often thwarted human life looks; it relies on that fact. It is precisely because a life's promise is so rarely cashed out in full that Gödel thinks reason demands a sequel.

I am tempted to close the piece by suggesting a family resemblance between this argument and Gödel's own incompleteness theorems — an otherwise well-behaved system whose consistency and point can only really be secured by appeal to something beyond itself. But I suspect that comparison trades on two quite different senses of "incomplete" and is closer to wordplay than to a genuine structural analogy.

Could you comment on: (a) whether the reconstruction above is a fair and accurate account of Gödel's actual reasoning, as opposed to a conflation with his better-known ontological proof of God's existence or with generic theism; (b) which of premises (1) through (3) you would target if you had to reject the conclusion while granting the rest; and (c) whether the closing comparison to the incompleteness theorems is doing any genuine philosophical work, or whether I should just cut it?
