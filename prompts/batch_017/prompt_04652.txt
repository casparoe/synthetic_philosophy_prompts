Six weeks ago, my co-founder and I unwound our partnership through a five-stage buyout clause that I've since realized is a real-life centipede game: at each stage, either of us could trigger a buyout at a fixed split of a jointly growing pot, and triggering it yourself always paid a little more than letting the other person trigger it at the next stage, even though passing all the way to the end would have left us both far better off than triggering it early. She triggered the buyout at the very first opportunity and defended this to me purely by backward induction: since I would rationally cash out at the last node, she must rationally cash out one node earlier, and so on back to the start.

I've since been reading into the so-called "backward induction paradox," and I want its strongest form argued out properly, not the familiar appeal to experiments in which people fail to defect immediately. Please write a dialogue between two game theorists: one who holds, in the spirit of Aumann's proof, that common knowledge of rationality forces mutual defection at the very first node because rationality must hold as a standing disposition even at nodes the game will never actually reach; and one who holds, in the spirit of the critique due to Binmore, Reny, and Pettit and Sugden, that this is incoherent, since it requires settled beliefs about how a rational opponent would behave at a node that, by the argument's own premises, a rational opponent would never let the game reach — beliefs an actual "deviation" gives every reason to revise. Have them engage with each other's real, strongest moves, including what it is rational to infer and do once someone has actually passed, rather than talk past one another, and don't let either side come off as the obvious strawman.
