Two friends of mine, both novelists, fell into an argument after one of them, Nadia, shared with the other the author's note she'd drafted for a new historical novel. It read, in part: "I've verified every historical fact in this book as carefully as I can and stand by each one; still, given how many hundreds of facts it contains, I'd be amazed if not one were wrong." Felix said this was self-undermining: believing each fact commits you to believing their conjunction, so believing, in addition, that one of them is false amounts to a contradiction. Nadia answered that by that logic, no historian could ever honestly publish a long book; justification for each claim needn't transfer to their conjunction, and flagging the expected error rate once is exactly what a preface is for. Felix replied that this move only works for something like a lottery, where the setup guarantees exactly one winner among, say, a thousand tickets -- nothing guarantees Nadia's book contains a single error, so she isn't facing a real paradox, just dressing up humility as one. Nadia answered that she doesn't need an exact count, only the rough, experience-based sense that books this long reliably contain a few mistakes, which is enough to expect one without doubting any particular fact. Neither budged, and I honestly couldn't tell you who's right. Could you go through their four moves in turn and say who has the better of it at each, flagging anything both of them may be missing? Then close with a one-line verdict: \verdict{Nadia} or \verdict{Felix}, whichever of them is more right overall.
