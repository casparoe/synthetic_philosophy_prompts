I'm taking a seminar this term, cross-listed between the philosophy and computer science departments, called "Minds, Models, and Machines," and this week's material has produced a genuine disagreement among my classmates that I'd like help sorting through. We've been covering mechanistic interpretability research on large language models, starting with the "superposition hypothesis": the idea that a network with n neurons can represent far more than n features by encoding them as almost-orthogonal directions in activation space, so that individual neurons end up firing for several unrelated concepts at once. We then looked at how Anthropic's sparse autoencoders are used to decompose this superposition into sparser, more human-interpretable "features," including the well-known case from Claude 3 Sonnet in which clamping the direction identified as the "Golden Gate Bridge" feature to roughly ten times its normal maximum activation made the model steer almost every conversation back to the bridge, regardless of topic.

During discussion, several classmates invoked Ian Hacking's manipulability criterion for scientific realism from Representing and Intervening—roughly, that if you can reliably manipulate something to produce a predicted effect, that is good evidence for its existence, whatever your view of its ultimate metaphysical status—and concluded that the Golden Gate Bridge case settles the question in favor of realism about these features. I'm not convinced this transfers so smoothly, mainly because of the "feature-splitting" phenomenon that Anthropic's own papers document: the very same activations get decomposed into different, non-equivalent families of features depending on the size of the dictionary the autoencoder is trained with, which makes me doubt there is a single privileged decomposition for a manipulability argument to be latching onto in the first place. For a short position paper, I want to push back on my classmates' inference. Please argue that we should remain anti-realists about sparse-autoencoder features—treating them as artifacts of the analytic method rather than as mind-independent constituents of the network's computation—even while granting that intervening on a feature like the Golden Gate Bridge direction produces reliable, predictable changes in model behavior.
