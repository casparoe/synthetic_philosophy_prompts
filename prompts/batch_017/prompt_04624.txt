Been down a bit of a rabbit hole this week that started with re-reading some old LessWrong reductionism posts (the ones about how there's no "wing" in the fundamental ontology, just quarks arranged wing-wise doing aerodynamic stuff at a higher level of description) and ended with me actually reading van Inwagen's Material Beings. Turns out theres a whole formal debate in metaphysics about basically this exact question, under the name "the Special Composition Question" (great name, very evocative) -- roughly, under what conditions do some things compose a further thing? My flatmate and I have basically turned into the two extreme camps on it while assembling a flat-pack shelf: I'm firmly in the nihilist camp (composition never happens, there's just simples "arranged shelf-wise"), and she's a died-in-the-wool universalist (any things whatsoever compose a further thing, no restriction needed -- screws, dust bunnies, and the Andromeda galaxy all included).

Could you write me a dialogue between the two of us, picking up mid-argument while we're still on the floor with the instructions -- so a committed mereological universalist vs. a committed mereological nihilist, arguing about whether the half-assembled pile of panels and screws in front of them counts as "a further thing" over and above its parts. I want it to escalate past the shelf pretty fast into the actual arguments people make in the literature: the universalist leaning on stuff like the argument from vagueness (why would there be some sharp point in the assembly process where composition suddenly switches on?) and Lewis's claim that unrestricted fusion is "ontologically innocent," while the nihilist pushes back with how weird the resulting ontology gets (feel free to bring up Lewis's own trout-turkey case) and defends "arranged X-wise" paraphrases as doing all the work ordinary object-talk needs without positing anything new. I don't want either side to be a strawman -- both of them have clearly done the reading, I want them actually responding to what the other just said rather than repeating talking points, and ideally by the end they've at least localized the disagreement more precisely (is this really about vagueness, or about what "exists" even means, or something else entirely?) rather than just agreeing to disagree. Doesn't need to resolve, just needs to feel like a real argument between two people who know what they're talking about.
