Been down a bit of an EA Forum rabbit hole this week. It started from a thread arguing about whether it's fine to just adopt GiveWell/Open Phil's cause prioritization wholesale instead of grinding through the underlying moral reasoning yourself, and someone in the comments pointed out that this is basically a special case of a broader puzzle philosophers apparently have a whole literature on: why does it feel totally normal to believe a bridge is safe because a structural engineer told you so, but wierdly off to believe factory farming is wrong just because a friend you trust told you so — even though in both cases you're outsourcing the actual reasoning to someone else? I'd always sort of assumed moral deference was fine by default (isn't "defer to people who've clearly thought about this more than you" like half of what makes any epistemic community function), so it was interesting to learn theres a whole camp of philosophers who think there's something specifically, distinctively bad about taking moral beliefs on pure say-so, over and above the generic risk of just being wrong. Kind of reminded me of the old modest-epistemology-vs-inside-view fights that used to run hot on LW, except this version is specifically about moral beliefs rather than empirical ones, which is apparently supposed to change everything, though I can't fully articulate why.

Anyway, I tried sketching out the main positions people take on this and immediately got stuck trying to figure out which ones I actually find persuasive versus which ones just sound clever, so I'd rather crowdsource it than litigate it with myself at 1am. Below are six stances on moral testimony/deference I've pieced together from various papers and comment threads. Use these exact strings as the items:
- "Full deference is fine": moral testimony is epistemically on par with any other testimony, and discomfort with it is just squeamishness with no principled basis.
- "Right act, wrong credit": you can permissibly act on someone else's say-so and even land on the true moral belief, but the act/belief lacks the special worth or credit that comes from understanding the reasons yourself.
- "Outsourcing agency": deferring specifically on moral questions (as opposed to empirical ones) cedes something essential to being a self-governing moral agent, in a way plain factual deference doesn't.
- "Defer on facts, not values": it's fine to take someone's word for the non-moral facts feeding into a moral question (e.g. how fish experience pain), but not fine to take their word for the evaluative upshot itself.
- "The asymmetry is an illusion": the apparent difference between moral and non-moral testimony is fully explained by confounds like stakes and verifiability, not by anything special about morality, so there's no real asymmetry to explain.
- "Just a 'who to trust' problem": deference is exactly as legitimate as your track record at identifying genuinely reliable testifiers, with no extra hurdle specific to moral content.

Not fishing for the "official" academic consensus here, if such a thing even exists — just want your actual ranked take. Ties within a tier are fine.
