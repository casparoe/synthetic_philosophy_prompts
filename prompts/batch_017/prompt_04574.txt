A friend and I spent a good chunk of last night's formal-epistemology reading group, and another hour over drinks afterward, arguing about van Fraassen's Judy Benjamin problem, and I don't think either of us moved the other an inch. It's a fairly obscure example even by the standards of probability kinematics, so let me lay out the setup before getting to where we disagree.

Judy Benjamin's patrol is dropped into a region split into Red and Blue Army territory, each further divided into a Headquarters Company Area and a Second Company Area, so her prior assigns probability 1/4 to each of the four resulting cells. A radio message then informs her that, although headquarters cannot say whether she has strayed into Red territory, if she has, the probability is 3/4 that she is in the Red Headquarters Company Area — at which point the radio cuts out. Van Fraassen shows that if Judy updates by minimizing relative information (relative entropy) with respect to her prior, subject to that single constraint — the very rule that, he points out, recovers ordinary conditionalization and Jeffrey's rule as special cases — her posterior credence that she is in the safer Blue territory actually rises, from 1/2 to about 0.532, even though the message seems to say nothing whatsoever about Blue territory. He calls this a glaring feature of the solution and half-suspects Judy of a kind of wishful thinking. He offers the case as evidence against minimizing relative information as the fully general rule for updating on this sort of conditional constraint — though in a note added after the fact, he also concedes that the result converges, in the limit, to what ordinary conditionalization on the corresponding extreme case would deliver.

My friend takes the shift toward Blue territory as a near-decisive reductio: no updating rule that lets a Blue-army probability move, and move even further as the stated danger of the Red Headquarters increases, in response to information that is ostensibly all about the internal Red/Red split deserves to be called rational. I'm less sure our shared intuition of "irrelevance" deserves that much authority, given that the constraint Judy receives only pins down the ratio between the two Red cells and leaves entirely open how much mass Red as a whole should have relative to Blue — that extra degree of freedom has to be resolved somehow. Could you help me build the strongest version of my side? Specifically, please argue that the shift toward Blue territory is not a defect but the better-motivated answer given the structure of the constraint, and that it is the "nothing about Blue should change" intuition, rather than the updating rule, that ought to be given up. I'd like the argument to actually engage with why that extra degree of freedom must be settled one way or another, rather than simply insisting that the mathematics is correct.
