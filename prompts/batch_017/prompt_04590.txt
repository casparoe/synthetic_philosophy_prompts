I'm drafting a novel whose central figure, a retired scholar of early Chinese philosophy, responds to her husband's death less like a conventional mourner and more like the Zhuangzi who is said to have drummed on a basin and sung after his own wife's death. At my manuscript group, two of my readers, Renata and Owen, fell into a genuine disagreement about whether the scene earns its philosophical claim, and I'd like a careful, move-by-move adjudication rather than a verdict on the novel itself.

(1) Renata argued that the scene skips the part of the parable that does the actual philosophical work: Zhuangzi tells Huizi that he grieved like anyone else when his wife first died, and only reached equanimity by reasoning through how she passed from formlessness to breath to body to life to death, the way the seasons turn. My character never grieves at all, so her calm reads as denial rather than earned insight.

(2) Owen answered that novels compress time constantly, and skipping the mourning is no different from cutting to the next scene; what matters is that the transformation argument itself appears on the page, not how much prior grief we're shown.

(3) Renata replied that the compression isn't neutral here, because the sequence is the argument. She also pointed out that elsewhere, when Huizi asks Zhuangzi whether a sage can really be without feeling, Zhuangzi says that's not his meaning at all -- only that a sage doesn't let likes and dislikes get in and do him harm. A character who feels nothing from the outset is closer to the unfeeling figure Zhuangzi is denying being than to the sage he actually describes.

(4) Owen countered that Renata is treating the book as one author's consistent position, when the wife's-death and deathbed scenes come from the Outer and Miscellaneous chapters, of disputed and probably composite authorship, while the passage about likes and dislikes comes from the Inner Chapters usually attributed to Zhuangzi himself -- so there's no guarantee they describe the same position at all.

Please go through this move by move, saying who has the better of it at each point rather than just who wins overall, and note anything about Zhuangzi's actual view of death and feeling that both readers seem to be missing.
