Georg Cantor did not think of himself merely as the inventor of transfinite set theory but as a kind of discoverer, even a messenger: he was convinced that the hierarchy of transfinite numbers had been communicated to him by God rather than invented by him. This underwrote a robust mathematical Platonism, on which questions like the continuum hypothesis (CH) — his conjecture that no infinite set of real numbers has a cardinality strictly between that of the natural numbers and that of the continuum — must have a determinate, discoverable answer. This theological commitment was not merely private: in an 1886 exchange of letters with Cardinal Johannes Franzelin, Cantor took pains to distinguish the "Absolute Infinite," which he identified with God and held to be strictly beyond mathematical comprehension, from the merely "transfinite," a lesser, created actual infinity that could be studied mathematically without theological danger. Cantor spent much of his later career trying, without success, to prove CH outright, and died in 1918 still believing the matter would eventually be settled one way or the other.

I've been sitting in on a graduate seminar on the history and philosophy of set theory, and this week's session ended on a question nobody in the room felt equipped to answer: what would Cantor have made of what actually became of his hypothesis? In 1963, Paul Cohen's method of forcing showed that CH cannot be proved from the standard axioms of set theory (ZFC), complementing Gödel's earlier result that it cannot be disproved from them either, so that CH turned out to be strictly independent of the axioms mathematicians actually use. More recently, some set theorists, Joel David Hamkins most prominently, have pushed further still, defending a "multiverse" picture on which there is no single privileged universe of sets for CH to be true or false in, so that the question may not have the kind of determinate answer Cantor was seeking at all. Drawing on what is known of Cantor's mathematical Platonism and his theologically inflected picture of the infinite, how do you think he would have reacted, first to Cohen's independence result and then to the multiverse view — as a vindication, a refutation, or something his framework could still absorb? Is there a version of Cantor's own commitments, theological or otherwise, that would let him respond to indeterminacy about CH without simply abandoning his life's project?
