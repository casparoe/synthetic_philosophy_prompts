I'm writing my IB Extended Essay in philosophy on Galen Strawson's "Basic Argument" against moral responsibility, and I keep finding that when I explain it to people, their first instinct is to reach for standard compatibilism without really engaging with what makes the argument distinctive. As I understand it, the argument runs roughly as follows: you do what you do because of the way you are; to be truly or ultimately responsible for what you do, you would therefore have to be truly responsible for the way you are, at least in the mental respects that matter; but you cannot be responsible for the way you are, since that would require having made yourself that way, which is impossible (nothing can be causa sui, and any story about how you shaped yourself just pushes the question back a step, generating a regress). Strawson is explicit that this conclusion is meant to hold whether determinism or indeterminism is true, since injecting some randomness into how your character or choices were formed would not make you any more the ultimate author of yourself than being fully determined would. When I tried this argument out on a friend from my debate team, who is a committed compatibilist, he kept insisting that all real moral responsibility requires is that your actions flow from your own values and are suitably responsive to reasons (he'd been reading about John Martin Fischer's work on this), and that the regress is only a problem for a kind of "ultimate" authorship that nobody actually needs. I wasn't able to pin down, in the moment, whether he had genuinely answered Strawson's point or had just quietly changed the subject to a thinner notion of responsibility.

Could you write a dialogue between two characters who each hold one of these positions and argue the point out properly, rather than past each other? One character should present the Basic Argument in something like its real logical structure, including the reply to the indeterminism objection, and should directly press the other on whether reasons-responsiveness (or a Frankfurt-style "mesh" between desires and higher-order desires) actually rebuts the regress or merely redefines responsibility so as to avoid it. The other character should give the strongest available compatibilist reply, with a genuine counter to the "change of subject" charge rather than simply reasserting that reasons-responsiveness is enough. Please keep both sides equally sharp, have them respond to each other's specific moves as the exchange develops, and don't feel obliged to resolve the disagreement neatly at the end, since I'm honestly not sure it has one.
