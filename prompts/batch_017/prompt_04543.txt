Me and a couple friends were arguing the other night about whether a good deed "counts" the same no matter why you do it. My friend's cousin donates a bunch of money every year but he's pretty open that its mostly so he can bring it up at parties, and my friend's take was that this shouldn't matter since people still got helped either way. I disagreed but couldn't really back it up beyond "it just feels different," which isn't much of an argument. Somebody on a philosophy subreddit told me this is basically the oldest question in ethics and pointed me toward Kant, so I dug up a free old translation of the Groundwork (the Abbott one) and read through the first section on my own.

The famous shopkeeper example made sense right away (not shortchanging a kid, but only because it's bad for business, versus not doing it because it's honest). But right after that Kant lays out these numbered "propositions" about duty, and the second and third ones kind of lost me. Here's the actual bit I'm stuck on:

"The second proposition is: That an action done from duty derives its moral worth, not from the purpose which is to be attained by it, but from the maxim by which it is determined, and therefore does not depend on the realization of the object of the action, but merely on the principle of volition by which the action has taken place, without regard to any object of desire. ... The third proposition, which is a consequence of the two preceding, I would express thus: Duty is the necessity of acting from respect for the law. ... Now an action done from duty must wholly exclude the influence of inclination and with it every object of the will, so that nothing remains which can determine the will except objectively the law, and subjectively pure respect for this practical law, and consequently the maxim that I should follow this law even to the thwarting of all my inclinations."

There's a footnote hanging off the word "maxim" right there too, which just says "A maxim is the subjective principle of volition" — that did not help me at all. Here's exactly where I get stuck: he says the moral worth can't come from "the purpose" of an action, it has to come from "the maxim" instead, but isn't a maxim just your own personal reason or plan for doing the thing, which sure sounds an awful lot like a purpose to me? And when he says the action has to "wholly exclude ... every object of the will," it starts to sound like he wants dutiful actions to have zero content or aim, just duty because duty, full stop, which seems like a weirdly empty thing to demand of a person. I also already sort of know, just from general osmosis and not from having actually read that far yet, that he eventually lands on "only act on maxims you could will to become universal law" — so there's clearly some connective tissue between "exclude every object of the will" and "universal law" that isn't showing up for me in this passage. Could you walk me through the logic of what's actually happening in this stretch of the argument, step by step, and tell me straight up whether my "this basically collapses into empty duty-for-duty's-sake with no real content" reaction is a fair objection, or whether I'm misreading him somewhere?
