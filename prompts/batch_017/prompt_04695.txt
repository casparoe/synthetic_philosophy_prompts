I'm taking a first-year Jurisprudence seminar, and I have a short paper due Friday on the Hart–Fuller exchange over the 1949 "grudge informer" case: in 1944, a German woman denounced her husband to the authorities for offhand remarks about Hitler he'd made while home on leave (she was also keen to be rid of him). A court-martial sentenced him to death under a 1934 statute, though he was ultimately sent to the front instead of executed. After the war, a German court convicted the wife herself of unlawfully depriving him of his liberty, holding that the wartime statute had been "contrary to the sound conscience and sense of justice of all decent human beings." Hart thought the court reached the right result the wrong way; Fuller thought Hart's own fix missed something more basic. Two people in my study group spent our whole session re-fighting this instead of outlining, and I'd like a second opinion before I commit five pages to a position.

(1) Owen, on Hart's side, said the postwar court should never have declared the statute devoid of legal force -- better to leave the wife unpunished, or have the legislature pass an openly retroactive statute and be honest that a real rule-of-law value was being sacrificed for a moral good, than to let judges quietly redefine "law" whenever its content offends them. (2) Naomi, on Fuller's side, answered that this wasn't a court voiding an ordinary bad law for its content: the tribunal that condemned the husband hadn't been applying pre-announced general rules to begin with, since a 1935 revision to the criminal code already let courts punish whatever deserved it "according to the healthy sentiment of the people" with no matching statute required, plus confidential ministry letters dictating verdicts in whole categories of cases. So there was a structural reason -- about institutional pedigree, not wickedness -- to doubt this was ever law in the relevant sense. (3) Owen replied that she still owes a threshold: every system has some gap between announced rule and practice, so unless she can say how much gap disqualifies a system without simply measuring how appalling the results are, her "structural" story is Hart's forbidden move by another name -- and by her own test, plenty of imperfectly administered systems closer to home would fail too. (4) Naomi's comeback was that the 1935 change wasn't slippage but an explicit abandonment of deciding cases by pre-existing rule at all, something identifiable without weighing anyone's wickedness -- and it cuts against Owen too: his own remedy has the postwar system doing exactly what he objects to in the original scheme, deciding the wife's fate under a rule that did not exist, in announced form, when she acted. His approach was more candid about it, she granted, but candour about breaking a rule-of-law principle isn't the same as not breaking one.

Neither budged. Could you go through moves (1) through (4) and say who has the better of each -- not just hand down an overall winner? I'm especially curious whether Naomi has found a way to say "this wasn't law" on grounds independent of moral judgment, as she claims, or whether Owen's disguise objection sinks her; and, separately, whether Owen's "at least be honest about the trade-off" proposal survives her closing point. If you think they're both missing some third way of handling the case, I'd rather hear that than be talked into picking a side.
