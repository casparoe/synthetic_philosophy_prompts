I got interested in the Church-Turing thesis a while back because of all the debate around whether large language models, or some future AI, could ever really "think" the way people do, and whether the brain itself is fundamentally just a very elaborate computer. I don't have any formal training in computer science or philosophy, so most of what I know comes from lecture videos, a couple of secondhand textbooks, and a lot of late nights following footnotes online. My understanding was that the Church-Turing thesis is a proven result establishing that a Turing machine can carry out any computation there is, and since an ordinary laptop is just a physical Turing machine, this settles, mathematically, that any physical process whatsoever, including whatever the brain does when it thinks, could in principle be reproduced by a computer given enough time and memory. That felt satisfying, like one of the few places where a huge question about the mind had actually been nailed down by a proof rather than left to endless argument.

Then I ran into Roger Penrose's argument that human mathematical insight involves something no algorithm could ever reproduce, which he connects to Gödel's incompleteness theorems and, more speculatively, to quantum effects in the brain. When I mentioned this to a friend who works as a software engineer, he said something like "well, it's called a thesis and not a theorem for a reason," but that felt like a throwaway line rather than a real answer, since plenty of unproven things are still treated as settled for all practical purposes. So I'd like to understand what's actually going on here: is Penrose, in effect, disputing something that has already been mathematically established about what physical systems like brains can do, or is there a more basic gap between what the Church-Turing thesis actually claims and the much larger claim, about brains and physical processes in general, that I'd been assuming it backed up?
