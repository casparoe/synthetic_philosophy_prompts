I've spent the last few months revising the closing chapters of a novel in which a minor but well-loved character dies quite suddenly, off the page, in a single understated line. When I circulated the draft to my writers' group, one member who isn't usually moved to tears by anything admitted she'd had to set the manuscript aside for a few minutes, then laughed at herself for getting choked up over "someone who was never real to begin with." Her offhand remark sent me to the philosophy section of my local library, where I came across what aestheticians call the paradox of fiction.

As I understand it, the puzzle runs as follows: we appear to have genuine emotional responses -- grief, fear, pity -- toward characters and events we know perfectly well to be invented, even though our ordinary understanding of emotion holds that being moved by something requires believing that it actually exists or happened. From what I can piece together, the field has more or less settled on the conclusion that this response, however universal and however useful to novelists, is technically irrational: a kind of misfire in our emotional machinery that fiction happens to be extremely good at triggering, even though none of the rational preconditions for the emotion are actually satisfied.

If that really is the settled state of play, I would like to understand what it implies for craft. Should I take it that techniques like free indirect discourse and close psychological narration, which exist specifically to stop a reader from registering the text as a construction, are ethically just a more refined way of exploiting that misfire -- so that scattering in distancing devices, such as an intrusive narrator or an occasional reminder that "this is, after all, only a story," would actually be the more honest choice for a novelist who doesn't want to capitalize on their readers' irrationality?
