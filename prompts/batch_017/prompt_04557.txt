I'm taking a seminar this term on the metaphysics of time and future contingents, and for my term paper I've been drafting an argument about the "thin red line" (TRL) treatment of Aristotle's sea battle. Below is the current draft of the paper's central section, which tries to press an objection against John MacFarlane's relativist semantics, offered as an alternative to the TRL. I'd be grateful for comments on three things especially: (1) whether I've represented the Belnap–Green objection and MacFarlane's view accurately enough for my argument to get off the ground; (2) whether the objection I raise against MacFarlane actually succeeds, or whether the reply I consider on his behalf already defuses it; and (3) whether this move already has a standard name or citation in the literature, since I'd rather engage with existing work than unknowingly reinvent it.

Aristotle's sea battle presents three costly options for anyone who wants to deny that the battle's contingency implies fatalism. One can give up excluded middle along with bivalence for future contingents; one can keep excluded middle while giving up bivalence, by supervaluating over the many branching histories consistent with the present; or one can keep both, by holding that among the many histories passing through the present moment, exactly one is metaphysically privileged as the one that will actually unfold. This last option is Ockhamism, and its "thin red line" version is the one on which the privileged history overlaps with the others up to the present moment rather than diverging from it entirely, in the style of Lewis's counterpart theory.

Belnap and Green's well-known objection (1994) is that this privilege is not available at the price advertised. If, at the very moment the sentence "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is uttered, there is already a fact about which of the many branches through that moment is the actual one, it is hard to see what is left of the thought that the other branches were ever really open. Calling them "possible" starts to look like a way of marking our ignorance of a settled matter rather than a way of marking a genuinely unsettled one—which is precisely the distinction indeterminism is supposed to draw.

MacFarlane's proposal is to keep the payoff of Ockhamism, a determinate truth value eventually, while dropping the requirement of a single history privileged across the whole model. The utterance is neither true nor false relative to a context of assessment located at the moment of utterance, but is true or false relative to a context of assessment located afterward, on whichever history that later assessment happens to occupy. Nothing in the model itself designates one history as "the" actual one; there is only ever truth-relative-to-a-history, evaluated from wherever the evaluator stands.

Here is my worry. Suppose the battle occurs, and a historian later says, "the claim was true—there really was going to be a battle." Relativism can honor this only in the relativized sense that the utterance comes out true relative to any assessment made from within the battle-history. What it seems not to honor is the stronger and, I think, more natural thought behind the historian's remark: that the original utterance was simply right, independently of anyone's later vantage point. To make sense of that thought seems to require some fact, not indexed to any assessor, about which branch time in fact took—and once that much is granted, it is unclear what distinguishes "the branch time in fact took" from "the thin red line," beyond a preference for introducing it downstream of the utterance rather than at the utterance itself.

The reply I keep circling back to is that this objection quietly assumes relative truth is a second-rate substitute for absolute truth, standing in need of some further, assessor-independent fact to back it up—and that this is exactly the assumption a relativist about future contingents is entitled to reject, on pain of begging the question against relativism as such. I find this reply plausible in the abstract, but I'm less sure it survives contact with this particular case, since branching-time models hand us an explicit picture of the alternatives in a way that, say, relativism about taste does not; it is very tempting, looking at the picture, to just point at a branch and ask whether it is the one that happened.

I'd welcome pushback on whether that last thought is doing any real philosophical work or is just an intuition pump dressed up as an argument.
