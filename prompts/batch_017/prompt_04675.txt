I'm working on a piece about functionalism in philosophy of mind — the idea that what makes something a "belief" or a "pain" isn't what it's made of but the causal role it plays, which is basically why people casually say a computer could in principle have mental states even though it's built out of silicon instead of neurons. A philosopher I interviewed for background mentioned, almost in passing, that Aristotle basically got to "multiple realizability" (the technical term for that substrate-independence idea) well over two thousand years before Hilary Putnam coined the phrase, and that Putnam himself later co-wrote an essay defending exactly that reading. So last night I dug up an old 1907 translation of De Anima (Hicks, I think), somewhere in book two, to find the actual passage everyone points to, and now I'm stuck on it — it's rough going:

"It has now been stated in general terms what soul is, namely, substance as notion or form. And this is the quiddity of such and such a body. Suppose, for example, that any instrument, say, an axe, were a natural body, its axeity would be its substance, would in fact be its soul. If this were taken away, it would cease, except in an equivocal sense, to be an axe. But the axe is after all an axe. For it is not of a body of this kind that the soul is the quiddity, that is, the notion or form, but of a natural body of a particular sort, having in itself the origination of motion and rest. Further, we must view our statement in the light of the parts of the body. For, if the eye were an animal, eyesight would be its soul, this being the substance as notion or form of the eye. The eye is the matter of eyesight, and in default of eyesight it is no longer an eye, except equivocally."

Could you actually unpack this for me? Two things specifically. First, just walk me through what work "axeity" and "quiddity" are doing here in plain English — what does Aristotle think literally happens to the axe, or the eye, once you take away its function. Second, and this the bit I actually need for the piece: does this passage really support the modern functionalist claim that the "form" is substrate-independent and could be realized in different materials, or does it maybe cut the other way, since Aristotle seems to insist the axe still has to be a particular kind of natural body to have that form at all, not just any material shaped like an axe? My source mumbled something about a whole fight over this between Nussbaum/Putnam and a guy named Burnyeat, but didn't have time to actually explain it. I'd love an answer that goes beyond the "Aristotle was basically a proto-functionalist" one-liner I keep running into everywhere.
