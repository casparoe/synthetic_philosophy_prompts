Ok so over winter break I fell into this whole rabbit hole about whether AI can actually "think" (mostly because people on my robotics team won't shut up about whether LLMs are really reasoning or just doing pattern matching), and it led me to this old argument called the Lucas-Penrose thing. The simple version, which is the one everybody quotes, goes: for any consistent formal system or machine S there's a "Gödel sentence" G(S) that basically says "I can't be proven inside S," and S itself can never prove it, but a person looking at S from the outside can just see G(S) has to be true. So there's something a mind can do that no single machine can, which means minds can't just be Turing machines. I also immediately found the classic comeback: how do you know that YOU are consistent? Gödel's second theorem says a system can't prove its own consistency from inside itself, so it looks like humans are stuck in exactly the same boat as the machine.

Here's the part that's actually been bugging me though. I checked Shadows of the Mind out of the library (skimmed most of it, not gonna lie) and it turns out Penrose knows about that comeback and builds this weirder, fancier version of the argument specifically to dodge it. From what I can piece together it goes: suppose your reasoning really is captured by some formal system F. Since you know your sound, you know F is sound, so the slightly bigger system F' (just F plus the extra belief "I am F") is sound too. But then you can see that G(F'), the Gödel sentence belonging to F' itself, is true -- which is exactly the one thing F' itself can never prove. And since you literally ARE F' under the assumption, that's a straight up contradiction, so F could never have captured your reasoning in the first place, no matter which F you picked. The whole appeal is supposedly that, unlike the simple version, it doesn't need you to independently verify your own consistency first -- the contradiction just falls out once you assume you're some formal system at all.

Except then I found someone arguing in a forum thread (which I now cannot find again to save my life) that this whole F/F' maneuver doesn't actually buy Penrose anything new, that it just relocates the exact same "how do you know you're sound" assumption to a sneakier spot in step 2, where it's harder to notice. I brought this up with my math teacher and she just kind of blinked at me, so now I'm stuck with it on my own. Could you actually build the strongest possible case FOR that criticism -- not a wishy-washy "well there's something to both sides" answer, but an actual walkthrough of exactly where in the F/F' setup the old consistency problem creeps back in, and why relabeling it as "F is sound" instead of "I'm consistent" doesn't really get Penrose out of the original hole. If there's a genuinely good comeback he could make, feel free to mention it at the end, but I want the actual case made first.
