I'm on my school's policy debate team, and one of the resolutions we're prepping for an upcoming tournament asks whether the United States and China should accept binding limits on frontier AI development, including export controls on advanced chips (sometimes called "compute governance") meant to slow down or steer the technology. While digging for historical framing, one of my teammates found that John von Neumann gets invoked constantly in AI-safety writing, largely because Stanislaw Ulam, in a tribute written the year after von Neumann's death, recalled him musing that accelerating technological change seemed to be heading toward something like an "essential singularity." My teammate wants to cite von Neumann as an intellectual forerunner of the AI-safety side of our resolution, but when I actually read the 1955 Fortune essay he wrote not long before his death, "Can We Survive Technology?," the picture seemed a lot messier than that.

In that essay, von Neumann argues that trying to inhibit or separate out the dangerous parts of a technology from its beneficial parts is essentially hopeless, since useful and harmful applications lie so close together that, in his words, it is never possible to "separate the lions from the lambs," and any success in doing so is temporary at best. His actual proposed answer to the crisis is not any kind of control regime but a much vaguer appeal to "human qualities" — patience, flexibility, and day-to-day improvisation rather than a permanent fix. Yet this is the same man who, as a member of the Atomic Energy Commission and a longtime RAND consultant, had a reputation as one of the most hawkish voices in Cold War nuclear strategy, someone who trusted American technological superiority far more than any negotiated restraint on a rival power.

Given that combination, I don't think it's obvious which side of our resolution von Neumann would actually land on, and I'd rather work through the reasoning carefully than just repeat the line that "he predicted the singularity, so he'd back AI safety," which feels like it skips the hard part. So: would von Neumann treat chip export controls as a legitimate instance of the kind of temporary, partial restraint his essay seems to allow for? Or would he dismiss them as exactly the sort of doomed attempt to separate good technology from bad that he thought never really works — in which case, would his Cold War instincts push him toward favoring an unrestrained American race for AI dominance instead? Please build your answer around the specific tension between these two sides of his thinking, rather than resting on either one by itself.
