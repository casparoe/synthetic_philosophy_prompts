At a philosophy reading group, two members fell into an argument after finishing the chapter on Nozick's experience machine. (1) Naomi said the thought experiment succeeds against hedonism: an overwhelming majority of people, when asked, say they would not plug in, which shows that we care about actually being a certain way and doing certain things, not merely about how our experience feels from the inside. (2) Marcus objected that this reads too much into the data: when subjects are instead asked to imagine they are already living inside the machine and are offered the chance to unplug into reality, only a small minority choose to leave. Since the "stay put" answer wins either way, he argued, the aversion to plugging in is best explained by a garden-variety status quo bias -- a preference for wherever one already happens to be -- rather than by any real verdict about the prudential value of reality. (3) Naomi granted that the bias is real but said it can't carry all the weight Marcus puts on it, because some redesigned versions of the thought experiment never ask anyone to imagine changing their own situation at all: subjects are simply asked to compare two already-completed, experientially identical lives, one lived in touch with reality and one lived inside the machine, and reality still wins by a wide margin. Since status quo bias has nothing to grip onto in a third-person comparison like that, she concluded, the preference for reality survives its best debunking explanation. (4) Marcus replied that this redesigned version has a different problem: since no one is fully certain hedonism is true, a rational chooser should prefer the reality-connected life anyway, purely as a free hedge against being wrong about which theory of well-being is correct -- so the result shows only that people hedge under uncertainty, not that they regard reality as intrinsically part of a good life.
