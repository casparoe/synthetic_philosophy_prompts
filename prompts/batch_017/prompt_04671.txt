I'm about two-thirds of the way through a novel set in Harlem between 1926 and 1928, in which my protagonist -- a young writer -- has two older mentors who are transparent stand-ins for W. E. B. Du Bois and Alain Locke, and who keep clashing over what her fiction is actually for. Before I put words in my own invented characters' mouths, I want to see the real disagreement staged properly between the two men themselves, because every secondary account I've read compresses it into the same tired opposition -- "political art" versus "art for art's sake" -- that I suspect neither man would have accepted as a fair description of his own position.

Could you write me that dialogue? I'd like it anchored specifically in Du Bois's 1926 essay "Criteria of Negro Art," including his notorious insistence that all art is already propaganda whether or not it admits to being so, and in Locke's answer to that whole way of thinking, made most explicit in his own 1928 essay "Art or Propaganda?," built around the artist's individual self-expression and universal human appeal rather than didactic racial representation. I don't want either man reduced to a mouthpiece for a position I've already decided on: let Locke press Du Bois on what is actually lost, artistically and even politically, when Black art is made instrumental to racial uplift, and let Du Bois answer him on his own terms rather than simply repeating the "propaganda" line back at him -- he was, after all, quite capable of anticipating and rebutting exactly this kind of objection.

If it feels natural, have the two of them test their principles against a concrete case, such as a novel like Claude McKay's Home to Harlem, with its unvarnished picture of Harlem's cabarets and underclass, which was already dividing critics along similar lines by the time these essays were written. I'm not looking for either man to win outright -- I'd rather the disagreement stay genuinely live, the way it must have felt at the time. A page or so is plenty; I mainly need the philosophical spine right, since I'll dress it in period voice and stage business myself later.
