In a course on the philosophical side of theoretical computer science, I came across a distinction that has stuck with me: a problem being hard to solve, it turns out, does not by itself guarantee that the hardness is good for anything. A one-way function—easy to compute, but infeasible to invert on typical inputs—is what modern cryptography actually depends on, and its existence is a strictly stronger assumption than the mere claim that hard problems exist at all. In a well-known 1995 survey, Russell Impagliazzo described five hypothetical worlds, of increasing friendliness to cryptography, corresponding to how these two assumptions might actually play out: Algorithmica, Heuristica, Pessiland, Minicrypt, and Cryptomania. The bleakest of the five, Pessiland, is a world where hard problems remain genuinely hard even in the typical case, so real difficulty and real effort are unavoidable, and yet no one-way functions exist either, so none of that hardness can ever be turned into a secret—whatever one person can efficiently disguise, another could, on average, efficiently uncover. Nobody has ever proven which of these five worlds we actually inhabit; modern cryptography is, in effect, a bet that we live in the friendliest one.

Write a short story set in a society that has become convinced—through whatever means you find dramatically credible, whether a mathematical proof, a long history of failed ciphers, or something stranger—that it lives in Pessiland. Take the premise seriously rather than treating it as mere set dressing: this should be a world in which mastery, achievement, and struggle remain completely genuine, since nothing can be shortcut, but in which privacy in any deep sense has become structurally impossible, since no cipher, diary, or secret plan can be built that sufficiently persistent effort could not eventually undo. You might center the story on a cryptographer, a pair of lovers, a government keeping (or failing to keep) a secret, or another character entirely; what matters is that the specific, counterintuitive shape of this world—hardship with no possibility of security—is what drives the plot and its emotional stakes, rather than a more generic story about surveillance or the end of privacy.
