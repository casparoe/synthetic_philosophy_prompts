I'm working on a column about how older philosophical traditions handled the fear of death, and I've drafted a section on Zhuangzi that I'd like you to look over before I go further. Rather than lean on the well-worn scene of him drumming on a basin after his wife dies, I've built the passage around two less-cited vignettes from the sixth of the Inner Chapters. Here's the draft:

"Zhuangzi's sixth chapter introduces Ziyu, a man whose illness has left him grotesquely hunched, his organs squeezed up into his chest, his chin bent down toward his navel. Examining his reflection in a well, he tells his friend Zisi that he isn't troubled: if the process that shapes all things turns his left arm into a rooster, he'll use it to mark the hours of the night; if it turns his right arm into a crossbow, he'll shoot down an owl for the pot; if it turns his tailbone into a wheel and his spirit into a horse, he'll simply climb aboard and ask for no better mount. A few lines later, his friend Zilai is dying while his family wails around him. Another friend, Zili, hushes them, then turns to the dying man and asks, almost teasingly, what he'll be remade into next — a rat's liver, perhaps, or the arm of an insect. Zilai's answer is that he will follow the transformation exactly as a son follows a parent's command — Yin and Yang, he says, matter more to him than his actual parents do — since molten ore doesn't get to leap up out of the crucible and insist on becoming a famous sword. As he puts it himself, in one standard translation, 'what has made my life a good will make my death also a good.'

It's tempting to read all this as an early Chinese cousin of the Stoic amor fati: stop resisting what you can't control, and your suffering eases. But Zhuangzi seems to be after something more than resignation — an almost cheerful readiness to become whatever comes next, rooster-arm included, on the grounds that the boundary we draw around a single continuous 'self' was only ever a convenient fiction."

Could you comment on three things: whether I've represented Ziyu's and Zilai's speeches accurately and in proportion; whether the Stoic comparison illuminates Zhuangzi's position or flattens what's distinctive about it; and whether my closing claim — that Zhuangzi is describing something stronger than mere equanimity — is a reading scholars would actually defend, or whether I'm smoothing over real tensions elsewhere in the text (moments where a life cut short by violence or misfortune seems to be treated as a genuine loss) that a careful reader would expect me to address?
