I've spent a lot of time over the past couple of years reading the mechanistic interpretability threads on the Alignment Forum and LessWrong, and this same disagreement came up again just last week in my old philosophy-of-mind reading group, where a friend insisted the whole debate is confused. Interpretability researchers, working under the now more or less standard "linear representation hypothesis" (the claim that a network's features are directions in activation space), talk about "features" and "circuits" as things they discover inside trained networks: the Golden Gate Bridge feature and the sycophantic-praise feature from Anthropic's monosemanticity work, or the curve detectors and other low-level features that recur across independently trained models (the "universality" result from the old Circuits thread). When steering or clamping one of these directions changes model behavior exactly as predicted, researchers tend to take that as confirmation that the feature was really there all along, doing genuine explanatory work — a network-level analogue of finding orientation-tuned cells in visual cortex.

But there is also a serious deflationary case. Superposition means individual neurons are polysemantic because networks try to represent more features than a layer has dimensions to spare; sparse autoencoders are the standard fix, but a recent paper — pointedly titled "Sparse Autoencoders Do Not Find Canonical Units of Analysis" — shows that a small SAE's feature list is incomplete relative to a larger one's, and, using what it calls "meta-SAEs," that even individual features aren't atomic: a latent that looks like it just means "Einstein" turns out to decompose into more basic latents like "scientist," "Germany," and "famous person." There is also a track record of apparently clean single-neuron or single-direction "explanations" turning out, on closer causal testing, to have been tracking something quite different from whatever they were named after — the original "interpretability illusion" result on BERT is the classic case. On this view, "feature" talk looks less like a discovery of joints the network was already carved at, and more like a useful, non-canonical redescription that we impose on the activations from outside, with no fact of the matter about the "right" level of granularity.

I'd like you to write an essay that takes an actual side on this rather than surveying it politely. You're welcome to draw on older debates in philosophy of mind. Dennett's instrumentalism about the intentional stance — and specifically his idea in "Real Patterns" that a pattern is real just insofar as describing data in its terms compresses the data more efficiently than the bit map does — seems especially apt given that a sparse autoencoder is quite literally a compression scheme, though you might instead want to defend something closer to Fodor-style realism about representational content. Weigh the strongest form of each side (universality and successful causal interventions for realism; incompleteness, non-atomicity, and interpretability illusions against it), but don't hedge at the end. Defend a real verdict about whether, and in what sense, neural networks have representations — a qualified verdict is fine (realist about some posits and deflationary about others, say), but "reasonable people disagree" is not an acceptable ending.
