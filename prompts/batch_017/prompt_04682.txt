I am a PhD student in philosophy working on moral responsibility, and for a dissertation chapter I need the strongest possible defense of a view that most of the recent literature treats as refuted. The standard "dilemma defense" (due to Robert Kane and David Widerker) against Frankfurt-style counterexamples to the principle of alternative possibilities (PAP) holds that any case built on a prior sign either makes that sign causally sufficient for the agent's choice, thereby assuming determinism and begging the question against the libertarian, or leaves it causally insufficient, in which case a genuine alternative possibility remains open. Cases designed to escape this dilemma altogether — Mele and Robb's case, which pairs the agent's indeterministic deliberation with a simultaneous deterministic backup process; Hunt's blockage case; and Pereboom's Tax Evasion case, where the prior sign is necessary but not sufficient for choosing otherwise — are generally treated as having rehabilitated the Frankfurt strategy. Please argue that none of them actually succeeds, because in each case some alternative possibility survives that is robust enough, in John Martin Fischer's technical sense of "robust," to ground the agent's moral responsibility, so that the dilemma defense's underlying insight has not been overturned.
