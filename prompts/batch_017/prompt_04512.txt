A friend and I spent much of last night arguing over a part of Turing's 1950 paper that almost never comes up in discussions of the Turing test: the ninth objection he considers, "The Argument from Extrasensory Perception." Turing worried that a telepathic or clairvoyant human witness could give the game away by outguessing a machine at identifying the suit of a hidden playing card, and he even entertained the thought that a psychokinetic interrogator might bias a machine's internal random-number generator. His proposed fix, offered almost as an afterthought, was to run the imitation game inside a "telepathy-proof room." My friend maintains that this fix is adequate, at least in principle, to neutralize each of the four faculties Turing names at the outset of the passage: telepathy, clairvoyance, precognition, and psychokinesis. I disagree: I think at least one of the four poses a difficulty that no amount of shielding could ever fix. Argue for my side of the dispute: identify the faculty I have in mind, and explain why no telepathy-proof room, however well built, could neutralize it in the way Turing's argument requires.
