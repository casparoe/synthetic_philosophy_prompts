I don't have any kind of philosophy background, I just like going down rabbit holes with old texts when I can't sleep, and I've been stuck on George Berkeley for like two weeks now. Everyone who's ever taken a philosophy 101 class (which I haven't, but you pick this stuff up online) knows the famous idealism thing: esse est percipi, to be is to be perceived, there's no "matter" sitting behind our sensations holding everything up, objects are just bundles of ideas, and it all keeps existing when nobody's looking because God's mind never blinks. Fine, weird, but I get the logic of it.

Then totally separately, I got into an MMT/history-of-money youtube rabbit hole and found out this exact same Berkeley wrote this strange little book in the 1730s called The Querist -- its literally just a few hundred numbered yes-or-no questions, trying to fix Ireland's economy. And one query just stopped me cold: he asks whether "the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter." Basically, money isn't some special stuff with value baked into it, it's just a token for tracking and moving around the real wealth, which he says elsewhere is labour, calling it (paraphrasing a bit) the true source of wealth, not gold or silver sitting in a vault somewhere.

I can't shake the feeling he's doing the exact same trick twice: "stop assuming there's some hidden essence backing up the thing you're looking at -- there isn't one, everything real about it is already right there in how it's used, perceived, and related to other things." Matter is to ideas what gold is to paper tickets, kind of. I mentioned this to a friend who actually studied philosophy in college and she said I was probably just pattern-matching two things that rhyme rhetorically without them actually being the same type of claim. So I want you to settle it for me: make the strongest case you honestly can that Berkeley's theory of money in the Querist really is an application of the same underlying philosophical move as his immaterialism, and not just a cute surface-level echo -- actually engage with the best version of my friend's objection instead of dodging it -- and then land on a real verdict, not some wishy-washy "well there are similarities and differences" cop-out.
