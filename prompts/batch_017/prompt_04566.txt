I'm a retired geophysicist (spent about three and a half decades doing seismic inversion, of all things), and in retirement I've fallen into a philosophy-of-language rabbit hole -- currently chewing through a photocopy of Dorothy Edgington's old essay "On Conditionals" that a friend from my reading group lent me. That's how I ran into David Lewis's famous 1976 triviality result about indicative conditionals. I get the shape of the claim: if you want a conditional connective ">" to denote an honest-to-goodness proposition (so it lives in the same probability space as everything else, per Stalnaker's picture) AND you also want P(A > B) to always equal the conditional probability P(B|A) -- the so-called "Equation," or Adams's Thesis -- then Lewis shows your probability function has to collapse into something almost trivial, with hardly any distinct values left. What I can't get is an intuitive feel for why forcing that identity has to be so destructive. Every proof I've seen partitions things into A&B, A&not-B, not-A and then starts conditionalizing on the conditional itself, and by the time I reach the punchline I've kind of lost the thread and am just taking it on faith rather than seeing it.

Could you build me some real intuition here, ideally walking through a small toy case (four or five possible worlds is plenty) where I can actually watch the Equation strangle the probability function, instead of just quoting the general algebra at me? And once that part clicks, I'd love your take on the aftermath: as I understand it, Adams himself dodges the whole result by denying conditionals are proposition-shaped things to begin with (so there's nothing for the Equation to be "about"), while McGee built fancier probability models where the Equation survives for simple conditionals but only by refusing to hand out sensible probabilities for compounds or nestings of them, and I gather Kaufmann later tried to push that project further to cover more of those compound cases. I also have some vague memory of "Popper functions" showing up in this neighborhood, though that might actually be Van Fraasen's move and I'm mixing my names up. Of these responses, which one do you think genuinely earns its keep as a real solution, versus just being a polite way of changing the subject?
