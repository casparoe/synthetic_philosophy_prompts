I'm taking a philosophy of medicine seminar this term, and I have a short response paper due on this week's unit, which covers the naturalism/normativism debate over how to define health and disease. We've spent most of our time on Christopher Boorse's biostatistical theory (BST), which analyzes disease as a part or process making a statistically subnormal contribution to survival and reproduction relative to an age- and sex-specific reference class; Boorse insists this makes disease attributions fully value-free and naturalistic. As a foil, we've also read Jerome Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis (HDA), which instead makes disorder depend on two separate ingredients: a dysfunction, meaning an internal mechanism's failure to do what it was designed by natural selection to do, plus a further judgment that this failure actually harms the person, assessed against prevailing social standards.

A footnote in one of our secondary readings mentioned a case I can't stop thinking about: commensal viruses, which infect host cells and hijack their replicative machinery in essentially the same way disease-causing viruses do, yet are classified by virologists as compatible with health rather than as diseases, apparently just because they cause no harm to the host. I'd like to use this case to stress-test Boorse's and Wakefield's theories against each other before I commit to a thesis. First, does Boorse's BST actually have a principled, purely statistical basis for calling such an infection healthy, or does reaching the intuitively correct verdict quietly require smuggling in something that functions just like a harm judgment? Second, on Wakefield's side, if the dysfunction condition is satisfied identically in the commensal and the pathogenic cases — cellular machinery gets hijacked either way — doesn't that mean the harm criterion is doing essentially all of the classificatory work, leaving the supposedly naturalistic half of his hybrid analysis idle in this instance? I'm ultimately trying to work out whether either theory delivers on its central promise: full value-freedom for Boorse, or a genuine synthesis of naturalist and normativist insights for Wakefield. Or do both end up relying on the same kind of evaluative judgment, just relocated to a different part of the analysis? Which theory, if either, comes closer to escaping this problem, and why?
