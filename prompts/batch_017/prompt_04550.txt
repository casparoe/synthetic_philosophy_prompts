I'm taking a philosophy of religion seminar this term, and this week's session is devoted to Hume's essay "Of Miracles" (Section X of *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*). We're reading it in Peter Millican's Oxford World's Classics edition, which numbers paragraphs rather than pages, so I'll cite it that way below. I've now read Part I of the essay several times and keep getting stuck on what looks like a shift in how Hume uses the word "proof" between his opening statement of epistemic principles and the argument he actually runs a few paragraphs later.

Early on, he lays out a distinction between two grades of evidence:

"A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence. In such conclusions as are founded on an infallible experience, he expects the event with the last degree of assurance, and regards his past experience as a full proof of the future existence of that event. In other cases, he proceeds with more caution... and when at last he fixes his judgment, the evidence exceeds not what we properly call probability." (E 10.4)

A few paragraphs later, having introduced the case of a witness reporting a genuine miracle, he writes:

"...and suppose also, that the testimony, considered apart and in itself, amounts to an entire proof; in that case, there is proof against proof, of which the strongest must prevail, but still with a diminution of its force, in proportion to that of its antagonist." (E 10.11)

"...there is here a direct and full proof, from the nature of the fact, against the existence of any miracle; nor can such a proof be destroyed, or the miracle rendered credible, but by an opposite proof, which is superior." (E 10.12)

"The plain consequence is (and it is a general maxim worthy of our attention), 'That no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish: And even in that case there is a mutual destruction of arguments, and the superior only gives us an assurance suitable to that degree of force, which remains, after deducting the inferior.' ... I weigh the one miracle against the other; and according to the superiority, which I discover, I pronounce my decision, and always reject the greater miracle." (E 10.13)

Here is my difficulty. In paragraph 4, "proof" names a fixed, maximal category: it's what uniform experience yields, and it is explicitly contrasted with "probability," which alone comes in degrees because it rests on a mixture of favorable and unfavorable instances. Yet from paragraph 11 onward, Hume treats two rival "entire" proofs as quantities of variable strength that can be set against each other, so that one turns out "superior," the other "inferior," and even the winning proof suffers, in his own words, "a diminution of its force." That looks to be in tension with the degree-free, maximal notion of proof defined back in paragraph 4: if two pieces of evidence are each already "entire" and leave no room for doubt, it's unclear what exactly is left to be measured when we set them against one another. Is there a reading of "proof" across these paragraphs that doesn't convict Hume of simply equivocating? I notice that Hume introduces the whole scenario with "let us suppose," and that in Part II he goes on to insist that no actual testimony for a miracle has ever amounted even to a probability, "much less to a proof" — so perhaps the "proof versus proof" case is only a deliberately generous concession for the sake of argument, and not a situation he thinks ever really arises. But even granting that, I'm not sure it dissolves the puzzle of how two pieces of evidence that are each supposed to be "entire" could still come apart in strength. I'd appreciate help thinking through what's actually going on logically in this stretch of the text — a few of us argued about it after class without landing on an answer we all found satisfying.
