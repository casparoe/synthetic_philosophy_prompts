I'm a retired physicist who's finally gotten round to reading through the philosophy classics I always meant to get to. Right now I'm stuck on a bit of Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, Book V, ch. 5 (somewhere around 1133a-b, though my copy doesn't number things finely enough for me to pin it down exactly), on justice in exchange. Here's the passage that's tripping me up (Ross translation):

"All goods must therefore be measured by some one thing, as we said before. Now this unit is in truth demand, which holds all things together (for if men did not need one another's goods at all, or did not need them equally, there would be either no exchange or not the same exchange); but money has become by convention a sort of representative of demand; and this is why it has the name 'money' (nomisma)-because it exists not by nature but by law (nomos) and it is in our power to change it and make it useless."

And a little further on, after some stuff about houses and beds:

"Money, then, acting as a measure, makes goods commensurate and equates them; for neither would there have been association if there were not exchange, nor exchange if there were not equality, nor equality if there were not commensurability. Now in truth it is impossible that things differing so much should become commensurate, but with reference to demand they may become so sufficiently."

As an old lab guy, "measure" and "commensurate" are words I take pretty seriously - two quantities either share a unit or they don't, theres no "sufficiently commensurate" in physics. So I can't work out what Aristotle thinks money actually is here. Is it a genuine unit, like a measuring rod for value, that really makes a house and a pile of shoes comparable? Or is the "real" unit demand, which itself resists measurement, making money just a conventional stand-in for something intrinsically unmeasurable - in which case isn't he undercutting his own point about commensurability right there in that last line? Could you walk me through the logic step by step, and maybe say a word on why this bit still gets fought over by translators and commentators?
