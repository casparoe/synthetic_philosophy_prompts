I'm a second-year philosophy PhD student, and a few of us in the department have kept an informal reading group going this term on modal metaphysics. We recently spent two sessions on David Lewis's counterpart theory, working through "Counterparts or Double Lives?" in On the Plurality of Worlds alongside Kripke's original remarks on the Humphrey objection in Naming and Necessity, and afterward an argument broke out between two of the regulars, Marcus and Ines, that I haven't been able to let go of. Neither of them specializes in modality, but they've apparently been circling this dispute for weeks, and by the end of the most recent round neither had conceded a point. I work on philosophy of mind myself, so I don't entirely trust my own instincts about who had the better of it, and I'd like a more careful, move-by-move adjudication than I can give.

The exchange went roughly like this. (1) Marcus opened by granting that Lewis can answer the "easy" half of the Humphrey objection: since "Humphrey might have won" is analyzed as Humphrey's having a counterpart who won, and having a winning counterpart is a genuine, if relational, fact about Humphrey, the statement does turn out to be about Humphrey after all. But, he insisted, this leaves the harder half untouched: our modal concern is supposed to track some way Humphrey himself could have been, not a numerically distinct individual's victory that gets attributed to Humphrey only courtesy of a similarity relation. (2) Ines replied that this just restates the intuition instead of arguing for it, since any reductive analysis can be resisted the same way, by insisting the analysans doesn't "really" capture the analysandum and calling the leftover a brute intuition. She pointed to the stage view of personal identity, on which what makes a future stage "mine" is itself just a matter of a temporal counterpart relation to my present stage, and asked why ordinary self-concern about the future is supposed to survive that reduction while ordinary concern about Humphrey's prospects can't survive the modal analogue. (3) Marcus granted the parallel's force but pressed a disanalogy: stages related by a temporal counterpart relation are still stages of a single spacetime worm, tied together by causal dependence and qualitative continuity within one world, whereas Lewis's worlds don't stand in spatiotemporal or causal relations to one another at all. So temporal counterpart theory trades on a real intra-world unity that modal counterpart theory has no access to, which is presumably why Lewis treats the "aboutness" half of Kripke's objection as easily answered but calls the residual worry about de re representation genuinely troublesome, tying it to the separate dispute about haecceitism rather than dismissing it. (4) Ines conceded the disanalogy but turned it around: granting that counterpart theory owes us more here doesn't show that transworld identity is in better shape, since Kripke buys his "unity" by stipulation rather than by explaining it. Being told to simply stipulate which possible Humphrey is under discussion is not an account of transworld sameness, and it isn't obvious that stipulated sameness does any more explanatory work than resemblance. (5) Marcus answered that this conflates two different projects: Kripke isn't offering a metaphysical account of what transworld identity consists in, but making the metasemantic point that reference to Humphrey under a counterfactual supposition was never secured by qualitative identification to begin with, so no such account is owed him, and an objection to counterpart theory doesn't need a rival positive theory in hand to succeed. Neither of them moved from there.
