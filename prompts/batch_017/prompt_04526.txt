So this happened about three weeks ago and I'm still kind of stuck on it. My grandma passed away in March, and while we were sorting out her estate we got everything appraised, including this small oil painting that's hung over her dining table my entire life. Family legend always had it down as a genuine 17th-century Dutch piece, "circle of" some minor master, never a huge name but still supposedly worth real money. The appraiser called back last week and said it's actually a much later imitation, probably painted a hundred-plus years after the fact specifically to pass as the real thing. Same canvas, same paint, same everything - but the estimated value dropped by like 95% overnight. My brother wants to sell it anyway "for what its worth now," my mom wants to keep it because she just likes looking at it, and I've become weirdly obsessed with the fact that literally zero atoms of the object changed between the day before that phone call and the day after, and yet everyone's relationship to it (mine included) completely flipped.

Naturally I fell down a rabbit hole reading old philosophy-of-art essays about this, starting with Denis Dutton's "Artistic Crimes" piece (it's free online) and then chasing footnotes into Nelson Goodman, Alfred Lessing, Mark Sagoff, and Monroe Beardsley. I expected them to basically agree and instead they're all over the map. Lessing just bites the bullet and says there's zero aesthetic difference, full stop - the real loss is "originality" as a kind of achievement-value that's totally separate from beauty. Dutton thinks that's too fast, because for him appreciating a painting is secretly appreciating a performance, so the forger's brushstrokes really are aesthetically worse, not just historically tainted. Goodman's argument is the one that messed with my head the most: since we can't be sure we wouldn't eventually learn to see a difference with enough training or better tools, treating "indistinguishable right now" as "identical forever" is premature. Sagoff has this more technical move where aesthetic properties are relational/comparative, so a genuine piece and a forgery might just not be similar enough "kinds of things" to even be compared on the same scale. And Beardsley wants it both ways - no built-in aesthetic difference, but forgeries still corrupt everyone's perceptual "training" down the line, which honestly felt like a bit of a dodge to me. Then theres just the boring economist take that none of this is aesthetic at all, its a scarcity/status thing and the price is just tracking that.

I can't shake the question of which of these six actually gives the best account of why the reveal should (or shouldn't) change how we value or relate to the object.
