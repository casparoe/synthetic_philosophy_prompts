Write a dialogue between a philosopher who defends coherence arguments for expected utility maximization and one who is skeptical of them, in which they debate what such arguments actually establish about the behavior of advanced artificial agents. The defender holds that Dutch-book and money-pump arguments show that any sufficiently capable, reflectively stable agent must eventually act as an expected utility maximizer, and that this licenses real predictions about how such systems will behave. The skeptic disputes this on two fronts: first, that being representable as an expected utility maximizer is nearly empty of content, since a utility function can always be constructed after the fact to rationalize whatever the agent actually did; second, that the standard money-pump constructions never actually show mere incompleteness of preference to be exploitable, since an agent can dodge every pump simply by declining trades between options it has no preference between. Have each side respond directly to the other's strongest objections rather than simply restating their own position, and let the dialogue end with the disagreement still genuinely unresolved.
