ok so a while back i got into an argument with someone about whether green card holders should be allowed to vote in city elections, and that sent me down a rabbit hole into this thing political philosophy people call the "boundary problem" - basically the puzzle that you can't use a vote to decide who gets to vote without already assuming who counts, so its circular. apparently a guy named whelan wrote the main paper on this back in 1983, and theres this old quote from a british lawyer (jennings i think, from the 50s) that nails it: "the people cannot decide until someone decides who are the people."

since then ive collected a handful of the proposed fixes for this but honestly can't tell which ones actually solve the puzzle vs which ones just quietly assume an answer and hope you don't notice the circle they drew. the ones ive found so far:

- all-affected interests principle (anyone affected by a decision should get a vote on it)
- all-subjected/coercion principle (anyone who has to obey the laws should get a vote)
- stakeholder principle (baubock's version - long-term stake in the place, not necessarily citizenship)
- nationality principle (the demos just is a pre-existing nation - miller/walzer type view)
- territorial/residency principle (you live there, you're in)
- the "whoever's already a citizen gets to decide" default answer
