I've recently been reading Plato's Crito out of personal interest. In it, Socrates refuses his friend's offer to help him escape prison, defending his choice through a speech he imagines the personified Laws of Athens delivering. Their argument is that anyone who has lived his whole life under a city's laws, and who was always free to leave but chose to stay, has thereby agreed to obey them, so once one judges a particular verdict unjust, one's only legitimate options are to persuade the city to change its mind or to accept the punishment — never simply to flee or disobey. I keep wondering how Socrates, using that exact reasoning, would judge a case like Edward Snowden's: someone who leaked classified NSA documents because he believed the government's own surveillance programs were unjust, and who then left the country rather than stay and make his case before an American court. Would the Laws' argument condemn Snowden just as completely as it condemns Socrates's own hypothetical escape, or is there a step in it that could actually be turned around to defend what Snowden did? Please work through the specific moves in Socrates's argument rather than falling back on generic talk about the social contract.
