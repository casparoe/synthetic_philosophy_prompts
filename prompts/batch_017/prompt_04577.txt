This term I find myself auditing a philosophy of physics seminar alongside my regular physics PhD coursework, mainly on my advisor's suggestion that it would sharpen how I think about the foundations of quantum statistics. Two sessions ago the topic was the puzzle of "identical particles": the fact that the formalism treats two electrons in the same system as completely interchangeable, and yet quantum mechanics still counts them as two particles rather than one. The instructor pointed out that this is really a modern descendant of a much older metaphysical dispute, and had us read Max Black's 1952 dialogue imagining a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar spheres, explicitly framed as a counterexample to Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles.

What I haven't been able to shake is a claim she made in passing: that Black's two spheres are already "weakly discernible," since each stands in an irreflexive relation to the other, namely being some distance away from it, and that this is exactly the move Simon Saunders and F. A. Muller later used to argue that fermions such as electrons in an entangled singlet state are likewise discernible, via the relation of having opposite spin, so that quantum mechanics doesn't actually violate Leibniz's principle after all. She added that several philosophers of physics accept this maneuver for Black's classical spheres but reject it as illegitimate once it is applied to the quantum case — and then the seminar ran out of time before she could explain why.

I would rather work this out properly than wait for next week's discussion section to find out secondhand. Could you first reconstruct, as precisely as you can, why Black's spheres and Saunders and Muller's entangled fermions are supposed to be relevantly analogous under this "weak discernibility" strategy, and then lay out the strongest case for thinking the strategy succeeds for the spheres but fails once it's applied to quantum particles? I would also value your own verdict: on balance, does weak discernibility genuinely rescue the identity of indiscernibles in physics, or does the quantum case reveal that the whole strategy was only ever cosmetically successful, even for Black's original spheres?
