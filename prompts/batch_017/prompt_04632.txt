I've spent a long time puzzling over the objection that consequentialism is undermined by our inability to trace the long-run ripple effects of our actions -- what's sometimes called "cluelessness." The more I think about it, the less worried I am, since it seems to me like just a special case of an everyday problem: decision-making under uncertainty. Nobody thinks it's impossible to rationally choose an insurance policy just because the future is unknown; you simply assign probabilities to the possible outcomes and go with whichever option has the highest expected value. It seems like a charity evaluator, or anyone else, could handle their uncertainty about an action's unforeseeable downstream effects in exactly the same way, which would fully dissolve the worry. Given how straightforward this fix seems, I'm puzzled about why philosophers who work on cluelessness still treat it as a serious, unresolved problem for consequentialism.
