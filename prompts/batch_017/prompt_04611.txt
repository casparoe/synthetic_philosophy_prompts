Me and my friend Alex got into it pretty badly last night over drinks about whether you should ever refuse to put a single number on your beliefs. Alex does quant trading and is a hardcore "sharp Bayesian" type - his whole worldview is that if you can't state a precise probability for something you just haven't thought about it hard enough, and that gathering more evidence should never leave you MORE confused than you started out, only more (or at least equally) confident. I was arguing the other side, that for a lot of real questions - stuff with basically no reference class, like "will this specific new technology cause a catastrophe in the next 20 years" - the honest move is to represent your credence as a whole range or set of probabilities instead of just picking one number because you're being forced to.

Alex went home and apparently spent an hour down a wikipedia/philosophy-blog rabbit hole, because he came back the next day super smug saying he'd found "the perfect counterexample" to imprecise credences: something called dilation. From what he described (and he was pretty pleased with himself so I don't totally trust his summary), its a real, accepted result in the imprecise-probability literature where conditioning on some new piece of evidence can take your credence in a claim from a single precise value, like exactly 1/2, all the way out to the maximally uninformative interval [0,1] - total ignorance - even though the evidence you learned seems, on its face, to have nothing to do with the claim in question. He's using this to argue imprecise probabilities are secretly incoherent, since apparently learning stuff can make your beliefs strictly less informative, which he says no sane epistemology should ever allow.

I don't buy that this is some kind of knockdown argument, but I also honestly don't understand HOW dilation is even mechanically possible - it seems to violate everything I thought I knew about conditional probability. Can you walk me through, on a fairly intuitive gut level rather than full-blown measure theory, how a totally "irrelevant-seeming" piece of evidence can dilate your credence like that? And once I've got the mechanism down, I'd definately love your take on whether Alex is right that this is a fatal problem for taking imprecise probabilities seriously, or whether there's a good comeback to his "gotcha."
