I'm working on a piece about the intellectual backstory of "post-truth" politics, and I keep circling back to Richard Rorty. Everyone's favorite party trick these days is pulling up that passage from his 1998 book, "Achieving Our Country," where he basically calls a Trump-like strongman a decade and a half early: economic resentment building while the left gets absorbed in cultural battles, until someone shows up promising to sideline the smug experts and turn back the clock on decades of social progress. I brought this up with a philosopher friend at dinner and she said something like, "of course he saw it coming -- his whole neopragmatist, antirepresentationalist thing was already committed to there being no fact of the matter about truth beyond what your community lets you get away with." Then she actually quoted him: "truth is what your contemporaries let you get away with saying."

That's the bit I can't shake for the piece, and I want to make sure I'm being fair to a dead guy who can't clarify himself anymore. If Rorty genuinely thought truth was nothing but social consensus, with no outside check on it at all, doesn't that mean his own philosophy leaves him with no principled way to object when a demagouge's supporters simply decide the crowd size was the biggest ever, or that an election everyone watched was actually stolen? It feels almost too neat -- the philosopher who "called" post-truth politics turns out to have also quietly supplied its alibi, unless antirepresentationalism doesn't actually cash out the way my friend made it sound. Is that a fair reading of Rorty, or is there some distinction I'm flattening that would let him keep the prediction without the self-own?
