Ok so my friend Priya and I got into it pretty hard last night over that G.E. Moore thought experiment in Principia Ethica - the one where he wants you to imagine two totally lifeless worlds, one stunningly beautiful and one just deeply hideous (something like a heap of filth, might be misremembering his exact words), and crucially NOBODY will ever exist to see either one, not now, not ever, no exceptions. He still insists the beautiful world existing is better than the ugly one existing, purely on its own merits, zero observers required. Priya thinks that's basically incoherent, her whole argument was that "beauty" stops meaning anything once you strip out every possible viewer, so the comparison is empty from the start. I kept arguing there's something to the intuition even if its hard to justify logically. Then somehow we drifted into a totally different part of the book, where he ranks personal affection (friendship, love, that whole category) right alongside aesthetic appreciation as literally the two greatest goods that exist, and claims that when you get both at once - like sharing something beautiful with someone you love - the combination is worth way more than the two parts added up seperately, because of his "organic unities" idea, that wholes aren't just sums of their parts. We went back and forth for like two hours and never landed anywhere, typical.

Instead of hashing it out abstractly again, I want to see these two claims actually get tested against each other in fiction. Could you write me a short story (a page or two is plenty) where a character is forced, under real time pressure, to choose between (a) finishing or creating something beautiful that, once it's done, will genuinely never be seen or experienced by anyone ever again - not even by them - versus (b) spending that same scrap of time with a friend or loved one who's about to become permanently unreachable somehow, doesn't have to be death, could be way more mundane than that, I just want the tension between the two goods to feel real instead of obviously resolved in advance. Bonus points if the story manages to gesture at that "organic unity" idea somewhere, like some moment where combining the two goods produces something that feels bigger than either one alone, without just having a character stop and explain the philosophy out loud.
