After thirty-five years as a field geologist, I never had much occasion to read philosophy or theology, but in retirement I have been working through the Church Fathers with a small reading group consisting mostly of former colleagues, partly out of curiosity about how thinkers writing long before modern logic handled arguments that still strike me as remarkably careful. This month we are reading Augustine's short treatise "On Lying" (De Mendacio), and while his central thesis, that asserting a falsehood is never permissible no matter the good it might achieve, is easy enough to state, I find myself stuck on the passage where he confronts what looks like the obvious hard case: someone is sheltering a person from unjust pursuers, and the pursuers arrive and begin asking direct questions about that person's whereabouts.

The passage, in the nineteenth-century Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers translation, reads as follows:

But one sometimes comes to a case of this kind, that we are not interrogated where the person is who is sought, nor forced to betray him, if he is hidden in such manner, that he cannot easily be found unless betrayed: but we are asked, whether he be in such a place or not. If we know him to be there, by holding our peace we betray him, or even by saying that we will in no wise tell whether he be there or not: for from this the questioner gathers that he is there, as, if he were not, nothing else would be answered by him who would not lie nor betray a man, but only, that he is not there. So, by our either holding our peace, or saying such words, a man is betrayed, and he who seeks him has but to enter in, if he have the power, and find him: whereas he might have been turned aside from finding him by our telling a lie. Wherefore if you know not where he is, there is no cause for hiding the truth, but you must confess that you know not. But, if you know where he is, whether he be in the place which is named in the question or elsewhere; you must not say, when it is asked whether he be there or not, "I will not tell you what you ask," but you must say, "I know where he is, but I will never show."

What I cannot work out is why Augustine treats plain silence, or an outright refusal to answer, as effectively betraying the fugitive, while the alternative formula he recommends is supposed to leave the questioner none the wiser, even though both responses are given in reply to exactly the same question and withhold exactly the same fact. He clearly believes there is a principled difference between the two, and not merely a difference of tone, yet he never states outright what that difference is. Is there a genuine logical or communicative distinction here, one that an attentive reader in his own day would simply have taken for granted, or is Augustine really just trading one form of concealment for a form of words that happens to sound less like lying? I would also value your view on how this passage sits alongside his official definition of a lie elsewhere in the treatise, namely as a matter of the speaker's own intention to assert what he believes to be false, rather than a matter of how much the listener actually learns.
