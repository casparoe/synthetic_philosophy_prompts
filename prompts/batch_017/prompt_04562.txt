I don't have any formal background in philosophy — I've spent most of my working life as an electrician — but I've been sitting with a question for months now and I'd like to work it up into a proper essay rather than just turning it over in my head. The question grew out of watching my father spend the last year of his life insisting, against what his doctors were telling him and against what I could plainly see, that he was going to beat his cancer. At the time his certainty struck me as a kind of denial he'd talked himself into, but the more I've thought about it, the more it seems to bear on a real dispute I only learned about afterward, through my own reading: the argument between William James's "The Will to Believe" and W.K. Clifford's "The Ethics of Belief" over when, if ever, it's permissible to believe something the evidence doesn't support.

Here is roughly the shape of the essay I want to write, and I'd like your feedback before I commit to writing the whole thing. I'll start from Clifford's evidentialism, using his shipowner who talks himself into believing his unsound vessel is seaworthy as the anchor case, and then bring in James's response that Clifford's rule can't govern every belief, only what James calls a "genuine option" — one that is simultaneously live, forced, and momentous. My worry is that my father's belief doesn't obviously pass James's own test, because it doesn't look forced in the way he needs it to be: nothing seems to have been lost, in the way James thinks something is lost when a person refuses to decide whether to trust a friend or commit to a faith, by simply declining to form any view about his odds one way or the other. And yet the belief still seems to have done real psychological work that merely hoping for the best, or acting as if he might recover while withholding actual belief, would not have done — something closer to the case James describes of trusting that someone likes you, where the trust itself helps bring the liking about, than to ordinary hope. My tentative thesis is that James's three conditions aren't exhaustive: that there is a further class of belief, adopted not because the situation forces a choice but because holding the belief outright, rather than merely hoping or acting on a possibility, is what does the psychological work — and that this amounts to a different defense of non-evidential belief than the one James actually gives.

Before I go further, I'd like your honest feedback on three things. First, whether I've got James's conditions right, and in particular whether his own remark that declining to decide is itself "a passional decision" already closes the gap I think I've found, so that my father's case turns out to be forced after all on his terms, and I've simply misread him. Second, whether the distinction I'm reaching for, between believing something outright and merely hoping for it while acting on it, is doing real philosophical work, or whether it collapses back into James's own position once it's stated carefully. Third, whether this whole problem — non-evidential belief that seems to matter even where no option is strictly forced — already has a standard treatment in the literature on James that I ought to read before I say anything that sounds like I think it's original. I would rather hear that now than after I have written the essay.
