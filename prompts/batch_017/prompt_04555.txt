A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument after a reading group on mental causation, and neither of us managed to budge the other, so I want a more disinterested judgment on the matter. My friend, who is much more attached to non-reductive physicalism than I am, was defending Yablo's determinable/determinate treatment of mental causation, buttressed by an interventionist, difference-making account of causation along the lines of List and Menzies. Her line was essentially that in Yablo's famous example, the pigeon Sophie's pecking is proportionally caused by the target's redness rather than by its specific scarlet shade, precisely because redness (not any particular physical realizer of it) is what tracks the relevant counterfactual pattern of pecking, and that this generalizes into a genuine, non-derivative causal role for mental properties even granting the causal closure of the physical.

I wasn't convinced, and pushed the standard "relocation" worry: that for any mental determinable supposedly proportional to some behavioral effect, you can usually gerrymander an equally proportional coarse-grained physical determinable (some disjunction of realizers), so the exclusion problem just resurfaces one level down within the physical, rather than being solved for the mental as such. I also didn't think Bennett's proposed fix to the overdetermination premise -- adding a further counterfactual condition to rule out only the "bad" cases of overdetermination -- helps much once you've already let in that much cheap counterfactual dependence. We went around in circles for a couple of hours and ended up more entrenched than when we started, which suggests beer-fueled debate is not the ideal venue for resolving this.

So I'd like a proper essay instead. Please lay out Kim's exclusion argument in its strongest form, then present the best versions of at least two leading non-reductive responses to it (Yablo-style proportionality and an interventionist/difference-making response such as List and Menzies' are obvious candidates, though you're welcome to bring in others), taking the relocation objection seriously rather than waving it away. Don't hedge your way to "reasonable people disagree": commit to an actual verdict, either that non-reductive physicalism can be vindicated against the exclusion argument (and say by which strategy, and why that strategy specifically escapes the relocation worry), or that it can't (in which case say which fallback -- reduction, epiphenomenalism, or denying closure or the exclusion principle outright -- is least costly, and why). A precise conditional verdict, e.g., "non-reductive physicalism survives only if causation is fundamentally interventionist rather than governed by strict laws," is fine, so long as you actually argue for the conditional rather than just asserting it.
