Below is a short essay on a narrow point in Alan Turing's discussion of extrasensory perception in "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950). I would welcome comments on it.

Among the objections to the imitation game that Turing considers late in the paper, the last is what he calls the Argument from Extrasensory Perception. Turing does not treat it dismissively: he tells us that the statistical case for telepathy is, in his view, overwhelming, and he is willing to take clairvoyance, precognition, and psychokinesis seriously as live possibilities. His worry is that a human participant with strong telepathic or clairvoyant ability could supply correct answers to an interrogator's questions (his example involves guessing the suit of a card) through a channel that bypasses ordinary conversational or computational competence entirely, giving the interrogator a way to unmask the human that the test was never designed to guard against. Turing's proposed fix is to house the participants in a "telepathy-proof room."

I want to argue that this fix is in tension with the evidence Turing is relying on. The "overwhelming" statistical case he had in mind was almost certainly the card-guessing research conducted in Britain over the 1940s by the parapsychologist S. G. Soal, whose experiments placed sender and receiver in separate, sometimes distant rooms and, in one well-publicized series appearing the same year as Turing's paper, purported to confirm accurate scoring at distances of up to two hundred miles; the parallel American program run by J. B. Rhine made analogous claims using Faraday cages and lead-lined chambers. In both cases, the effect's apparent insensitivity to intervening physical media was not an incidental detail: it was offered as the reason to think telepathy could not be a disguised, ordinary physical signal, and hence as the reason it deserved to be called "extrasensory" in the first place. But if that is the relevant notion of telepathy, then a room that is "telepathy-proof" is close to a contradiction in terms, since the very studies Turing found convincing were understood by their own proponents to show that no such room could work. So the proposed fix does not neutralize the objection on its own terms; it succeeds only by quietly substituting a more conventional, blockable notion of telepathy for the one his own cited evidence was supposed to establish. On this reading, the passage is not merely a curious lapse but a small, telling case of Turing reaching for an engineering solution that works only once he has stopped taking the metaphysics of his own premise seriously.

I would welcome comments on: (1) whether this is a textually accurate and reasonably charitable reading, rather than an overinterpretation of a few sentences; (2) whether the historical claim about Soal- and Rhine-era parapsychology's emphasis on distance- and shielding-independence is solid enough to bear the argumentative weight I am putting on it; and (3) whether a more charitable reconstruction of the "telepathy-proof room" remark is available that dissolves the tension I claim to have found.
