Last winter I came across a slim New York Review Books edition of Simone Weil's "On the Abolition of All Political Parties" in a secondhand shop, and picked it up mainly because I already knew her as a religious writer and was curious what a mystic could have against something as ordinary as party politics. The essay has stayed with me more than I expected, especially while following the news, and I eventually wrote up a short piece trying to apply her argument to the present day. I have no background in philosophy beyond what I have taught myself through reading, so before I show this piece to anyone else I would like an honest outside read, since I cannot tell whether I have represented her fairly or whether the argument I build on top of hers actually holds together. I would rather hear the real problems with it than encouragement.

Here is what I wrote:

Weil's claim is not that particular parties are badly run, but that the party form itself is close to pure evil no matter what it claims to stand for. Working in a broadly Rousseauian tradition, in which honest public deliberation is supposed to let private passions cancel each other out and leave behind something like a shared, truthful "general will," she argues that every party blocks this process in three related ways: it manufactures a collective passion among its members, it keeps up steady pressure on each member to think and vote with the group, and underneath whatever it officially stands for, it pursues only one real aim, its own unlimited growth. Put those three together and a party becomes an arrangement for trading your own sense of what is true and just for loyalty to the side, which is why she says party organization exists, in her own words, "for the purpose of killing in all souls the sense of truth and of justice." Tellingly, she does not treat every party as equally guilty: she distinguishes Anglo-American parties, which she thought kept something of the character of a sporting contest between legitimate rivals, from the continental European kind she traced back to the Jacobins, which aimed at total power for the winner and nothing at all for anyone else.

What strikes me is that her three mechanisms describe our current politics, especially online, at least as well as they describe a mid-century parliamentary party. You do not need a membership card anymore to be swept into a passion, pressured into line by your own side, or caught up in something whose only real goal is to keep growing; a social-media platform or a partisan media ecosystem can do all three on its own. But here I get stuck: if Weil herself thought the American style of party was the milder, sportier kind, then either the comparison I want to draw does not really apply to it, or I need to argue that today's polarization has pushed American politics toward the total-power kind she actually feared, and I cannot tell which of those is true, or whether either move rescues my argument. There is also the more basic objection, going back to Madison's Federalist No. 10, that people will always sort themselves into rival factions once they are free to disagree, so that the real task is to manage that tendency rather than eliminate it; Weil's own proposed substitute of independent citizens and one-off issue groups, instead of standing parties, sounds to me like it would just regrow parties in miniature as soon as the same people kept ending up on the same side more than once.

I would be grateful for comments on three things in particular: whether my summary of Weil's argument is accurate rather than a flattened version of it, whether the parallel to online political tribalism is a legitimate extension of her reasoning given her own distinction between Anglo-American and continental parties, and whether there is a genuine answer available to a defender of Weil against the "factions are unavoidable" objection, or whether that objection basically sinks her proposal.
