I've been kind of obsessed lately with this thing from decision theory called Good's theorem (I.J. Good, the statistician who apparently worked with Turing at Bletchley?). The basic idea, as I understand it, is that the "value of information" can never be negative for a rational Bayesian agent: if you're about to make a decision and someone offers you a free peek at some extra evidence beforehand, you should always take it, because in expectation you can only do as well or better with it, never worse. I actually sat down and half-convinced myself of this with a toy example — deciding right now is like picking the best option on average, while waiting to see the evidence first is like taking the average of "best option for each thing you could've learned," and that second number can never come out smaller. Felt like a genuinely clean, satisfying little proof once it clicked for me.

Except it bugs me alot, because it clashes with stuff I keep noticing where getting extra (free!) information before deciding seems to leave people, or even whole groups of people, worse off, and not just because they panicked or updated on it badly. I vaguely remember some econ blog post about how, in insurance-type markets, the mere fact that some accurate info is about to become public can kill off deals both sides would've happily made beforehand — wrecking the "risk sharing," or something like that, though honestly the actual math went over my head. That feels categorically different then the usual "I googled my symptoms and spiraled" story, since nobody there is being dumb about it, it's more structural than that.

So here's what I'm actually asking: can you give me one specific, fully worked-out example along those lines — a case where everyone involved is reasoning correctly, the information is genuinely free, and yet they'd have been better off, in expected-utility terms, if that information had never become available to them at all? And then point to exactly which step of the little proof I sketched above quietly stops working in your example, i.e. where the theorem's guarantee actually runs out. Please don't just tell me "well, real people aren't perfect Bayesians" — that feels like it dodges the actually interesting part of the question.
