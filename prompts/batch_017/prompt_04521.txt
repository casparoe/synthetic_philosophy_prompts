During my thirty-year career as a condensed-matter physicist, I had little occasion to read philosophy of science. Retirement has finally given me the leisure to correct that gap, and for the past several weeks I have been working through Thomas Kuhn's The Structure of Scientific Revolutions with a small reading group made up of former colleagues. We found the account of normal science, anomaly, and crisis fairly congenial to our own experience of research, but the incommensurability thesis has left several of us unsettled, and I would value your help thinking it through.

As I understand Kuhn's argument, when a revolution occurs the central terms of the old paradigm do not simply become better specified; they change meaning outright, so that the two paradigms are not really offering rival answers to the same question but are, in some sense, talking past one another. His standard illustration is that Newtonian mass and relativistic mass are conceptually distinct quantities—one conserved, the other convertible with energy—that happen to coincide numerically only in the low-velocity limit, rather than being the same quantity simply measured with greater precision. If that is right, it seems to follow that there is no shared, paradigm-independent standard by which to judge that the newer theory is actually better rather than merely different, and that the process by which physicists abandoned phlogiston theory, or the Ptolemaic system, or classical mechanics, must ultimately have been driven by something other than the accumulation of good reasons—generational turnover, the rewriting of textbooks, and the rhetorical skill of the revolutionaries, much as Kuhn's harshest critics have charged. On this reading, the confidence most working scientists have that modern physics is simply an improvement on what came before, rather than just a different and equally arbitrary way of carving up the world, turns out to be something the profession tells itself after the fact rather than a conclusion actually supported by evidence and argument.

Is this really what the incommensurability thesis commits one to, or have my reading group and I fallen into a trap that Kuhn himself would have rejected?
