I am several drafts into a historical novel set in the late 1720s, centered on a young Philadelphia printer who, like Benjamin Franklin himself, takes up the habit of keeping a small ruled notebook in which he scores himself, week by week, against a private list of virtues. In researching the texture of that habit, I have been rereading Part Two of Franklin's Autobiography: the "bold and arduous project of arriving at moral perfection," the thirteen virtues taken up one at a time in weekly rotation, the wry parable of the man who abandons the grindstone and decides he likes a speckled axe best, and, above all, Franklin's admission that of all our passions, pride is the hardest to subdue — so persistent, as he puts it, that "even if I could conceive that I had completely overcome it, I should probably be proud of my humility."

For the sake of the novel, I want to give this printer a foil: a stiffer, New England-bred young man pursuing moral improvement by an entirely different method. For him I have been drawing on Jonathan Edwards's seventy youthful Resolutions, composed only five or six years before Franklin conceived his own project and, like Franklin's chart, meant to be reviewed on a fixed schedule rather than drawn up once and shelved. Formally, the two documents are strikingly alike: a young man commits himself, in writing, to an explicit and periodically audited program of self-transformation. Substantively, though, they seem to rest on almost opposite pictures of what a person is and what moral striving can accomplish, with Edwards prefacing his entire list by confessing that he is "unable to do anything without God's help."

Before I let these two characters argue past one another in a scene I have in mind, I would like to understand the real philosophical stakes rather than settle for the convenient shorthand of "Enlightenment versus Puritan." Could you compare the two projects in some depth: what conception of moral agency and human nature does each presuppose, and how does that conception bear on the particular self-referential trap Franklin identifies in himself? Does Edwards's theological starting point genuinely protect him from a version of the same trap — being unable to secure a virtue without that very success becoming a fresh occasion for pride — or does it merely relocate the problem onto different terrain? And if it is the latter, is there anything in Edwards's own body of work to suggest that he recognized the same difficulty under a different name?
