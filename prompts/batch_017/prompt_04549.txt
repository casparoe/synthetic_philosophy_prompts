My supervisor lent me Jay Garfield's translation as a starting point, and I've mostly been able to follow the accompanying commentary, but I've gotten stuck trying to reconcile two verses that seem to use the same four-part structure — what Garfield calls the tetralemma, or catuṣkoṭi in Sanskrit — in what look like opposite ways, and my supervisor isn't available again until next week.

In Chapter 18, verse 8, Nāgārjuna writes that "everything is real and is not real, both real and not real, neither real nor not real" (MMK 18.8), and the commentary treats this as an affirmation of all four options at once, presented as the Buddha's own teaching rather than as an error to be corrected. But in Chapter 22, verse 11, while examining whether the term "Tathāgata" picks out anything that exists in and of itself, he seems to do the reverse: "'empty' should not be asserted, 'nonempty' should not be asserted, neither both nor neither should be asserted" (MMK 22.11), adding that such words are only used nominally rather than as literal descriptions. So in one place all four corners of the tetralemma get affirmed together, and in another all four get rejected as things nobody should even assert.

I can't tell whether this is a genuine tension I should address as a problem in my essay or whether I'm missing some feature of how the tetralemma is meant to work that would dissolve it. Is Nāgārjuna making essentially the same logical move in both passages — always operating at the level of ultimate truth, say, or always exploiting the fact that the four options wrongly presuppose svabhāva (intrinsic essence) — just with different results depending on context, or are these really two distinct uses of the fourfold schema? And more basically, does the first passage commit him to a straightforward contradiction (asserting "real" and "not real" as both true of the same thing), the way I've read that some modern logicians such as Graham Priest argue, or is there a more conservative reading, perhaps turning on the two-truths doctrine or on different kinds of negation, that avoids saddling him with an outright contradiction? I'd like to actually defend a position on this in my essay rather than just describe the debate, so I'd really appreciate your reasoning on which interpretation holds up best and why.
