I spent my career as an experimental physicist, and now that I'm retired I've been falling down all sorts of philosophy rabbit holes for fun. Lately its this "no-trade theorem" business -- Milgrom and Stokey, built on top of the older Aumann "agreeing to disagree" result -- that's stuck in my head. As I understand it: if two rational people share the same prior, and it becomes common knowledge that both of them are still willing to go through with a proposed trade (or bet) even after factoring in that fact itself, that alone is supposed to be enough information to make any purely speculative trade unravel before it ever happens. I can follow the "iterated reasoning" steps of the proof one at a time -- it has a whiff of a perturbative expansion to me, each order cancelling the last -- but strung together the whole thing still feels like a card trick, and I can't quite spot where the real work is being done. Could you walk me through a genuinely gut-level, intuitive story for why the unraveling has to happen, rather than just re-deriving the formal steps? And then, since actual markets are absolutely drowning in speculative trading, what's the more interesting lesson here -- are real traders just violating one of the theorem's premises (common priors, common knowledge of rationality), or does this expose something deeper about how much we should trust market prices as trackers of truth?
