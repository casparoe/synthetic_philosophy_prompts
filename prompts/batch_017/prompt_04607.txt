For my decision theory seminar, I've been working through the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms underwriting expected-utility theory, alongside the Kelly criterion for sizing bets. This led me to Scott Garrabrant's "geometric rationality" sequence, which treats Kelly betting, Nash bargaining, and Thompson sampling as instances of one alternative to expected-value maximization: instead of an arithmetic average of outcomes, the agent maximizes a geometric one, which for repeated betting means maximizing expected log wealth rather than expected wealth. Garrabrant is explicit that this requires giving up the independence axiom, not just adopting a logarithmic utility function within orthodox expected-utility theory. I'd like this tension dramatized.

Please write a dialogue between two portfolio managers who must decide, before the next wager is placed, what fraction of their shared bankroll to stake on a favorable but volatile repeated bet. One is a VNM loyalist who thinks abandoning independence invites a money pump and suspects "geometric rationality" is really just a logarithmic utility function in disguise. The other is a geometric-rationality advocate who denies this, arguing that expected wealth is the wrong target for an agent who will live through one long sequence of bets rather than an ensemble of parallel ones. Have them go several rounds, each genuinely engaging with the other's strongest objection, so neither simply concedes or is made to look foolish.
