In my seminar on moral uncertainty, we've been comparing two proposals that both use the language of bargaining to defuse disagreement over ethics, but which seem to operate at very different levels. Toby Ord's "moral trade" has two people with different moral commitments strike a deal — one becomes vegetarian, say, while the other donates to an anti-poverty charity — so that each judges the world improved by their own lights, just as ordinary trade leaves both parties better off by their own preferences. Nick Bostrom's "parliamentary model" of moral uncertainty, later formalized by Ord and Newberry, instead has the rival moral theories in which a single agent has credence send delegates to an internal parliament, where they bargain and vote to decide what the agent should do.

Compare the two proposals: what precisely is being exchanged, and between whom, in each case, and does moral trade's justification — voluntary participation producing a Pareto improvement by each party's own values — really transfer to a setting with only one agent, where no theory can consent or walk away? Which proposal do you find the more defensible response to moral disagreement and uncertainty, and why?
