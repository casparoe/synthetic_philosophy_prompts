Retired marine biologist here -- spending retirement finally reading philosophy i never had time for, and ive gone down a bit of a Kant rabbit hole. Two things about him I hadn't known: in his 1755 Universal Natural History he actually argues that a planet's inhabitants get more refined and rational the further out the planet sits from the sun, since matter close to the sun is too coarse to properly express thought -- so Mercury and Venus get the dullards, we're stuck on the "middle rung," and Jupiter and Saturn get beings well ahead of us. Then decades later in the first Critique he turns around and uses "are there inhabitants on other planets" as his flagship example of a doctrinal belief, something he says he'd stake his whole fortune on, precisely because no possible experience could ever settle it either way.

Taking those two actual commitments seriously, not the pop-science "Kant believed in aliens" version -- what do you reckon he'd say if he could see where things actually stand now: thousands of confirmed exoplanets, several of the best habitable-zone candidates tucked in close around dim little red dwarf stars, and decades of SETI turning up nothing? Does his own epistemology mean that silence is exactly what he'd have predicted, so it shouldn't move him at all? And does the red-dwarf business break his distance-equals-perfection scheme, or does his own reasoning have some way of surviving contact with it? Genuinely curious what actually falls out of his framework here, not just a guess.
