I do not have any formal background in philosophy or the history of science, but I have spent a good deal of my spare time over the last year reading old essays about technology and the future, and one piece that has really stuck with me is John von Neumann's 1955 Fortune magazine essay "Can We Survive Technology?" It is not just a vague warning that technology is dangerous — von Neumann actually builds a fairly precise argument for why he thinks the danger can never be permanently solved. His case rests on a few linked claims: that the useful and harmful applications of any given technology are so entangled that no one could cleanly ban "the dangerous part" without banning the underlying science itself; that any prohibition strict enough to actually work would require a worldwide enforcing authority so powerful that creating it would already amount to solving the very political problem it was meant to prevent; and that prohibiting technological development is simply incompatible with the spirit of an industrial civilization, so that no real restraint will be accepted short of a catastrophe severe enough to destroy people's faith in progress itself.

Almost as an aside, von Neumann also predicts in that essay that humanity will soon be able to deliberately warm or cool the planet — he even cites industrial carbon dioxide as already nudging the climate — and states flatly that once such power exists, "they will be exploited," for good or as a weapon. His overall conclusion is that there is no "cure-all" waiting to be discovered, only an unending series of ad hoc, day-to-day judgment calls, guided by what he calls, in the essay's closing words, "patience, flexibility, intelligence."

What I cannot stop turning over is what von Neumann would say about solar geoengineering today, meaning proposals to cool the planet by injecting aerosols into the stratosphere. By most accounts this would be cheap enough that a single mid-sized country, or even a sufficiently determined wealthy individual (people sometimes call this the "Greenfinger" scenario), could attempt it alone — the so-called "free driver" problem, as distinct from the more familiar free-rider one — and there is a real risk of "termination shock" if whoever is running it ever stops abruptly. In January 2022, more than sixty senior scientists published an open letter calling on governments to commit to never developing or deploying the technology at planetary scale, rather than trying to regulate its use once it exists. Reasoning strictly from the argument von Neumann actually makes, rather than from his general reputation as a hawkish, technology-optimistic Cold War figure, do you think he would have expected such a non-use agreement to hold? Would he see today's situation as exactly the pattern his essay predicted, or is there something about solar geoengineering — who is capable of doing it, how visible or attributable its effects would be, how reversible or irreversible it might turn out to be — that breaks the analogy with nuclear weapons or his own climate-control scenario, and might push him toward a different conclusion? I would like your best reconstruction of the reasoning he might actually have used, rather than just a guess at his general mood.
