In a 1961 letter to his mother, Kurt Gödel argued that because the world is rationally organized and meaningful, some form of afterlife — a "Wiedersehen" with those we have lost — must exist. A decade earlier, in his 1951 Gibbs lecture, he had defended a disjunction that he believed followed with mathematical certainty from the incompleteness theorems: either "the human mind infinitely surpasses the powers of any finite machine," or there exist absolutely undecidable mathematical propositions. Gödel left little doubt about which disjunct he personally accepted, holding throughout his life that the mind cannot be fully captured by any mechanical or computational system.

Taking both commitments seriously, how do you think Gödel would have judged contemporary proposals for technological substitutes for an afterlife — such as whole-brain emulation ("mind uploading"), cryonic preservation followed by algorithmic revival, or the claim that a sufficiently detailed simulation of a person would constitute a genuine continuation of them? Would he have regarded these as possible realizations of the reunion his letter promised, as engineering projects built on a picture of mind that his own work gave him reason to reject, or as something else again? Ground your answer in the specific structure of his two arguments rather than in generic impressions of his outlook.
