Ok, this is a bit of a random crossover question but it's been living in my head for weeks. I'm doing a PhD that straddles philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and for a history-of-philosophy requirement I've been working through Locke's Essay Concerning Human Understanding. Completely separately, my actual lab spends half of every meeting arguing about whether LLMs understand anything or are just shuffling symbols around based on their relations to each other -- the classic Harnad-style "symbol grounding" worry, where a system that only ever maps symbols onto more symbols (his go-to example is trying to learn Chinese from a Chinese-Chinese dictionary) never actually reaches meaning, no matter how big the dictionary gets. What's been messing with me is how much Locke's own setup in Books II and III is doing basically the same move: every idea has to cash out eventually in simple ideas from sensation or reflection, complex ideas are just combinations of those, and then in Book III he spends chapter after chapter (the "abuse of words" material especialy) complaining that people pass around words that have come completely unglued from any actual idea and mistake the fluent talk for knowledge. He also went on record, in the Molyneux letters, guessing that a newly-sighted blind man would NOT be able to match a globe to a cube by sight alone -- no free transfer of grounding across the senses, you have to actually build the connection through new experience.

So here's my actual question: if you sat Locke down in front of a modern LLM confidently generating fluent paragraphs about color, pain, weight, and physical space, despite (on the usual telling) never sensing or acting on anything, what do you think he'd conclude, arguing strictly from his own machinery rather than vibes? Would he treat it as the ultimate confirmation of the abuse-of-words diagnosis he already had, language at its most unmoored from any idea? Or is there a way, using his own categories, that he could be talked into granting it something -- like, does training on an enormous pile of other people's descriptions of sensations count as some secondhand, degenerate "idea," or could a model computing over its own previous outputs count as anything like his "reflection," even minus actual sensation? I want the real argument worked out through Locke's own categories, not just "he was an empiricist, so obviously he'd say no."
