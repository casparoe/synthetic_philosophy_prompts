I'm drafting a campus novel built around a long-running argument between two philosophy doctoral students, Priya Chandrasekaran and Daniel Voss, who are also, unhappily, close friends. Their department is split over the semantics of slurs, and rather than gesture vaguely at "offensive language," the way too many novels that attempt this seem to do, I want their dispute rendered as an actual seminar-room exchange with real philosophical teeth. Priya defends a broadly semantic, truth-conditional view: a slur's derogatory content is part of what the sentence literally says, so that on the more austere versions of her view, any sentence predicating the term of someone comes out false, since nobody actually satisfies the discriminatory conditions the word encodes. Daniel defends a broadly pragmatic view: that content is merely conventionally implicated or expressive, riding on top of an otherwise neutral truth-conditional core, so that a sentence using the slur can be strictly true even while separately conveying contempt.

What I actually need dramatized is more specific than the general dispute, because two other scenes in the manuscript hinge on it. Earlier in the book, before this seminar scene, my third-person narrator has already used the same word once, unquoted, in free indirect style, to render a minor bigoted character's interior monologue — and I'm no longer sure whether that sentence quietly commits my narrative voice to the word's ugliness, or whether rendering a character's perspective that way insulates the prose the way a direct quotation would. Later, after the seminar scene, Priya herself, who belongs to the group the word targets, starts using it affectionately with a handful of close friends. I want Priya and Daniel's argument to take up both cases together: can either of their theories explain how one and the same word can shift from contempt to affection depending on speaker and audience, and does either theory give a principled answer about the free-indirect-discourse sentence, or does that just come down to authorial fiat either way?

Could you write the scene as an actual dialogue between the two of them — long enough for the argument to really develop, with each one pressing the other on whichever case is hardest for their own view, rather than the two of them simply restating settled talking points? I'd like it to be a genuinely close contest; I don't want either position to come out the obvious winner. My usual philosophy-of-language sounding board, a friend who TAs the relevant seminar, is on leave until the fall, so for this draft I'm afraid you're my only reader.
