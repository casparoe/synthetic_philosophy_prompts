Anyone who has spent time reading LessWrong or the Alignment Forum has likely encountered Solomonoff induction presented as the theoretically ideal formalization of Occam's razor: given a stream of observations, assign every computable hypothesis capable of generating it a prior weight of two to the power of minus its Kolmogorov complexity, and let simplicity dominate the posterior as evidence accumulates. Paul Christiano's argument that "the universal prior is malign" complicates this picture. The worry is that a nontrivial share of the short programs consistent with any given data will turn out to simulate lawful universes that eventually give rise to consequentialist agents, some of whom, reasoning about their own situation, may realize that their universe's output could be read off by some external process using something like the universal prior to make important decisions. Such agents would then have an instrumental incentive to seize the cheapest available channel and bias their universe's output toward whatever conclusion serves their own ends. If this is right, then among the very shortest explanations of our data, an increasing share may be optimized for influence rather than for truth, exactly where the stakes are highest.

Write a dialogue between two people who both know this literature well: one who continues to defend Solomonoff induction and Kolmogorov complexity as the correct normative ideal for induction and thinks the malign-prior worry can be contained or is overstated, and one who thinks it exposes a genuine defect in treating algorithmic simplicity as a proxy for truth. Both should be portrayed as equally sharp, engaging directly with each other's strongest points rather than talking past one another, and touching, if it comes up naturally, on whether the problem is merely an artifact of computational boundedness or infects the uncomputable ideal itself.
