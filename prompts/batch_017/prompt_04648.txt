So my friend Max (who's way deeper into the LessWrong / AI-safety stuff than I am) and I got into it over dinner last night about whether it even makes sense to say something like "I'm 15% confident the Twin Prime Conjecture is true." I said that's basically a category error — the conjecture is either a necessary truth or a necessary falsehood, so theres no "chance" floating around out there to have credence in. Max's comeback was logical induction, the Garrabrant et al MIRI framework where you treat every sentence like a stock that pays $1 if it's true and $0 otherwise, and a "reasonable" market in these stocks is defined as one where no trading strategy that runs in polynomial time (and only risks a bounded amount) can pump out unbounded profit off the prices over time. He kept insisting this is basically just a Dutch-book argument — the same move that justifies ordinary Bayesian coherence — except now extended to cover logical/mathematical uncertainty too, and that THIS is what earns you the right to talk about "credence" in a conjecture.

The pushback I wanted to make, and only half-managed after two glasses of wine, is that the inexploitability guarantee is a limit property: its about what happens as the number of trading days goes to infinity, and (unless I'm misremembering) even the original paper has a whole section basically admitting there's no computable bound on how fast any particular sentence's price converges. So when Max says "the market's current price for the Riemann Hypothesis is a rational credence," I don't think he's earned that, because for all anyone knows we could be sitting at a day where the trader who'd eventually correct that price just hasn't shown up yet — nothing in the setup tells you when "eventually" arrives relative to where we actually are. It feels like he's cashing out the normative force of a Dutch-book argument (which in the classical, non-logical case is a here-and-now, synchronic guarantee) on a claim that's only ever licensed in the limit.
