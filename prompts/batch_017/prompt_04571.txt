I'm a PhD student in philosophy currently drafting a dissertation chapter on Descartes's beast-machine doctrine, and for this chapter I've been trying to work from primary sources rather than leaning too heavily on secondary summaries. That has meant sitting with the first English translation of the Discourse on the Method, published in 1649, instead of defaulting to the modern Cottingham/Stoothoff/Murdoch rendering I'd normally cite. The seventeenth-century prose is clumsier, but I've found that its very clumsiness sometimes preserves an argumentative seam that gets smoothed over in modern translations. I've run into exactly such a seam near the end of Part V, and before I commit myself to a reading of it in the chapter, I'd like a second opinion.

Here is the passage, reproduced with its original spelling and punctuation intact:

"Its likewise very remarkable that although there are divers creatures which express more industry then we in some one of their actions; yet we may well perceive, that the same shew none at all in many others: So that what they do better then we, proves not at all that they have reason; for by that reckoning they would have more then any of us, and would do better in all other things; but rather, that they have none at all, and that its Nature onely which works in them according to the disposition of their organs. As wee see a Clock, which is onely composed of wheels and springs, can reckon the hours, and measure the times more exactly then we can with all our prudence.

"After this I had described the reasonable Soul, and made it appear, that it could no way be drawn from the power of the Matter, as other things whereof I had spoken; but that it ought to have been expresly created: And how it suffiseth not for it to be lodg'd in our humane body as a Pilot in his ship, to move its members onely; but also that its necessary it be joyned and united more strongly therewith to have thoughts and appetites like ours, and so make a reall man.

"I have here dilated my self a little on the subject of the Soul, by reason 'tis of most importance; for, next the errour of those who deny God, which I think I have already sufficiently confuted, there is none which sooner estrangeth feeble minds from the right way of vertue, then to imagine that the soul of beasts is of the same nature as ours, and that consequently we have nothing to fear nor hope after this life, no more then flies or ants. Whereas, when we know how different they are, we comprehend much better the reasons which prove that ours is of a nature wholly independing from the body, and consequently that it is not subject to die with it. And that when we see no other cause which destroys it, we are naturally thence moved to judge that it's immortall."

What I can't settle to my own satisfaction is the logical status of that final move. The bulk of Part V argues for the beast-machine thesis on broadly behavioral grounds: the language test, the versatility test, and the observation that uneven competence across tasks - skilled at one thing, hopeless at another - points to mere disposition of organs rather than reason, illustrated by the clock that keeps better time than we can reckon by "prudence." But the closing paragraph treats the entire preceding discussion as though its real point all along were to secure the immortality of the human soul: denying thought to animals is presented as what lets us "comprehend much better" that our own soul is independent of the body and so does not die with it. I can't see the entailment. It seems perfectly coherent to hold both that animals think in some sense and that the human soul is nonetheless an immaterial, separable substance - or to deny both claims together - so the two issues look logically independent to me. Is Descartes here just running two arguments together for rhetorical or pastoral reasons (denying beasts any inner life makes it easier to accept that our own does not perish), or is there a real premise, perhaps drawn from elsewhere in his system, that would make the inference from "animals have no thought" to "our thought is separable from the body" actually valid? I'd be grateful for help reconstructing the strongest version of the argument he might have intended, even if the conclusion is that this closing paragraph is doing more emotional than logical work.
