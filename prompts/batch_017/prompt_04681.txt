I've been slowly working through Rawls with a few people from my rationalist-adjacent meetup (we're doing a "political philosophy for people who mostly read LessWrong and ACX" reading group, don't ask), and we hit a snag in the public reason stuff that's been bugging me for like a week now, so I'm hoping you can untangle it, because right now it just looks like a straight-up motte-and-bailey to me.

Everyone in the group agreed on the basic reading going in: Rawls's public reason doctrine says comprehensive-doctrine arguments, religious ones especially, are illegitimate currency in political debate -- you're supposed to justify coercive laws using reasons that citizens of any reasonable view, or none, could accept, full stop. That matched my prior of Rawls as a guy who quietly wrote religion out of politics, especially once I found out he'd been devout enough as an undergrad to almost go into the seminary and write an earnest Christian senior thesis, before doing a total 180 during WWII -- theres this essay he never published in his lifetime, "On My Religion," where he traces loosing his faith partly to a chaplain who preached a sermon implying God was personally steering enemy fire away from Americans and onto the Japanese. Felt like, ok, of course this guy's mature theory ends up allergic to religious reasoning in public life.

But then somebody in the group dug up "The Idea of Public Reason Revisited," where Rawls goes out of his way to praise the abolitionists and MLK's heavily religious activism as basically the model case, not a violation of public reason at all. That looks to me like he wants it both ways: "comprehensive doctrines are out" is the motte, and then the second anyone brings up MLK he retreats to some much weaker bailey where religious reasons are fine actually, as long as you approve of where they end up. So which is it -- is there some real principled line here I'm missing, or is this basically Rawls giving himself an ad hoc pass so he doesn't have to bite the bullet on condemning the civil rights movement?
