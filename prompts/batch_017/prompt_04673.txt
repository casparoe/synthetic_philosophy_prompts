I've been working through Frank Ramsey's 1926 essay "Truth and Probability" for a graduate reading group on the history of subjective probability. It's usually treated as the ancestor of the Dutch Book argument for probabilism, even though Ramsey never uses that phrase, and the "sure loss" argument itself only shows up several pages after the part I'm stuck on. Before he can even state that later argument, Ramsey has to solve a prior problem: how to define an agent's degrees of belief, and the utilities they attach to outcomes, purely out of their preferences among options, without presupposing a numerical scale for either in advance. The move he makes to do this is notoriously compressed, and it's the part I can't quite get straight.

Here is the passage (both parts are from p. 177 of the original; I've bracketed a short description of what I've cut between them):

"There is first a difficulty which must be dealt with; the propositions like p in the above case which are used as conditions in the options offered may be such that their truth or falsity is an object of desire to the subject. This will be found to complicate the problem, and we have to assume that there are propositions for which this is not the case, which we shall call ethically neutral. [Ramsey adds a footnote about the theory of propositions he is assuming, and only states as an axiom, on the next page, that at least one such proposition exists.] We begin by defining belief of degree 1/2 in an ethically neutral proposition. The subject is said to have belief of degree 1/2 in such a proposition p if he has no preference between the options (1) α if p is true, β if p is false, and (2) α if p is false, β if p is true, but has a preference between α and β simply."

What I'd like help with is the logic of this move, not just a restatement of it. First, why does Ramsey need the notion of an "ethically neutral" proposition at all here — what would go wrong if he instead tried to define belief of degree 1/2 in an arbitrary proposition p directly, by indifference between betting for and against p at some fixed stakes? Second, is Ramsey actually entitled to simply posit that such propositions exist, or does picking one out for a given agent already require exactly the kind of preference comparisons the whole construction is supposed to be generating, so that there's a whiff of circularity here? And third, if there is a real problem in this part of the argument, does it end up threatening the later Dutch Book argument for the probability axioms, or is it a separable difficulty peculiar to Ramsey's utility-first construction — one that an approach like de Finetti's, which measures belief directly through betting quotients on money rather than via an ethically neutral proposition, might simply not inherit?
