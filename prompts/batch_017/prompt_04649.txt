I spent the better part of thirty-five years doing research in statistical mechanics before retiring, and over the past year I have finally been indulging a long-deferred interest in philosophy, working through the classic literature on the foundations of probability in a rather unsystematic, autodidactic way. I mention my background because it is precisely what made me pick up John Venn's "The Logic of Chance" (I am reading the third edition of 1888): in my own work I computed probabilities as long-run relative frequencies over ensembles without ever seriously asking what licensed that identification, and Venn is usually credited with the first fully worked-out defense of exactly that frequency view. I got through the early chapters without much difficulty, but I have been stuck for several days on a passage from the chapter on induction, where Venn turns from inference within a given series to the harder question of how one assigns a series to a newly presented individual case, and raises what strikes me as a rather deep difficulty:

"...it must be remembered that each individual thing has not one distinct and appropriate class or group, to which, and to which alone, it properly belongs. We may indeed be practically in the habit of considering it under such a single aspect, and it may therefore seem to us more familiar when it occupies a place in one series rather than in another; but such a practice is merely customary on our part, not obligatory. It is obvious that every individual thing or event has an indefinite number of properties or attributes observable in it, and might therefore be considered as belonging to an indefinite number of different classes of things. By belonging to any one class it of course becomes at the same time a member of all the higher classes, the genera, of which that class was a species. But, moreover, by virtue of each accidental attribute which it possesses, it becomes a member of a class intersecting, so to say, some of the other classes. John Smith is a consumptive man say, and a native of a northern climate. Being a man he is of course included in the class of vertebrates, also in that of animals, as well as in any higher such classes that there may be. The property of being consumptive refers him to another class, narrower than any of the above; whilst that of being born in a northern climate refers him to a new and distinct class, not conterminous with any of the rest, for there are things born in the north which are not men. When therefore John Smith presents himself to our notice without, so to say, any particular label attached to him informing us under which of his various aspects he is to be viewed, the process of thus referring him to a class becomes to a great extent arbitrary." (The Logic of Chance, 3rd edn., 1888, ch. IX, §§12-13.)

I can see, in outline, that the worry concerns an individual belonging simultaneously to many different classes at once, each presumably with its own statistics, but I am having real trouble grasping why Venn treats this as a deep difficulty for his theory rather than a mere practical inconvenience that careful bookkeeping ("just use the narrowest class for which reliable statistics exist") would dissolve. A former colleague of mine from the lab, who has since become a rather dogmatic Bayesian, insists that this passage alone shows frequentism was doomed from the outset; I would like to be able to judge for myself whether he is right, overstating things, or simply rediscovering something Venn had already anticipated. Could you help me reconstruct, in plain terms, exactly what is at stake here for the frequency interpretation specifically? I would also value your sense of whether this is essentially the difficulty that later became known as the "reference class problem," and, if so, whether a subjectivist about probability or a propensity theorist would regard themselves as facing the very same difficulty, or whether their accounts furnish some resource for escaping it that was simply unavailable to Venn given his commitments.
