During my working years as an experimental physicist, I grew accustomed to counterfactual claims that could never be cashed out empirically — what a photon "would have" done under a polarization measurement that was never actually performed, and how much weight assumptions like counterfactual definiteness can bear in debates around Bell's theorem. Since retiring I have been reading through philosophy of religion for pleasure, and Luis de Molina's doctrine of middle knowledge brought me up short, because the whole apparatus of "counterfactuals of creaturely freedom" seemed to pose a structurally similar puzzle in an entirely different key. I already have the basic architecture down — natural knowledge and middle knowledge as logically (not temporally) prior to God's creative decree, free knowledge as posterior to it — so there's no need to rehearse that part. What I would value help with is the grounding objection itself, stated precisely rather than merely gestured at: what exactly is supposed to be missing from the truth of a proposition like "were S placed in circumstances C, S would freely do A," given that S may never exist and C may never obtain?

Two people in my philosophy reading group disagree sharply about this, and I would like your own considered verdict rather than a mere summary of the dispute. One maintains that the standard Molinist reply succeeds: such a counterfactual is true now in just the way an ordinary future contingent is true now, namely because the relevant circumstance will (or would) eventually supply the grounds, so anyone already committed to bivalence for future-tense claims has no principled basis for singling out counterfactuals of freedom for suspicion. The other insists there is a genuine disanalogy — an ordinary future contingent will eventually be made true by an actual event, whereas a counterfactual whose antecedent circumstance is never actualized can never receive its grounds even in principle, no matter how much time elapses.

Separately, but perhaps not entirely separately, I keep running into the claim that middle knowledge buys God a providential advantage that the rival "simple foreknowledge" view cannot supply, on the grounds that simple foreknowledge is only available to God once everything is already, in effect, settled, whereas knowledge that is logically prior to the creative decree lets God select among feasible worlds in advance. I can restate that argument well enough, but I cannot tell whether its force is fully independent of how the grounding dispute above gets resolved, or whether a successful grounding objection would take the providential-advantage argument down with it too. I would be grateful if you could work through both issues in some depth and then say something about how tightly you think they are actually connected.
