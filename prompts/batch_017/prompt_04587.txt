I write fiction that keeps circling back to brilliant, cold-minded men whose formal genius sits uneasily beside their private convictions, and lately I've been reading up on Gottlob Frege as real-world ballast for a character I'm building. Everyone who spends time with Frege eventually learns that the diary he kept in the last year of his life is startlingly antisemitic. Fewer people know what the philosopher Stephen D'Arcy documents in his book *Frege and Fascism*: that Frege didn't merely hold these opinions alongside his logic, he pressed its own vocabulary into their service. To defend favoring Germans of "Aryan descent" over Jews against the charge of arbitrariness, Frege argued that the relevant bond was "hereditary" in exactly the technical sense he had spent decades formalizing: a property that a genealogical sequence transmits from each member to the next — the very relation, later named "the ancestral" by Russell and Whitehead, on which his logical definition of the natural numbers depends.

Argue for the following uncomfortable thesis: that this was a logically valid use of Frege's own apparatus, not a fallacy of equivocation or simply a wicked man's abuse of a neutral tool. To be clear, this is a claim about formal validity, not about the moral standing of Frege's conclusion, which is monstrous beyond any doubt; you should locate the flaw strictly in the premises he was willing to accept, not in the inference he drew from them. Use this to rebut the more comfortable position — one that even Frege's greatest champion, Michael Dummett, seemed to hold in practice, despite being, in his own words, "deeply shocked" by the diary — that a thinker's technical philosophy and his politics are separate rooms in the same house, each unaccountable to the other.
