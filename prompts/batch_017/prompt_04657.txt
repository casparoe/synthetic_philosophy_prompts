I've been teaching myself Mandarin over the past couple of years, mostly through apps and graded readers, and a while ago I came across the four-character phrase 白马非马 (bái mǎ fēi mǎ, "a white horse is not a horse") glossed as an idiom for hair-splitting or sophistic argument. Curious about where it came from, I dug a little further and found that it's actually the title of a short dialogue attributed to Gongsun Long, a thinker associated with the "School of Names" during the Warring States period, who apparently argued in earnest that a white horse is not a horse. I read through a couple of English translations of the whole piece, and most of it made a certain kind of sense once I sat with it: the point that "horse" names a shape while "white" names a colour, and the point that if you ask someone to bring you "a horse" any colour will do, but if you ask for "a white horse" a yellow or black horse won't do, so the two expressions can't be picking out exactly the same thing.

Where I got completely stuck is the last move Gongsun Long makes, after the person questioning him has pushed back several times. Piecing together a couple of translations I found (my classical Chinese isn't remotely good enough to read the original, though I can at least pick out the characters in the key phrase, 白者不定所白), the argument goes roughly like this:

"Whiteness, considered just by itself, does not fix on any particular white object — and there is nothing wrong with leaving it that way. But 'white horse' is a phrase in which whiteness has been fixed onto something white, and what has thus been fixed is no longer whiteness taken alone. Now 'horse' excludes nothing on the basis of colour, so a yellow horse or a black horse can equally answer to it. 'White horse' does exclude on the basis of colour: a yellow or black horse is ruled out, so that only a white horse can answer to it. What excludes nothing is not the same as what excludes something. Hence, a white horse is not a horse."

I mentioned this to a friend who took a couple of philosophy courses years ago, and even she just shrugged and said it sounded like wordplay dressed up as logic. What I can't work out is whether this final step is actually doing new argumentative work or is just the earlier "seeking a horse vs. seeking a white horse" point dressed up in different terms. Is there a genuine distinction here between "whiteness on its own" and "whiteness as pinned to some particular thing" — something like the difference between a general property and one of its instances — or is Gongsun Long just trading on a hidden shift in what "is not" means, between "is not identical to" and "is not a case of"? And however we read it, does the argument actually hold up on its own terms, or does it just push the same sleight of hand one step further back? I'd genuinely like to understand the mechanics of this passage rather than just filing it away as ancient nonsense, so I'd appreciate help working through the logic.
