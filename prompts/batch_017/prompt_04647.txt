A forecasting group I belong to, which grew out of a local LessWrong meetup, has spent weeks re-litigating an argument between two of its more stubborn members. I'd like your help adjudicating it more carefully than either of them will accept from me.

It started when Ilana submitted "20-40%" on a question the group had no real base rate for, defending the range as an honest way of representing severe uncertainty rather than false precision. Marcus objected: (1) imagine a coin of totally unknown bias. If your credence in heads is genuinely the entire interval [0,1], then a bet that loses $10 on heads and wins $15 on tails, and a second bet that wins $15 on heads and loses $10 on tails, are each something your own decision rule permits you to decline -- yet declining both leaves you strictly worse off than accepting both, since the pair's payoffs net out to a guaranteed +$5 regardless of how the coin lands. Ranges aren't humility, he said; they're a way of quietly leaving money on the table. Ilana answered that (2) this only defeats decision rules that judge each bet in isolation: judge the pair as a single offer and its payout is a guaranteed +$5 under every distribution in the interval, so a rule that looks at the whole menu, not one bet at a time, never declines it -- which is exactly what the theory is supposed to guarantee, namely that you might still take a bet that turns out badly, but never one that was a sure loss going in. Marcus came back with what he considered the real knockdown: (3) there is a documented result, apparently called "dilation," in which conditioning an imprecise credence on new evidence that doesn't even seem to bear on the question can make the interval wider rather than narrower -- which he took as proof that the whole approach gets the direction of learning backwards, since no honest epistemology should let gathering more evidence leave you strictly more ignorant than you started. Ilana replied that (4) dilation only shows up for a specific, identifiable pattern of dependence between the evidence and the hypothesis -- roughly, when what you learn is uninformative except insofar as it's tangled up with some further quantity you have no opinion about -- and that a sharp Bayesian facing the same setup would simply have to assume some particular correlation, silently resolving an ambiguity that the widened interval is at least being honest about. Neither budged, and both clearly know more of the relevant literature than I do.

Could you go through the exchange move by move -- (1) through (4) -- and assess who has the better of it at each step, rather than just declaring an overall winner? Please also flag whether either of them is resting on a premise that the other should have challenged but didn't.
