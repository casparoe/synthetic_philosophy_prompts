so I've been down a rabbit hole on the open-borders-vs-right-to-exclude debate lately (read alot of stuff about it after doom-scrolling twitter/X during a slow sprint, dont ask) and a bunch of people keep quoting this one bit from Kant's "Perpetual Peace" essay from 1795 - I think it's the "third definitive article" or something, about hospitality and foreigners. Here's the actual passage, from an old 1897 translation so the phrasing is a bit stiff:

"Here, as in the former articles, the question is not one of philanthropy but of right. Hospitality here signifies the right of a foreigner, in consequence of his arrival on the soil of another, not to be treated by him as an enemy. He may be expelled, if that can be done without his destruction; but so long as he keeps his place and conducts himself peacefully, he must not be treated in a hostile way. He cannot lay claim to be treated thus because of any right as a guest, for this would require a special friendly agreement to consider him for a time as a member of some household. His claim is based on a right of visitation, common to all men, by virtue of which he may join any society of men, on account of the right of the common possession of the surface of the earth, over which people cannot spread abroad indefinitely, but must finally endure living near one another. Originally, however, no one had any more right than another to occupy any particular portion of the earth's surface."

Some of the pro-open-borders folks quote the "common possession of the earth" part like it settles things in their favor, and then other people quote literally the same paragraph to argue Kant is totally fine with states kicking foreigners out whenever they want, since he straight up says there's no right to be treated as a "guest." I've got zero philosophy background (just a backend dev) so I honestly can't tell if these two readings are compatible or if someone's cherry picking. Like, what work is this "right of visitation" actually doing if you can be expelled the second you show up anyway - is it just a fancy way of saying "don't murder people at the border" and nothing more, or is there something stronger buried in there? Would appreciate a straight answer on what Kant is actually committing himself to.
