Is religious devotion really a peacock's tail?

The handicap principle, as I understand it, holds that ornaments like the peacock's tail are honest signals of quality precisely because they are costly: a sickly male can't afford to grow and haul around such an extravagant tail, while a genuinely healthy one can. But what actually does the explanatory work here isn't just that the tail is expensive in some absolute sense — it's that the expense is differential. It has to be relatively cheaper, in terms of the animal's own prospects, for a high-quality male to bear than for a low-quality one. If the cost fell equally on everyone regardless of quality, nothing would stop weak males from paying it too, and the tail would stop carrying any information at all. From what I can tell, this was close to the original objection to Zahavi's idea — Dawkins among others apparently thought it looked too easy to cheat — and the theory only became widely accepted once someone put it on firmer game-theoretic footing and showed that an honest equilibrium really does exist, but only under that differential-cost condition.

Somewhere in my reading I ran into people extending this same logic to human religious behavior: fasting, circumcision, strict dietary laws, distinctive dress, tithing away a chunk of one's income, all treated as costly signals that filter out people who aren't genuinely committed to a religious community, so that whoever remains can trust and cooperate with one another. There's apparently an old comparison of nineteenth-century American communes showing that the ones demanding more sacrifice from their members survived longer, and this gets cited as evidence for the theory.

When I tried to hold this analogy to the same standard as the peacock case, though, I couldn't locate the differential cost. A sincerely devout person who fasts gets exactly as hungry as someone going through the motions to keep up appearances — the stomach has no access to what the mind believes. So the ritual's cost, taken by itself, doesn't obviously fall more lightly on the "high-quality," genuinely committed type, the way the tail's cost falls more lightly on a healthy bird. That leaves me wondering whether at least two or three different things are being flattened into a single label. One possibility is that the immediate cost really is identical for everyone, but what it buys — long-term trust, mutual aid, standing in the community — is worth more to someone who is genuinely invested, so the same price is a better deal for them even though it hurts exactly as much upfront; that looks like a different kind of logic from the peacock's, closer to a bet that only pays off for people who expect to stick around. A second possibility is that the ritual's cost is barely doing any of the honesty-guaranteeing work, and what actually deters cheating is simply that the community is watching and would punish or expel anyone caught cutting corners, which sounds less like a handicap and more like an arbitrary badge enforced by social retaliation. Hazing-type initiation rituals in fraternities or gangs seem like a cleaner test of this second possibility, since there's no obvious "delayed payoff" story available to rescue them.

If any of this is right, then "costly signaling," at least as it gets used outside strict biology, might be quietly gluing together two or three distinct honesty-guaranteeing mechanisms that only really coincide in the original peacock case.
