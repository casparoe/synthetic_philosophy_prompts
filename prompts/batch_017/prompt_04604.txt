For my early modern philosophy seminar this semester, we've been working through Book II of Locke's *Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, and this week's session centered on the chapter on perception, where he brings in Molyneux's famous puzzle about the newly sighted blind man. I think I follow the thought experiment itself well enough, but a remark our professor made in passing — that several commentators think Locke's answer to Molyneux sits awkwardly with something he says earlier in Book II — has been bothering me ever since. A few of us stayed after class arguing about it and couldn't reach a consensus, so I wanted to get another perspective. Here is the passage in question, from Book II, Chapter IX, Section 8:

To which purpose I shall here insert a problem of that very ingenious and studious promoter of real knowledge, the learned and worthy Mr. Molyneux, which he was pleased to send me in a letter some months since; and it is this: "Suppose a man born blind, and now adult, and taught by his touch to distinguish between a cube and a sphere of the same metal, and nighly of the same bigness, so as to tell, when he felt one and the other, which is the cube, which the sphere. Suppose then the cube and sphere placed on a table, and the blind man be made to see: quaere, whether by his sight, before he touched them, he could now distinguish and tell which is the globe, which the cube?" To which the acute and judicious proposer answers, "Not. For, though he has obtained the experience of how a globe, how a cube affects his touch, yet he has not yet obtained the experience, that what affects his touch so or so, must affect his sight so or so; or that a protuberant angle in the cube, that pressed his hand unequally, shall appear to his eye as it does in the cube." I agree with this thinking gentleman, whom I am proud to call my friend, in his answer to this problem; and am of opinion that the blind man, at first sight, would not be able with certainty to say which was the globe, which the cube, whilst he only saw them; though he could unerringly name them by his touch, and certainly distinguish them by the difference of their figures felt.

What's tripping me up is this. Earlier, in Chapter V ("Of Simple Ideas of Divers Senses"), Locke says that figure — along with space, motion, and rest — is among the ideas "we get by more than one sense," since such ideas "make perceivable impressions, both on the eyes and touch." That sounds like the claim that the very same idea of, say, cubical figure is available through either sense-channel, rather than there being two distinct ideas, a tactile one and a visual one, that merely happen to correlate. But if that's right, I don't understand why any additional "experience" linking touch-impressions to sight-impressions should be needed for the newly sighted man to recognize the cube: surely he already possesses the idea of that very figure from touch, and all sight would need to do is deliver that same idea through a different door. Yet Locke sides with Molyneux's negative answer anyway, and rests it precisely on the man's lack of experience correlating the two senses. Could you help me spell out, in Locke's own terms, what exactly is doing the work here — what this missing "experience" amounts to on his view — and whether his verdict on the Molyneux question can really be squared with the doctrine of common sensibles from Chapter V, or whether our professor's aside was pointing to a genuine tension in Locke's account? I'd appreciate a fairly detailed reconstruction, since I'd like to bring a clear position back to seminar.
