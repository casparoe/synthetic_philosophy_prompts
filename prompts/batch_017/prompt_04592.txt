For my seminar on the philosophy of law, we have spent the last few weeks working through the Hart–Fuller exchange: first Hart's 1958 Harvard Law Review article, which uses the "grudge informer" case (the postwar German prosecution of a woman who had denounced her husband to the Nazi authorities under wartime anti-sedition laws) to argue that the Nazi statute remained valid law, however wicked, and that a court wishing to punish her should have done so openly through retroactive legislation rather than by pretending the statute had never been law at all; and then Fuller's reply, later elaborated in The Morality of Law, where he sets out the eight principles of the "inner morality of law" — generality, promulgation, prospectivity (non-retroactivity), clarity, non-contradiction, possibility of compliance, constancy through time, and congruence between the rules as announced and as actually administered — through the parable of the hapless lawgiver Rex. By now, though, we have picked apart the grudge informer case so many times in section that I would like to test my grasp of Fuller's framework on a scenario nobody in class has already worked through for me.

Here is the case I have constructed for my term paper. A newly founded student union at my university has adopted a disciplinary code for academic misconduct. The code is posted on the union's website and, on its face, applies to every student alike.
