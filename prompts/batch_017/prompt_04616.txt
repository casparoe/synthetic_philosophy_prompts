A friend and I spent most of a long train journey arguing about something that started as a practical question and ended up as a dispute about the foundations of rational choice. We were splitting a shared bankroll across a series of positive-expected-value bets, and I argued for sizing our stakes with the Kelly criterion; she was happy to go along with it but insisted the whole thing was philosophically uninteresting, since betting to maximize the geometric growth rate of one's wealth is nothing more than ordinary expected-utility maximization performed by an agent whose utility happens to be logarithmic in wealth. Pick the right utility function, plug it into the von Neumann–Morgenstern machinery, and Kelly betting falls out as a special case, no different in kind from any other risk-averse expected-utility maximizer.

I half-remembered an argument, from Scott Garrabrant's writing on "geometric rationality," that this deflationary story gets things backwards: that Kelly betting, alongside things like Nash bargaining, is supposed to be an instance of a broader geometric-rationality principle, and that this whole cluster is explicitly presented as violating the independence axiom and therefore as failing to be VNM-rational at all -- not reducible to any expected-utility maximizer, log-utility or otherwise, because such an agent can end up with a strict preference for randomizing between two options even when neither option is independently better, something no fixed-utility-function maximizer could ever rationalize. I could gesture at the shape of the argument -- something about aggregating over uncertainty as to which of several candidate utility functions, or people's preferences, is the one that counts, in a way that behaves like a geometric rather than an arithmetic average -- but I could not reconstruct it precisely enough to convince her, and we left it unresolved.

Could you settle this properly? I would like a precise explanation of the actual argument for treating geometric rationality as a genuinely distinct, strictly weaker standard of rationality than expected-utility maximization, rather than as expected-utility maximization wearing a log-utility costume -- including exactly why it is supposed to conflict with independence, and what it would even mean, mechanically, for maximizing a geometric rather than an arithmetic expectation to make randomizing between two options strictly better than either option on its own. And if, having reconstructed the argument carefully, you conclude that my friend's deflationary reading is basically right after all, I would like to hear that too, and why.
