so i went down a youtube rabbit hole on philosophy of mind stuff and ended up reading this old bit from William James' "Principles of Psychology" (1890), from the chapter where he talks about the "automaton-theory" — basically the idea that consciousness is just along for the ride and never actually does anything, kind of like dead code that never gets executed. he describes the view like this:

"So the melody floats from the harp-string, but neither checks nor quickens its vibrations; so the shadow runs alongside the pedestrian, but in no way influences his steps."

then a bit later he tries to argue against it:

"It is very generally admitted, though the point would be hard to prove, that consciousness grows the more complex and intense the higher we rise in the animal kingdom. That of a man must exceed that of an oyster. From this point of view it seems an organ, superadded to the other organs which maintain the animal in the struggle for existence; and the presumption of course is that it helps him in some way in the struggle, just as they do. But it cannot help him without being in some way efficacious and influencing the course of his bodily history."

i get the general gist (mind-does-nothing = bad) but i can't pin down the actual logic here. is he just saying "evolution wouldn't carry around useless baggage," full stop, or is there something more specific about the complexity thing hes leaning on that i'm missing? and second question — even if his argument totally works, doesnt he just walk face first into the modern problem where the physical stuff already seems causally sufficient to explain all behavior on its own, leaving nothing left over for the mental to actually contribute? or are these two separate arguments that don't really bear on each other? genuinely can't tell if james would even have a response to that, or if it just wasn't a thing yet in his day.
