I've been teaching the Hart-Fuller exchange in my jurisprudence seminar this week (right after Austin, before we get to Dworkin), and a student cornered me after class with an objection I haven't been able to shake. Her argument: Hart spends the first half of "Positivism and the Separation of Law and Morals" insisting that legal validity and moral merit are seperate questions -- that's the whole point of the seperability thesis. But then he gets to the grudge informer case and says the honest solution is a frankly retroactive statute punishing the wife, rather than pretending along with the German court, Radbruch, or Fuller that the Nazi denunciation law was never really valid in the first place. Her point is that this is Hart himself reaching for a moral judgment to override something he's just finished telling us was, as a matter of positive law, perfectly valid -- so isn't he doing exactly what he accuses the natural lawyers of doing, just one step later, at the point of enforcement rather than the point of definition? She thinks this shows positivism only looks clean on paper, and that Fuller was basically right that you can't keep law and morality apart once an actual case is on the table.

I didn't have a great answer for her on the spot, and the more I turn it over the more I suspect she's found a genuine crack rather than just a rhetorical gotcha. Before I write back to her, can you help me think it through: is there a principled reason Hart's move in the grudge-informer case doesn't actually concede anything to Fuller, or has she caught him in a real inconsistency?
