I'm building a lecture for a graduate seminar that pairs philosophy of mind with philosophy of religion, and I wanted to use Gödel as the throughline connecting the two halves. Here's the story I've been telling myself: the incompleteness theorems show that no single consistent formal system can prove every arithmetical truth, even ones the mind can plainly see are true, which is why Gödel concluded, in his 1951 Gibbs lecture, that the mind can't just be equivalent to a finite machine running an algorithm. What I hadn't quite clocked until I started rereading Hao Wang's book this week is that this is apparently also the technical engine behind Gödel's private argument for an afterlife: since the mind provably outruns any mechanical/physical system, its not the kind of thing that has to perish along with the body, so it must carry on existing after death. So on my reading its really one long, continuous argument running from the 1931 theorem, through anti-mechanism about the mind, to survival of bodily death, rather than three seperate things that just happen to share an author.

A colleague I ran this by thinks I'm being sloppy, but I suspect she's just being extra cautious the way logicians always are about this sort of thing. Before I put "incompleteness → non-mechanical mind → immortality" on a slide, I wanted a sanity check: is that really the shape of Gödel's own reasoning, and does it also mean that anyone nowadays who accepts the Lucas-Penrose argument (that Gödel's theorem shows minds aren't machines) is thereby committed to something like Gödel's belief in life after death, whether they like it or not?
