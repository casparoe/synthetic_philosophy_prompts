Okay so this isn't an argument I was actually part of, I just watched it happen at my school's philosophy club and I still can't decide who came out on top. Our club's advisor had us read the private-language section of Wittgenstein's Philosophical Investigations (roughly §243 through §315 — the diary case and the beetle-in-a-box bit) for that week, and afterward two members, Kayla and Marcus, got into a whole thing about what the argument actually proves. I'll number their points so its easier to reference: (1) Kayla said the diary case in §258 shows that talk of a private inner sensation was never coherent to begin with — the guy in the example has no independent way to check whether he's using his made-up sign "S" correctly, so whatever he judges to be right just counts as right by default, which really just means "right" has stopped meaning anything there, so there was never a real private item for "S" to succeed or fail at naming. (2) Marcus said that's too fast, because a few sections later, in §304, Wittgenstein directly denies he's claiming sensations don't exist — he's only denying that a word can get its entire meaning from a private ostensive definition with zero outside check. The sensation itself could be completely real, it's just not what's doing the meaning-fixing work for the word "pain."

(3) Kayla pushed back with the beetle-in-the-box passage (§293): if what's inside the box makes zero difference to how the language actually functions, to the point that the box could be empty and nothing about the language-game would change, then insisting there could still be something in there, we just can't talk about it, is a distinction without a difference — that's eliminativism about the inner item whether or not Wittgenstein wants the label. (4) Marcus answered that saying the item drops out as irrelevant is a claim about what fixes the meaning of "pain" (public criteria — behavior, context, all that), not a claim that nothing's there, which is supposedly the entire reason Wittgenstein bothers adding, right after, that a sensation is "not a something, but not a nothing either."

Then it escalated: (5) Kayla brought up Kripke's book on all this, saying his reading ties the private-language bit to the rule-following paradox from earlier in the Investigations (the plus-vs-quus thing) and concludes there's no fact of the matter about correct usage anywhere, public or private — it's all just communal agreement — so the private side was never uniquely worse off than the public side, which she thinks means her original point survives, just one level up and more general. (6) Marcus said that's circular, since a lot of Wittgenstein scholars (he specifically name-dropped Baker and Hacker) think that whole "Kripkenstein" skeptical-paradox reading is something Kripke brought to the text rather than something actually argued for in the Investigations, so you can't use it to settle what §243-315 itself shows.

Neither of them budged, and honestly I don't know this material well enough to referee it myself, so I figured I'd just wrote it all out and get an outside opinion. Could you go through moves 1 through 6 and say who's got the stronger position at each individual step, instead of just handing down one overall winner, and call out anything you think they're both getting wrong or just talking past each other on?
