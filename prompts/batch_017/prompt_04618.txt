Henry Sidgwick devoted much of the last two decades of his life to psychical research, serving as the founding president of the Society for Psychical Research from 1882 and personally overseeing investigations into telepathy, mediumship, and reported apparitions -- investigations rigorous enough that his own circle uncovered cheating among some of their most promising subjects, the Creery sisters among them. This was no idle curiosity apart from his moral philosophy: The Methods of Ethics ends by identifying what Sidgwick called the dualism of practical reason, his finding that rational egoism and utilitarianism each present themselves as self-evident axioms of practical reason, yet can issue conflicting verdicts in practice, with no higher-order principle available to rank one above the other short of some guarantee that duty and self-interest coincide in the end. Sidgwick seems to have hoped that empirical evidence of personal survival after death, or of a benevolent order governing the cosmos, might supply exactly this missing premise; yet he died in 1900 still unable to say with confidence whether he believed such an order existed or merely needed it to. Write an essay assessing whether Sidgwick's psychical research program is best understood as a rational, if unsuccessful, response to a genuine gap in his ethical theory, or as a case of motivated reasoning at odds with the critical rigor he prized everywhere else in his philosophy. Reach and defend a definite verdict rather than simply cataloguing considerations on both sides.
