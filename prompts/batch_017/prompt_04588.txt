I'm working on a piece about the long-running "levels of selection" dispute in evolutionary biology, and I keep returning to a single passage that both camps—multilevel-selection theorists in the mold of Elliott Sober and David Sloan Wilson, and their kin-selectionist critics—claim as a forebear. It's from Chapter 5 of Darwin's *The Descent of Man* (1871). Having just argued that a tribe with more courageous, sympathetic members would triumph over a rival tribe, Darwin pauses to flag a difficulty:

"It is extremely doubtful whether the offspring of the more sympathetic and benevolent parents, or of those who were the most faithful to their comrades, would be reared in greater numbers than the children of selfish and treacherous parents belonging to the same tribe. He who was ready to sacrifice his life, as many a savage has been, rather than betray his comrades, would often leave no offspring to inherit his noble nature... Therefore, it hardly seems probable that the number of men gifted with such virtues... could be increased through natural selection, that is, by the survival of the fittest; for we are not here speaking of one tribe being victorious over another."

He then gestures at a couple of possible fixes—reciprocity ("if he aided his fellow-men, he would commonly receive aid in return") and reputation ("the praise and the blame of our fellow-men")—before returning to the tribal story: "an increase in the number of well-endowed men and an advancement in the standard of morality will certainly give an immense advantage to one tribe over another. A tribe including many members who... were always ready to aid one another, and to sacrifice themselves for the common good, would be victorious over most other tribes; and this would be natural selection."

Here is what I can't settle on my own, and would value your help with. First, is Darwin quietly running together three mechanisms that modern theory treats as rivals—reciprocal altruism, reputation effects, and genuine between-group selection—or is there a reading on which they cohere as a single process? Second, since he never mentions relatedness, and these tribes were presumably kin groups, do the kin-selectionists have a point that this is inclusive fitness in disguise, or is there a principled sense in which it counts as group selection proper, rather than the difference being an artifact of how one partitions fitness effects after the fact—a worry familiar from debates over the Price equation?
