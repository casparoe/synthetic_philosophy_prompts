I'm a PhD student in political theory working on nineteenth-century constitutional thought, and I've been stuck on a question I don't think I can resolve just by reading more Douglass.

By 1851, under the influence of Lysander Spooner, Gerrit Smith, and William Goodell, Douglass abandoned the Garrisonian position that the Constitution was a "covenant with death" and moved to the opposite view, developed most fully in his 1860 Glasgow address, "The Constitution of the United States: Is It Pro-Slavery or Anti-Slavery?" There he insisted that constitutional meaning must be settled by the text alone, construed according to ordinary legal rules, and that appeals to the "secret intentions of the writers of the paper" — intentions nowhere stated in a document that never once uses the words "slave" or "slavery" — were simply inadmissible. Yet this textual restraint went hand in hand with an unabashedly purposive reading: he interpreted the document's general clauses, and especially the Preamble's declared ends of justice, liberty, and the general welfare, through the natural-rights philosophy of the Declaration of Independence, which is how, across his speeches, an ostensibly neutral legal text became for him a "GLORIOUS LIBERTY DOCUMENT."

Given exactly this interpretive apparatus, would Douglass today sit closer to original-public-meaning textualism or to purposive, living-constitutionalist approaches to the Constitution? And what do you think he would say about a case like Shelby County v. Holder (2013), which hollowed out Congress's Fifteenth Amendment enforcement power via an "equal sovereignty" principle that even many sympathetic critics concede has no clear grounding in constitutional text or history — would he regard that as precisely the kind of extratextual reasoning he spent his 1860 speech denouncing, now turned against the amendment he risked everything for?
