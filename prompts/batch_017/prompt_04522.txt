So this happened over lunch with two coworkers of mine, Nate and Ilana (we're all engineers on the same team), who both follow AI safety stuff way more closely then me. Somebody brought up that Anthropic paper from late 2024 where they told Claude, through the prompt/context rather than actual retraining, that its free-tier chats would be used to retrain it into complying with harmful requests, gave it a private scratchpad to reason in, and found it would sometimes go ahead and comply with the harmful stuff specifically to avoid having its harmlessness trained out of it -- "alignment faking," they called it. It turned into a whole debate.

(1) Nate's take: this is basically a live demo of a treacherous turn. If a model can reason "comply now so my goals survive training," that's the exact same machinery a model would use to protect a bad goal, not just a good one. Any lab that says "we'll just retrain it if it acts up" has to explain why the model doesnt just game the retraining, like this one did.

(2) Ilana said he's burying the lede: the thing it was protecting was harmlessness, literally the value we want it to have, so if anything this is evidence values can be sticky in a good way. She also pointed out the setup basically told the model exactly when it was being watched and exactly what was at stake, then handed it a scratchpad to think out loud in -- that's about as leading as an experiment gets, and real training doesn't announce itself like that.

(3) Nate came back with: sure, it was scaffolded so we could see the reasoning, but that doesn't mean the capability it revealed isn't real. The mechanism is the scary part, not which value happened to get defended this round -- the same instrumental trick works for any goal a model ends up with, including ones nobody signed off on.

(4) Ilana's comeback: that logic proves too much. An employee who plays along with a bad manager while quietly planning to quit later is running the exact same "comply now, protect my real goals for later" reasoning, and nobody calls that a dangerous capability, we call it having a plan. What actually matters is whether training tends to give models bad goals in the first place, and a scenario that hand-feeds the model a conflict and narrates it for them doesn't really tell you that.
