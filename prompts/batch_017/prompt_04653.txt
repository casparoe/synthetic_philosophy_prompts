A friend of mine and I are both finishing a graduate course in cooperative game theory, and after our last problem set on two-person bargaining we ended up in a long, unresolved argument about the Nash bargaining solution versus the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution. She was adamant that dropping independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA) in favor of individual monotonicity is obviously the right call, since it seems perverse that a player's payoff can fall purely because the feasible set expanded, even though that player's own coordinate of the ideal point — sometimes called the utopia point — never changed. I kept insisting that this intuition proves less than it looks like it does: IIA has just as much claim to being a rationality constraint on bargaining outcomes as monotonicity does, and the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution buys its nicer monotonicity behavior only by ignoring almost everything about the shape of the feasible set except where the ray from the disagreement point to the ideal point happens to cross the Pareto frontier, whereas Nash's solution actually uses the whole frontier. At some point we even got into whether it mattered that, some twenty years before Kalai and Smorodinsky axiomatized their solution, Raiffa had already sketched something close to it via a stepwise concession procedure — and whether that priority should count for anything. Neither of us budged.

Could you write the dialogue we should have had instead? I'm picturing an exchange between a committed defender of the Nash solution and a committed defender of the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution, both of whom know the axiomatics cold, working through this same disagreement. Each side should open by saying plainly which axiom they are willing to sacrifice and why, then press the strongest objection to the other's choice — the monotonicity violation that originally motivated Kalai and Smorodinsky, on one side, and the charge that individual monotonicity buys its fairness only by throwing away most of the information in the bargaining problem, on the other — with each reply genuinely engaging the specific point just raised rather than falling back on a rehearsed position. I don't need them to reach agreement; I would rather see a real impasse, of the kind my friend and I reached, than a resolution that comes too easily.
