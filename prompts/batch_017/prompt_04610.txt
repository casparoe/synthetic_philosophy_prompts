A few of us in my rationalist reading group have spent the last several weeks working through Popper, partly by way of David Deutsch, who is something of a touchstone figure in that world. In the process we stumbled onto a corner of Popper's philosophy that I have never once seen discussed on LessWrong or Astral Codex Ten, even though it seems exactly like the sort of thing this audience ought to enjoy dissecting: his attempt to give a formal definition of verisimilitude, or "closeness to the truth." The motivation, as I understand it, was to rescue falsificationism from a bleak implication. If every scientific theory ever held has eventually been refuted, then unless some false theories can be said to be closer to the truth than others, the history of science looks less like progress and more like an endless sequence of misses.

Popper's actual proposal, though, turns out to be a mess, and I would like to understand it well enough to explain it properly rather than hand-wave through it. He defined a theory's truth content as the set of its true logical consequences and its falsity content as the set of its false ones, and said that a theory B has more verisimilitude than a theory A when B's truth content is at least as large as A's and its falsity content is no larger, with strict improvement somewhere. What apparently sank this proposal is a result David Miller and Pavel Tichý proved independently in 1974: whenever a false theory B says strictly more than another false theory A, the extra content it adds is guaranteed to include new falsehoods along with any new truths, so B's falsity content grows in lockstep with its truth content, and Popper's ordering can never actually settle which of two false theories is closer to the truth. The obvious patch, comparing truth content alone and ignoring falsity content, avoids that trap but falls into a worse one: it lets you inflate a theory's apparent verisimilitude simply by conjoining arbitrary falsehoods onto it, so long as the result is logically stronger than what you started with.

What I would like from you is not a summary of any of this (I already have the Stanford Encyclopedia entry open in another tab) but a worked example of your own devising. Invent a pair of rival false theories, beliefs, or models such that any reasonable person would judge one clearly closer to the truth than the other. Draw the example from any domain other than "counting some quantity" (à la counting planets) or toy weather-predicate logic, since those are the only cases I ever see used. Then show, in enough detail that the underlying logical structure is actually visible, how Popper's official qualitative definition handles your pair: does it deliver the intuitive verdict, deliver the opposite one, or fail to rank the two at all? If it fails, tell me whether a similarity-based repair — one that compares theories by how close the possible worlds consistent with them are to actuality, rather than by counting entailed sentences — rescues your intuitive verdict, or whether it merely relocates the problem, for instance by making the ranking flip under an equally natural but differently chosen descriptive vocabulary.
