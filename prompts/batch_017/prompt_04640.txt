A friend of mine and I spent most of Sunday evening arguing about Isaac Newton, of all people, after I mentioned that he wrote far more on biblical chronology and prophecy over his lifetime than he ever did on physics. My friend's position was that this is little more than a curiosity to file alongside the alchemy: an embarrassing private obsession of an otherwise brilliant man, not something anyone should mistake for real scholarship. I pushed back, because I'd read that Newton's rejection of the Trinity wasn't just a private theological hunch but was grounded in what he considered rigorous textual criticism of the Greek New Testament, well before that kind of source criticism became a standard part of biblical studies. Neither of us had actually read any of the argument itself, though, so afterwards I went digging to see who was closer to right.

What I found was a chunk of "An Historical Account of Two Notable Corruptions of Scripture," a long letter Newton sent to John Locke in November 1690 (published, despite its subtitle "in a Letter to a Friend," under no such anonymity once it finally appeared in print in 1754, nearly thirty years after Newton's death). In it, Newton tries to show that two of the New Testament's clearest prooftexts for the Trinity, 1 John 5:7 and 1 Timothy 3:16, are not original but later scribal corruptions. Here is the portion dealing with 1 Timothy 3:16, where he lays out the philological case:

"What the Latines have done to this text the Greeks have done to that of S. Paul 1 Tim. 3.16. For by changing Ο in ΟΣ & both into ΘΣ (the abbreviation of Θεὸς) they now read *Great is the mystery of godlinesse, God manifested in the flesh*: whereas all the Churches for the first four or five hundred years, & the authors of all the ancient Versions, Ierome as well as the rest, read, *Great is the mystery of godliness, which was manifested in the flesh*. For this is the common reading of the Ethiopic, Syriac & Latine Versions to this day, Ierome's manuscripts having given him no occasion to correct the old Vulgar Latine in this place. Grotius adds the Arabic, but the Egyptian Arabic Version has Θεὸς, & so has the above mentioned Sclavonian Version of Cyrillus. For these two Versions were made long after the sixt Century wherein the corruption began. With the ancienter Versions agree the writers of the first five Centuries both Greeks & Latines. For they in all their discourses to prove the Deity of the Son, never allege this text (that I can find,) as they would all have done, & some of them frequently, had they read God manifested in the flesh, & therefore they read Ὃ."

I can follow the general shape of the claim, that "which was manifested in the flesh" somehow turned into "God manifested in the flesh," but I lose the thread on the mechanics. Newton seems to describe this as happening in two distinct steps at the level of individual Greek letters rather than in one jump. Could you reconstruct exactly what those two steps are and explain why each would have been graphically plausible for a scribe to make, whether by accident or design? I would also appreciate help with the logical structure of his second argument, the claim that Tertullian, Cyprian, and other early writers "never allege this text" in their defenses of Christ's divinity: why does Newton think their silence counts as positive evidence rather than a mere absence of evidence, and does that inference actually hold up? Finally, I'm curious whether, in your assessment, this sort of argument met the best textual-critical standards available in 1690, given that Newton was, to put it mildly, not a disinterested party on the underlying theological question.
