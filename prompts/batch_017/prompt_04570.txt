so this semester I'm taking a seminar (cross listed with the law school) called "Obligation, Promising, and Contract," and this week's topic kind of broke my brain: why are we actually bound to keep our promises? My professor walked us through basically the whole standard menu of answers people have given: Hume and Rawls's conventionalist "practice" theory (a promise only obligates you because there's a valuable social institution around it and you're taking advantage of that institution), Raz's normative-power theory (promising is just you exercising a power to bind yourself, a power that's valuable because it lets you shape your own normative situation), David Owens's "authority-interest" theory (we have a basic interest in having the say-so over what someone else does, seperate from just wanting things to turn out a certain way), Scanlon's assurance-based "Principle F" from What We Owe to Each Other, Judith Thomson's reliance theory (a promise as an invitation to rely on you), and Margaret Gilbert's joint commitment view, where a promise is a sui generis commitment undertaken jointly by promisor and promisee together.

We spent almost the whole seminar throwing these six at what my prof kept calling a "desert island"/deathbed-promise case (I guess theres literally a paper out there called something like "the trouble with deathbed promises"): say I promise my dying grandma, alone with her in the hospital room, that I'll keep watering her ridiculous prize-winning orchids after she's gone. Nobody else ever finds out the promise happened, she has no other family, and there is zero chance anyone will ever know if the orchids die. Pretty much everyone in the room still felt like I'd be doing something wrong if I let them die anyway, but apparently a bunch of these theories have a genuinely hard time with this exact case, since there's no live expectation to violate, no reliance or harm happening to anyone, no ongoing convention-policing going on, and depending who you ask, not even anybody left to hold "authority" over me anymore.
