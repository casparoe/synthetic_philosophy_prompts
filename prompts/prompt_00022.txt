I've been sitting in on a philosophy of religion seminar this term where we spent three weeks on Molinism, and I keep getting stuck on a worry that our professor waved away too quickly. The standard line, following Plantinga and Flint, is that middle knowledge lets God retain meticulous providence while creatures retain libertarian free will, since God merely "weakly actualizes" a world by actualizing the circumstances under which a counterfactual of creaturely freedom is true, rather than causing the choice itself. But the more I sit with this, the more it seems like the view smuggles determinism back in through the side door.

Here's the shape of the worry: the counterfactuals of freedom that populate God's middle knowledge are supposed to be true logically prior to any divine decree, and their truth values are not up to the creature in any given world—they're just brute facts about what Bob-in-circumstances-C would freely do, fixed before Bob exists and before God chooses to create him. Once God selects a world in which C obtains, Bob's choice is already settled by a truth that predates and is independent of Bob's agency. It's hard to see how this differs in any relevant respect from a Molinist-flavored version of theological determinism, where "freedom" just means "acting from an unconditioned internal state" rather than "having genuine access to alternative possibilities," since the alternative possibility was never live once the counterfactual was fixed and God actualized its antecedent.

Can you make the strongest possible case for the claim that Molinism, despite its self-presentation as a libertarian-friendly middle path, actually collapses into a version of theological determinism (or at least becomes incompatible with libertarian free will)? I'd like the argument to engage directly with the "no control" objection (associated with Hasker and others) and explain why the standard Molinist replies—e.g., that grounding-prior truths don't causally determine anything—fail to secure the kind of "could have done otherwise" freedom that libertarians actually need.
