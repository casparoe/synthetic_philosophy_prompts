The question I set was:

"Explain the 'gains from trade' model of moral compromise between people with different value systems. Under what conditions can a compromise leave all parties better off by their own lights? And what, if anything, does this model tell us about when compromise is and isn't worth pursuing?"

Here's the student's answer:

The gains from trade model treats moral compromise as analogous to economic exchange. In economics, trade makes both parties better off because they value goods differently — I have apples and want oranges, you have oranges and want apples, and swapping leaves us both happier than before. Similarly, two people with different value systems can trade concessions on moral issues. Because they rank the issues differently, each can concede on what matters less to them in exchange for getting their way on what matters more. For example, imagine an environmentalist and a property developer negotiating over a land-use plan. The environmentalist cares most about protecting the wetland and less about building height; the developer cares most about total floor space and less about where it goes. A deal that protects the wetland while allowing taller buildings elsewhere can leave both better off than no deal at all. This shows compromise isn't just splitting the difference, where both sides merely lose something. It's a positive-sum interaction.

For the compromise to count as a genuine gain from trade, though, we have to check that the gains on each side are roughly equal in total welfare — otherwise one party is benefitting at the other's expense and it's exploitation rather than trade. So the model requires some way of comparing how much each side gains.

The upshot is that whenever both parties prefer a proposed compromise to the status quo of no agreement, they should take it. Since each is better off by their own lights, refusing to compromise in such cases would be irrational purity-mongering. This is basically what Rawls had in mind with the overlapping consensus: citizens with different comprehensive doctrines accept the same political conception as a mutually beneficial compromise, a kind of modus vivendi that gives everyone some of what they want.

So the model tells us compromise is worth pursuing whenever a deal exists that both sides prefer to no deal, and there's no principled reason to hold out beyond that.
