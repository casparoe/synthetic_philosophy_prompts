I run a small reading group that has been working through some metaphysics classics, and last week one member circulated a handout summarizing Bradley's regress for the others, since most of them hadn't encountered it before. The handout reads as follows: 'F. H. Bradley pointed out a fatal problem for any philosophy that admits relations. If a relation R holds between A and B, then R itself must be related to A and to B, which requires two further relations, and each of those requires still more, so relating anything to anything launches an infinite regress and can never be completed. This is essentially the same argument as Plato's Third Man Argument, which made the same point about Forms. Bradley concluded that relations are unreal, and therefore that nothing is really related to anything else, so reality is a single undifferentiated blob about which nothing true can be said of any part. Modern philosophers generally consider the problem solved: the standard view is that the regress is harmless, since infinite regresses are only vicious when they involve circularity, and this one is not circular.'

Something in the handout feels off to me, but I can't put my finger on it — I suspect the move from 'relations generate a regress' to 'relations are unreal' is being presented as more straightforward than it is, and the dismissal at the end sounds too quick given that people still write about the unity of the proposition as an open problem. Could you go through the handout claim by claim and tell me which parts a metaphysician sympathetic to Bradley would accept, which parts get the argument's structure wrong or conflate it with a different regress, and which parts misstate Bradley's actual conclusion? In particular, I'd like to know: whether the regress as stated is the one Bradley actually pressed, or a flattened version; whether the Third Man comparison is apt or misleading; whether 'undifferentiated blob about which nothing true can be said' is a fair rendering of his monism; and whether the claim that the problem is now regarded as solved survives scrutiny, given the range of contemporary responses (relations relating themselves, unsaturatedness, grounding, truthmaking, and so on). I'd like to bring a corrected version to next week's meeting, so please also indicate which of the handout's overall conclusions — that the regress shows something deep about predication and unity, or that it shows nothing at all — is better supported once the errors are removed.
