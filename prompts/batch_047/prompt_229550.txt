I am a PhD student in the history of science, and I am writing to ask for help with an exchange from my historiography seminar that has been bothering me. We have been working through the debates surrounding what Butterfield called the Whig interpretation of history — the temptation to write the past as if it were progress toward the present. Last week, while we were discussing a paper that narrates early modern natural philosophy as a steady march toward the Scientific Revolution, one participant dismissed the entire worry with the line 'history is written by the victors': of course the victorious research programme casts its predecessors as stepping stones, he said, and there is no standpoint from which anyone could do otherwise. Twenty minutes later a different participant invoked the very same slogan to argue roughly the opposite — that any narrative in which the eventual winners come out looking rational is thereby compromised and should be read as propaganda.

I would like to be able to respond to both uses precisely rather than by instinct. Please take the slogan 'history is written by the victors' and lay out the distinct, precise claims it could be making. My own provisional menu includes a descriptive-sociological reading (a claim about who controls archives, professorships, and curricula), an epistemic reading (a claim about what evidence survives for the rest of us and what we can therefore justifiably believe), a stronger sceptical reading (that historical truth is inaccessible or perhaps not there at all), and a moral reading (that success retrospectively launders itself) — but I would rather you generate the taxonomy yourself than simply accept mine, since I am not confident it is exhaustive or that my categories do not overlap.

For each reading: state it as a thesis sharp enough to be true or false; give a verdict on whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false, taking account of the obvious awkward counterexamples (the enormous historiography produced by the losing side of the American Civil War comes to mind, as do the many losing sides that have dominated their own commemoration); and then say whether that reading would actually support either of my colleagues' uses of the slogan. The part I care about most is whether any plausible reading of the slogan dissolves the Whig-history problem or merely renames it. My current sense is that Whiggishness is not fundamentally about victors suppressing truths but about the selection and teleological arrangement of truths — that even a chronicle containing nothing but accurate facts can be Whiggish in its structure — but I cannot yet state this cleanly, and I would like your analysis either to sharpen that thought or to refute it.

Please deliver the readings as a list with verdicts, followed by a short concluding note identifying which reading each of my two colleagues was most likely invoking and whether the slogan was doing any genuine argumentative work in either case.
