I am a novelist, twenty years and four books into the trade, and I keep noticing that a quarrel I know intimately from writers' workshops seems to shadow an older one I have only read about secondhand: the disagreement between Mencius and Xunzi over whether human nature is good. The workshop version runs like this. One school holds that a first draft contains something living and true -- the voice, the instinct, the thing that made you write it -- and that the work of revision is essentially protective: feed it, don't smother it, cut away what chokes it, and distrust anything that feels imposed from outside. The other school holds that a first draft is precisely the problem -- undisciplined, self-flattering, in love with its own habits -- and that anything worth reading is made by technique, constraint, tradition, and the ruthless application of craft to raw material. I want to know how deep the parallel goes.

Please restate both positions, Mencius and Xunzi, as carefully as you can in the vocabulary of fiction-writing as I actually use it: drafting and revision, voice, craft, workshop advice, the relation between a writer and her own raw material, the role of convention and of the tradition of finished books. I am fluent in that vocabulary; assume no philosophy background beyond a general reader's. Translate each thinker separately, not just the debate between them, since I suspect the two workshop schools do not line up neatly with the two philosophers.

Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually want. Which parts of each position translate cleanly into writing-talk, and which had to be bent to fit? Here are three places where I already suspect strain, and I'd like your judgment on each: first, a sprout that tends toward the good is not obviously like raw talent, because talent doesn't seem to point anywhere in particular on its own; second, a draft has an author, and it is not clear who, if anyone, plays that role for human nature; third, the workshop argument is about how to produce something good, and I am unsure whether the philosophers are arguing about that or about something more like what goodness is. Finally, give me your honest verdict: is the workshop debate a genuine descendant of the philosophical one, such that progress on one could teach me something about the other -- or is it merely a verbal resemblance riding on the shared metaphor of cultivation, one that a novelist finds flattering and should therefore distrust?
