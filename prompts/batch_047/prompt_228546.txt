I'm a software engineer (distributed systems, lately some work on content-ranking infrastructure) with no formal philosophy training, currently auditing an undergraduate political philosophy seminar. For the final assignment I have to submit a research proposal for a term paper of about 6,000 words, to be written over one semester, and I'd like your help drafting the proposal itself. Please take on the role of a political theorist with deep familiarity with Simone Weil's late writings, and think carefully before committing to a framing — I'd actually like you to begin with a short brainstorm of two or three candidate ways to pose the central question, then settle on the strongest one and build the proposal around it.

The paper will engage Weil's essay On the Abolition of All Political Parties (written 1943, published posthumously in 1950; I'm reading the Simon Leys translation). What struck me is a restriction she builds in right at the start, before her main argument gets going:

"The word 'party' is taken here in the meaning it has in Continental Europe. In Anglo-Saxon countries, this same word designates an altogether different reality, which has its roots in English tradition and is therefore not easily transposable elsewhere. […] In the Anglo-Saxon world, political parties have an element of game, of sport, which is only conceivable in an institution of aristocratic origin, whereas in institutions that were plebeian from the start, everything must always be serious."

As an engineer, this reads to me like a special-case flag guarding the code path — and in my experience those flags are exactly where the bugs live. My half-formed idea is this: Weil's core argument, as I understand it, is that parties are defined by three features — they generate collective passion, they exert collective pressure on the minds of their individual members, and they aim at their own unlimited growth, which gives them a built-in totalitarian tendency. But nothing in that mechanism seems to depend on national tradition. If the mechanism is sound, it should apply to any disciplined, platform-bearing electoral organization, Anglo-Saxon ones included; if the Anglo-Saxon exemption is warranted, then it must be because those parties lack one of the three features, and Weil owes us an account of which one and why — an account the essay never gives. Meanwhile, the actual behavior of modern two-party systems (whipped votes, message discipline, primary challenges) looks nothing like an aristocratic game. So the exemption seems either unstable or empirically obsolete, and I'm not sure which.

Please write the proposal (roughly 900 words) making this precise. It should: state the research question so that it has a determinate answer — roughly, whether Weil's Continental-only scope restriction is entailed by her own analysis of what a party is, or is instead an undefended historical observation that her argument actually contradicts; explain why the question is open (most readers I've encountered treat the essay either as a utopian broadside to be dismissed or as a source of anti-party slogans, and in neither case does the scope restriction get scrutinized); lay out the approach — a close reconstruction of the three criteria from the essay itself, then a stress-test of the exemption against them, including the strongest counterargument I can see, namely that the exemption is merely descriptive sociology rather than a premise of the normative argument — and say why this close-reading approach is better than the obvious alternatives (e.g., a broad survey of anti-party thought, or an empirical paper on modern party discipline); enumerate the possible conclusions and what textual or philosophical considerations would decide between them, including what would count as the project failing; and give an honest assessment of scope risks — for instance, I can't read French, so I can't check the original, and placing the essay in the context of the collapsing Third Republic and Vichy might be necessary but could easily consume the whole semester. Please do not include a bibliography or literature citations; I'll fill those in myself with my instructor's help.
