I am a philosophy professor with two recurring responsibilities that generate the same problem. I referee about a dozen manuscripts a year for journals in philosophy of psychiatry and philosophy of medicine, and I grade a graduate seminar on the philosophy of classification. In both capacities I constantly encounter invocations of Ian Hacking's idea of the "looping effect" of human or interactive kinds -- the notion that classifying people, unlike classifying chemicals or stars, can change the people classified, because those people (or the institutions and experts around them) respond to the classification, which in turn feeds back into the classification itself. Perhaps one submission in three uses the idea, and in my judgment the large majority misuse it: they treat it as a fancy synonym for labeling being harmful, or for a diagnosis being "socially constructed," or they gesture at a loop without specifying any mechanism by which it turns.

What I want is a vetting procedure I can actually run -- something I can work through in the first hour of reading a manuscript, before I invest in the details, and that I can also hand to my graduate students as a self-check before they submit papers to me. Assume the person running it is a philosopher without clinical training, has only the manuscript in front of them, and owes an editor a report within three weeks, so the procedure cannot require empirical detective work beyond what a careful reader can do.

Please write the procedure as an ordered sequence of tests, where each test has: the question to ask of the manuscript, what counts as a sufficient answer (with at least one example of an answer that would pass and one that would fail), and what the procedure does on failure -- whether that is a stop with a verdict ("this is not a looping-effect claim; recommend the author redescribe it as X"), a downgrade ("the loop is asserted but not shown; treat as a promissory note"), or a handoff to referee judgment, in which case say explicitly why the question cannot be settled procedurally. The tests must at minimum force the following distinctions, and you should tell me if I have drawn any of them wrong: (a) interactive versus indifferent kinds -- the procedure needs an early screen for whether the kind in question is even the right sort of thing to loop; (b) a genuine feedback mechanism, with some specification of who becomes aware of or responds to the classification and how their response modifies either the people, the criteria, or the practice, versus mere downward causal influence of a label on behavior, stigma, self-esteem, or outcomes; (c) looping in Hacking's sense versus generic social construction, historical contingency, or interest-driven revision of diagnostic criteria; (d) change in the classified individuals versus change in the classificatory scheme itself, since a full loop arguably requires movement on both sides, and a manuscript often shows only one.

Then run the finished procedure on three hypothetical submissions, showing its work step by step: (1) a paper arguing that short-video platforms carrying first-person symptom accounts of attention deficit disorder have created a loop -- viewers reinterpret their own histories through the symptom lists, present to clinicians already scripted, clinicians anchor on the presentation, and diagnostic practice drifts toward the platform's picture; (2) a paper arguing that classifying a condition as a mental disorder loops because the stigma of the label worsens the prognosis of those labeled; (3) a paper arguing that the expansion of a diagnosis's criteria over three decades was a looping effect, where the sole documented mechanism is lobbying by an organized patient advocacy group and the corresponding committee decisions. At least one of these runs should produce a verdict I will dislike -- either passing a paper I would instinctively reject or failing one I would instinctively accept -- and when it does, say whether the discomfort points to a defect in the procedure or in my instinct.

Finally, close with two honest sections: a list of the distinctions the simplification had to sacrifice to stay runnable (and which of those sacrifices you expect to generate false passes or false fails), and a statement of what the procedure cannot do at all -- where it must hand the decision back to me, and what kinds of manuscripts it will systematically mishandle.
