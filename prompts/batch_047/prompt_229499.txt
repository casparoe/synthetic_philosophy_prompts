so i'm making a worksheet for my intro theory-of-history seminar, the week we do Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation, On the Uses and Disadvantages of History for Life (1874). the problem i keep hitting: students read the three modes -- monumental, antiquarian, critical -- as a fixed menu and just pick a favorite, instead of seeing them as answers to prior questions about what the past is even for. so i want you to build me a taxonomy of positions on "what should a person (or a community) do with its past?" built from the logical space up, not from a list of names.

ground it in section 2 of the essay, where Nietzsche says history belongs to the living in three respects -- the active and striving person, the preserving and revering person, and the suffering person who needs liberation -- and these correspond to the three modes. dont quote it, just take the triangulation seriously: the modes get their identity from what the knower needs, not from any feature of the past itself. the taxonomy should exploit that, but also go beyond it, because Nietzsche himself spends the essay complaining about people whose relation to the past doesnt fit any of the three.

here's what i need:

1. a decision tree where every branch is labeled with a diagnostic question a person could answer without knowing any of the jargon -- concrete stuff, like: a town finds out its beloved founder funded the town hall with slave-trade money, what does knowing this change? or: is forgetting ever a legitimate goal rather than just a failure? or: can there be too much truth about the past? the questions should sort by what people actually think, not by labels theyd self-apply.

2. these must show up as leaves: the three Nietzschean modes; the view that historical knowledge is valuable for its own sake and needs no justification from life (the "objective history" type he savages); the unhistorical stance (deliberate forgetfulness as a condition of action); and the suprahistorical stance (turning from history entirely toward the eternal). if a leaf is a position nobody in the essay or the real debate actually holds -- some combination of answers the questions allow -- mark it uninhabited and say in one line whether its incoherent, just unattractive, or unexplored.

3. each inhabited leaf gets one sentence on its most awkward commitment. e.g. the antiquarian has to explain why mere age confers value; you find the awkward thing for each.

4. flag any two leaves the tree cant separate, rather than papering over it. i suspect monumental history and certain kinds of heroic presentism will collapse into each other, but i might be wrong.

5. end with a note on where Nietzsche himself lands on the tree, since he plainly endorses all three modes in doses while despising the objective type -- if the tree cant represent "the healthy culture balances all three," thats a defect of the tree, so say whether that counts as a position of its own or a meta-claim about the others.

lead with the tree itself, no preamble, no restating what i asked. audience is smart undergrads whove read the essay once, badly.
