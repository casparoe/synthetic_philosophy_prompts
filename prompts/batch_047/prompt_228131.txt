I'm a novelist, and moral worth is basically my professional obsession — I'm constantly deciding whether what a character did was admirable, and I've noticed I keep reaching for a set of working assumptions that might not actually cohere. Can you audit them? Here they are:

1. Whether something has moral worth depends on why the person did it, not on whether it turned out well or even on whether it was the right thing to do.
2. The people I most admire, on the page and off, act from immediate unreflective compassion — they jump in the river before there's been time to think. Visible deliberation about what one ought to do is, if anything, a bad sign about a person's character.
3. Moral worth can't be a matter of luck: two people who would behave identically in every possible situation, differing only in which feelings happen to well up in them, deserve the same moral grade.
4. Impure motives don't ruin anything. Almost nobody's motives are spotless, and if some vanity or self-interest rides along with the good motive, the act still counts as admirable.
5. The way to find out what actually moved someone is counterfactual: take away the audience, the good mood, the crush, and ask whether they still would have done it.

Tell me which of these are genuinely in tension and roughly how severe each tension is, which pairs only look like they're in tension but aren't, and for the real ones what the cheapest fix would be and what it costs me. This matters alot for the book I'm drafting, but I don't want a lecture on the history of ethics — just the audit.
