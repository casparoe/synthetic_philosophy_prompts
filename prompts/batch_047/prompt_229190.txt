I'm a high school junior writing a paper for my ethics elective on whether torture can ever be justified, and I keep running into the ticking time bomb scenario. Everyone treats it as the decisive test case, but when I actually go through the variations, my own answers don't line up in any way I can articulate. So here are my verdicts on a set of cases, and I want to know what principle, if any, is generating them.

Case 1: A captured bomber knows where a device is hidden in a city, it goes off in six hours, and interrogators have exhausted everything else. Torturing him to get the location seems permissible to me. Ugly, but permissible.

Case 2: Same bomber, same bomb, but he won't break, so interrogators torture his innocent ten-year-old daughter in front of him to make him talk. I think this is categorically wrong, full stop. Yet it looks like it should follow from whatever justifies Case 1, since the stakes are identical.

Case 3: Police torture a serial killer who has already confessed everything, purely as retribution for his victims. I think this is clearly wrong, even though he's guilty and the suffering is "deserved" in some loose sense.

Case 4: Sleep deprivation, stress positions, and blaring noise used on a terrorism suspect who might have actionable intelligence, with no specific bomb ticking. I'm uneasy but I lean toward calling this permissible in genuine emergencies, even though I can't point to a clean line separating it from Case 1's torture.

Case 5: Waterboarding a suspect who turns out to know nothing. Once we learn he was innocent, I feel the act becomes retroactively worse than if he'd been guilty — but the interrogators did the exact same thing either way, and I'm not sure that feeling makes sense.

Case 6: A soldier under torture reveals the location of his unit to save himself. I don't blame him at all — I think virtually everyone breaks — which makes me wonder why in Case 1 I treat the bomber's refusal to talk as something we're entitled to override by force, when in Case 6 I treat the soldier's talking as understandable and excusable. The symmetry here bothers me.

Cases 1 and 2 are the pair I can't reconcile. Every principle I try that licenses Case 1 seems to license Case 2, and every principle that forbids Case 2 seems to forbid Case 1 too. I've considered that the difference is the daughter's innocence, but the bomber in Case 5 might be innocent too, and I didn't think that possibility alone settled Case 1. I've also considered that Case 2 tortures someone as a means to pressure someone else, but I'm not confident that distinction does the work I need, since the daughter's suffering is also a means to the information, just routed through her father.

Could you analyze my verdicts and figure out what I'm actually committed to? Please structure your answer in these sections, in this order:

"The Pattern" (roughly 150 words): State the principle or principles that best capture my verdicts, precisely enough that someone could apply them to a new case without me.

"Where the Principle Strains" (roughly 200 words): Identify which of my verdicts the best principle cannot accommodate, focusing on the Case 1 / Case 2 tension and on whether my reaction to Case 5 is coherent. Tell me honestly which verdict I'd most plausibly have to give up, and what it would cost.

"Alternative Principles" (roughly 150 words): Sketch one or two rival principles — for instance an absolute prohibition, or a pure expected-harm calculation — and say which of my verdicts each one saves and which it overturns.

"New Cases" (roughly 100 words): Give the best principle's verdict on two cases I didn't raise: torturing a suspect to locate a kidnap victim who will die otherwise, and a legalized torture warrant system with judicial oversight. If the principle gives an answer I wouldn't like, say so.

"My Verdict" (roughly 75 words): Tell me which principle you'd recommend I defend in my paper and the single strongest objection I'll face from a classmate who disagrees.
