I belong to a reading group that has spent the semester on Aristotle's discussion of acquisition in the first book of the Politics: his contrast between natural household management, which aims at securing the goods a household genuinely needs, and the unnatural art of money-making, which treats wealth as something to be increased without limit, along with his notorious verdict that lending money at interest is the most perverse form of acquisition because it makes money breed from money rather than from the goods money was invented to exchange. One member of the group, a classicist with a taste for applying old doctrines to new facts, has taken to issuing verdicts on contemporary transactions whenever they come up in conversation, and a few of us have an informal wager running on who can predict her calls. So far her record is this. A farming household that feeds itself and sells its genuine surplus at market: entirely unobjectionable, acquisition as it should be. A surgeon who charges high fees that reflect decades of training and real skill: unobjectionable. A shopkeeper who buys goods wholesale and resells them at a markup as his full-time livelihood: objectionable, however legal and convenient. A bank living off interest on consumer loans: the paradigm case of what is wrong with commerce, worse than ordinary trade. The scheme Aristotle himself recounts, in which Thales cornered the region's olive presses ahead of a bumper harvest and rented them out at his own price: objectionable, for all its cleverness. And a speculator who contracts to buy a farmer's grain cheap before the harvest and sells it dear afterward, never touching a sack of it: objectionable.

The case that has now come up, and on which the wager turns, is this one. An investor funds a merchant's overseas trading voyage on the following terms: if the ship returns, the investor receives his principal back plus a very large premium; if the ship is lost at sea, the investor loses everything and the merchant owes nothing. The merchant could not otherwise afford the voyage, and the premium is understood by both parties as compensation for the investor's bearing the entire risk of shipwreck. Some of us think she must condemn this as interest under another name, since the investor's money grows from money and no new good is produced by his contribution. Others think the risk-bearing distinguishes it from the consumer loans she condemned most harshly, since there the lender profits no matter what happens to the borrower, and that the investor here is arguably selling a genuine service, something closer to the surgeon's fee than to the banker's interest. The rest of us are simply unsure which way the pattern of her verdicts cuts.

I would like you to respond as someone with deep expertise in ancient Greek moral and economic thought who is also comfortable reasoning carefully about modern financial arrangements. Before committing to a prediction, please brainstorm the candidate principles that could be generating her verdicts: identify at least two distinct rules, each fully consistent with her entire track record, that diverge on the maritime loan case, and state precisely what verdict each rule predicts and why. Then give me your best prediction of her actual verdict, with your reasons for thinking the rule behind it fits her better than the alternatives, and tell me which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analog to the new case and why. Finally, if you judge that her record genuinely underdetermines the answer, say so honestly, and describe the one further hypothetical case you would most want to put to her in order to settle which principle she is really running.
