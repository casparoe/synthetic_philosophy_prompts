So I've been chewing on an argument that keeps resurfacing whenever satisficing comes up on LW-adjacent threads, and I've talked myself into a position I want properly stress-tested rather than just nodded at.

The position: bounded rationality isn't actually a rival theory of decision-making to expected utility maximization. It's just EU maximization with deliberation costs priced in. Every satisficing rule -- stop searching once you hit your aspiration level, take-the-best, 1/N portfolios, whatever -- can be reproduced as the output of some expected utility maximizer facing search costs, attention costs, or a finite compute budget. Simon needed the satisficing vocabulary because optimization-under-costs was mathematically intractable at the time, but conceptually the maximizer always swallows the satisficer. So when people say humans "satisfice rather than maximize," they aren't describing a different kind of agent, they're describing a maximizer whose cost function nobody bothered to write down. And the whole fast-and-frugal heuristics programme is at best a nice collection of engineering tricks for compute-limited agents, and at worst an unfalsifiable umbrella that can claim any behavior post hoc by positing whatever environmental constraints make it "ecologically rational."

What would count against this? Specifically:

- Empirically: what behavioral findings, if they hold up, can't be redescribed as constrained maximization without doing violence to the very idea of a cost function? People point at aspiration levels that shift based on recent search experience -- does the maximizer picture handle that cleanly, or only by baking the observed behavior into the utility function, which feels like cheating?
- Formally: is there any result that actually separates the two? E.g. a class of problems where the optimal-with-deliberation-costs policy provably doesn't look like any simple heuristic, or the reverse -- a case where a fixed heuristic beats every feasible approximation to the optimal policy in a way that the "you're really just maximizing" story can only accommodate by gerrymandering.
- Conceptually: does MY position have its own falsifiability problem? If any behavior whatsoever can be fitted with some cost function, then "it's all maximization" is trivially true and therefore empty. Tell me whether that's a knock-down objection to what I'm saying or just pedantry.
- And what would the bounded rationality camp itself accept as settling things? The less-is-more results -- heuristics beating full optimization in noisy environments with small samples -- are those genuine differential predictions that the maximize-with-costs picture fails to make, or the same predictions dressed in different clothes? If a committed ecological-rationality person and a committed constrained-maximization person can each explain every experiment the other one runs, I want that stated plainly.

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely annoying, and be explicit about which ones the satisficing side would themselves concede as damage versus dismiss as missing the point. Also, per usual: say what would change your mind about your own answer -- which argument, if it turned out to go through, would flip your verdict, and where your reasoning here is most likley to fail. And if your honest conclusion is that this whole dispute is terminological because the two camps are one utility-function redefinition away from each other, I'm open to that, but argue for it instead of asserting it, because "it's all semantics" is also a claim that should have to earn its keep.
