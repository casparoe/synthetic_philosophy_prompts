Two of my colleagues have been going back and forth about the ethics of having children, and they've asked me to settle it. The first holds the so-called procreation asymmetry: bringing into existence someone whose life will contain serious suffering is a moral wrong, because there is now a person harmed; but declining to bring into existence someone who would have been perfectly happy is no wrong at all, because there is no one thereby deprived. Conjoining the two, she says, it follows that procreation is always at least somewhat bad, since every actual life contains some real suffering while the absent pleasures are no loss to anyone. The second replies that the asymmetry is an artifact of thinking only about what is owed to particular people. If we allow that a possible flourishing life would add value to the world simpliciter — which her opponent's own intuitions seem to rely on when she insists a possible miserable life would subtract value — then the ledger is symmetric after all, and a sufficiently good life can justify its creation. The first answers that impersonal value can't be doing the work the second needs: if a possible happy life counts as a reason to create it, then childless couples who could raise a flourishing child are doing something wrong, and she takes that consequence to be plainly absurd. The second retorts that the absurdity is the first's own: on her view, a couple deciding between conceiving a child who would be ecstatically happy and one who would be merely content has no moral reason at all to prefer the first, which he finds at least as hard to accept. Both think the other has bitten an intolerable bullet. I genuinely can't tell. Please work through their exchange step by step — who is right about which move, which premises actually carry the weight, and whether there is a coherent position both have overlooked.
