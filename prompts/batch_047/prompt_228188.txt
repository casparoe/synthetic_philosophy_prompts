I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the ethics of artificial intelligence, and I'm drafting this year's final examination. One unit of the course covered what is often called the narrow alignment problem: the contrast between intent alignment, roughly the project of building a system that faithfully does what its operator intends it to do, and value alignment, the project of building a system that does what is actually good, or what its operator would endorse under suitably idealized reflection. Last year I set a question on this material and was disappointed: nearly every student simply reproduced the two definitions, gave one stock example of each, and gestured at the difficulty of specifying human values. I want this year's question to be unanswerable by that route.

The crux I'd like students to confront is what we might call the convergence thesis: the claim that the distinction between the two notions collapses, because a genuinely intent-aligned system would honor hedged intentions of the form "do what I would want if I had thought the matter through," so that intent alignment done properly just is value alignment under another name. Pushing against the thesis is a class of cases where the operator's intent is clear, stable, well-informed, and fully specifiable, yet morally contested — and the question should build such a case directly into its wording so that students must engage with it rather than inventing an easier one. I have in mind something like a hospital's automated triage-allocation system that implements the director's explicit, carefully considered, and publicly stated allocation policy, where that policy is one the wider community reasonably regards as unjust. Students should have to say whether such a system counts as aligned, and on which of the two notions; whether the convergence thesis survives cases where the gap lies not in the operator's ignorance or inarticulateness but in the operator's values themselves; and whether the two notions are best understood as rival analyses of a single concept or as answers to two genuinely different questions.

Please write: the essay question itself, including the scenario stated tightly enough for exam conditions (the essay will be 45 minutes, worth 40 of 100 marks); a model answer of roughly 600 words; and a marking scheme that spells out, move by move, what distinguishes a first-class answer from a merely competent one — for instance, an excellent answer should state the convergence thesis in its strongest form rather than as a straw man, and should either produce a genuine counterexample to it or argue that apparent counterexamples trade on treating operator intent as less rich than it really is. The scheme should also name the specific mistakes that should cap marks: conflating intent alignment with obedience to literal instructions; assuming operator intentions are transparent or complete; treating value alignment as alignment to whatever values the marker happens to hold; or answering both horns of the dilemma without adjudicating between them.
