So I work at a mid-size software company, about 3 million users, and I ended up kind of accidentally becoming the person who runs our weekly "should we ship this" review for changes that affect the whole user base. The decisions are stuff like: do we add four seconds of load time to save a bunch of engineering cost, do we turn on a notification campaign that mildly annoys everyone but gets some people to actually finish setting up their account, do we make the cancel button one click harder to find to reduce churn. Each individual effect is trivial. But multiplied by 3 million people, is it? This came up because a coworker and I got into an argument about that old thought experiment where you ask whether it's better for one person to be horribly tortured for decades or for some astronomically huge number of people to each get a single dust speck in their eye, and apparently most people pick the dust specks, which would mean no number of tiny harms ever adds up to one big one. And I get the intuition, but taken literally it seems to imply that basically everything my review board does is fine as long as each person's harm is small enough, which can't be right either, because "annoy everyone a little forever" is also how you end up being a company people hate.

What I want is an actual decision procedure my team can run in about thirty minutes per proposal, for people with no philosophy background. Walk me through building it: the questions to ask in order, what a good enough answer to each looks like, and specifically the part where it has to decide when you're allowed to just multiply a tiny harm by a huge number of people and when that move breaks down -- like, is there a principled line between harms that aggregate and harms that don't, and what do you do with harms you suspect are real but can't measure (like a slow erosion of trust in the product). I also want it to be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and it should have explicit points where it just says "stop, this one goes to a human for a values call" rather than pretending the checklist settles it.

Then run the finished procedure on three of my actual pending cases, including at least one where it gives an answer I won't like: (1) the four seconds of load time, which saves us roughly one full-time engineer's year of work, (2) making cancellation slightly harder to find, which our data says mostly delays people who were going to cancel anyway by a few weeks, and (3) a default-on data collection toggle that's genuinely privacy-relevant for each user but only in a small way, though it meaningfully improves the product for everyone else. If the procedure spits out something on one of these that feels clearly wrong to a normal person, don't just present the output -- tell me whether that's the procedure failing or my intuition failing, and how I'd even tell the differance.
