Analyze the Tammany Hall political machine — at its height, roughly from the 1860s to the early 1930s — as a test case for competing conceptions of corruption. I know the broad historical outline; you do not need to supply new facts, but reason from the salient ones: the machine secured the loyalty of immigrant and working-class voters through a steady flow of material benefits (jobs on public works, coal and food in hard winters, help navigating naturalization and the courts, the famous Christmas turkeys) at a time when no public welfare system existed; in return it expected votes, and it extracted tribute from contractors, saloon keepers, and anyone who needed a license or a franchise; and the Tammany ward boss George Washington Plunkitt, in interviews recorded by a journalist, famously defended his own enrichment by distinguishing what he called honest graft — using advance knowledge of public projects, say buying land one knows will rise in value — from dishonest graft such as outright theft from the treasury or blackmail.

The theories I want you to put to work are three. First, the standard public-office-centered definition associated with Joseph Nye and much contemporary anti-corruption practice: corruption as conduct that deviates from the formal duties of a public role for private gain. Second, the anthropological or moral-economy account of clientelism, on which patronage and gift-giving within networks of personal obligation are not defective versions of impersonal administration but a functioning alternative order of reciprocity — corrupt only when the reciprocity itself is violated. Third, Plunkitt's own internal standard, the honest/dishonest graft distinction, treated seriously as a normative theory rather than as a rationalization.

For each theory, work the mapping in both directions. On the theory-to-case side: what does the framework count as corrupt here, what does it count as legitimate, and where does its verdict surprise — note, for example, that Nye-style definitions condemn the entire machine at a stroke, while the moral-economy view appears to vindicate much of it, and Plunkitt's line condemns some insiders the first two would treat alike. On the case-to-theory side: what does the episode reveal that each framework cannot see or cannot classify? Does the fact that Tammany provided real, badly needed services to people the formal state ignored put pressure on the universality of public-office standards, or does it show only that corruption can be convenient for its beneficiaries? Does the moral-economy defense survive the machine's treatment of those outside its networks — Black New Yorkers, reformers, businesses that refused to pay — or does the clientelist order have a built-in answer about outsiders? Is Plunkitt's distinction a genuine moral line that the standard definitions miss, or does the Tammany case show precisely why the line cannot hold, since the opportunities that made the graft honest existed only because of the machine's grip on public decisions?

Then the harder question the whole exercise is aimed at: the debate over whether Western anti-corruption standards are universal or a parochial export. Say what, if anything, Tammany contributes to it. The machine operated inside the supposed heartland of the standards, was defended by its participants in the language of reciprocity and loyalty that anthropologists use for non-Western clientelism, and was dismantled in the name of impersonal administration — does that make the episode evidence that the universalist standard is a local achievement rather than a universal truth, or evidence that the anthropological critique overreaches, since New Yorkers themselves ultimately judged the machine corrupt by criteria they regarded as binding? Say which way you think the case cuts and why, but be disciplined about scope: where a theory is silent rather than merely awkward, say it is silent instead of forcing it to deliver a verdict.

Finally, a note on confidence. Please mark clearly what is settled and what is genuinely contested: the historical facts above are not in serious dispute, and Nye's definition really is the mainstream one in the literature and in institutional anti-corruption practice; but whether the moral-economy account vindicates or merely redescribes clientelism, and whether cases like Tammany bear on the universality question at all, are matters on which knowledgeable people disagree, and I want those presented as open, not as problems with an agreed solution.
