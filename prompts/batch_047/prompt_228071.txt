I teach an undergraduate seminar on formal systems, minds, and machines, and every year I open the unit on Hofstadter's MU-puzzle by reading something aloud before I say a word about it. This year I would like that something to be a poem, and I would like you to write it.

For readers who need the puzzle stated: the MIU system has one axiom, the string MI, and four rules of production. First, if a string ends in I, you may append U. Second, from any string beginning with M, you may double everything after the M. Third, any occurrence of III may be replaced by U. Fourth, any occurrence of UU may be deleted. The puzzle is to derive MU. It cannot be done, and the reason is an invariant: the rules can change the number of I's in a string but can never make that number divisible by three; MI starts with one I, and MU has zero. Someone working inside the system can grind away forever without seeing this; the insight comes from stepping outside the rules and looking at what they preserve.

Here is what I want the poem to do. It should consist of a sequence of short stanzas in which each stanza is obtained from its predecessor by applying one of the four rules, analogized to language rather than carried out literally on letters -- for instance, a stanza that doubles its content, one in which a threefold repetition collapses into a single word, one in which a doubled word is struck out. The poem should be pursuing a final line, MU or some plain-language stand-in for it, that never arrives, and a reader who knows the puzzle should be able to see that the pursuit is doomed in exactly the way the derivation is doomed: some invariant running through the stanzas, a count or a threeness that the transformations preserve, which keeps the goal line out of reach. Then I want a closing coda in free verse that does what the rules cannot -- steps outside the poem's own machinery, names the invariant, and says the unreachable line. The coda should enact the ambiguous lesson I want my students to sit with: that the capacity to jump out of a system feels like the signature of mind, and yet the jump itself can seem, on reflection, like just another move in a larger system we have not stepped out of.

Roughly twenty to thirty lines in total. The transformations must be genuinely traceable -- I intend to put the poem on the projector and ask the class to identify which rule governs each transition, so a student who checks should find that the poem obeys itself. No commentary or explanation appended; the poem should carry its logic internally, as the seminar's opening text.
