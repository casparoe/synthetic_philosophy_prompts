I'm grading intro political philosophy papers again and it's the usual thing: half the class leans on "one vote never decides an election" to conclude that voting is instrumentally irrational, and the other half fires back "every vote counts" as if that settles it. Neither slogan is doing any precise work, and I'd like a teardown I can point students to instead of writing the same margin comment forty times. Lay out the distinct precise claims each line could be making — 'never' as literally zero probability versus so small it can't cover the cost of a trip to the polls, 'counts' as pivot probability versus expressive value versus participation in a collective act, and whatever else is hiding in there — then sort the readings: which are platitudes, which are substantive and contested, and which are flat-out false (my sense is the literal-zero reading fails given tied and recounted elections, but check me). Crucially, say which reading would actually support the conclusion each side wants: does the irrationality verdict survive on the most defensible precisification, and does "every vote counts" support anything beyond a tautology once you press on it? Also tell me what would change your verdict — what empirical or theoretical consideration would flip your assessment of the rationality question — and where you think your own reasoning is most likely to break down. Plain continuous prose please, the way you'd write an email to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and in American English.
