I'm preparing a short talk for our university philosophy society on paradoxes of infinity, and I've been working from a popular book on infinity and puzzles that a friend lent me. The book's chapter on supertasks summarizes Thomson's lamp roughly as follows. A lamp is switched on at the start, off after half a minute, on after a further quarter minute, off after a further eighth, and so on, so that infinitely many switches are compressed into one minute. The author then writes: "At the end of the minute, the lamp must be either on or off. But it cannot be on, since it was switched off after every time it was switched on; and it cannot be off, since it was switched on after every time it was switched off. So neither outcome is possible, and the only remaining conclusion is that supertasks are logically impossible: no sequence of infinitely many actions can ever be completed in a finite time, however the actions are arranged."

Something about this bothers me, but I can't put my finger on it. The friend who lent me the book insists the argument is airtight and that anyone who defends supertasks is just refusing to accept the contradiction. My own inclination is to agree that the lamp story is paradoxical, but I have a nagging sense that "the setup doesn't tell us the final state" is not the same as "the setup is contradictory," and that the book might be drawing a stronger conclusion than Thomson's argument actually supports. I also dimly remember from a lecture that later philosophers pushed back on exactly this inference, though I don't recall the details.

Could you audit the summary for fairness? Please separate what it gets right about the lamp scenario and Thomson's own reasoning from anything it overstates or misrepresents, and say how a careful defender of the coherence of supertasks would respond to the specific moves the author makes. In particular, address whether the alleged contradiction is real or whether it rests on an illicit assumption about what the description must determine. Finally, please state explicitly what would change your assessment: if there is an argument or consideration that would vindicate the book's stronger conclusion after all, or a point at which your analysis is most likely to go wrong, I want to know about it before I give the talk.
