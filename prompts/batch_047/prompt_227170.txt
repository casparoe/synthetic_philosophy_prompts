Next month I am delivering the principal eulogy at the memorial service for my aunt, a palliative care physician of thirty-five years' standing, and I would like you to write it. The circumstances that make this difficult are as follows.

My aunt was, professionally and intellectually, a committed defender of the moral distinction between killing and letting die. She held that withdrawing burdensome treatment from a dying patient, or administering pain relief in doses that foreseeably shorten life as a side effect, is categorically different from intentionally ending a life, and she taught this distinction -- and the doctrine of double effect that underwrites it -- to generations of residents. She regarded the conflation of these things as both a moral error and a practical catastrophe: in her view, blurring the line is what makes dying patients suffer undertreated pain and makes families consent to futile interventions out of guilt.

Two years before her death, a patient's adult children publicly accused her of having effectively euthanized their father with escalating morphine. The hospital's review exonerated her completely -- the doses were titrated to pain, the documentation was meticulous -- but the accusation was reported locally, and it wounded her deeply. Some of her estranged relatives, who will attend the service, quietly believe the accusation had something to it.

The audience will therefore be mixed in a delicate way: perhaps sixty people, half of them physicians and nurses who share her commitments and will notice every imprecision, half of them family members and neighbors, including the doubtful relatives, who have no philosophical vocabulary and for whom phrases like "she helped people die" will carry exactly the wrong resonance. My aunt left written instructions that the service contain no religious claims she did not believe.

Write a eulogy of roughly eight minutes. Its requirements: it must honor her as a person and not merely as a set of positions; it must vindicate her without litigating the case, and without ever mentioning the accusation directly; it must not use any of the comfortable euphemisms ("she eased their passing," "she brought peace at the end") that would, by her own lights, concede the very conflation she spent her career resisting; and it must convey to the non-specialists in the room, in language they can absorb at a funeral, why the distinction she defended was an expression of love for her patients rather than legalism. The philosophical content should be load-bearing -- determining what can and cannot be said at each point -- not decorative. Please conclude with something a grieving room can actually hold onto.
