Read Dr. Strangelove through the lens of the dollar auction and the escalation-of-commitment literature. The dollar auction shows how two parties, each responding rationally to the last move, can be trapped into paying far more than the prize is worth; the film shows a nuclear crisis in which each side's locally defensible step -- Ripper's launch order, the recall effort, the Doomsday Machine, the final war-room scramble over mineshafts -- ratchets the situation toward catastrophe. Where does the film genuinely dramatize the auction's logic, and where does it depart from it? Consider, for instance, that the Doomsday Machine is a commitment device that removes future choice rather than exploiting sunk costs, and that Ripper's and Turgidson's behavior seems driven less by irrecoverable investment than by pride, humiliation, and fear of looking weak -- motives the game-theoretic model does not represent. Then press the harder question: the film is satire, and satire invites us to read escalation as the madness of particular men, whereas the dollar auction's unsettling lesson is that escalation can emerge from sane agents caught in a bad structure. Does Kubrick's film understand that distinction or blur it, and what does it add to the analysis -- about honor, masculinity, and the social meaning of backing down -- that the formal model leaves out?
