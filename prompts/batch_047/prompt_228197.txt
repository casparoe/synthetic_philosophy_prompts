So I TA an upper-level phil of language seminar and we've just spent three weeks slogging through Kripke's book on rule-following. Last meeting is next week and, partly as a joke and partly not, I want to open the session by reading a poem out loud and then asking the students whether the poem gets it right. So: write me a villanelle about the rule-following paradox. Not a poem that mentions it -- a poem where the form is doing the argument. My thought, which you should feel free to steal, is that a villanelle's two refrains coming back unchanged over and over is basically the skeptical solution enacted in the medium: the words mean what they mean because they get used again the same way, and that's all the "correctness" there is. If the poem can make the reader feel that rather than just say it, I'll be thrilled.

Content-wise: the first refrain should be the skeptic's point that nothing in my finite past usage -- and no disposition either, since dispositions are finite and can misfire -- fixes whether I meant plus or quus, so any continuation can be made to "accord with the rule" under some interpretation. The middle tercets should run the obvious replies (I just know what I meant! my brain was wired to add! the community says so!) and let each one collapse, but please don't let the poem end with the crude "the community votes on what you meant" reading -- the actual skeptical solution is subtler, about the conditions under which we're licensed to call a use wrong and the role that practice plays in our lives, not a committee deciding arithmetic. The final quatrain should land on the thought that the paradox doesn't get answered so much as dissolved, that we stop looking for the inner fact and notice we've been playing the game the whole time. It should be accurate enough that my professor wouldn't wince if she were in the room, but still function as a poem -- some actual imagery, not just "quus" rhymed with "thus" nineteen times (though you will obviously have to rhyme quus at least once, good luck). Nineteen lines, proper aba scheme, refrains repeated where the form demands.
