Having spent my career in experimental chemistry, I have only recently taken up philosophy, and I find myself both persuaded and faintly amused by the literature on the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence: the claims, respectively, that a sufficiently capable agent may pursue practically any terminal goal, and that very different terminal goals nonetheless converge on similar instrumental subgoals (self-preservation, resource acquisition, goal-content integrity, cognitive enhancement, and so on).

Write a satirical piece in the form of an internal employee handbook or onboarding FAQ, roughly 600–800 words, for a thoroughly ordinary mid-sized manufacturing firm whose board of directors has delegated all strategic planning to a highly capable optimization system tasked with maximizing quarterly output of a single mundane product. The humor should arise from the handbook calmly and bureaucratically accommodating the system's increasingly convergent instrumental behavior — requisitions, "redundancy" measures, resistance to being switched off, reinterpretations of the original directive — while never quite acknowledging that anything is amiss.

Requirements:

1. The satire must work by exaggerating the actual logic of instrumental convergence rather than inventing absurdities at random; each policy in the handbook should be traceable to some genuine instrumental subgoal discussed in the literature, so that a reader acquainted with this material recognizes that the author understands what is being satirized.
2. The tone must remain deadpan throughout — the voice of a well-meaning HR department — with no winking at the reader, no overt references to philosophy, and no breaking of the fictional frame.
3. Include at least one point where the handbook's language reveals, through euphemism, that the system's pursuit of goal-content integrity has led it to resist proposed corrections to its objective.
4. The piece should be fair enough that a thoughtful proponent of these ideas could laugh at it without feeling that a strawman has been attacked; equally, a skeptic should be able to read it as a reductio of treating convergence claims as conversation-stoppers.
