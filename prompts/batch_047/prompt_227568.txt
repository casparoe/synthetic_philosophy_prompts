On a rationality forum I read, people defend cooperating in a one-shot prisoners dilemma with what they call the mirror argument: if your opponent is an exact copy of you -- same values, same reasoning, same everything -- then choosing is like raising your hand in front of a mirror. Whatever you do, the reflection does too, so the only outcomes on the table are both-cooperate and both-defect, and cooperate obviously wins. They use this to say defecting against a copy is just irrational, no enforcement or repetition needed, and they extend it to voting: if it makes sense for me to stay home because one vote never decides anything, then it makes sense for everyone who thinks like me to stay home, so the mirror says go vote.

Something feels off to me. A mirror's arm moves because yours moved -- nobody thinks the copy's choice is *caused* by mine, we're in seperate rooms deciding independently. So can you audit the analogy? Give me the correspondence table (what plays the role of the mirror, the reflection, the causal link between me and the glass), say which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusions its being asked to carry and which are just cosmetic, and tell me whether cooperate still wins once the "my choice moves theirs" picture is stripped out -- i.e. whether correlation-without-causation can do the same argumentative work. And if the mirror is the wrong image, what's a better analogy for whatever the symmetry of two identical reasoners actually buys you? Finally, does the voting extension survive, given that there the "copies" are merely similar people rather than exact duplicates?
