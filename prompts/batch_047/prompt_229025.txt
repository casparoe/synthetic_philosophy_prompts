I'm in a graduate seminar on population ethics, and this week we worked through the procreation asymmetry — the four-part claim associated with David Benatar that (1) the presence of pain in a life is bad, (2) the absence of pain is good even if there is no one who enjoys that absence, (3) the presence of pleasure is good, and (4) the absence of pleasure is not bad when there is no one deprived of it. This is used to argue that, evaluatively speaking, coming into existence is always worse than never existing: existence contains pains whose absence would have been good, while the pleasures of existence have no counterpart whose absence would count against never existing.

Here's where my study group got stuck. It seems to us that the argument runs entirely on clauses (1) and (2). Pain is the whole engine: a life containing any suffering at all is already worse than the good absence of that suffering, so the two pleasure clauses look like dispensable bookkeeping — the conclusion would follow just as well if you simply ignored pleasure altogether. Given that, we've been puzzled that critics of the argument spend most of their energy attacking clause (4) rather than clause (2), which strikes us as a strange choice of target, almost as if they're fighting the part of the argument that does no work. Are the critics simply mistaken about where the argument's weight rests, or is there a reason clause (4) is indispensable — such that denying it while keeping (1)–(3) would block or reverse the conclusion? If the pleasure clauses really are load-bearing, I'd like to see exactly where in the inference they get used, why the pain clauses alone can't carry the argument to the preferability of never existing, and what the dialectic looks like for someone who accepts (2) but rejects (4).
