I'm writing the final exam for my upper-level undergrad seminar on probability and anthropic reasoning, and I need one big question on the self-indication assumption (SIA) versus the self-sampling assumption (SSA) that students can't answer just by reciting the definitions and mentioning Sleeping Beauty. Here's the setup I want the question built around -- feel free to adjust the framing but keep the numbers clean.

Cosmologists have narrowed things down to two theories that agree on every observation we could ever make. SPARSE says ours is the only technological civilization that will ever exist, about 10^14 observer-lives total. CROWDED says there are about 10^6 such civilizations, causally isolated from each other, so 10^20 observer-lives total. On the physics alone the priors are 50/50. The question should make students, in order: (a) compute what SIA does to the odds and explain exactly why SSA leaves the odds untouched here; (b) handle a twist -- they learn that they themselves are an astronomer, and under CROWDED only one civilization in a thousand ever develops astronomy (assume any astronomy-doing civilization produces about 10^8 astronomer-lives) -- so they have to show the likelihoods and where each principle lands now (if I've done the math right, SIA ends up favoring CROWDED 1000:1 and SSA ends up favoring SPARSE 1000:1, and part of the point is seeing why the same evidence rides on top of the anthropic update so differently); (c) say whether SIA's willingness to become near-certain about CROWDED from the armchair, before any telescopes get involved, is a reductio of SIA or a bullet its defender should just bite, and a good answer should note SSA has symmetric armchair embarrassments of its own (birth-rank / doomsday style reasoning) so "that's presumptuous" alone can't adjudicate; and (d) discuss whether either side can escape trouble by adjusting the reference class, where the strongest answers will notice SSA's verdict in (b) flips or vanishes depending on the reference class chosen, while SIA avoids reference classes but blows up in infinite universes and so needs its own restriction anyway.

Deliverables: the exam question as it would appear on the paper (assume 75 minutes, 30 marks), a model answer of about 600 words that actually carries out the calculations in (b) rather than gesturing at them, and a marking scheme with five must-hit moves, the marks each is worth (totalling 30), and a one-line note per move on how students typically mess it up -- e.g. in (b) applying the likelihood ratio to the wrong hypothesis, or in (a) claiming SSA gives zero update because "indexical evidence is impossible," which conflates SSA with something stronger.
