I was recently elected to the management council of a housing co-operative of some four hundred households, and within three months we must decide whether to sell our original 1920s block in order to fund urgent repairs across the rest of the estate. Feelings are running high, and at our last meeting a council member proposed that we settle the question not by referendum, as our rules would ordinarily require, but by a conditional prediction market. Two markets would be opened. Members would trade contracts whose payout depends on the co-operative's audited financial position and its annual member-satisfaction survey five years from now; trades in one market would be voided if we sell the block, trades in the other if we do not, and the decision would follow whichever market implies the better outcome. To meet the objection that this simply lets wealthier members buy the result, stakes would be denominated in service credits earned through voluntary work for the co-operative, capped per member per year, though the credits could also be redeemed against service charges and so would carry genuine value. Half the council is enthusiastic, arguing that markets will aggregate what members actually know better than a referendum decided by whoever makes the most emotional speech at a general meeting. The other half finds it illegitimate to let what is, in effect, a betting pool determine where people live; they add that a market with perhaps forty traders could be moved by one determined member, and that credits merely substitute the time-rich for the money-rich, favouring the retired over working parents. Please draft the decision memorandum I will table at next month's meeting. It should recommend adoption, rejection, or adoption with stated conditions, give reasons that the proposal's author could actually respond to, and take seriously the strongest consideration on whichever side loses rather than simply restating our procedures. Although it is a memorandum, please write it as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a formal letter.
