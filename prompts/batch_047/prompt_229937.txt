A friend in my reading group has been working through Sleeping Beauty-type cases for a paper, and I've been keeping a running tally of her verdcits because I suspect she's committed to more than she realizes. All the cases involve the standard setup where everything is explained to the subject in advance and memory wipes work perfectly. Her calls so far:

1. Classic Sleeping Beauty (fair coin; heads she's woken Monday only, tails she's woken Monday and Tuesday with amnesia): credence in heads = 1/3.
2. The coin only decides which single day she's woken (heads Monday, tails Tuesday, exactly one awakening either way): heads = 1/2.
3. Same as (1) but the coin is biased, only a 1/4 chance of heads: heads = 1/7.
4. Same as (1), except that on her awakening the experimenter immediately walks in and tells her it's Monday: heads now = 1/2.
5. Duplication case: overnight the lab makes an exact copy of her in an indistinguishable room, and a fair coin decides which room gets a red folder on the desk: credence she's in the red-folder room = 1/2.
6. Fair coin again, but heads she gets two awakenings and tails she gets three: heads = 2/5.

Next case on the docket, which she hasn't seen yet: classic setup, fair coin, but if heads the lab also wakes 1,000 other volunteers, each exactly once, each in their own room, all fully informed of the setup. If tails, it's just her, woken Monday and Tuesday as usual. On awakening, what's her credence in heads?

Predict her verdict and give me the number. More importantly, tell me which rule she's implicitly running: counting only her own awakenings when weighting the worlds, weighting worlds by the total number of observer-moments they contain, or some third thing. As far as I can tell, verdicts 1 through 6 are jointly consistent with at least two rules that diverge sharply on the new case -- one gives 1/3, the other gives roughly 1001/1003 -- so say which you think she's actually committed to and why. If you think one of her earlier verdicts already settles it (she insists (4) is neutral, I'm not so sure), say so. And name the further case, if there is one, that would cleanly tell the candidate rules apart if she gives an unexpected answer.
