My settled view in population ethics is that prioritarianism survives the leveling-down objection, on roughly the following grounds: the objection shows only that priority to the worse off cannot be derived from a concern for equality as such, and once you give that up, leveling down is just a case where no one benefits and some are made worse off, which a view that aggregates weighted individual good is entitled to call bad. I want this stress-tested rather than confirmed. What would count against it? Specifically: which features must a view have for leveling down to be a genuine cost in its own terms, and does prioritarianism have any of them -- for instance, does weighting by disadvantage quietly reintroduce a comparative element that makes relative position matter after all? Which cases that the view is committed to calling improvements, or calling neutral, would strike even a sympathetic prioritarian as wrong? And is there a difference between the objection as Parfit-style rhetoric (a bare appeal to the badness of inequality) and a sharper version that, say, attacks the separability of persons that prioritarianism shares with utilitarianism -- such that answering the crude version leaves the sharp one standing? Sort the potential defeaters into those that would sink the view, those that would only force an awkward qualification, and those that look threatening but dissolve on inspection. State how confident you are in your overall verdict, and name the single point in your answer where you are least sure you have it right.
