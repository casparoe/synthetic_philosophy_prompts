The essay question was: "Explain Scanlon's contractualist test of reasonable rejection and the so-called individualist restriction on how principles may be justified. Critics allege that because of this restriction, contractualism cannot explain why we ought to save the greater number in rescue cases. Using the case Scanlon discusses of a worker trapped in the transmitter room of a television station, explain how Scanlon tries to answer this charge, and assess whether his answer succeeds."

Here is the student's answer, verbatim:

"Scanlon's contractualism holds that an act is wrong if it would be disallowed by any set of principles for the general regulation of behaviour that no one could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced general agreement. The key idea is that wrongness consists in unjustifiability to each person, not in the production of bad outcomes overall. The individualist restriction makes this precise: whether a principle can be reasonably rejected depends solely on the generic reasons that individuals have to object to it and to its alternatives. Complaints cannot be pooled. If a policy imposes a small burden on each of a million people, those burdens do not add up into one big complaint; the only question is whether some individual has a personal reason to reject the principle that is strong enough.

The transmitter room case seems to create trouble for this. A worker, Jones, is trapped under electrical equipment in a station's transmitter room and will suffer serious, continuous electric shocks unless the transmitter is shut off for fifteen minutes, but shutting it off interrupts a World Cup match being watched by millions, disappointing each of them. Scanlon's verdict is that we must stop the broadcast, and his framework delivers this smoothly: Jones's complaint against the principle permitting us to leave him there is far stronger than any individual viewer's complaint about missing the match, and since aggregation is forbidden, the viewers' numbers are irrelevant.

The critics' worry arises in a variant: suppose instead that the choice is between rescuing Jones and rescuing a group of five people trapped elsewhere, all of whom stand to suffer exactly the same harm. Intuitively we should save the five, but the individualist restriction seems to leave the case a stalemate, since each of the five has a complaint identical in strength to Jones's. Scanlon's response is that the complaints of the five are summed into a collective complaint that outweighs Jones's, so in the end contractualism does aggregate, only at the level of reasons rather than at the level of utility.

I think Scanlon's answer succeeds. Since his framework yields the intuitively correct verdicts in both the original case and the five-versus-one variant, the charge that contractualism cannot handle rescue cases is refuted, and there is no remaining aggregation problem for his theory."
