A colleague in our physics reading group has assembled an argument that purports to show the second law of thermodynamics can be beaten in principle, and although each step looks defensible to me, I cannot accept the conclusion. The setting is the standard Szilard engine: a single molecule confined to a box in contact with a heat bath at temperature T; a partition is inserted, the molecule's location is measured, a piston converts the resulting isothermal expansion into work W = kT ln 2, and the apparatus is restored. The argument runs as follows:

(1) Extracting the work requires the demon to record one bit of information — which half of the box the molecule occupies.

(2) Erasing a bit from a memory register dissipates at least kT ln 2 of heat into the environment; this is Landauer's principle, and it is what ordinarily saves the second law in such cases.

(3) Writing a bit onto a register that is already in a known, standard state, by contrast, is a logically reversible operation, and logically reversible operations can in principle be carried out with arbitrarily little dissipation.

(4) So suppose the demon simply never erases. Equip it with a very long tape of registers, all initially in the standard state; on each cycle it writes the measurement outcome onto the next fresh register and moves on.

(5) Per cycle, then, the engine delivers kT ln 2 of work, the demon incurs no erasure cost, and no dissipative step occurs anywhere in the process.

(6) Nor is the tape exotic: any large, cold, ordered system contains effectively unwritten degrees of freedom in enormous supply, so a practically inexhaustible stock of blank registers is physically available.

(7) Therefore the second law is violable in principle: heat drawn from a single bath is converted entirely into work, with no compensating entropy increase anywhere.

I take (1) through (3) to be the established physics of this problem and would not want the resolution to touch them; and given the choice, I would much rather conclude that something is wrong with the demon's design than that the second law fails. But when I press my colleague on where the entropy goes, he replies that no information is ever destroyed, so Landauer's principle is simply never triggered, and that the tape is inert — it passively accumulates records and never again interacts with the gas, so its condition is thermodynamically irrelevant. I cannot articulate why he is wrong. Which step should I reject, what made it look innocent, and what does rejecting it commit me to? If the argument is in fact valid as far as it goes, then tell me instead which premise I should be least willing to keep, and what it costs to abandon it.
