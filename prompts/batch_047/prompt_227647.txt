I recently became chair of the grants committee of a small family foundation, which distributes about two million dollars a year, after my predecessor resigned in frustration over our recurring deadlock. The committee has seven voting members who cluster into three stable blocs with genuinely different moral outlooks: three members judge every proposal strictly by expected benefit per dollar wherever in the world that benefit falls; two hold that our foundation's primary duties are rectificatory and local, owed to the community our family made its money in, whatever the aggregate arithmetic says; and two give substantial moral standing to nonhuman animals and regard both other blocs as myopic. This year's three finalists map exactly onto the blocs: a malaria-prevention program abroad, a bail fund and legal-defense clinic in our own city, and a corporate animal-welfare campaign. Every cycle ends the same way — each bloc votes its conscience, we deadlock 3-2-2 — and the trustees have now ruled that simply rolling the money back into the endowment, which had been our default, is itself a decision and an unacceptable one.

At our last meeting, two procedural proposals surfaced. One member suggested we treat the committee as a miniature parliament of moral outlooks: divide the budget in proportion to bloc size and let each group direct its share as it sees fit. Our lone economist proposed instead that every member state a confidence that their own outlook is roughly correct, and that we fund whichever project scores best when each bloc's verdict is weighted by those confidences — maximizing, as he put it, how right the portfolio is in expectation. I was initially drawn to the proportional split as a fair compromise, but I've come to worry that it is a solution no one at the table actually endorses: each bloc believes the others' spending is, to varying degrees, money wasted or worse, so the split satisfies nobody's theory, only everybody's fatigue. The confidence-weighting scheme, meanwhile, seems to require comparing degrees of rightness across outlooks on a single scale, and I cannot make sense of what it would even mean to say the bail fund is, by the rectificatory bloc's lights, half as choice-worthy as the malaria program is good by the welfarists' lights.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before our next meeting. It should recommend a concrete allocation of this year's two million dollars — you may recommend a division of funds, but only if you can defend it on grounds at least one bloc actually holds, rather than as a splitting of the difference — along with a standing procedure for future cycles. It should also give an honest treatment of the strongest argument for each option you reject, and if you reject the confidence-weighting approach, it needs a real answer to the economist's rejoinder: that we all implicitly perform cross-outlook comparisons whenever any one of us changes our mind about morality, so refusing to do so inside the committee room is not principled but merely squeamish.
