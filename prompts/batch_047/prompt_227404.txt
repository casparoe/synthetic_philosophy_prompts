im writing the final exam for my upper-level undergrad course on free will and moral responsibility (juniors and seniors, about 25 students, they've all read strawson's piece and the standard replies). i need one essay question on galen strawson's basic argument against moral responsibility, answerable in a 2-hour sitting, worth 20 marks out of 60 on the paper.

what i don't want is "explain the basic argument and critically discuss" -- that just tests who memorized the summary. so build the question around this case i made up: mira grows up in an isolated community that drills into her from childhood that loyalty to the group trumps everything, including honesty toward outsiders. at 25, after exposure to outside ideas, she steps back, deliberates seriously, knows she could walk away, and reflectively endorses the loyalty principle as genuinely her own. she then lies to an outsider, in service of the group, in a way that causes real harm.

the question should make candidates run the basic argument on this case rather than recite it: does mira's reflective endorsement stop the regress (is she responsible for the way she is because she chose to endorse it?), does her case differ in any responsibility-relevant way from an ordinary agent whose values came from an ordinary non-manipulative upbringing, and whatever they conclude about mira, what follows for the ordinary case?

deliverables:

1. the exam question as it would appear on the paper, with sub-parts and mark allocations shown
2. a model answer a strong candidate could realistically produce in the time, so roughly 800-1000 words, not a treatise
3. a marking scheme built around five must-hit points with marks per point adding to 20, and for each point a one-line note on the characteristic way a mediocre answer fumbles it

the must-hit points should definately include: stating the argument precisely enough that the causa sui requirement is visible (not just "your character causes your actions"); why strawson thinks reflective endorsement or self-revision is itself just more of how you already are; why appeals to indeterminism or self-forming choices don't rescue her; the objection that the argument proves too much because it would equally wipe out epistemic responsibility for one's beliefs, and whether that's a reductio or a bullet to bite; and a defended verdict on mira vs the ordinary agent. if you think one of those doesn't deserve a slot, swap it out, but say why.

also have the marking scheme name what loses marks automatically: treating the basic argument as an argument against compatibilism specifically, assuming determinism is doing the work, or offering "she could have done otherwise" as if that settled anything.
