I teach philosophy at a mid-sized university and, for my sins, I also sit on our college's student grievance committee, which means about once a month I help decide what to do with complaints that range from plagiarism accusations to "Professor X humiliated me in seminar." At a recent meeting a colleague dismissed one complaint as "just honor culture stuff," borrowing from that Campbell and Manning line of work on moral cultures -- the distinction between honor cultures (slights must be answered personally or status is lost), dignity cultures (your worth isn't up for grabs, so you shrug off insults and use formal channels for serious harms), and victimhood culture (appeals to third parties and public displays of victimization as a way of mobilizing support). I've been thinking the typology might actually be useful as triage: not to decide who's right, but to figure out what kind of conflict we're even looking at and what response each kind calls for. But I don't want to improvise it anew each meeting, and the other committee members aren't going to read the sociology literature.

So here's what I want: turn the honor/dignity/victimhood framework into a classification-and-response procedure the committee can actually run in the first twenty minutes of looking at a complaint. It should give us questions to ask in order, say what answers sort a case into which category (including what a genuinely hybrid case looks like, since real complaints are usually messier than the theory), and then attach a recommended handling style to each category -- e.g., what mediation looks like when the underlying logic is honor-based versus when the complaint is structured as an appeal to third parties. It also needs to say explicitly where classification must stop short: the diagnosis of which moral culture is operating can't be allowed to settle the merits, and I'd like the procedure to build in a guard against that, plus a guard against us treating "this is victimhood culture framing" as itself a reason to dismiss. Run the procedure on three worked examples I'll supply: a student demanding a public apology from a classmate who mocked his hometown accent; a student filing a formal complaint that a professor's repeated sarcastic comments about her work amount to harassment; and a group of students circulating a petition calling for a lecturer's removal over a single classroom remark. Include at least one case where the procedure spits out a recommendation I'll find uncomfortable -- say, advising formal mediation for a complaint I personally think is frivolous -- and mark clearly the points where the classification gives out and it's just committee judgment.

Two requests about how you answer. First, give me the bottom line in the opening sentence -- whether the typology can actually bear the weight of being operationalized like this -- then the procedure itself, then the worked cases, then the caveats; no warm-up and no restating my question back at me. Second, push back where warranted: I know this literature has critics who say the three cultures are ideal types at best and maybe a moralized caricature at worst, so if you think the premise of building committee procedure on it is confused, or that I've misdescribed one of the categories, say so plainly rather than politely working around it.
