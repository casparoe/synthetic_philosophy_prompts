In a graduate seminar on moral philosophy, a classmate presented an argument that has been nagging at me for weeks, and I would like help locating exactly where it goes wrong. The setup is a rescue dilemma with the numbers tuned to be uncomfortable. A fire breaks out at a chemical plant on the edge of a coastal town, and the town's single firefighting helicopter can do one of two things: it can rescue Mara, a worker trapped on a collapsing gantry who will certainly die without it, or it can dump retardant on the blaze before a toxic plume forms that would leave each of ten thousand residents downwind with permanent, severe paralysis. It cannot do both. Stipulate, as is standard in such cases, that death is a somewhat worse outcome for Mara than lifelong paralysis is for each individual resident.

Her argument ran as follows. (1) An act is wrong just in case it would be disallowed by any principle that no one could reasonably reject. (2) Whether someone can reasonably reject a principle is a matter of the strength of her personal complaint against it: how much worse off that principle leaves her, given her position, than the available alternatives would. (3) Complaints must be assessed one person at a time; the fact that a burden falls on many people does not make any one person's complaint against it stronger. This individualist restriction, she noted, is precisely what is supposed to distinguish the contractualist comparison from the aggregative, utilitarian one. (4) To choose between principles, then, one compares the strongest complaint on each side. (5) Mara's complaint against a principle that lets her die is stronger than any single resident's complaint against a principle that lets the plume form, because death is worse for Mara than paralysis is for each resident. (6) A principle requiring the town to save the ten thousand is therefore one Mara can reasonably reject, so the town is instead required to save her. (7) Since nothing in this reasoning depends on the numbers, contractualism requires sparing one person a slightly greater harm rather than sparing any number of people a slightly lesser one — a million paralyzed, a billion, it makes no difference. She concluded that contractualism, consistently applied, proves too much and should be abandoned.

I do not want to abandon it, but I cannot find the error. Premise (3) strikes me as the heart of the view — it is what keeps the theory from collapsing into aggregation by the back door — and (5) is true by stipulation, so my hunch is that the trouble lies in (2) or (4), though I cannot articulate what is wrong with either. Please tell me which step I should reject and explain why it looked plausible in the first place; then price the main alternatives: dropping or softening the individualist restriction (and what then distinguishes contractualism from a pluralist consequentialism that simply weighs complaints); taking the route usually associated with Scanlon himself, on which numbers may break ties among "relevantly similar" claims (and whether that route can even reach this case, where the claims are dissimilar, without smuggling in the very aggregation the view was built to avoid); or building more into the notion of what a person can reasonably be asked to bear (and whether that risks making the theory circular by encoding the verdict into the standard of reasonableness). It would also help to know what a consistent contractualist should say about the pure tie case — Mara's life against two strangers' lives — and whether the answer there forces an answer here.
