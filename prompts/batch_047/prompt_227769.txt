I am taking an upper-level undergraduate seminar on effective altruism and political philosophy. For a class exercise, write a dialogue of roughly 1,000–1,300 words between two equally sophisticated students debating the following case.

Mira has two job offers. The first is at a proprietary trading firm whose activities seem socially neutral rather than beneficial; she expects that, after several years, she could donate about $100,000 annually to highly evidence-backed global-health charities. The second is as a researcher for a small housing-policy organization advocating reforms that could substantially reduce urban poverty, although its causal impact is difficult to measure and any success would be shared with many other actors.

Daniel defends the trading position as a morally responsible form of earning to give. Elena argues that the choice illustrates what is wrong with effective altruism as an institutional practice, even if Mira’s donations would do genuine good.

The dialogue must remain tied to this particular decision rather than becoming a generic debate about philanthropy. Elena should develop at least three distinct objections: the diversion of talented people away from reforming public institutions, the cumulative pipeline effect by which prestigious career choices alter the ambitions of peers, and measurability bias in favor of outcomes that are immediate, attributable, and quantifiable. Daniel should respond using counterfactual replaceability, the distinction between marginal and average impact, and skepticism about whether well-intentioned political work reliably produces systemic improvement.

Both characters must engage with the other’s actual arguments. In particular, Daniel may not treat an effect that is hard to measure as though it were known to be small, while Elena may not assume that institutional work is valuable merely because it sounds structural. Each should concede at least one substantive point, identify one empirical or moral premise that would change their mind, and explain whether their disagreement concerns Mira’s individual duty or the social norms that effective altruism encourages.

End the dialogue without an authoritative resolution, but make the remaining source of disagreement philosophically precise. Do not include citations, quotations, or a concluding narrator explaining which character is correct.
