I'm giving a ~20 minute talk at a small AI safety workshop next month and need to survive the Q&A. Audience is roughly thirds: philosophers (phil mind / phil language types), ML evals and interpretability people, and alignment generalists who've read all the same posts I have.

My thesis: we should give up on intentionalist definitions of deception for LLMs (the model believes X, outputs not-X in order to make you believe not-X) and define it selection-theoretically instead: a behavior is deceptive iff the training process selected for it *because* it induces false beliefs in the user. Belief-mismatch has to be part of the causal story of why the behavior survived training, not just an effect of it. Consequences I definately want to defend: most of what evals currently flag as "deception" (sycophancy, confident wrongness) is bullshit in Frankfurt's sense rather than deception, and a real deception eval would have to produce evidence about selection pressure, not just behavior.
