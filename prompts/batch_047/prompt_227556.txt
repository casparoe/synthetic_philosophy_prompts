I am a high school junior, and our debate club is currently doing a unit on the epistemology of conspiracy theories. To prepare, I would like you to play a committed generalist: someone who holds that conspiracy theories, as a class, carry a presumption of irrationality, such that we are entitled to distrust them on the basis of general features — a self-sealing structure that absorbs counterevidence, an ever-expanding scope of the alleged plot, and the corrosive distrust of public institutions they presuppose — without investigating each theory's evidence case by case. Play this position seriously, not as a strawman: you may concede small points, but never the main one, and you should press back on me hard. Keep each reply to a paragraph or two so it feels like a real exchange, and stay in character until I say "stop." Here is my opening move: several real conspiracies, such as the Tuskegee syphilis experiment and the FBI's COINTELPRO operations against domestic political groups, were dismissed as paranoid fantasy for years before documentation proved them. Had your presumption been in force, reasonable suspicion of those cases would have been written off as well. So isn't generalism simply a recipe for missing the conspiracies that turn out to be true — and doesn't that defeat the entire epistemological purpose of having such a presumption?
