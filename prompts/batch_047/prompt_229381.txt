I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about why political philosophers still can't agree on what egalitarians are supposed to be equalising. The equality-of-what debate has been running for forty-odd years with, as far as I can tell, zero movement. Roughly: the welfarists say the currency is how well people's lives actually go for them; the resourcists say the state should sort out holdings and opportunities and then stay neutral on what people do with them; and the capability lot say it's real freedoms to achieve functionings that matter. And everyone still argues by wheeling out the same elderly counterexamples — the person with expensive tastes who needs caviar to feel as good as everyone else, the person who adapts cheerfully to a rotten situation and so scores fine on welfare, the person born disabled who burns through extra resources just reaching the baseline. Each camp knows the other's examples and replies perfectly well, and nobody ever changes sides, which seems odd for a debate this old.

My working hypothesis for the piece — and please poke holes in it — is that this was never really a disagreement about metrics at all, but three seperate jobs being asked of one theory: compensating for brute bad luck, respecting people as agents responsible for their own choices, and guaranteeing everyone basic social standing. If that's right, intuition-pumping about cases was doomed from the start, because each camp's killer example is only killer if you've already decided which job the theory is for.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict and not a survey. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about the word equality, or three camps answering different questions under one heading? And what would progress even look like — a clarifying distinction, an explicit decision about what egalitarianism is for, some empirical finding about adaptive preferences — or is the impasse itself evidence the question is ill-posed?

Give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, no introduction and no conclusion.
