After thirty-odd years as a marine geologist I retired, got bored within six months, and somehow ended up volunteering for the professional conduct commitee of my old scientific society. Now I wish I'd done the philosophy reading earlier. Here's our case. A member, a hydrologist, was asked during a grant audit whether her lab's monitoring wells had shown any contamination near a client's construction site. She answered that the wells had never recorded contaminant levels above the regulatory threshold. That sentence is literally true. What she left out is that the threshold she used was a superseded one, roughly ten times looser than the current standard, and that under the current standard two wells had exceeded the limit for months. So nothing she said was false -- she answered the question asked, with a true sentence, and let the auditors walk away with the wrong picture.

Our code of conduct prohibits members from knowingly making false or deceptive statements in professional contexts, but it never defines "deceptive," and the committee is split down the middle. One faction says wrongdoing here requires actually saying something false, so this is at worst a minor offense or none at all -- the auditors should have asked better questions and checked which standard applied. The other faction says deliberately engineering a false belief in your audience is just as blameworthy whether you do it with a false sentence or a carefully chosen true one. I've been reading a bit of philosophy of language in my retirement (implicature, what you communicate versus what you literally say, that sort of thing) and I honestly can't tell whether the moral line is supposed to track the linguistic one, or whether the linguistic distinction is doing no real moral work at all.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before our vote. It should recommend a concrete outcome -- violation, no violation, or a lesser violation with a suggested sanction -- and take a clear stand on whether the lying-versus-misleading distinction is morally relevant to what she did, with actual reasons rather than just asserting it. Whatever side the memo lands on, it has to give the strongest version of the losing argument a fair shake and explain why it doesn't win. And since this will come up again, the memo should close by proposing a general rule for how the committee handles technically-true-but-misleading statements going forward.

After the memo itself, put a short structured summary at the end as a JSON object with exactly these fields: recommendation (string: the bottom-line outcome), sanction_if_any (string), treats_lying_misleading_distinction_as_morally_relevant (boolean), core_reasoning (string, two or three sentences max), strongest_losing_argument (string), response_to_that_argument (string), and proposed_general_rule (string).
