I'm a novelist and my writing partner sprung this on me over drinks and it's been eating at me because it seems to wreck the book I'm halfway through. The novel follows one character, Ada, from age 8 to about 60. His argument, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) A person at one time is literally the same person as someone at an earlier time only if some essential core carries over -- same matter, same memories, same character, *something* continuous.
(2) Ada at 8 and Ada at 60 share basically nothing: her cells have turned over many times, she barely remembers her childhood (and what she does remember is reconstructed and partly false, which is actually a plot point), and her whole temperament and value system flip after the war chapters.
(3) So the old woman in the final chapters is not literally the kid from chapter one -- at best a very close successor.
(4) But my ending only works if it's literal sameness: she forgives the brother who betrayed *her*, and you can't forgive a wrong that was done to a stranger, no matter how similar to you the stranger was.
(5) So either the ending of my novel rests on a mistake, or one of (1)-(3) is wrong, and neither of us could say which.

He also claimed this isn't just about fiction, that if the argument works it shows forgiveness between actual people decades apart is "metaphysically confused" too, which seems like proving way too much.

I've poked around and I know the endurantism/perdurantism thing is roughly the neighborhood here, but please don't just tell me to pick a side in that debate and call it a day. Which step should I actually reject, why did it look so plausible, and what does rejecting it cost me? If I'm forced to choose I'd rather keep the ending than whatever premise threatens it, but I want to know exactly what the ending commits me to -- and whether (4) is maybe smuggling in something stronger than forgiveness really needs.
