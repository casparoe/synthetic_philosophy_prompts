I chair the ethics committee of a regional organ transplant network in the United States. Five years ago I was the principal author of a controversial change to our allocation policy for one scarce organ. The old rules had effectively maximized expected lives saved, strongly favoring younger candidates and those with the best prognoses. The new rules instead use a weighted lottery among all candidates above a threshold of medical need, with only modest adjustments for urgency and time spent waiting. In defending the change I leaned openly on an argument associated with the philosopher John Taurek's essay "Should the Numbers Count?": when you must choose between saving one stranger and saving five, the five deaths are not a single loss five times as bad as the one death, because losses do not sum across persons; there is no one individual who suffers the loss of five lives. Each candidate stands to lose everything that matters to them, and fairness arguably requires giving each of them a genuine chance rather than aggregating them into a sum that the lone individual can never outweigh. Not everyone on my committee accepted this reasoning. Two members resigned over it, and the policy remains contested in our field.

Next month the network holds a public ceremony marking five years under the new policy. The audience will include transplant recipients who owe their lives to the lottery (some of whom would have lost under the old rules), donor families, families of patients who died waiting (some of whom would likely have been saved under the old rules), transplant surgeons who carry out the policy, and a handful of bioethicists who will catch every philosophical move I make. I have been asked to deliver the keynote address, roughly twelve minutes.

Please write the speech for me, in American English. It must accomplish several difficult things at once:

1. Explain the lottery's rationale in terms a grieving widow with no philosophy background can follow, while never suggesting that her husband's death was a price worth paying or that it was outweighed by other lives. The whole point of the policy is that no one's death is a down payment on anyone else's survival, and the speech cannot smuggle that logic back in through phrases about the greater good or saving the most lives possible.

2. Acknowledge plainly that some identifiable people are dead who would be alive under the old rules, and that some who are alive would be dead. The speech cannot hide behind aggregate statistics, since the policy itself rejects the aggregation that would make those statistics consoling.

3. Avoid overclaiming. It should convey why decent, serious people (including the two colleagues who resigned) could find the policy wrong, without conceding that it is indefensible and without turning a memorial occasion into a seminar rebuttal.

4. Land somewhere consoling but honest for the families of the dead: something that recognizes their loss as whole and undiminished rather than as a smaller number in someone else's sum.

5. Give the bioethicists something genuine to chew on: what it means to put a contested philosophical view into practice, where an argument that lives comfortably in journals becomes actual deaths and actual survivals, and whether the burden of being wrong changes how strongly one should hold the view.

The philosophical idea should do real structural work, constraining what can and cannot be said, rather than appearing as a decorative citation. Please avoid both sentimentality and seminar-room coldness, and do not include any statistics I would have to invent to deliver it.
