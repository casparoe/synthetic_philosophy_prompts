I lead the model oversight group at a mid-sized software firm that deploys LLM agents in three production workflows: drafting first-pass responses to regulatory correspondence, making autonomous changes to internal infrastructure code, and producing long-horizon market research memos whose conclusions will only be confirmed or disconfirmed months after delivery. My team reviews roughly ten new or modified agent tasks per month and must assign each one a supervision mode: outcome-only grading, stepwise process supervision of the reasoning trace and intermediate actions, a hybrid of scheduled milestone audits, or no automation. Human reviewer hours are the binding constraint — a careful process audit of a single task costs about twenty times what an outcome grade costs — two of my five reviewers are non-technical, and the head of product has to sign off on any task we refuse to automate, so refusals need to be defensible.

Turn the distinction between process-based and outcome-based supervision into a decision procedure I can actually run in a one-hour task review meeting. I want an ordered sequence of tests, not an essay. The procedure should tell me what to check first and what each result rules in or out, and it should at minimum weigh: whether the outcome is verifiable at all and on what lag, including the risk that any cheap proxy outcome will be gamed; whether the agent's reasoning trace is both legible to my reviewers and faithful, meaning what I should do when I cannot tell whether the trace caused the answer or is a post-hoc narration that happens to read well; the reversibility and blast radius of the agent's actions; and the adversarial pressure that applying any grading scheme creates, since once the agent is optimized against a process grader it can learn to produce plausible-looking steps just as it can learn to game outcomes. Where a test cannot yield a number or even a reliable qualitative read, say what the default is and why. Mark explicitly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and include trigger conditions for re-reviewing a task's mode after the agent has been fine-tuned against our grading.

Then apply the procedure to my three workflows, showing the reasoning test by test. At least one application must reach a verdict I will not like, such as confining our flagship regulatory-correspondence product to outcome-only grading or refusing to automate the research memo workflow, and say what would have to change for that verdict to flip. Close by stating plainly what the procedure cannot do: which failure modes it is structurally blind to, what it assumes about trace access and reviewer competence, and what remains the responsibility of the human who signs off.
