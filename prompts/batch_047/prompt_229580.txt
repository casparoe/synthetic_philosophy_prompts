A friend and I spent all evening arguing about Dennett's taxonomy of minds -- Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures -- and we kept converging on the same verdicts while justifying them in completely different ways. Here are mine, and I'd like to know what single principle, if any, they add up to. A spider spinning a species-typical web: purely Darwinian, since the competence was tested by natural selection rather than by the spider. A rat learning a maze through rewarded trial and error: Skinnerian. A chimpanzee that pauses at a branch, watches it bend, and backs off: Popperian, because it tests candidate actions in some inner arena before risking them. A student who gets through an exam by means of mnemonics and written notes: Gregorian, since the thinking is offloaded onto inherited cultural tools. Now the trouble. First, habituation in a sea slug: my friend calls it learning and therefore Skinnerian; I say a response that merely diminishes with repetition isn't the testing of anything, so the slug stays Darwinian. Second, and harder to reconcile with the rest: a chess engine whose policy was shaped by something structurally like selection over millions of self-play games, but which at move time searches a tree of candidate futures. I want to call it Popperian because it evaluates consequences before acting -- yet everything it knows was installed by a Darwinian process, which by my spider verdict should settle the matter the other way. Is there one criterion -- presumably something about where the testing of hypotheses happens: in the lineage, in the lifetime, in the head, or in the culture -- that delivers all of these verdicts, or must one go? If one must go, say which and why. And tell me what your principle predicts for a case I haven't listed: the adaptive immune system, which within a single organism's lifetime selects among antibody variants that bind an invader -- Darwinian machinery doing a Popperian job, or something the taxonomy can't cleanly place at all?
