You are a philosophical coach with deep expertise in the metaphysics of dispositions and in academic job talks: you know the literature on finks, masks, and conditional analyses cold, and you have sat through enough Q&As to know which objections actually get asked, by whom, and how they land. Think carefully before writing. First, brainstorm a long list of possible objections, including weak ones; then filter for what this particular audience would raise; then draft the prep sheet.

Here is my situation. Next month I am giving a job talk defending a paper on dispositions. The thesis, in a nutshell: the standard taxonomy that treats finks (something that would remove the disposition if the stimulus occurred) and masks or antidotes (something that blocks the manifestation while the disposition remains) as two separate problems calling for two separate fixes is a mistake. I argue there is a single underlying phenomenon -- interference with a causal basis -- and that a unified analysis in terms of the disposition's causal basis, rather than in terms of counterfactuals about stimulus and manifestation, handles both cases with one mechanism. I also argue that the residual cases that look like "intrinsic finks," where the interfering factor is the object's own structure rather than an external device, show that any conditional-style analysis is beyond repair, not merely in need of qualification.

The audience is a department with no dispositions specialists. It is roughly half philosophers of science whose home framework is causal modeling and interventionism (they think in terms of graphs, interventions, and mechanisms, and are mildly suspicious of modal metaphysics), plus a handful of modal metaphysicians trained on possible-worlds semantics who know the fink literature only at the level of the classic examples, plus a couple of philosophers of language who will hear everything I say as a claim about the semantics of disposition ascriptions.

Prepare my Q&A sheet. Specifically:

1. Give me the eight to ten objections this audience will actually raise -- not the objections a room of dispositions specialists would raise. Order them by expected damage, which you should treat as a product of how likely each person in the room is to raise it and how bad it looks if I fumble it.
2. Phrase each objection the way that audience member would phrase it. The causal modelers should sound like causal modelers (they will want to know what my "causal basis" talk buys over an interventionist treatment, and whether I am just redescribing their framework in modal vocabulary); the modal metaphysicians should sound like modal metaphysicians; the philosophers of language should sound like philosophers of language.
3. For each objection, give me a reply deliverable in under two minutes, then a candid note on where the reply is soft -- the premise I am most likely to be pressed on if the questioner follows up.
4. Tell me what to concede up front, ideally in the talk itself, to disarm the two most damaging objections before they are asked.
5. Identify the one objection I am hoping nobody asks -- the one my unified account handles worst -- and give me the best available holding response plus advice on how to redirect without appearing to dodge.
6. Flag any place where my framing invites a misunderstanding specific to a non-specialist audience: terms of art from the fink and mask literature that this room will hear in their home-framework sense rather than the literature's sense.

Keep the whole sheet usable the night before the talk: compact enough to review in twenty minutes, with the replies written as things I could actually say out loud.
