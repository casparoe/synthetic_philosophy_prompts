I'm a PhD student in philosophy of biology, and I'm helping my advisor assemble the final exam for our graduate seminar on functions and teleology. Please draft one essay question on the dispute between etiological (selected-effects) and causal-role accounts of function, answerable in 90 minutes, along with a model answer and a marking scheme.

The question should be built around a case we've constructed to get students past the stock heart-and-kidney examples: a bioengineered microbial consortium in which a gene in strain A produces a protein that incidentally detoxifies the shared medium, which keeps strain B alive; A's own lineage never underwent selection for this effect, and the effect matters only at the level of the consortium. The question should force candidates to say, for each account, whether the protein has detoxification as its function; to identify where each account's verdict collides with intuition (liberalism and the function/accident conflation on one side, the first-token and novel-trait problems on the other); and to address whether either account can license saying that a broken copy of the protein is malfunctioning rather than merely absent.

The marking scheme should name five must-hit points with marks summing to 20, and for each point include a one-line note on the specific way candidates typically go wrong (e.g., treating group-level benefit as automatically sufficient for an etiological function, or assuming causal-role accounts simply have no resources for malfunction).

In the model answer, please be careful to separate what is genuinely settled in this literature (for instance, the standard objections each side concedes it must answer) from what remains contested among philosophers of biology (for instance, whether selection history is necessary for genuine function, or whether a dispositional or organizational variant of the causal-role view can recover normativity), and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
