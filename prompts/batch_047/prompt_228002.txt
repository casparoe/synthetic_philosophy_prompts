I am preparing a satirical feature for a general-interest ideas magazine and want the philosophy of science beneath the comedy to remain exact. Write a deadpan, approximately 750-word parody of an institutional white paper announcing the founding of the Institute for Deferred Vindication, an organization devoted to protecting long-run predictions—especially Malthusian forecasts of resource scarcity—from premature falsification.

The white paper should have sober headings, numbered principles, and the confident administrative tone of a policy document. Its rules might include a rolling prediction horizon that resets whenever a deadline passes, a taxonomy for classifying failures as delayed confirmations, and a procedure by which an originally predicted catastrophe can be declared fulfilled by a different catastrophe with a sufficiently related causal mechanism. The institute should also explain why every unsuccessful forecast remains right but early, while every ambiguously bad development counts as preliminary vindication.

Use Malthusian reasoning as the central example without turning the piece into a history lecture. The satire should exaggerate recognizable practices in long-range prediction: revising auxiliary assumptions after the fact, extending time horizons, switching from population collapse to subtler indicators of strain, treating failed deadlines as evidence of insufficient patience, and interpreting both abundance and scarcity as compatible with the underlying theory.

At the same time, keep the satire intellectually fair. A reader should be able to distinguish genuine reasons why a long-run theory can be vindicated later—lags, thresholds, changing technologies, and initially unstated scope conditions—from maneuvers that merely make a claim impossible to lose. Do not state that distinction in an editorial aside; let it emerge from the institute’s increasingly ingenious standards. By the end, the institute should have solved the problem of falsification so completely that its forecasts can no longer count as predictions in any useful sense.
