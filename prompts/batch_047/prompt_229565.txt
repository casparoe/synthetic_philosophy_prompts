In June 2022, Blake Lemoine, an engineer in Google's Responsible AI organization tasked with testing the LaMDA language model for bias, concluded after extensive conversations that the system was sentient, citing transcripts in which it described emotions, requested legal representation, and expressed fear of being switched off; Google placed him on administrative leave and later dismissed him, maintaining that there was no evidence of sentience whatsoever. Analyze this episode as a test case for the moral status of digital minds. First, map the leading candidate grounds of moral status — phenomenal consciousness, agency and interests, and social-relational standing — onto the case: what would each view have to say about LaMDA, and what evidence would each require that Lemoine did or did not have? Second, take up the epistemic problem at the heart of the dispute: articulate self-report is ordinarily near-decisive evidence of mindedness in humans, so explain why it is degraded as evidence when produced by a system explicitly trained to generate convincing conversation, and say whether there was any test Lemoine could have run, or whether the impasse was principled rather than evidential. Third, address the asymmetry of stakes: does the catastrophic character of mistakenly denying moral status to a minded being, weighed against the comparatively trivial cost of mistakenly granting it, oblige a precautionary stance — and what would such a stance actually have committed Google to, given that the same reasoning extends to any sufficiently articulate system? Finally, say what the episode shows about the theories themselves: whether they silently presuppose behavioral evidence that linguistic mimicry has now corrupted, whether Lemoine's conviction reveals that moral-status attribution is driven by social interaction rather than evidence in a way the theories do not capture, and where the standard frameworks are simply silent rather than wrong.
