I'm advising a small foundation that can fund roughly six person-years of conceptual work over the next three years, split between philosophy-of-mind generalists and morally serious people with physics training. Rather than asking whether panpsychism is true (which I doubt three years will settle), I want a research agenda conditional on it being a live possibility: given that we face massive moral uncertainty over where experience sits in nature, which open questions at this intersection are most worth attacking first?

Here are some candidate questions I've been kicking around, which you should treat as seeding rather than a fixed menu: whether there is any principled way to assign hedonic valence (as opposed to mere proto-phenomenality) to micro-level or field-level experience, and whether valence might even require cognitive structure that fundamental processes lack; whether the combination problem has a moral shadow — i.e., if micro-subjects don't combine, are we committed to astronomically many negligible moral patients, and does that swamp or trivialize ordinary ethics; whether any physical theory of consciousness (integrated information, global workspace, higher-order, or something more exotic) yields different cause-prioritization implications, so that funders face a portfolio problem across theories rather than a single bet; whether large-scale physical processes we currently ignore — stars, plasma dynamics, planetary magnetospheres, industrial processes like electrolysis or large-scale computing — plausibly constitute or destroy experience at rates that would matter under expected-value reasoning; and whether the whole agenda collapses into fanaticism worries (tiny probabilities, enormous stakes) in a way that can be handled with tools like moral uncertainty under normative disagreement, or whether it resists them.

Please adopt the role of a professional philosopher who works on both consciousness and population ethics, and who is sympathetic to but not credulous about longtermist cause-prioritization reasoning. Think carefully before answering; I'd suggest starting by brainstorming a wider candidate set of open questions than my seeding provides, then winnowing. The deliverable is a defended agenda, not a survey: rank the top three to five questions for this specific funding context, and justify the ordering in terms of explicit criteria — how upstream each question is of the others, tractability for philosophers versus physicists, neglectedness relative to existing work, and how much the answer would actually change resource allocation. For each item, say what evidence or results over the next three years would move it up or down your ranking, and flag any question I should actively avoid funding because its answer wouldn't change anything downstream.
