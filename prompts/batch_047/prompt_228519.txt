Two friends of mine, Dan and Mira, fell into an argument at dinner last week that has been replaying in my head since, and I genuinely cannot settle it, so I'm hoping for a careful referee. It started when Dan, who eats factory-farmed chicken without apparent guilt, offered what he called the only honest defense of his habit: his individual purchases change nothing. Supermarkets order in bulk; one customer's abstention will not, in expectation, prevent a single chicken from being raised, because supply responds to demand only in large threshold jumps his behavior will never trigger. If the wrongness of eating factory-farmed meat is supposed to lie in the harm it causes, he argued, then an act that causes no harm cannot be wrong on those grounds.

Mira answered that Dan was flattening the math. Purchases, she said, are like lottery tickets toward crossing an ordering threshold: no single one is the trigger, but each carries a small probability of being the one that prompts the next bulk order, and over a lifetime the expected number of additional chickens attributable to his consumption tracks his share of demand. Dan replied that this proves too much and too little at once. Too much, because the same infinitesimal expected-harm logic would condemn nearly every purchase anyone makes in a modern economy. Too little, because Mira insists voting matters, even though the probability of her vote deciding an election is far smaller than the probability her chicken purchase triggers an extra order -- so either she should abandon voting, or she needs some argument for abstaining from meat that doesn't run through expected marginal harm at all. Mira then shifted ground: the point, she said, was never only about causing harm but about not participating in and profiting from a practice one would condemn if asked to endorse it; a person who knowingly buys goods produced by grave wrongdoing is implicated in the wrong even where her purchase changed nothing. Dan called this a feel-good posture that explains nothing, since calling participation "wrong" just re-labels the harm-based objection the purchase fails. He then offered what he considered a checkmate: if the ethics is really about expected consequences, he can keep buying the chicken and donate a few dollars a month to an effective animal charity, producing far more expected good than abstention ever could. Mira shot back that this treats harms as fungible indulgences one can offset at a discount, and that some things one simply may not do regardless of what else one buys.

Please adjudicate this as a point-by-point referee: assess the threshold-and-expected-value reply, whether Dan's voting analogy actually forces Mira into a dilemma, whether the participation move escapes the inefficacy objection or merely relocates it, and whether the offsetting proposal deserves to win. Then say who is more right overall, and what -- if anything -- both of them are missing.
