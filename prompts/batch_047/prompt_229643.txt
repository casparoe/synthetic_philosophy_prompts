I am a novelist, and in six weeks I am to give an evening talk at a small workshop on Wittgenstein's later philosophy, hosted by a university whose philosophy department I know only by reputation. My invitation came on the strength of my most recent novel, whose narrator, a lighthouse keeper, records a recurrent and nameless sensation in his log by means of a mark of his own devising, a sign no one else is ever taught to read. The plot turns on his realization, years on, that he can no longer be sure he is still applying the mark to the same sensation he began with, and on the very different meanings this doubt takes on for him over the course of the book. In my talk I intend to argue that Wittgenstein's case against a private language does not reach what my novel depicts. The argument, as I read it, targets a philosopher's fantasy in which a sign is given its meaning in a single moment of private ostension, whereas my keeper's mark belongs to a sustained practice of one, carried forward through time, in which his later uses can be correct or incorrect relative to a standard that his earlier uses set, even though no second person could ever check them. My thesis, in short, is that the argument forbids instantaneous private definition but not solitary rule-following, and that the novel functions as a thought experiment showing where the line falls.

Let me describe the room. There will be a dozen or so specialists. Two of them read Wittgenstein in the resolute manner associated with Diamond and Conant, and I expect them to bristle at the very idea that the argument has a scope inside which my keeper safely sits, since on their reading the point is not a restriction on a certain kind of language but the dissolution of a confused picture. One participant works in the communitarian tradition descending from Kripke's reading and will want to press me on whether a standard that only one person can apply is a standard at all. A philosopher of mind sympathetic to recent work on phenomenal concepts is, I believe, quietly on my side, though she will not rescue me in public. The rest are graduate students who will ask either very literal or very grand questions. What this audience cares about is fidelity to the text and suspicion of anything that smells like converting Wittgenstein into a thesis one might either accept or reject. What they will not care about is how the novel made readers feel.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections in roughly the order I am likely to receive them, phrased the way these particular people would phrase them, in their vocabulary, not in mine. For each one, tell me how much damage it does if I answer badly, what I should concede without being pressed, what I should hold, and a reply I can deliver in about two minutes at the podium. Flag where each reply of mine is soft, and identify the weakest point in my overall position, the place I should plan to defend rather than hope they overlook. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and tell me honestly whether, if it comes, I have anything better than an evasion. One further request about form: I know that a preparation sheet ordinarily wants headings and tables, but please give me the whole thing as continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables, paragraphs flowing one into the next as in a long letter, since that is how I will actually read it aloud to myself the night before.
