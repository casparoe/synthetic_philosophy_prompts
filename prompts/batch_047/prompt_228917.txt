I am a retired biochemist who spent forty years measuring things for a living — enzyme kinetics, mostly — and who has spent the last two of them reading political philosophy. I have been offered the chance to spend a semester as a visiting scholar at a nearby university, and they want a one-page research proposal. I would like you to draft it for me.

Here is my half-formed idea. In the "equality of what?" debate, the competing answers — welfare, resources, capabilities, and their variants — are usually compared on intuitive grounds: which currency matches our judgments about cases, which one respects the difference between choice and circumstance, and so on. What I have not seen foregrounded is that each currency makes different demands on measurement. Welfare comparisons, as I understand it, typically need cardinal and interpersonally comparable information; resource-based approaches get by with less but pay for it elsewhere; capability approaches sometimes look as if they can remain purely ordinal, until you ask how to aggregate across a person's many functionings, at which point weights have to come from somewhere. My conjecture is that the choice between currencies is not a free one to be settled by intuition: each currency carries hidden measurement-theoretic commitments, some of which may be impossible to justify in practice, and an honest "measurement audit" of the leading candidates might rule some out, or at least show that the real disagreement is about which unjustified assumption to swallow.

Turn this into a proper proposal. State the question precisely enough that it could have a determinate answer, explain why the existing debate as usually framed leaves it open, and lay out the method concretely — what I would actually do month by month over one semester. Be candid about scope: I am one person with a scientist's training, not a philosopher's, so say plainly which parts of the project risk being too big and should be cut. Also say what would count as the project failing — for instance, if it turns out the measurement demands of every currency collapse into the same one, or if the audit changes nothing about how anyone should choose. Do not include citations or a reading list; I will track down the sources myself, and I would rather the proposal describe positions and arguments than name works.

One further request. Where the proposal makes substantive claims — about what a given currency presupposes, or about what is and is not established in the debate — indicate how confident you are in each, and at the end single out the single claim you are least sure of and why.
