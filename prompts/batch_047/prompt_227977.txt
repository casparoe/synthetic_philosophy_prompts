I'm a PhD candidate in political theory drafting the prospectus for a dissertation chapter defending a fairly specific institutional proposal, and I want to stress-test my own position before my committee does. The view: for second chambers in large democracies, sortition is not merely a check or an epistemic supplement to elected bodies but should carry co-equal legislative power, with members drawn by stratified random sampling from the full citizenry, serving staggered three-year terms, compensated at roughly an MP's salary, voting secretly, and supported by permanent nonpartisan staff. My core argument is that elections systematically select for a cluster of traits (ambition, fundraising skill, party loyalty, tolerance for self-presentation) that are at best orthogonal to good legislative judgment, and that the standard objections from competence and accountability rest on an inflated picture of what electoral accountability actually delivers in low-information, high-abstraction settings. My advisor is broadly sympathetic but keeps warning me that I've built a position that can absorb any objection, which I want to take seriously rather than dismiss.

So: what would count against this position? I want a genuine map of its exposure, not a literature survey. Which empirical findings about existing sortition bodies (citizens' assemblies, deliberative polls, jury behavior under load) would, if they held up, actually sink the co-equal-power version of the view rather than just complicate the details? Which objections are structural -- for instance, that agenda-setting and drafting power migrate to whoever staffs and briefs the chamber, that non-response and self-exemption quietly reintroduce exactly the selection effects the lottery was meant to remove, or that secret voting severs the accountability link so completely that the chamber is accountable to no one at all, not even poorly -- and which of these could in principle be answered by institutional design versus being load-bearing problems? I'm also interested in what the view predicts that electoral and hybrid alternatives don't, so I know what observations would discriminate between us, and in the collapse risk: are there plausible conditions under which my proposal becomes, in effect, rule by the facilitation and briefing class with a lottery as legitimation theatre, and how would I know if I were there?

Finally, tell me what a committed sortition defender would themselves concede as counting against the view, as opposed to what critics say -- I'm trying to locate where the honest fault lines run inside the position. Please rank the defeaters from potentially fatal to merely awkward, flag any qualification I've already made (or am tempted to make) that insulates the view at the cost of making it risk nothing, and say plainly if you think the position as stated is well insulated and whether you take that to be a vice in this kind of normative institutional argument or an acceptable feature of it.
