I teach a final-year module on the philosophy of prediction in the social sciences, and this term I am staging an adversarial collaboration on the long afterlife of Malthus. The two parties represent real positions, though I shall present them anonymously: a defender of the view that Malthus's principle of population was essentially right but early — a true tendency claim whose enabling conditions were temporarily suspended by industrialisation and which reasserts itself wherever those conditions lapse — and a critic who holds that the theory generated definite expectations that failed, and that every subsequent rescue (longer horizons, ceteris paribus clauses, the language of 'postponement') is a textbook case of immunising a refuted theory against evidence.

Draft the joint statement such a collaboration ought to produce. Specifically:

1. A numbered list of claims both parties can sign. I expect these to include the actual content of the principle (population's tendency to grow geometrically against subsistence's arithmetic growth, checked by misery or by restraint), the broad historical facts neither side disputes (the Malthusian character of pre-industrial economies, in which gains in output were absorbed by population growth; the sustained divergence of income per head from population growth after roughly 1800; the demographic transition), and the methodological commitments both accept (that a respectable theory must, at least eventually, issue in claims upon which evidence can bear).

2. A table of residual disagreements, each formulated in wording its own side would accept. Candidate rows: whether the first-edition claims were ever falsifiable in principle; whether 'right but early' differs from 'unfalsifiable'; whether the demographic transition refutes the premise of the constancy of the passions or merely complicates it; whether neo-Malthusian revivals, such as the population-bomb literature of the 1960s and 1970s, inherit the same methodological vice or have been partly vindicated.

3. For each disagreement, the crux: the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one party. I suspect one crux is the line between a legitimate tendency law (comparable to ceteris paribus claims elsewhere in economics or in physics) and a claim insulated from counter-evidence; another is whether a theory confirmed by its own epoch but disconfirmed thereafter has thereby been falsified or merely bounded in scope.

4. For each crux, the case, episode, or observation both parties accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance — for example, what each expects of societies in which population growth currently outpaces institutional and technological adaptation, or what each would conclude if income per head were to stagnate for a century under continued population growth.

Where a disagreement dissolves on inspection into a dispute about words — I suspect part of the quarrel over 'falsification' does, since the two sides may be applying Popperian and Lakatosian standards at cross purposes — say so, identify which part, and restate what remains once the verbal element is removed. Conclude with a short note on whether the collaboration, in your drafting, has genuinely narrowed anything, or merely restated the impasse more politely.
