I organize a monthly discussion evening at our local library -- usually ten to fourteen people, a mix of retirees, students, and working folks, none of us with any formal training in philosophy -- and we get ninety minutes per session. A few months back we drifted into a pattern I haven't been able to shake: whenever someone defends a strange conclusion by multiplying a very small probability by a very large payoff, the rest of the room goes quiet and grudgingly concedes, even when the conclusion seems plainly absurd. It came up with lottery tickets, then with whether it's worth funding asteroid detection over the food bank, then with an argument one member brought in that tiny odds of affecting an enormous future could make one charitable donation worth more than a million others. Each time the arithmetic felt like a kind of bullying: nobody could say where the reasoning broke, yet nobody wanted to live by it either. Someone mentioned that Pascal's wager has this same shape. I'd like to build next month's session around this, but as something the group experiences rather than something I lecture about -- I want them to feel the pull of that multiplication from the inside and to locate, for themselves, the moment where following it starts to feel like being mugged.

Design me a game or structured activity that does this. My rough thought is a series of rounds in which each participant (or small teams, if that works better for fourteen people) repeatedly chooses between a modest guaranteed payoff and a long-shot payoff, all in points rather than money, with the probabilities shrinking and the prizes swelling from round to round so that the multiply-it-out logic keeps recommending the long shot even as choosing it starts to feel ridiculous -- and so that the dutiful odds-followers visibly end up somewhere the more "irrational" players don't. But please improve on that sketch or replace it if there's a better design. What I need is fully runnable: concrete rules, a materials list, a payoff scheme that costs nothing real but still stings a little (I'm open to points converting into something trivial like control of next month's topic or refreshments), and -- this seems crucial -- some physical randomizing mechanism like dice, cards, or drawn lots so that the tiny probabilities are genuinely instantiated in the room rather than just asserted on a slide, because I suspect the felt reality of the odds is where the trouble lives. The participants should not hear the phrase "expected value" or any name for the problem until the debrief; they should construct the difficulty themselves first. Please also include: a debrief plan that maps particular rounds and particular moments (the first defection from the arithmetic, the groans, the bargaining) onto the structure of the problem and onto the live options for responding -- cap the payoffs, discount tiny probabilities, refuse to treat the numbers as precise, or bite the bullet -- with what each option costs; a note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm especially worried about the one member who already knows this territory and may spoil the reveal, and about participants treating it as an ordinary gambling night); and a frank statement of which feature of the real philosophical problem the game cannot reproduce -- for instance, the fact that the serious versions involve genuine uncertainty about the odds rather than honest dice, and stakes that are real.

Finally, I'd like you to be explicit about your own confidence in the design. Tell me which of your choices rest on assumptions about how people actually behave under these incentives that might turn out to be wrong, what I should watch for during the session as evidence that the activity is teaching the wrong lesson (or no lesson), and how you would revise the payoff structure or the debrief if that evidence shows up. If there is a plausible version of this problem on which the odds-following players are simply correct and the game unfairly rigs things against them, say so and tell me what a version of the activity sympathetic to that side would look like instead.
