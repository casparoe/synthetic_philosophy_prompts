I spent my working life as an experimental scientist and have only lately turned to philosophy, so I beg your patience if I put things clumsily. I have come to accept the so-called level-splitting view in epistemology: the claim that first-order and higher-order justification can come apart, such that a rational agent may believe that p while also believing that her total evidence does not support p — in other words, that epistemic akrasia is sometimes the rational state rather than a failure. What I want from you is a map of this view's exposure: what would count against it? Please go beyond the familiar objection that it sounds paradoxical on its face. Which concrete cases does the view mishandle — for instance, situations where one knows one's evidence was corrupted, or cases of peer disagreement where one cannot point to any asymmetry in competence? Are there formal principles it must reject, say coherence constraints of a reflection type or demands that one defer to one's idealized future judgment, and how costly is that rejection for someone who wants probability theory to govern credences? Which potential defeaters would a committed level-splitter herself concede as genuine, and which would she dismiss as question-begging or merely awkward? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to cosmetic, and a candid closing assessment of whether the view has been so qualified that it risks nothing at all — and if so, whether that is a defect in a position of this kind.
