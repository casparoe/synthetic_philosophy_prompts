I'm in my final year of high school and writing an extended essay for my civics class about the unemployment benefit system in my country (we have something broadly like the UK's, with a jobcentre system), and I've run into a puzzle about what the whole setup takes for granted philosophically. Here's the practice as I understand it: to keep receiving benefits, an unemployed person has to prove they're actively looking for work -- logging a set number of job applications each week, attending scheduled meetings with an advisor, and accepting any job deemed suitable, even one that pays barely more than the benefit or requires a long commute. If they miss an appointment or turn down a placement, their payments get cut or suspended for weeks. Some claimants are also required to do unpaid work placements to keep their payments. The government justifies all of this in terms of "fairness to taxpayers" and "making sure work always pays." But here's what struck me: the same state pays pensions to retired people with zero behavioral conditions attached, hands out agricultural subsidies and corporate bailouts without requiring the recipients to prove they deserve them, and doesn't means-test or condition child benefit on whether parents are doing anything socially useful in return. So conditionality seems to be reserved almost exclusively for working-age unemployed people.

My question is: what does this practice presuppose, philosophically? My teacher says every institution has an implicit theory baked into it, and I want to reconstruct the one baked into this. Does the sanction system only make sense if you assume unemployment is usually a choice -- that without the threat of losing money, claimants would simply prefer not to work? That seems to presuppose a contested empirical claim dressed up as policy. Or does it rest on a reciprocity principle, where benefits are a kind of contribution you must earn by demonstrating effort -- in which case, why are pensioners and subsidized farmers exempt from the same logic? Or is it secretly a view about desert, where the "deserving" and "undeserving" poor can be sorted by their observable behavior, and the job-search requirements are really a sorting mechanism rather than a job-finding mechanism? I'd also like to know whether the practice is underdetermined here -- could someone who rejected all of those views still defend conditionality on, say, purely consequentialist grounds (it reduces caseloads, whatever it assumes about the claimants)? And if so, is the rhetoric of "fairness to taxpayers" just decoration on a policy that doesn't philosophically need it?

Finally, I'd like the essay to end with something constructive, so: if we replaced the implicit theory with a different one -- say, the idea that welfare is an unconditional right of citizenship, the way defenders of a basic income frame it -- what specifically in the machinery would have to change? Presumably sanctions go, but would the job-search logging have to go too, or could a basic-income state still ask people to look for work without cutting them off? Conversely, if we took the desert-based reading seriously and made it consistent, would that commit us to imposing conditions on pensioners and corporations as well, or is there a principled line that separates them from the unemployed? Please distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely sits comfortably with, since I suspect a lot of the debate is people attributing to the system a theory it doesn't actually require.
