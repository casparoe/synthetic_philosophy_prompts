I'm a novelist, and at a writers retreat last month two friends got into an arguement I keep chewing on. It started over twist endings. Dana guesses the murderer by page 50 of any thriller — pure gut, no reasons she can articulate — and she's right almost every time. Marcus reads slowly and only commits once the clues genuinely add up. Dana's claim: if both of them end up believing the truth about who did it, her conclusion is worth exactly as much as his, and calling his "knowledge" and hers a "lucky guess" is just snobbery about method, since the endpoint is identical. Marcus said careful reading gets you something extra, but when Dana pressed him on what, all he could offer was "I could defend my answer if challenged" — and Dana pointed out she defends hers fine, she just says "I'm always right," and she is. Then Marcus muddied his own position: he said the real difference shows when the two methods conflict, but admitted that whenever his clue-reading clashes with Dana's gut, he defers to her gut, because her track record beats his.

Later I stumbled on this in Plato's Meno (Lamb's translation), Socrates talking about a slave boy who's produced a correct answer to a geometry problem without being taught: "And at this moment those opinions have just been stirred up in him, like a dream; but if he were repeatedly asked these same questions in a variety of forms, you know he will have in the end as exact an understanding of them as anyone."

So: adjudicate their exchange move by move, not just who wins overall. Does this passage vindicate Marcus — is surviving repeated questioning the thing Dana's hunches lack? Or does his habit of deferring to her gut concede that unexamined true belief can be just as valuable as knowledge? Keep it under 300 words, the quote doesn't count.
