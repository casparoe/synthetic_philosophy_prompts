I am a novelist at work on a book whose protagonist, Clara, spends much of the story unsure whether her late father favored her sister. In drafting her, I noticed something that has grown into an argument, and the argument now unsettles me, because its conclusion would make my own profession a kind of chronicler of nothing. I would like to know where it goes wrong.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) A novel can be complete as a work of fiction while leaving countless questions about its characters forever unsettled. I have decided nothing about whether Clara believes her father's watch kept good time, and no reader, however attentive, could fault the book for that omission.

(2) Where the text is silent and the author has decided nothing, there is simply no fact of the matter about what a character believes. Clara neither believes nor fails to believe the thing about the watch. Fictional minds are radically indeterminate, and the fiction is none the worse for it.

(3) Our ordinary practice of ascribing beliefs to real people is of a piece with our practice of ascribing them to characters. In both cases we work from sparse behavior and context, attribute whatever makes the subject intelligible to us, and tolerate persistent disagreement among competent interpreters — biographers, juries, spouses — without insisting that some deeper fact must adjudicate.

(4) A practice that functions perfectly well amid such indeterminacy cannot be tracking determinate inner states. If beliefs were real explanatory posits, their presence or absence would force convergence among interpreters or show up in predictive gaps, the way the presence of a toxin or a debt eventually does.

(5) Nor does belief-talk add predictive power. We forecast what a person will do from her circumstances, incentives, and past behavior; the vocabulary of belief and desire is applied afterward, as a convenient narration of a forecast already made.

(6) Therefore beliefs and desires, construed as determinate states inside people, do not exist — not in fiction, and not in life. Folk psychology is a storytelling idiom we happen to speak about ourselves, which is precisely why novelists are so fluent in it.

I find I cannot accept (6). If the argument is sound, then my craft, which purports to render the inner lives of persons, renders something that real persons never had either, and the difference between a penetrating psychological novel and a horoscope collapses. Yet I am much more reluctant to surrender (1) and (2) than anything else — the indeterminacy of fictional minds seems to me the one thing a novelist knows from the inside.

So: which step should I reject, and why did it look so innocent? In answering, I would ask you to price the alternatives. What does it cost to accept the conclusion outright, as some philosophers frankly do? What does it cost to deny (3) by insisting that real minds differ from fictional ones in a way my analogy ignores? And what does it cost to deny (4), given that its principle — that reality must discipline our talk of it — has a distinguished pedigree?
