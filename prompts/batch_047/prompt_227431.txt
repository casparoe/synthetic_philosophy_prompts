Construct a worked example of Simpson's paradox — for a lecture arguing that "when the trend reverses, trust the subgroups" is not a universal rule — in which the reversal is genuine but the correct practical conclusion comes from the aggregated table. Constraints: the setting should be mundane and credible (a clinic, a firm, a school; no casinos or contrived coin flips); the numbers should be concrete and small enough to verify by hand; and the partitioning variable must not be a pre-treatment confounder — it should be a variable the treatment itself affects, or one where conditioning would otherwise bias the comparison. Begin with the scenario and its numbers, with no preamble. After the scenario, state in one sentence which table the decision-maker should trust, show the reversal arithmetically, explain the causal structure that makes conditioning the wrong move here, describe the minimal change to the story that would flip the verdict back to the disaggregated table, and say which criterion from causal reasoning settles the question in each version.
