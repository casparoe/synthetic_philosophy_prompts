Ok so i teach an upper-level undergrad seminar on the philosophy of death and im putting together the take-home final. The badness-of-death unit is the usual Epicurus/Nagel/Lucretius cluster, and the Death in Damascus fable keeps coming up in the readings — you know the one: a servant bumps into Death in the market at home, she makes what looks like a threatening gesture, he borrows a horse and flees to a faraway city, and it turns out she was only startled because her appointment with him was *there*, that same night.

Problem: every year i get the same essay back. Epicurus says no subject no harm, Nagel says deprivation, bit of Lucretius symmetry, done. I want a question they cant answer with that template.

Here's the device i want it built around. Give the students a variant of the fable: in this version Death had no appointment with the servant at all, anywhere — the gesture in the market genuinely was nothing, mistaken identity — but his panicked overnight ride is what kills him, thrown from the horse an hour outside the city. So in the original, fleeing death is futile; in the variant, fleeing death is fatal. The essay question should then make them use the two versions to pull apart things the fable usually gets flattened into one: the fatalist moral, the timing problem (when is death a misfortune for the person who dies?), the subject problem, and the Epicurean line that the real harm is the fear of death rather than death itself. My hunch is the variant is awkward for both camps in an interesting way: it looks like a gift to Epicurus (its the fear that kills him, not death), but the deprivationist can reply that the death is exactly as bad as in the original and only the cause has changed — while the Epicurean now owes an account of why this death is "nothing to him" given everything he stood to lose. Also theres something worth probing about how the variant seems to locate the misfortune in time (the ride, while hes still alive) which should please people worried about the timing problem, but only at the cost of relocating the harm from the death to the choice — which is basically the Epicurean's point again. Write the question so it forces that comparison instead of just gesturing at it.

Deliverables: (1) the question exactly as itd appear on the exam paper — spell out both versions of the fable inside the question itself so its self-contained, and note the constraints (they have a week, ~1500 words, marked out of 40); (2) a model answer of about 800 words, pitched at what your best student could realistically produce in a week; (3) a marking scheme that breaks down the 40 marks and, crucially, states which concrete moves distinguish an A (36+) from a B (30–35) — e.g. i suspect A answers will notice the variant relocates the timing pressure rather than dissolving it, while B answers treat each fable fine but never connect them — and which errors should hard-cap the mark, like reading the fable as an argument for fatalism full stop, or running Epicurus's two arguments (no-subject and symmetry) together as if they were one.
