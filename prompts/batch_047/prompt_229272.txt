I got into a stupidly heated argument at Thanksgiving about whether my cousin is obligated to vote. She says her one vote is astronomically unlikely to swing anything so she might as well stay home, and my uncle shut her down with "what if everyone did that?" She shot back that everyone *isn't* doing that and won't, so who cares, and then everyone just talked past each other for twenty minutes. I keep running into this move — people deploy it about littering, free-riding on public transit, eating meat, whatever — and I've also noticed it gets treated as if it's just Kant for the masses, but then I took a philosophy course a few years back and remember Kant's actual universalizability tests being much weirder and more specific than "imagine everyone doing it and see if things go badly."

So: can you lay out the distinct precise principles that "what if everyone did that?" could be standing for? I can gesture at some candidates — something like a genuinely Kantian test where the problem is a contradiction in willing or conceiving the universalized maxim, a more consequentialist generalization argument where the problem is just that bad results would follow if everyone did it, a fairness principle about not making an exception of yourself when you're counting on others to do their part, and maybe a causal claim that your behavior actually influences what others do — but I'm sure there are more and I'm sure I've mangled the Kant one. For each reading, say what kind of "what if" it's asking (logical, counterfactual, predictive?), whether it's a platitude, a substantive-but-defensible claim, or just false, and whether it actually delivers the conclusion my uncle wanted — i.e., that *this one person*, holding everyone else's actual behavior fixed, has a reason to vote. Please pay special attention to the objection my cousin would raise if she were cleverer: that for lots of these cases "everyone doing it" is either harmless, impossible, or so far-fetched that the thought experiment seems rigged, and that you can redescribe the maxim ("everyone whose vote has a less than one-in-a-billion chance of mattering stays home") to make any universalization come out fine.

End by telling me which reading you think people who reach for the slogan in arguments like ours actually mean — maybe different people mean different ones — and whether the slogan is doing any real work or is just a rhetorical shortcut for something weaker.
