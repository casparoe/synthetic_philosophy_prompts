I'm a third-year mathematics major currently taking a history and philosophy of mathematics seminar, and for my term paper I've been assigned Hobbes's philosophy of geometry, centered on De Corpore (1655). My background is in real analysis and a bit of differential geometry and computable analysis, so the seventeenth-century idiom is hard for me to evaluate, and I want to know how much of it is merely archaic phrasing and how much is a genuinely different conception of the continuum.

Here is the position as I understand it, in paraphrase: Hobbes holds that the objects of geometry must be defined through the motions that generate them, so that a line is to be understood as the trace of a moved body or point, a surface as the trace of a moved line, and so on, and he treats this generative definability as a condition on genuine geometrical knowledge, since in his view we can only demonstrate properties of things whose manner of production we ourselves stipulate. Correlatively, he rejects the indivisibles and infinitesimals then in use, denies that a line is composed of points or that anything is actually infinitely small or infinitely divisible in the way Wallis's arithmetic requires, and on this basis disputes the legitimacy of the new infinitesimal methods; his own failed attempts at the quadrature of the circle flow from the same commitments. What I want is a disciplined translation exercise, not a summary: restate this cluster of doctrines in the vocabulary I actually work in -- parametric curves as images of maps from intervals, rectifiability and arc length, limits and completeness of the reals, Lebesgue measure and non-measurable sets, smooth versus merely continuous structures, and, where useful, constructive or computable analysis with its refusal of completed infinities -- as precisely as you can, flagging each move where you choose one modern rendering over another plausible rival.

The part I care most about is the residue report. Which of Hobbes's claims map cleanly onto something modern -- for instance, whether his kinematic generation is just parametrization, or something stronger, like a demand that objects come with a construction -- and which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in contemporary mathematics at all? I suspect his insistence that definitions be generative in order for demonstration to count as knowledge does something that no purely structural definition of curve can capture, but I also suspect his rejection of infinitesimals is undermined rather than vindicated by later developments, and I'm unsure whether nonstandard analysis rehabilitates Wallis against Hobbes or is beside the point. For each piece of residue, tell me whether the failure of translation indicts my modern framework (i.e., it lacks expressive resources for a coherent distinction Hobbes was drawing) or indicts the original doctrine (i.e., what seemed like a substantive thesis dissolves once you try to state it precisely). End with a verdict on whether Hobbes had a philosophically serious rival conception of the continuum that we have simply forgotten, or a confused one that the modern framework correctly discards.
