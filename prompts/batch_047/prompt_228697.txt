I TA for a history of modern philosophy course, and a friend who grades the same section and I got into a long argument last night about whether the standard reading of Leibniz's anti-voluntarism over-Platonizes him. We settled nothing, and now I have to grade. Below is our exam question and one student's answer. The question quotes from §II of Leibniz's Discourse on Metaphysics:

"In saying, therefore, that things are not good according to any standard of goodness, but simply by the will of God, it seems to me that one destroys, without realizing it, all the love of God and all his glory; for why praise him for what he has done, if he would be equally praiseworthy in doing the contrary? Where will be his justice and his wisdom if he has only a certain despotic power, if arbitrary will takes the place of reasonableness, and if in accord with the definition of tyrants, justice consists in that which is pleasing to the most powerful?"

Question: Identify the position Leibniz is attacking here and reconstruct his argument against it. Does this passage show that, for Leibniz, the standard of goodness is independent of God? Defend your answer.

Student's answer: Leibniz is attacking theological voluntarism, the view that things are good simply because God wills them. His argument is essentially the Euthyphro dilemma: if goodness consists only in God's will, then praising God for his works becomes empty, since he would deserve equal praise had he willed the opposite, and divine justice collapses into the justice of tyrants, where right is whatever pleases the most powerful. So Leibniz concludes that goodness is a standard external to God, one that God recognizes and consults when creating, much as a human agent consults a moral rule. Because God cannot make something good by willing it, Leibniz concludes that God had no genuine choice in creating: his wisdom necessitated this world, and talk of divine freedom is therefore misleading. Voluntarism, on Leibniz's view, also threatens mathematics, since the eternal truths of geometry would likewise be mere effects of God's will.
