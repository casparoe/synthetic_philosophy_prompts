I run a small dispute-resolution practice, mostly for academic teams -- co-authors who've fallen out, labs splitting over what their own results show, that sort of thing. I keep noticing the same pattern: two people going hammer and tongs at each other, and twenty minutes in you realise they don't actually disagree about anything factual, they're just using one key word differently. The philosopher Eli Hirsch and others have written about merely verbal disputes, and I've read enough to suspect half my caseload is this, but I've never seen anyone turn it into something a mediator can actually do in the room, in real time, with two angry people.

So: write me that procedure. I want something I can run during a session, in my head or on a notepad, in about ten minutes, that sorts a dispute into roughly three buckets -- purely verbal (dissolves once the contested word is banned and each side restates their claim without it), genuinely substantive (survives the ban), or the awkward third case where the fight isn't about facts at all but about which meaning of the word ought to be the standard one going forward, which I gather is a real category and not the same as either of the other two. For each bucket, tell me what the procedure instructs me to do next as the mediator, because I suspect the right move differs a lot -- dissolving a verbal dispute is a different job from refereeing a fight over how a term should be defined.

The procedure has to handle some hard cases I actually see. First, disputes where both sides accept the same definition but disagree about whether a borderline case falls under it (is that verbal, factual, or neither?). Second, disputes where the word is load-bearing in some document with consequences -- a grant condition, a job title, an authorship criterion -- so even if the conceptual disagreement is verbal, the fight isn't really about the concept at all, and telling them it's merely verbal just makes them angrier. Third, cases where one party's whole identity or prior work is invested in their usage, so the restatement step itself gets resisted. Be honest about where the procedure breaks down and where I just have to use judgement -- I'd rather you flag those points explicitly than pretend the checklist covers everything.

Work it through on at least two realistic examples, with the actual reasoning shown: one where a heated dispute about whether an algorithm discriminated turns out to be one side meaning disparate outcomes and the other meaning intent (and what I do once that's diagnosed), and one where the procedure gives an answer I won't like -- e.g. a dispute that feels deep and important to both parties gets classified as merely verbal and the procedure tells me to dissolve it, even though the parties clearly aren't done fighting, because the real fight is over something else like resources or status.

A few constraints on the reply itself: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no numbered steps set off on their own lines -- just continuous prose, the way you'd write a long email to a colleague. If the procedure has ordered steps, walk me through them in prose ('first you do this... then, if that's failed, you do that...'). And keep it usable by someone smart who hasn't read the philosophy literature -- translate the theory, don't just cite it at me.
