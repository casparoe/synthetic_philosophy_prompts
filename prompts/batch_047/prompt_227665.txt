Okay so I'm a novelist, my second book just sold into French and German, and I've been fighting (nicely) with both translators for months, and it's pushed me into this rabbit hole I can't get out of. The fight is the oldest one in translation: do you carry over the meaning or the form. My French translator wants to keep a chapter title's pun and is willing to mangle what the chapter is actually about to do it; my German translator says that's vanity and the sentence-level sense is the whole job. I've been reading around and everyone has been having this exact argument for basically forever -- literalists vs. free translators, Nabokov-style martyrs to the word vs. people who'd happily rewrite, and then the Hofstadter camp insisting that with something like that little Marot poem, the one with the tiny three-syllable lines and the singsong rhymes, the form IS the content and translating just the 'message' gets you a grocery list, not a poem. Smart people, same poem, same evidence, zero movement. What I want is not another verdict on who's right, god knows there are enough of those. I want a diagnosis of why it's stuck. Is it just a verbal dispute where both sides mean something different by 'faithful' and could shake hands if they noticed? Are they answering different questions -- one about what a translation owes the reader, one about what it owes the author? Or is there a real disagreement buried in there, about what a text even is, that no amount of examples will settle? Use what the sides actually say, tell me which diagnosis you buy, and given that, what would count as progress -- a distinction, a better test case, a changed question -- or whether I should just accept that this one never ends and flip a coin on the chapter title.
