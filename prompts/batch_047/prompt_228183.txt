So I had this huge argument with a mate last Saturday that went on until about 2am. It started with her saying she'd recently gone off coffee she'd loved for years, and I (smugly, regrettably) brought up Dennett's Chase and Sanborn — the two coffee tasters in his "Quining Qualia" essay, one of whom says the coffee tastes exactly the same as ever but his preferences have changed, while the other insists his preferences are unchanged and it's the taste itself that's shifted. My friend was adamant she'd just *know* which one she was, from the inside, no further question. I said that's precisely the intuition Dennett thinks won't survive scrutiny. We went round in circles and I'm not sure either of us argued it brilliantly, tbh.

Annoyingly, the timing is terrible, because I've now got to run our department's reading group for second-year philosophy students next week, and the topic is basically this: whether qualia are an incoherent posit. The students have done a first-year mind module, so they've met Mary and the inverted spectrum and mostly come away qualia-friendly. I don't need the argument explained to me — I need a plan for how to teach it in ninety minutes. What has to be in place first before the Chase and Sanborn case can do any work (I'm guessing they need the naive picture of qualia as the way things seem to you, cleanly separable from your judgements and reactions to those seemings, but maybe something needs to come even before that?). Then: which example should carry the session. Should I lead with both tasters or just one of them, or is there a better everyday case to build the whole thing around — and what does the carrying case need to have and to lack so students don't dodge it? Also the misconceptions I should expect and where in the sequence to head each one off: my guess is they'll first hear Dennett as denying that experience exists at all, then dismiss him as a reheated verificationist, then decide the whole thing just trades on fallible memory (which I think misses Sanborn, whose memory is meant to be fine). Tell me at which point in the plan each of those gets defused rather than letting them fester.

Finally, I want one question to ask at the end that shows whether the idea has actually landed — something like getting them to construct a case where "my qualia changed" and "my reaction to my qualia changed" come apart, where success is them realising they can't, but maybe you have a better suggestion. And tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level (the later replies, the inverted spectrum, heterophenomenology?) and say so, rather than letting the session sprawl. Basically: help me teach this better than I argued it at 2am.
