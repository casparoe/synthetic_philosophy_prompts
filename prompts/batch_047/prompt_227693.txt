Quick background: Sana has severe treatment-resistant OCD and qualifies for a deep brain stimulation trial. The procedure works well on the symptoms for most patients, but the follow-up reports are full of people saying it changed other stuff too -- different taste in music, more impulsive, less religious, shifted priorities -- and in a few cases family members said they got back a recognisably different person. Sana's current values are basically stability, her faith, and her careful routines. So she has to decide *now* whether to want to become someone who may not share those values.

Dev argued she literally cannot make this decision rationally. Rational choice, he said, means weighing outcomes by your values, but here both the evidence and the values are missing: she can't know what post-op life is like from the inside (testimony doesn't transmit that, only living it does), and the person doing the weighing after the operation will be using different weights than the person deciding today. So there's no standpoint from which "it's worth it" is even evaluable, and the rational default is to decline -- or at most to count only the values that would survive the change either way, like not being tormented by the OCD itself.

June said that argument proves way too much. By that standard nobody could rationally choose to have a kid, join a monastery, emigrate, or even pick a demanding career, since all of those change what you care about and none can be fully imagined in advance -- yet people make these calls constantly, partly on testimony and partly by treating the choice as deciding *who to become* rather than which outcome to pick. Dev answered that it's a difference of kind, not degree: ordinary big decisions are gradual and partly reversible, and imagination plus testimony gets you most of the way, whereas Sana's change is sudden, partly irreversable, and the testimony problem is total -- you can't simulate a new evaluative outlook the way you can simulate a new city. June pushed back that he still hadn't drawn the line anywhere non-arbitrary, and people are famously terrible at predicting how even mundane stuff will make them feel, yet we don't declare all decision-making irrational. Then Dev said fine, even granting it's a matter of degree, the deeper problem stands: when present-Sana and future-Sana rank the outcomes differently, whose ranking is the rational one? Restricting yourself to the stable values isn't arbitrarily privileging the status quo -- it's the only standpoint both versions of her can share. June's last move was that this would have told every convert, every recovered addict who now endorses the change, every person who left a stifling hometown, that leaping was irrational -- and besides, being someone who's *willing to be changed* is itself a value Sana holds right now, so the "stable values" test might actually cut in favour of the surgery.
