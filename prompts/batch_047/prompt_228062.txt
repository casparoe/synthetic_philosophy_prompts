I'm hoping you can settle an argument that's been raging at my company for two weeks. I'm on the hiring committee at a mid-sized software firm, and right now final hiring decisions work like this: five of us senior people each interview the candidate separately, write down our impressions privately, then we meet, discuss, and take a majority vote. Our new COO wants to blow this up. Her proposal is that we send a one-page summary of each finalist to a pool of about 40 employees across the company and let all of them vote, majority wins. Her argument, which she delivered with enormous confidence at an all-hands, is basically that this is just math: if each individual voter is even slightly better than a coin flip at spotting a good hire, then the majority of a large enough group is practically guaranteed to get it right, and 40 voters beats 5 voters every time. She called it "the wisdom of crowds" and said resisting it is elitism.

Something about this smells wrong to me but I keep losing the argument when I try to articulate why. Some relevant details: most of the 40 would never meet the candidate, they'd just read the summary doc. In past open votes at this company, people visibly pile onto whatever our charismatic VP of engineering says first, and the doc would go out with his comment attached. Also, if I'm honest, some of the 40 are great judges of people and some are... not, like there are folks I'd genuinely trust less than a coin flip on this particular question. Meanwhile the counterargument from the COO's camp is that our current 5-person committee isn't so hot either, we each catch different things but we all have blind spots, and anyway our "discussion" phase mostly means the two loudest members sway the other three, so our supposed independence is a fiction too.

Please act as someone who really knows social choice theory and the mathematics of collective decisions, and think carefully before answering. It might help to start by laying out, in plain terms, exactly what has to be true for the "more voters makes the majority nearly infallible" argument to go through, and then check each of those conditions against both our current system and the COO's proposal, because I suspect both systems fail some of them in different ways. Then give me an actual recommendation: if neither pure option is right, how would you structure the decision instead, and what's the honest thing to say to the COO about where her math argument goes wrong (assuming it does)? Don't just give me generic "it depends" consulting-speak, I want the real structure of the problem.
