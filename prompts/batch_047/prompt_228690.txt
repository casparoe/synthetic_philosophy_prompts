So I've been rereading some old LessWrong threads on memes and I want a real test case. Take the ALS ice bucket challenge from summer 2014: dump ice water on your head, post the video, nominate three friends who have 24 hours to do it or donate, donations to ALS research spiking by over a hundred million dollars, and then the whole thing basically vanishing within a couple months. Analyze it as a case study in the Darwinian theory of culture. Be disciplined about the mapping: what's the actual replicator here (the video format? the nomination mechanic? the 24-hour deadline?), what's heredity-with-variation in this case, and what were the selection pressures. Two pressure points I especially want pushed on: (1) memetics in the Dawkins/Dennett style usually treats hosts as passive vehicles for parasitic replicators, but this meme paid its hosts (social credit, warm glow) and paid its ostensible cause -- does that break the parasite framing, or is symbiosis already in the theory and people just ignore it? (2) the theory should have something non-tautalogical to say about why it died -- satiation, mutation into parody, the nomination chains exhausting the social graph -- and if every possible outcome (spread, death, persistence) is equally "explained" after the fact, is memetics doing explanatory work at all or just redescribing virality in bio vocab? Run the analysis both directions: what the theory illuminates about the episode, and what the episode shows about where the theory is thin.
