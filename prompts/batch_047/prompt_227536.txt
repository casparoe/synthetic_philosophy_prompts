I've been lurking in too many arguments about whether chatbots "really" understand anything, and I keep noticing the same move on both sides and I can't figure out if philosophers have a word for it.

The move goes like this. Someone wants to deny that a system genuinely means anything by its outputs, so they reach for where the system came from: it only "means" something because some programmer or trainer intended it to, so whatever meaning it has is borrowed, secondhand, not really its own. Fine so far. But then the other person points out that human brains were also put together by a process with no intentions whatsoever (blind selection), so by that criterion our meanings are just as borrowed. And here's the interesting part: instead of biting the bullet, the first person just... swaps criteria. Now suddenly it's not about origins at all, it's about what the internal states do, their causal role, whether they're hooked up to perception and action in the right way. And the other person does the mirror-image version: when defending the machine they talk about causal role and function, but when the evolutionary point gets turned on them they start talking about origins. Each side has two measuring sticks -- where it came from, and what it does -- and they pick whichever stick makes their preferred system come out as really-meaningful and the other one not.

Two concrete instances I've seen just this month: (a) someone insisted a chess engine doesn't "want" to protect its king, it's just executing what it was built to do, but five minutes later described their own aversion to pain as obviously genuine even though natural selection built that in too, for its own "purposes". (b) Someone argued a sunflower turning toward the light doesn't really track the sun because nothing intended it, but granted that a sun-tracking robot does track it -- and then, when asked about the robot's designers vs. the flower's evolution, got very slippery about which direction the borrowing goes.

I know this is somewhere near the Chinese Room debate and Searle's thing about intrinsic vs. derived intentionality, but that's a distinction between kinds of intentionality, not a name for the flip-flopping. It's also not just motivated reasoning in general -- it's more specific than that, it's a swap between two particular criteria that each side officially endorses only when convenient. Does this specific configuration have a name? And if it doesn't, could you coin one with a tight definition: what exactly distinguishes it from plain inconsistency, from equivocating on the word "meaning", and from honestly changing one's mind about which criterion is right? Bonus points if you can tell me which side of the Searle-vs-his-critics fight you think each measuring stick actually belongs to.
