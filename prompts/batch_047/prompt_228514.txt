I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm working on a piece about how historians handle moral judgment. The profession has this strong norm against "presentism": you're not supposed to judge people in the past by today's moral standards. A biographer who calls a slaveholding founder a monster gets accused of anachronism; grad students are trained to reconstruct what was even thinkable at the time. But the same discipline happily judges past beliefs as flatly wrong -- nobody says it's unfair to call geocentrists mistaken just because geocentrism was reasonable back then. And meanwhile the public-facing side of the field (museum labels, building renamings, commemoration fights) increasingly does make moral calls. So what theory of morality does the anti-presentism norm actually presuppose? That moral claims are time-indexed while factual claims aren't? Is that coherent, or does it smuggle in a selective relativism the discipline would never avow? Could you defend the norm on purely epistemic grounds -- we have no more reason to trust our moral instincts than they had theirs -- without any relativism at all? And what would have to change in how history gets written and taught if historians treated moral knowledge as cumulative, the way they treat scientific knowledge?
