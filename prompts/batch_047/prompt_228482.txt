I coordinate a monthly philosophy-and-science reading group at a public library, and next session I've volunteered to do a thirty-minute presentation on the prisoner's dilemma and the evolution of cooperation. The audience is about fifteen regulars: a retired accountant, two high-school teachers, a nurse, a software developer, and others -- intelligent adults, but nobody with game theory beyond the occasional newspaper column, and a few who confidently believe they already know what the prisoner's dilemma "proves." I don't want the presentation itself; I want a plan for the explanation. Specifically, I want to know the order in which the pieces have to be introduced and why that order is forced: which concepts must be in place before the payoff matrix will make sense as anything more than a table of numbers, whether the one-shot dilemma should come before or after the iterated version (and what breaks pedagogically if you reverse it), and at what point the shadow of the future can be introduced without it sounding like a mere slogan.

I'd also like help choosing the single carrying example to build the whole session around. I've considered the classic two-prisoners story, a price war between two gas stations on the same road, and trench-warfare live-and-let-live arrangements; tell me which one you'd pick, what properties the example must have to carry both the one-shot and the iterated analysis (and what it must conspicuously lack -- e.g., whether enforcement, communication, or repeated personal relationships help or quietly smuggle in assumptions that ruin the demonstration), and where in the sequence it should be switched or generalized if at all. Then map the misconceptions I should expect: that defection is simply "the rational choice" full stop, that cooperation evolving requires the agents to be altruistic or moral, that tit-for-tat won because it is clever or because it beats its opponents head-to-head, and that the results show cooperation is always stable once established. For each, tell me at what point in the sequence to head it off, and how -- before it forms, or after letting the audience articulate it.

Finally: what check for understanding would actually show the idea has landed rather than been memorized -- I'm thinking something like asking them to diagnose why cooperation collapses when players know exactly which round is last, but tell me whether that's a good probe or too tricky. And what should I explicitly refuse to explain at this level (evolutionary stability formalism? replicator dynamics? the contested details of specific tournament results?), and how do I say so without making the omissions feel like evasions?
