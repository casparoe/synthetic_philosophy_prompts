Here is the passage (it's from the 1700s, so some of the phrasing is old-fashioned):

"It was about this time I conceived the bold and arduous project of arriving at moral perfection. I wished to live without committing any fault at any time; I would conquer all that either natural inclination, custom, or company might lead me into. As I knew, or thought I knew, what was right and wrong, I did not see why I might not always do the one and avoid the other. But I soon found I had undertaken a task of more difficulty than I had imagined. While my care was employed in guarding against one fault, I was often surprised by another; habit took the advantage of inattention; inclination was sometimes too strong for reason. I concluded, at length, that the mere speculative conviction that it was our interest to be completely virtuous was not sufficient to prevent our slipping, and that the contrary habits must be broken, and good ones acquired and established, before we can have any dependence on a steady, uniform rectitude of conduct."

Franklin then explains that, because different writers lump different ideas under the same virtue names, he decided to use more names with fewer ideas attached to each, and he lists thirteen virtues, each with a short precept — for example, Order gets the precept about letting all your things have their places and each part of your business its time, and the precept for Humility is "Imitate Jesus and Socrates." He then describes a little ruled book in which he gave himself a black spot for each fault committed against each virtue on each day.

Here is what I genuinely don't understand, beyond just paraphrasing it:

1. Franklin starts by saying he already knew what was right and wrong, and his failure is blamed entirely on habit and inattention, not on ignorance. But then his whole solution is to redefine the virtues himself, splitting them into thirteen narrow items with his own precepts — which sounds like he did NOT already know what was right and wrong, or at least not clearly. Is this a contradiction, or is Franklin drawing a real distinction between two kinds of "knowing what's right"? If it's a distinction, what exactly is it, and does his little bookkeeping method even address the kind of knowledge he was missing?

2. What should I make of the parenthetical "or thought I knew"? My teacher hinted that the adult Franklin, writing this decades later, is quietly mocking his younger self. If that's right, how much of the passage is sincere self-improvement advice and how much is irony? Does the irony, if it's there, undermine the project or actually make it wiser?

3. Why would a precept for Humility be "Imitate Jesus and Socrates"? Those seem like strange choices to pair together, and "imitate someone" is a very different kind of instruction from the other precepts, which are rules about specific behavior. Does this precept fit Franklin's own stated method of giving each virtue a narrow, clear precept, or does it break his system?

4. My classmates keep reading this as Franklin being arrogant (he thought he could achieve moral perfection!) or as Franklin being humble (he admits he failed!). Based only on this passage, why are both of those readings too simple?
