I am a doctoral student working on formal epistemology and the foundations of probability, and I would like your help stress-testing a position I have provisionally adopted in my dissertation. Please answer as an expert in the philosophy of probability and formal epistemology; think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the widest plausible range of considerations before organizing your answer.

The position is a strong reading of the symmetry-based resolution of Bertrand's paradox. On this view, the classical paradoxes of the principle of indifference -- the chord problem, the cube-factory problem, the water-and-wine problem, and their relatives -- do not show that indifference reasoning is unreliable. They show only that indifference must be applied to a problem whose statement fixes a definite group of transformations under which the epistemic situation is invariant. Once the correct symmetry group is identified, the uniform distribution over the group's invariant measure yields a unique answer; every apparent paradox arises either from applying uniformity to a parametrization that fails to respect the problem's actual symmetries, or from a problem statement that is genuinely ill-posed because it underdetermines which group applies. So, on my position: wherever indifference reasoning misfires, the fault lies in ill-posedness or in the wrong choice of group, never in the principle of indifference itself, which is correct and uniquely applicable whenever the problem is fully specified.

What would count against this? I am asking for a map of the view's exposure, not a survey of the literature. Specifically:

1. What observations, constructions, or formal results would defeat it outright? For example: a fully specified problem, with no disputed symmetries, where indifference over the natural invariant measure demonstrably leads to error; or a proof that no uniqueness result of the relevant kind can be had under plausible conditions; or a case where two different symmetry groups are each faithfully suggested by a single problem statement with no principled tiebreaker -- is that last one merely logically possible, or are there realistic cases of it?

2. Which defeaters would a committed defender of this view concede as genuine threats, as opposed to ones they would dismiss as re-described ill-posedness? I am particularly interested in whether the response "your problem was just not fully specified" is always available to the defender, and if so, whether that availability immunizes the position rather than vindicates it.

3. Does the view stand or fall with anything outside philosophy of probability -- say, with a claim about when physical symmetries can be read off a problem statement, or with positions on how reference classes or partitions get fixed in other problems (the grue problem, Sleeping Beauty, the fine-tuning argument)?

4. Please rank the defeaters you identify from fatal to merely awkward, and state for each what the world -- mathematical or empirical -- would have to look like for it to obtain.

Finally, an honest verdict: is this position well insulated, and if it is, is that insulation a sign of its truth or a sign that it has been qualified into near-unfalsifiability? If the latter, I would like to know what a falsifiable successor position would look like -- one that keeps the symmetry intuition but genuinely risks something.
