I'm putting together a statement of my views for a graduate seminar on the ethics of rescue and triage, and before I circulate it I want to know whether the package I've assembled is actually coherent or whether I've just collected verdicts I find independently attractive. The seminar has been working through Taurek's challenge to number-counting in rescue cases, and I've found myself persuaded by more of it than most of my classmates — but I'm not sure I've followed my own convictions all the way through. Here is where I've landed.

First, I accept Taurek's core conclusion: if I can save either one stranger or five strangers, but not all six, it is permissible for me to save the one, and a fair coin flip would be a perfectly decent way to decide. The five's losses don't aggregate into some larger thing that outweighs the one's life, because there is no one who suffers that aggregate loss. Second, I think a public health agency allocating a single dose of a scarce treatment between one patient and five equally needy patients would act wrongly if it gave the dose to the one; an impartial public body in that situation ought to save the greater number, full stop. Third, if it were my own child against five strangers' children, I should save my child, and I don't think fairness requires me to flip any coins about it. Fourth, I think that whenever people are in equal peril and I can't save everyone, fairness requires giving each person an equal chance of being saved — this is part of what attracts me to Taurek's position in the first place. Fifth, I reject utilitarian aggregation generally, on separateness-of-persons grounds: the fact that five losses are numerically greater than one doesn't make them morally greater, because losses only matter as someone's loss.

My question: does this set hang together? Please go through the commitments pairwise and identify which ones are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, distinguishing these carefully rather than flagging everything. For each genuine tension, tell me exactly which commitment would have to be revised or abandoned to resolve it, and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the view. Rank the tensions by severity, and if one single revision would dissolve the most trouble, name it — but I want the audit to determine that, not my own sense of which belief I like least.
