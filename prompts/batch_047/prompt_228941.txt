I am a retired biochemist, and over the course of a forty-year career in the pharmaceutical industry I sat on more animal-research oversight committees than I care to count. Since retiring I have joined a philosophy reading group, largely for the company, and one member in particular — a former patent attorney with no formal philosophical training — has a way of delivering instant, confident verdicts on every moral case we discuss. I have started writing her verdicts down, because I suspect there is a coherent principle underneath them and I want to figure out what it is. Her record so far: battery-cage egg production justified by cheaper supermarket eggs — clearly wrong, she says, because the savings are trivial against the hens' misery; blinding tests of a new shampoo on rabbits — clearly wrong; invasive studies on a few dozen rats for a promising childhood leukemia drug, when no animal-free method exists — permissible, though reluctantly; the very same leukemia study conducted on chimpanzees instead — not permissible, find another way or wait; painless killing of healthy surplus lab mice at a study's conclusion — acceptable, though she adds that breeding fewer would be better.

The case I intend to put to her at our next meeting is this one. A compound shows early promise of substantially relieving chronic migraines for tens of millions of sufferers; testing it requires procedures that are brief but genuinely painful for a few hundred rats; the condition is debilitating but not life-threatening; and no animal-free testing method is available. My problem is that her track record seems consistent with at least three different rules, and they pull apart here. Under a straight aggregation rule, which weighs suffering and benefit across species impartially, the massive relief on the human side should settle the matter in favor of the tests. Under an asymmetry rule I have seen attributed, in essence, to Robert Nozick — the suggestion that something like utilitarian weighing is appropriate where animals are concerned while persons are protected by Kantian-style constraints that may not be overridden for aggregate benefit — everything turns on whether a non-life-saving benefit clears the seriousness threshold her leukemia verdict seems to set, and I genuinely cannot tell whether it does. Under a third rule, on which moral weight tracks a species' characteristic cognitive sophistication, rats rank low enough that the tests should pass — but then I would expect her chimpanzee verdict to have been about the apes' capacities rather than about the stakes, and the way she paired those two leukemia verdicts leaves that open.

So here is what I would like. First, predict her verdict on the migraine case, and say which of these three rules — or what fourth rule I have missed — best accounts for all five of her earlier verdicts taken together, explaining what each candidate rule predicts about the new case and why you favor your best guess. Second, tell me whether her apparent position amounts to that Nozick-style asymmetry in practice, and where the known pressure points of such a view lie. Third, describe the single further case — one I could pose at the following meeting — whose verdict would discriminate between the two strongest candidate rules, and explain why it would settle the matter. Please write your answer in American English, and please be concrete about the cases rather than abstract about the principles.
