EXAM QUESTION

(a) (2 points) State Adams's Thesis precisely, and distinguish it from the claim that indicative conditionals have the truth conditions of the material conditional.

(b) (5 points) Reconstruct Lewis's triviality argument. Your answer should identify (i) the assumption about which probability functions Adams's Thesis must hold for, (ii) the basic structure of the derivation, and (iii) exactly what the conclusion of the argument is.

(c) (3 points) Explain why the triviality argument leaves the material-conditional analysis of indicatives untouched, and state one cost of adopting that analysis as a way of escaping the argument.

STUDENT'S ANSWER

(a) Adams's Thesis says that the probability of an indicative conditional "If A, then C" equals the corresponding conditional probability: P(A→C) = P(C|A), provided P(A) > 0. This is a claim about probabilities, not about truth conditions. The material-conditional analysis, by contrast, says A→C is true just in case A is false or C is true. The two come apart, since P(¬A ∨ C) generally exceeds P(C|A) — for instance, when A is improbable, the material conditional is almost certainly true even if C is improbable given A.

(b) Suppose there is some proposition, expressed by A→C, whose probability always equals P(C|A). Crucially, Lewis requires this identity to hold not just for a single probability function but for a class of probability functions closed under conditionalization. Since A→C is a truth-functional compound of A and C, we can compute its probability under conditionalized distributions. Conditionalizing on C: P(A→C | C) = P(C | A∧C) = 1, so P(A→C ∧ C) = P(C). Conditionalizing on ¬C: P(A→C | ¬C) = P(C | A∧¬C) = 0, so P(A→C ∧ ¬C) = 0. Adding, P(A→C) = P(C): the probability of the conditional collapses onto the probability of its consequent, which is absurd — the language is "trivialized." Lewis's theorem therefore proves that indicative conditionals do not have truth values at all; their acceptability is instead a matter of conditional subjective probability.

(c) The material-conditional analysis escapes because it never claims that P(A→C) equals P(C|A). On that analysis, A→C expresses the proposition ¬A ∨ C, whose probability is perfectly well defined and simply differs from the conditional probability, so the triviality derivation never gets started. Since the analysis evades Lewis's argument, and since Grice's pragmatic story can explain away the cases where the material analysis seems to give the wrong verdicts, triviality on balance vindicates the material-conditional analysis and we should accept it.
