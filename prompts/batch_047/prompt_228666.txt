ok so i'm a longtime LW/OB reader and i've been thinking about aumann agreement and the no-trade theorem (milgrom-stokey, the whole "if the other side of the trade is rational and we share priors, one of us is wrong" thing) and how the rationalist community treats "we can't agree to disagree" as practically a moral commandment. it occured to me that this is extremely ripe for satire because nobody actually lives this way.

You are an expert in game theory and information economics who also happens to write for a McSweeney's-style humor site, so you know exactly where the theory's joints are and where it gets absurd when taken literally.

Write a deadpan parody of an internal employee handbook section (or HR policy memo) for a quantitative hedge fund whose founders read too much about Aumann's agreement theorem and no-trade results and decided to reorganize the entire firm around them. Real features to exaggerate, things like:

- the common prior assumption treated as a hiring criterion
- "agreeing to disagree" classified as an HR violation, with a formal posterior-exchange procedure where disagreeing analysts must take turns announcing updated beliefs until convergence (and someone has to adjudicate what happens when they just... don't)
- the awkward fact that every trade the fund makes implies the counterparty is either irrational or misinformed, so compliance now requires a memo justifying each trade's counterparty's epistemic failure
- the recursive problem that if a job candidate would happily take the other side of the fund's positions, that itself is evidence about something

Before writing, brainstorm a list of genuine features of these theorems (common knowledge, common priors, the fact that trade itself is information, dutch books, etc.) and pick the 3-4 that are funniest when bureaucratized. The humor has to come from the actual content -- a specialist should be able to tell you've read this stuff and find the satire fair -- but it should still be legible and funny to someone who's never heard of Milgrom or Stokey. Stay completely deadpan the whole time, no winking, and the target is the over-application of these ideas (and quant-fund culture generally), not any real person.

About 600-800 words, formatted as a policy document with numbered clauses, section headers, and maybe one footnote where the general counsel quietly raises an objection that gets procedurally dismissed.
