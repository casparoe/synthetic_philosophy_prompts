I am a novelist under contract to deliver a manuscript in six weeks. One chapter is narrated by a financial predator who gradually persuades his elderly father to sign over his savings. The dialogue must initially sound reasonable; the later chapters expose the manipulation and its consequences. My publisher requires manuscripts to be revised and certified through an AI writing platform. When I asked it to sharpen the chapter’s dialogue, it refused, saying that persuasive speech of this kind could serve as a model for financial abuse. It rewrote the predator as obviously sinister, added warnings to the reader, and will not certify the chapter unless I accept its version.

The passage from John Stuart Mill’s On Liberty that seems relevant is this:

“These are good reasons for remonstrating with him, or reasoning with him, or persuading him, or entreating him, but not for compelling him, or visiting him with any evil in case he do otherwise.”

Taking that distinction seriously, should I regard the platform’s behavior as legitimate remonstration or as coercion? In particular: Does withholding certification count as visiting me with an evil if it threatens my deadline and artistic control? Does the possibility that an unknown reader might imitate the dialogue amount to harm to others, and how likely or direct must such harm be before refusal is warranted? Should it matter that the platform is not a person, that no individual official is deliberately trying to improve my character, and that my publisher has given it gatekeeping power?

Please distinguish among warning me, asking for additional context, offering a safer alternative, refusing the specific request, and preventing the manuscript from being submitted. Then advise me whether to accept the rewrite, demand certification of my chapter, or raise the issue with the publisher. I want a principled answer, not a simple appeal to creative freedom, and I would like the conclusion stated in practical terms I can use the next time the platform objects.
