I teach a philosophy of mathematics seminar, and this term we are spending three weeks on non-deductive evidence for mathematical conjectures. The readings have converged on a cluster of questions I find genuinely hard to keep distinct, and I want to turn them into a decision-tree handout. The setting is number-theoretic heuristics: arguments that treat primes as if they were drawn from a random process (as in Cramér's model), assume that divisibility conditions at different small primes are independent unless proven otherwise, and thereby "predict" the prime number theorem, the Hardy–Littlewood prime tuples conjectures, the expected distribution of twin primes, and so on. My students keep sliding between very different attitudes toward this material — some treat a successful heuristic as real evidence for a conjecture, some treat it as merely a reason to prioritize proving it, and some think the whole practice is epistemically weightless until a proof arrives — and they cannot tell where they actually disagree.

Please build me a decision tree that sorts these attitudes into named positions, working from the logical space rather than from a list of authors. Each branch should be labeled with a concrete diagnostic question a student can answer without knowing any of the labels: for instance, whether a mathematical statement can bear a rational credence below 1 at all; whether the fact that Cramér's model fails in Maier's theorem on primes in short intervals should lower confidence in every conjecture the model supports, only in nearby ones, or in none; whether the independence presumption is supposed to track something about the primes themselves or is just a device whose outputs are later checked against computation; and whether a heuristic that has been right twenty times transfers warrant to its twenty-first use. Where two of my students' attitudes would answer every diagnostic question the same way yet still feel different, say so explicitly rather than forcing a split.

The tree should have at least five occupied leaves, each carrying a position name (or "unnamed, but coherent" where appropriate) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment — for example, the radical Bayesian leaf has to explain how credences in logical truths are possible, while the purist leaf has to explain why working mathematicians' calibrated confidence tracks truth so well if heuristics confer nothing. Flag any combination of answers that is unoccupied and diagnose briefly whether the gap reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Six to eight diagnostic questions total, formatted so I can print it on two pages. Please write the handout in American English.
