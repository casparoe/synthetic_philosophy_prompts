I am a doctoral student in philosophy of cognitive science, and each term I help convene a two-hour meeting of our department's graduate reading group in philosophy of mind: typically ten to fourteen participants, mixed disciplinary backgrounds, comfortable with formal notation but not necessarily with one another. This term's theme is emergence and downward causation, and I would rather open the session with an activity than with a précis. Design a staged simulation that makes participants feel the tension inside the simmballs-style picture, in Hofstadter's sense: low-level entities obeying purely local rules whose collective churn constitutes higher-level symbols, where the higher-level pattern then seems to organise and constrain what the low-level entities do next. The impasse I want the group to reach unaided is the question of whether the symbol level ever genuinely pushes the substrate around, or whether every apparent case of downward causation is fully mediated, token by token, at the lower level, and whether anything hangs on that difference beyond descriptive convenience.

Concretely: participants should physically enact the low level (index cards, tokens, floor markings, whatever is cheap and portable), each person bound to a local rule whose global consequences they cannot survey, so that a macro-pattern emerges which nobody intended and which then feeds back into what moves are permissible. There should be a second phase in which the group is set the task of altering the macro-pattern and must discover by trial which interventions actually work: addressing the pattern, as it were, versus touching individual actors one at a time. Include a scoring or incentive scheme with small non-monetary stakes that keeps people faithful to their local rule even once they have guessed what is forming. There must be no lecture beforehand, and the vocabulary of the debate should not be uttered until the debrief.

Please give me full rules and a materials list; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 120 minutes, debrief included; a mechanism for preventing participants from simply coordinating to manufacture the pattern once they smell the trick; the debrief itself, mapping identifiable moments of the game onto the symbol–substrate question and stating plainly where the analogy breaks (my assumption is that the widest gap is that the participants are conscious agents with memory and foresight, which simmballs are not); advice on what to do with the one participant who has read the relevant material and recognises the isomorphism within five minutes; a note on what will predictably go wrong the first two times the activity is run and how to recover in the room; and a closing paragraph I can read aloud saying what the activity cannot reproduce about the actual position and so must be supplied by discussion instead.
