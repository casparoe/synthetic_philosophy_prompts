So I just spent like three hours arguing with my friend Dan about the Church-Turing thesis and we got absolutely nowhere, and I want to figure out why, because I don't think either of us is stupid.

His position: the thesis is basically a piece of mathematics, or at most a really successful definition. Turing gave a precise formalization of "effective calculability," it turned out to be equivalent to every other reasonable formalization anyone came up with (lambda calculus, recursive functions, register machines, whatever), and that convergence is the whole content of the thesis. There's nothing left to be right or wrong about. If someone built a machine that computed the halting function, he'd say -- cool, that machine isn't "computing" in the sense the thesis is about, or it's using infinite precision or a closed timelike curve or whatever, and the thesis was never about *that*.

My position: the thesis is an empirical claim about the physical world, roughly that no physically realizable process can compute more than a Turing machine. And that means it's falsifiable in principle -- if you could actually build a hypercomputer, even a janky one that only worked under weird conditions near a black hole or something, the thesis would be *wrong*, not just "about something else." The fact that every proposed hypercomputer so far needs infinite precision or infinite time or unphysical stuff is evidence *for* the thesis, not a proof that the question is settled by definition.

We both know all the standard moves here. He knows about the physical versions of the thesis, I know about the definitional reading, we've both heard the "Turing was talking about human computors following rules, not physics" thing. And yet neither of us budged.

So my question: what kind of disagreement is this actually? A few hypotheses I've been turning over:

1. It's purely verbal -- we're just using "the Church-Turing thesis" for two different claims (a mathematical-conceptual one and a physical one) and there's a fact of the matter about which one Turing and Church actually meant, which would settle it historically but not philosophically.
2. It's substantive but hidden-empirical -- really we disagree about whether unbounded precision or supertasks are physically possible, and we've dressed that up as a philosophy of math dispute.
3. It's a disagreement about what a counterexample would even have to look like -- Dan's definitional reading makes counterexamples impossible by construction, mine makes them possible, and the real question is which reading does more work.

Or is it something else entirely? Give me your diagnosis with actual reasons, engage with what each side's position really commits them to (not a survey of the literature), and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis -- a theorem, a physics result, a distinction, a decision to just split the term in two. And be honest: if you think this one never gets resolved and why, say that instead.
