I'm a PhD student working on situated cognition, and I'd like a satirical piece for our department's humor column. Write a formal decision letter from a university's Academic Integrity Board, roughly 600–700 words, ruling on a student with severe memory impairment who was charged under the closed-book exam regulations for consulting a battered notebook he carries everywhere, writes in daily, and consults without the slightest impulse to double-check it. The Board, applying the extended mind thesis in complete earnest, overturns the charge: consulting the notebook was retrieval from memory, not use of unauthorized materials. The humor should come from the Board getting the philosophy exactly right while remaining serenely blind to how far its reasoning travels. It should recite the standard conditions for cognitive extension—constant availability, fluent accessibility, automatic endorsement—as if they were clauses of the student handbook; carefully distinguish this student's notebook from a classmate's smartphone (whose separate appeal, denied in a footnote, fails the conditions because he verifies its output); and firmly insist it is not confusing mere causal coupling with constitution. Without ever winking, the letter should let a reader feel the real objections—cognitive bloat, the coupling-constitution fallacy, whether functional parity settles what counts as part of the mind—simply by watching the Board follow the thesis wherever it leads. Deadpan bureaucratic voice throughout; the scenario should be recognizably the Otto case run through a disciplinary committee.
