When courts interpret statutes, a familiar ritual unfolds: judges reach for general-purpose dictionaries—often several, sometimes editions from the era of enactment—to pin down the ordinary meaning of a contested word, while treating the legislature's own accounts of its handiwork (committee reports, floor statements, testimony from the bill's sponsors) as optional at best and inadmissible at worst. The result is a curious division of epistemic labor: the people who actually wrote the law are distrusted as witnesses to its meaning, while a lexicographer who never read the statute is treated as an authority on it. I find this puzzling, and I'd like to understand what it commits the legal system to. What picture of linguistic meaning does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: what must meaning be like for a dictionary to outrank a drafter as evidence of it, and what must communication between a legislature and the public be assumed to consist in? Which rival accounts of meaning—speaker-intention views, use theories, audience-oriented or interpretive-community views—does the practice quietly rule out, and which does it merely happen not to need? Please distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely fits comfortably with: I suspect parts of it are philosophically underdetermined and could be licensed by more than one theory, and I'd like to know which parts those are. Then assess coherence. For instance, can a court that insists meaning is fixed by public convention at the moment of enactment coherently explain why it sometimes consults contemporary dictionaries instead—and can it explain why the conventions of the whole linguistic community, rather than of the legal community or of legislators, are the relevant ones? Finally, tell me concretely what would have to change in courtroom practice if the implicit theory were replaced with a forthright intentionalism about statutory meaning: what new evidence would become central, what familiar moves would become irrational, and would anything about the current practice survive?
