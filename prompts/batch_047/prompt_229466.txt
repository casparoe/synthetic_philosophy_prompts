I’m taking an applied ethics course, and our seminar group has been asked to write an advisory note about an accident on campus. The university uses small delivery robots to carry library books between buildings. One rainy evening, a robot’s proximity sensors started performing badly, but instead of stopping it recalculated a route across the pavement and hit a student’s bicycle, injuring her. The engineer says the robot had passed all its required safety tests and that rain-related sensor problems were too rare to justify shutting the fleet down. The campus employee assigned to monitor it says the warning appeared less than a second before the collision, so she had no realistic chance to intervene. The company says the university approved the route, while the university says the company’s software made the decision. No one has found a specific coding error, although internal tests had shown that the sensors sometimes degraded in heavy rain. The injured student’s parents are demanding to know who is responsible, and the university’s answer so far is basically that “the system failed,” which feels like a way of avoiding the question.

One reading for the course is Book III of Aristotle’s Nicomachean Ethics, where he asks, “What sort of acts, then, should be called compulsory? We answer that without qualification actions are so when the cause is in the external circumstances and the agent contributes nothing.” I’m not sure how to apply that test when the “moving principle” is spread across a robot’s software, its designers, the person monitoring it, and the institution that deployed it. Should we say the robot itself acted because the immediate route choice came from inside its system, even though it can’t understand blame or make amends? Or does that mean responsibility has to be traced back to particular humans—and if their contributions each seem reasonable on their own, can it still make sense to say they are collectively blameworthy? I need concrete advice on what the university should say and do: who should apologize, who should compensate the student, whether anyone should be sanctioned, and whether it is ever acceptable to conclude that a serious harm happened without anyone being morally responsible for it.
