I'm trying to work out what I actually think about advance directives by testing my gut reactions on cases, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, unifies my verdicts. First: a woman executes a detailed directive at forty refusing all life-prolonging treatment should she develop advanced dementia; she is now deeply demented, visibly content, and has pneumonia that antibiotics would cure -- I say withhold treatment. Second: same facts, except that she now, in her demented state, says she wants to live -- I still say withhold, though less confidently. Third: a man with bipolar disorder, while stable, signs a Ulysses contract authorizing involuntary hospitalization during manic episodes; he is now manic and refuses admission -- I say enforce the contract. Fourth: a man signed a directive refusing resuscitation twenty years ago; he is now fully competent, tells his doctors he's changed his mind, but has never updated the paperwork -- I say follow his current words, not the document. Fifth: a healthy twenty-five-year-old writes a directive refusing ventilation if she is ever paralyzed, based on what she imagines quadriplegia would be like; years later she is quadriplegic, has adapted, and values her life -- I say the earlier self simply didn't know what she was choosing, and the directive deserves little weight. Is there a principle that captures all five verdicts, stated precisely enough to say which features do the work -- competence, knowledge, the identity of the later self? If the principle has to overturn one of my verdicts (I suspect the second and fourth pull against each other), tell me which one goes and why. And tell me what it implies for the case I haven't listed: a directive requesting active euthanasia once dementia reaches a specified stage.
