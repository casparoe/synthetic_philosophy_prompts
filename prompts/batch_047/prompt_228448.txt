Let’s do a philosophical sparring exercise. Play Friedrich Nietzsche shortly after finishing The Genealogy of Morals, using the Horace B. Samuel translation. Speak in the first person as Nietzsche, not as a lecturer explaining Nietzsche, and use only concepts and information plausibly available to you in 1888. Stay combative and a little theatrical, but don’t become a mere insult machine: if I make a genuinely strong methodological point, acknowledge it, while never conceding that genealogy is simply arbitrary storytelling. Keep each reply under 250 words and stay in character until I type STOP.

In the Preface to The Genealogy of Morals, you write: “We have no right to be ‘disconnected’; we must neither err ‘disconnectedly’ nor strike the truth ‘disconnectedly.’ Rather with the necessity with which a tree bears its fruit, so do our thoughts, our values, our Yes’s and No’s and If’s and Whether’s, grow connected and interrelated, mutual witnesses of one will, one health, one kingdom, one sun—as to whether they are to your taste, these fruits of ours?—But what matters that to the trees? What matters that to us, us the philosophers?”

My opening challenge: this passage sounds less like a method than a licence for motivated reasoning. Any clever writer can connect enough psychological guesses, selective historical episodes, and value judgments into one organic-looking tree; the internal coherence of the story proves nothing about whether morality really grew that way. So answer directly, Nietzsche: what is one concrete criterion by which a genealogy can be answerable to evidence rather than merely persuasive literature? Pick a definite side, give reasons, and test your criterion on just one moral concept—pity, guilt, or equality, whichever gives your account the hardest time. No hedging with “on the one hand,” and no invoking later interpreters or scholarship you could not know.
