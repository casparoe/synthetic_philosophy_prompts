You are a professional moral philosopher with particular expertise in normative ethics and the ethics of rescue. I am a philosophy professor preparing a seminar on Singer's pond case and its descendants, and I want your help doing some reflective equilibrium on my own considered judgments. Please think carefully. I would like you to begin by brainstorming several candidate principles -- at least three -- before settling on the one you defend, and then to stress-test it rather than merely present it.

Here are my verdicts on a set of cases. In each, take the stipulations at face value; I am not interested in wriggling out via empirical doubt.

1. The pond: I pass a shallow pond in which a child is drowning. Wading in costs me a ruined suit worth a few hundred dollars and a missed morning of work. Clearly I must wade in, and it would be monstrous not to.
2. The donation: I can donate a few hundred dollars to a thoroughly vetted charity that will, with high confidence, use it to save a child's life overseas. I think I am obligated to donate, and my sense is that the obligation is nearly as strong as in the pond case, though I admit it feels less urgent.
3. The uncertain donation: Same as (2), except the charity's program is promising but genuinely contested -- perhaps a coin flip whether my money saves anyone. I still think I am obligated, and I do not feel the obligation has been halved.
4. The rough river: A child is drowning in a fast river. I am a strong swimmer, but attempting the rescue carries, say, a one-in-five chance that I drown too. I think attempting it is admirable but not obligatory; declining is no moral failure.
5. The redundant lifeguards: Five of us on a beach each know that a single text message to the lifeguard station will save a drowning swimmer, and each knows the others know. If everyone assumes someone else has texted, no one does, and the swimmer dies. I think each of us wrongs the swimmer by failing to text, and the wrongness does not feel one-fifth as bad as the pond case.
6. The permanent tithe: If (2) generalizes, then I am obligated not just to make one donation but to give away everything above a modest sufficiency, year after year, indefinitely, since the need never ends. I find I cannot accept that this is obligatory. At some point the demand becomes unreasonable, even granting that every marginal dollar would do as much good as the first.
7. The borrowed boat: Reaching the drowning child requires commandeering a stranger's moored rowboat without permission, scratching its hull. I think I am obligated to take the boat, even though the cost falls on an innocent third party and not on me.
8. The letter and the ledger: I can save an identifiable child whose photograph and name arrive in a charity appeal, or, for the same money, fund a program that will save one statistical child -- no one will ever know which. I feel the duty more strongly in the first case, and I suspect this feeling is indefensible, but it persists.

My request: give me the principle, or small set of principles, that best systematizes these verdicts. State each principle precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing which work, and identify which case motivates each clause. Two things matter especially. First, I suspect (3) and (4) are in tension: my verdict on the uncertain donation seems to ignore probability while my verdict on the rough river is governed by it, and I want you to tell me whether the relevant difference is cost-risk versus benefit-risk, something about whose life is at stake, or whether one of the two verdicts has to go. Second, (6) and (7) each seem to break the simple "if you can prevent something very bad at small cost, you must" formulation in different directions -- (6) by demanding a cap, (7) by permitting costs to fall on non-agents -- and I want to know whether a single repair handles both.

Then do three things: (a) tell me which of my eight verdicts your preferred principle cannot accommodate, honestly, and which one you would counsel me to abandon if I value simplicity over completeness; (b) apply the principle to two cases I have not listed -- first, a rescue that requires me to break a serious prior promise made to someone who will be badly harmed by the breach; second, the case where my donation is fully fungible against government funding, so that my gift causes the state to spend less and the net effect on the children is zero; and (c) say, in a sentence or two, what empirical fact, if changed, would most destabilize the principle you have offered.
