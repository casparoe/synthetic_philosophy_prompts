So I'm in a philosophy of math / logic course this term and we just did the unit on computability. Our prof made a big deal about how the Church-Turing thesis is a *thesis* and not a theorem -- you can't prove it, it just gets support from Turing's analysis of a human computing with pencil and paper plus the fact that all the proposed models (Turing machines, lambda calculus, recursion etc.) turned out equivalent. Fair enough. But then I tried to reconstruct Turing's analysis as an actual proof for my term paper, and I think I've shown it proves way too much, which now has me worried the original analysis is broken. Here's my argument:

(1) An effectively calculable function is one an idealized human "computor" could work out by following a fixed routine, with paper, pencil, and unlimited time and patience.

(2) Whatever such a computor does can be broken down into finitely many kinds of dead-simple steps: read a symbol, write a symbol, erase a symbol, shift attention one square over, branch depending on what you see.

(3) Every one of those simple steps is something a Turing machine can do.

(4) So anything effectively calculable by the human computor is Turing-computable. That's the Church-Turing thesis, and (2)-(3) look like a *proof* of it.

(5) But hold on -- a human computor is just a physical system. And the argument in (2) didn't use anything specifically about humans, only that the system works in discrete steps on a finite amount of data at any moment. Any physical device computing step by step on finite inputs works like that.

(6) So by the same reasoning, anything computable by *any* physical device (in discrete steps, on finite inputs) is Turing-computable. In other words the physical version of the thesis is proved too.

(7) But whether physics allows devices that outcompute Turing machines -- exploiting weird spacetime setups, or genuine continuous quantities with unlimited precision, or whatever -- is an empirical question about how the universe is built. No armchair analysis of pencil-and-paper routines could possibly settle that.

(8) So the argument proves something no such argument could prove. It proves too much. Which means the flaw is back in (2)/(3), and Turing's original analysis of the human computor doesn't establish the thesis either.

And I really don't want (8). If I have to choose I'd rather keep (7) than keep the proof -- the empirical point seems obvious to me -- but my TA insists Turing's analysis is the gold standard and everyone accepts it gives the thesis strong support. So which step actually goes wrong, and what does rejecting it cost? I suspect something slippery happens at (5) but I can't pin down exactly what assumption gets smuggled in there, since "discrete steps, finite data" seems like it should apply to any physical machine just as much as to a person.

One more thing: please be upfront about what's basically settled here vs what's genuinely contested among people who work on this. E.g. I gather the epistemic status of the thesis (theorem vs. inductive hypothesis vs. definition) is itself disputed, so don't present one take on that as the consensus if it isn't. Also, if the answer turns out to be "your step (5) equivocates between two readings and the whole thing collapses," definately say so plainly rather than hedging.
