I'm a university student taking an upper-level seminar on rational choice and game theory, and I'm designing a handout for our unit on symmetry problems. I'd like you to act as an expert in decision theory and the philosophy of rational choice. Please think carefully, and before writing the final handout, brainstorm briefly (in a clearly marked section) about the logical space of answers your diagnostic questions allow; then build the instrument from that space rather than from a list of names. Write everything in American English.

The handout is a decision tree that sorts a reader by what they actually believe about one-shot symmetric games, not by what they call themselves. Assume the reader starts from: "I understand and accept the rules of the following two games."

Game 1 (the Platonia dilemma, after Hofstadter): Twenty strangers are selected. If exactly one of them sends a telegram by noon the next day, that person wins a billion dollars; if zero or more than one send, nobody wins anything. Communication and cooperation are forbidden, and the players cannot confer.

Game 2 (the Luring Lottery, actually run by a popular science magazine in the 1980s): Readers may enter as many times as they like by mailing a single postcard bearing the number of entries they wish to submit. The prize is one million dollars divided by the total number of entries received, awarded to the submitter of one randomly chosen entry. Historically, some entrants submitted astronomically large numbers, making the prize worthless.

For the branch nodes, use the following diagnostic scenarios, including these variants I've devised for the course. Phrase each node as a concrete question a reader could answer without knowing which position it leads to:

(a) The No-Randomization Clause: the Platonia rules are amended so that all randomizing devices are banned and every player must decide deterministically, knowing the others face the same constraint.
(b) The Duplicate Announcement: players are told they were selected because a psychological profiling firm certified that all twenty run, for practical purposes, the same decision procedure -- versus a control version where players are told only that the others are ordinary strangers of similar temperament.
(c) The Self-Matching Ticket: in the Luring Lottery, would you submit a number of entries equal to your best estimate of everyone else's total, even knowing that if everyone reasons that way the entries spiral without bound?
(d) Whether it matters, for your choice in the ordinary Prisoner's Dilemma, that the other player is a recently manufactured copy of you rather than a stranger -- include this only if it earns its keep as a node.
(e) At least two further diagnostic questions of your own design, based on modified versions of the games above.

Each leaf should be a named position (superrationalist in Hofstadter's sense, causal decision theorist, evidential decision theorist, logical or functional decision theorist, mixed-strategy equilibrium theorist, and the view that rationality simply issues no unique verdict here), plus any position your tree needs that I haven't listed; if a coherent combination of answers has no defended position attached to it, mark it "unoccupied" and diagnose in one sentence whether it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Each occupied leaf gets one sentence stating its most awkward commitment -- the case where the view's recommendation is hardest to swallow.

Aim for seven to ten branch nodes and eight to twelve leaves. If any pair of leaves agrees on every diagnostic question your tree contains (I suspect superrationalism and logical decision theory may), flag the pair explicitly and state what further question, if any, could separate them. Format the tree as an indented outline with the splitting question at each node, and end with a short paragraph on what the tree reveals about where the real disagreement lies.
