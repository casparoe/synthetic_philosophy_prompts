My housemate is a cliodynamics enthusiast -- she has read her Turchin and Nefedov, talks about structural-demographic theory at breakfast, and has developed a habit of pronouncing on whether this or that historical episode counts as a genuine secular-cycle disintegration or something else. Over the years I've kept a rough tally of her verdicts. She holds that the late Roman Republic, from the Gracchi to Augustus, was a textbook disintegrative phase; that the French Revolution was one as well, with the monarchy's bankruptcy and the glut of frustrated educated elites doing the work; and that the American Civil War fits the pattern too, on elite-overproduction grounds. On the other side, she insists that the Black Death's devastation of fourteenth-century Europe does not count, because it was an exogenous shock rather than endogenous cycle dynamics -- if anything, she says, it temporarily relieved elite overproduction by thinning the elites and raising wages. She also denies that the Soviet collapse of 1991 qualifies, calling it a fiscal and imperial collapse without the long endogenous build-up of intra-elite competition and popular immiseration, and she denies that the Great Depression in the United States qualifies, despite the mass suffering, because the state remained solvent and there was no surplus of credentialed elites jockeying for too few positions.

Last week, over coffee, I asked her whether contemporary China is entering a disintegrative phase, and she refused to answer on the spot, which surprised me, since she usually pronounces instantly. I've been turning it over since. The case seems to sit oddly on her track record: China arguably shows something like elite overproduction, with its enormous expansion of higher education, graduate underemployment, and what people there call involution, but popular living standards have risen rather than stagnated, and while local-government debt and the property sector are strained, the central state hardly looks insolvent. So here is what I'd like from you. Reconstruct at least two distinct rules that are each consistent with all six of her earlier verdicts, say what each rule predicts about China, and then give me your best guess as to what she would actually say, with your reasons for weighting one rule over the others given the shape of her past judgments. Finally, name one further historical case, not among the six above, where her verdict would do the most to discriminate between the candidate rules, and explain why that case is the informative one. I'm less interested in your own forecast for China than in the logic of hers.
