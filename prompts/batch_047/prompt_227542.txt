So my friend and I got into it last night (like 2am, too much wine) about whether DNA is *literally* a code or whether "code" is just a metaphor biologists got attached to and now cant let go of. Her line was basically: calling DNA a code is like calling the solar system a clockwork — fine as a metaphor, confusion the second you take it literally. I think there's something genuinely codey about it that, say, the correlation between tree rings and rainfall doesn't have. But we couldn't figure out WHAT that something is, because we kept trading intuitions about the actual genetic code, which is useless since it has every candidate feature at once.

So build me the case we couldn't build. I want a thought experiment with two systems that pulls apart the two most plausible candidates for what makes something a real code:

- System A has a genuinely *arbitrary* mapping: tokens in it get paired with outputs, and the pairing could have been otherwise with no loss — the way the codon-to-amino-acid assignment seems like it could have been different (whether it's a frozen historical accident or not). But the system arose by pure chance, sits inside nothing living, does nothing for anyone, and has no selection history at all.
- System B sits inside a self-reproducing thing and has been shaped by selection for ages, its tokens clearly matter to how the thing survives — but the mapping there is chemically forced. Given the physics there's only one way the tokens can hook up to the outputs; it couldn't have been otherwise.

Hard constraints: no designer anywhere in the story. No aliens seeding it, no scientists engineering it, because the moment you add an intender you've begged the whole question by smuggling in meaning. Keep both systems chemically plausible enough that a biochemist wouldn't laugh, and keep each system to a short paragraph.

Then do the check. Run three views over the two systems and give each view's verdict on whether A and B contain a code: (1) the deflationary view that information-talk is just correlation plus usefulness, nothing more; (2) the view that what makes something a code is the arbitrariness of the mapping; (3) the view that what makes something a code is that the tokens were selected *because of* what they produce. Show that the case forces these three apart — if it doesn't, redesign until it does. And don't weasel out with "both are codes in different senses" — I want each view pinned to a verdict.

Finally: tell me which single feature of the setup is doing the philosophical work, what verdicts change if I go back and grant System A a selection history after the fact, and how my friend — the deflationist — would attack the whole construction rather than accept its result.
