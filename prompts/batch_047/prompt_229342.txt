I am drafting a novel about a celebrated but manipulative painter, and at a recent writers’ retreat I showed two fellow novelists a scene from the middle of it. The scene is narrated by the painter’s young assistant, who gradually realizes that the painter has engineered her dependence on him: he isolates her from friends, rewrites her recollections of their conversations, and then persuades her that every sacrifice is proof of artistic devotion. The prose closely follows the assistant’s confused perceptions, so the reader initially experiences the painter’s tenderness and authority exactly as she does; only later does the same language reveal its cruelty. Both readers thought the scene was psychologically acute, formally inventive, and far more memorable than the overtly virtuous passages elsewhere in the manuscript. They nevertheless disagreed about whether its success made the novel better.

The first novelist argued that the scene was morally defective because it made manipulation seductive for too long. He did not claim that fiction must present abuse from an external or condemnatory viewpoint. His objection was that the scene carefully arranges rhythm, imagery, and suspense so that the reader desires the very fusion between painter and assistant that the novel later exposes as destructive. In his view, this was not merely a disturbing subject treated with artistic excellence; the work had trained the reader’s sympathies in a direction the novel knew to be corrupt. The second novelist replied that this condemned every convincing interior portrait of evil. She said that if the painter’s power did not first feel attractive, the later recognition would have no force, and a novel that simply announced from the beginning that he was predatory would be morally cleaner but aesthetically thinner. The first answered that she was confusing understanding with endorsement: fiction can explain charisma without making the reader want to prolong it. She answered that aesthetic experience often depends on wanting what one also judges to be bad, and that the tension is the achievement rather than a flaw. He then said that the scene’s brilliance made matters worse, since the more successfully it aestheticized coercion, the more it risked teaching readers to mistake intensity for intimacy. She replied that this objection proved too much, since it would also count against tragedies, war novels, and crime fiction that make ruin, violence, or transgression compelling.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point rather than giving only an overall verdict. Identify which claims are about the morality of the represented conduct, which are about the novel’s manner of representation, and which are about the value of the finished work as a whole. Where possible, say whether one speaker has shifted between different senses of good art, such as technical accomplishment, emotional power, moral insight, and all-things-considered worth. I am especially interested in whether making evil attractive can itself be an artistic success while also being a moral defect in the same passage, and whether the novel’s later correction of the reader’s sympathies changes the evaluation of the earlier seduction. If both novelists are overlooking a distinction that would let each retain part of their position, make that explicit.
