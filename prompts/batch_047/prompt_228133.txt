Okay so, I write novels for a living, and I also run a little fiction workshop at our community arts centre — about 10 adults, mixed experience, we meet for 90 minutes on Tuesday nights. Lately I've gotten obsessed with something and I want to build a whole session around it.

Here's the thing. Compare two sentences about the same event: "If the butler didn't steal the letters, then somebody else did" — totally natural, the kind of thing a detective says while pacing the drawing room. Now: "If the butler hadn't stolen the letters, somebody else would have." Suddenly it sounds unhinged, like the theft was fated to happen no matter what. Same people, same event, but one sentence is a sensible inference and the other is basically superstition. I gather philosophers have a whole literature on this, indicative vs. counterfactual conditionals — I only know the broad strokes. But it occured to me that this split is secretly my entire job: mystery plots run on the first kind of conditional (if he didn't do it, who did?), and character psychology runs on the second (if she'd left five minutes earlier she'd have caught the train, and none of this happens).

I want an activity that makes the group FEEL this difference in their gut before anyone names it. So, role assignment: you are a philosopher of language who also has real experience designing workshops for non-academics. Think carefully about this. First, brainstorm three or four possible activity designs — games, structured writing exercises, whatever — and briefly tell me why you're picking the one you pick over the others.

For the chosen design, give me:
- Concrete rules and materials (index cards, timers, whatever it needs), something runnable with zero prep beyond what I can buy at a stationery shop
- A rough timeline for the 90 minutes
- A debrief that maps specific moments of the activity onto the indicative/counterfactual distinction — what participants should have noticed themselves doing before I ever say the terminology
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover
- An honest note on which feature of the distinction the activity can't capture and I'll just have to explain

And because this is a fiction workshop, not a philosophy seminar: end by connecting it back to craft. What does this distinction mean for how a narrator earns a reader's trust, or why counterfactual-heavy passages make a story feel fated while indicative-heavy ones feel like a puzzle? That's the part my students will actually care about.
