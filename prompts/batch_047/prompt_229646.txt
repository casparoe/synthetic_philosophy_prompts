I'm a journalist, I cover the mind/behavior beat for a general-interest mag. Lately I keep tripping over the "self-deception" literature -- Trivers, the motivated reasoning experiments, the whole thing where people apparently hold beliefs they'd be better off not holding because it helps them bluff others or protect their ego. Fine, great copy.

But then I was reading up on the philosophy side and it seems like philosophers think self-deception is straight-up impossible. The argument as I understand it: to deceive someone you have to believe the thing is false, and to be deceived you have to believe it's true, so self-deception would require believing p and not-p at the same time, which no one can do. So the whole phenomenon is a logical contradiction in terms, like a married bachelor.

Which raises an awkward question for my beat: if self-deception can't even be coherently described, what are all these psych experiments actually measuring? Am I wrong to keep writing stories premised on the idea that people deceive themselves? Part of me wants to pitch a piece called "the entire self-deception literature rests on a category error" but I suspect that's too cute and I'm missing something.

So: does the philosophical argument actually have the consequence I think it does, or is there a way to cash out self-deception that survives it? Where would you say the tension between the philosophers' puzzle and the psychologists' data is real and where is it me conflating two different things? And be honest about the soft spots -- if you conclude the literature is fine, what finding or argument would make you reverse that, and if you conclude it's in trouble, what would rescue it? I'd rather know where the reasoning is most likely to break before I file anything.
