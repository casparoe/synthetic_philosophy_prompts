You are a philosopher of biology with expertise in astrobiology, and I would like you to think carefully through a full audit of the analogy below before delivering a verdict; in particular, do not skip the correspondence-mapping stage.

I sit on the editorial board of a science magazine, and one of our columnists has drafted a piece defending the analogy that life is like a fire. Her argument runs as follows. A flame is a self-maintaining chemical process that takes in fuel and oxygen, exports waste heat and combustion products, keeps itself going far from thermodynamic equilibrium, grows when conditions allow, spreads by seeding new flames at its edges, and dies when its throughput of matter and energy is interrupted. Organisms, she says, do exactly the same things at a slower burn, so the essence of life is metabolism — a throughput of matter and energy sustaining a stable pattern — rather than replication or heredity. She draws two conclusions from this. First, any adequate definition of life must be metabolic, so the standard candidate definitions that make the capacity for Darwinian evolution the criterion (and thereby classify viruses as non-living) are on the wrong track. Second, the flame analogy shows life to be a matter of degree rather than a binary property, so questions like whether a virus, a dormant spore, or a growing crystal is alive are not yes-or-no questions at all, and the search for a sharp boundary between the living and the non-living is misguided.

Please audit the analogy in three stages. First, before committing to any assessment, build the full correspondence table: what plays the role of fuel, of the flame front, of the organism's boundary, of growth, of reproduction, of death, and of heredity — marking any role for which the fire side has no candidate at all, since an empty cell in the table is itself a finding. Second, separate the disanalogies that matter for her two conclusions from the ones that do not; a failed correspondence only counts against her if it undermines the specific work the analogy is doing. In particular, consider whether the fire's lack of heredity, of a self-produced boundary, and of individuated lineages breaks the metabolic conclusion; whether the fire's manner of spreading is genuinely analogous to reproduction or merely to enlargement; and whether the analogy's treatment of identity and persistence — what makes this flame the same flame over time, and whether a lit candle's offspring flames are the same fire as their parent — supports or actually undercuts the life-is-a-matter-of-degree conclusion, given that we do ask of fires whether they are one fire or two. Third, deliver the verdict: which of her two conclusions, if either, survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if the analogy carries less than she thinks, propose a better one — for instance, a whirlpool or a standing wave rather than a flame — and either show that it does the work she needs or explain why no familiar physical process can.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — which of the two conclusions survives, if either — and then give the correspondence table and the staged reasoning. No preamble, and do not restate the question.
