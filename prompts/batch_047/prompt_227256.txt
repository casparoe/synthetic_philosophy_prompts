After forty years as an experimental physicist — most of them in statistical mechanics, where probabilities were tools we wielded rather than objects we believed in — I retired and began reading philosophy of probability. The philosophy society at my former university has now asked me to conduct a recorded interview with a philosopher whose recent book defends a Humean best-system account of objective chance. Her central claims, as I understand them: the objective chances are whatever probabilities figure in the systematization of the world's particular facts that best balances simplicity, strength, and fit; and the connection between chance and rational belief is captured by the Principal Principle — roughly, that if you know the objective chance of an event is p, and possess no information that bypasses the chances, your credence ought to be p. Crucially, she holds that this principle is not an external norm imposed on chance but partly constitutive of it: a magnitude that failed to constrain rational credence in this way simply would not be chance.

Draft eight to ten interview questions, each followed by the reply she is most likely to give and a follow-up keyed to that reply. Order the questions so the pressure builds, and append to each a single sentence explaining why it belongs at that point in the sequence. I want, at minimum, the following territory covered:

- An opening question that invites her to state the view's main attraction — that it explains why the deference scientists show toward measured chances is rational rather than merely habitual — with a follow-up that begins to probe.
- A question from my own discipline: statistical mechanics assigns probabilities within a microdynamics we regarded as deterministic, so either determinism is compatible with objective chance or the probabilities of my entire career were a fiction born of ignorance. Anticipate her likely reply (a deterministic world can still support probabilistic generalizations in its best system) and press it: could communities with different interests then have different best systems, and hence different chances?
- A question on whether the view makes chance objectionably mind-dependent: the half-life of a nucleus appears to be a fact about the nucleus, yet her account ties chance to what ideal believers ought to think.
- A question on the principle's escape clause concerning information that bypasses the chances. Put to her my scenario: suppose a complete written chronicle of every particular event, past and future, came into our hands tomorrow. Does objective chance survive the chronicle's publication, and can she characterize the clause without defining it as whatever information would otherwise break the principle?
- A question on a self-referential wrinkle I cannot stop turning over: if the best system assigns positive probability to histories on which the actual chances differ from those it assigns, the principle seems to demand deference toward chances it simultaneously tells you may not be the chances. Does her preferred repair resolve this or change the subject?
- A question on what enforces the ought: is the rationality of deference derived from the fact that chances track long-run frequencies, and if so, what warrants deference in the single case?
- A closing question about what would count against the view — what evidence or argument she would accept as showing that the link between chance and credence is contingent.

No questions about her career or influences. Refer to positions rather than texts; I shall supply any citations myself.
