Two friends of mine, Mara and Deniz, got into a long argument after a cosmology pocast brought up Boltzmann brains, and now they've dragged me in as referee. Quick background they both accept: in some cosmological models the universe settles into a cold, near-empty state that lasts literally forever, and every so often random thermal fluctuations assemble a whole brain, complete with memories of a life like mine, before it falls apart again. Over infinite time these fluctuation-brains vastly outnumber ordinary evolved observers like us. The fight was about what follows from that.

It went roughly like this:

1. Mara: any such model is refuted by observation. If it were true, I'd almost certainly be a fluctuation-brain whose next moment is chaos, but my experiences keep obeying physical laws, so the model is empirically dead.
2. Deniz: that's not how the prediction works. A fluctuation-brain would by construction have exactly your experiences up to now -- the podcast, the memories, everything. Your current evidence doesn't discriminate between ordinary-you and brain-you, so you can't refute the model with evidence it says brains would have to.
3. Mara: fine, grant that the past matches. The hypotheses still differ about the future: ordinary me keeps seeing order, brain-me dissolves into noise almost immediately. Every second of continued lawfulness is evidence against the brain hypothesis, and after enough seconds the brain-heavy model is as dead as any theory whose predictions keep failing. That's just conditionalizing.
4. Deniz: conditionalizing presupposes that your memory of the previous seconds is accurate, which is exactly the thing in doubt. If you're a fluctuation-brain, your memory of "years of continued order" is a false memory that came packaged with the fluctuation. The test you want to run can't be run by someone whose record of past results is unreliable. So the model isn't empirically refuted -- it's something worse: it tells you all your evidence, including your evidence for the model itself, is junk, so you can never trust the reasoning that got you there. It's self-undermining, and that's the real reason to reject brain-heavy cosmologies.
5. Mara: "self-undermining" proves too much. Suppose we eventually get solid independent evidence that the universe really does last forever in the right cold vacuum -- the fluctuation-brains then just follow from statistical mechanics we trust everywhere else. Your principle tells us to throw out well-confirmed physics because we dislike where it leads, which lets meta-philosophy trump physics. Better to bite the bullet: the actual mistake is assuming we're typical observers. Reject the typicality assumption, keep the cosmology.
6. Deniz: ditching typicality just to dodge the brains is ad hoc -- you keep the theory but gerrymander the rule connecting it to your expectations, and you'd never let anyone else get away with that. If a theory's numbers say you're almost certainly X, you don't get to just declare yourself atypical without an independent reason.
7. Mara: the independent reason is right in my hand: I can tell right now that my experiences are coherent, so I'm entitled to condition on being an observer with coherent experiences whatever the population stats say. That's not ad hoc, it's using all my evidence.
8. Deniz: if "whatever the population stats say" is doing that much work, you've made the cosmological question unfalsifiable in the wrong direction -- no abundance of brains could ever count against the model, since you'll always condition them away. A theory you can reconcile with any observer distribution isn't predicting anything anymore.

Please adjudicate. I want the verdict as exactly eight numbered items, one per move above, each a sentence or two saying who comes out ahead on that move and why -- except the last item, which should say who's more right overall (or whether it's a genuine stalemate) plus one thing both of them seem to be missing. No intro or conclusion, just the list.
