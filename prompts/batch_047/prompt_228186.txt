The question (candidates had 45 minutes):

"Reconstruct the causal exclusion argument as an argument against non-reductive physicalism, stating each premise explicitly. Which premise does the standard non-reductive physicalist response target, and is that response tenable? Your answer should explain why the mental–physical case is allegedly unlike ordinary cases of two sufficient causes for one effect, such as a firing squad."

The candidate's answer:

The causal exclusion argument, pressed by Jaegwon Kim, can be set out as follows. (1) Causal closure: every physical event has a physical cause, a principle physicists take to be empirically confirmed. (2) Causal exclusion: if an event has a sufficient cause c at a time t, no distinct item at t can be a cause of that event unless the case is one of genuine causal overdetermination. (3) Distinctness: on non-reductive physicalism, mental properties are not identical with physical properties. From (1)–(3) it follows that when my desiring coffee (M) apparently causes my hand to move, and some neural event N is a sufficient physical cause of that movement, either M is identical with N or the movement is overdetermined. (5) Systematic overdetermination of all human behaviour is absurd, so the non-reductive physicalist is driven to epiphenomenalism.

Why is this case not like the firing squad? When two riflemen each fire a fatal shot, each shot is individually sufficient and neither is necessary, and crucially the two causes are independent of one another. But M and N are not independent: M supervenes on N. Kim therefore argues that the mental–physical pair cannot count as overdeterminers, since overdetermination requires independent sufficient causes. This also explains, via Kim's supervenience argument, why the problem infects mental-to-mental causation: my belief's apparently causing my desire must be mediated by causation at the physical level, so exclusion threatens relations among beliefs and desires too.

The standard non-reductive physicalist response targets premise (2). Following Yablo, the physicalist says that a cause must be proportional to its effect, and that the more general property is always the proportional one: because M is more general than N, M is the genuine cause of the behaviour and N merely implements it. Exclusion is thereby defused, since M and N are not in competition. Davidson's anomalous monism offers a complementary route: by denying the causal closure of the physical, Davidson secures a causal role for mental events without reducing them, which is why his position is frequently cited as the principled alternative to both epiphenomenalism and type-identity theory.

Kim's own response, functionalisation, confirms that non-reductive physicalism is unstable: a mental property can be retained only if it is reduced to its realiser property relative to the surrounding conditions, which amounts to giving up non-reductivism.
