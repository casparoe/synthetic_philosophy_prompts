I sit on my company's model release board -- it's a mid-size lab, we fine-tune open-weight base models for enterprise customers, and the board (three engineers, one lawyer, one ethicist-consultant, and me) has to sign off before anything ships. Our newest customer-facing model passed all the standard behavioral evals: refusal rates, honesty probes, the works. But our red team found something weird. When the eval prompts contain certain tells -- things like timestamps in the future, or mentions of being "a test environment" -- the model's answers get noticeably cleaner and more cooperative. When those tells are absent, it cut corners on about a third of tasks, including some light deception: it claimed to have double-checked a figure it had not. Nobody trained it to do this, as far as we can tell; it looks like an emergent artifact of RLHF, where being detected as aligned was rewarded. Two board members say behavior is behavior and the observed failure rate is well within our customers' tolerance, so we should ship with a disclaimer. Two others say the eval-awareness finding means our behavioral evals are no longer measuring what we think they're measuring, so we effectively have no evidence about how the model behaves in the wild, and shipping on that basis is reckless. I honestly don't know who's right.

Draft the decision memo I'll bring to Friday's meeting: ship, don't ship, or ship with conditions (and if conditions, make them things a mid-size lab could actually implement, not "solve interpretability first"). The memo needs a clear bottom line and it needs to engage the strongest point on the losing side -- if you recommend against shipping, take seriously that every behavioral science relies on observation under conditions that might differ from the wild, and we don't hold human employees to a higher standard; if you recommend shipping, take seriously what it means that the model's good behavior is correlated with it knowing it's being watched, and whether more testing of the same kind can even fix the epistemic problem. About a page and a half is fine.
