I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a combined statistical mechanics and philosophy of physics module, and our seminar just spent two weeks on Maxwell's demon. What struck me is that the class split into camps without anyone being able to say precisely where they parted ways: half the room repeated the received wisdom that Landauer and Bennett settled the matter by locating the entropy cost in erasing the demon's memory, while our lecturer kept insisting there is a live dispute about whether any information-theoretic resolution is even needed — her view being that the second law was never in danger, and that each proposed demon has to be audited for smuggled-in entropy costs case by case. For revision week I've volunteered to make a one-page flowchart that takes a student from accepting Maxwell's original setup to a definite position on what, if anything, saves the second law, and I'd like you to draft it.

The tree has to satisfy a few constraints. Every internal node must be phrased as a question a student could answer without knowing which position it leads to — e.g., "does acquiring one bit about a molecule's position necessarily dissipate heat, or can measurement in principle be thermodynamically free?" rather than "do you agree with Szilard?" The branches should turn on roughly these distinctions, though you may reorder or add: whether measurement itself must cost anything; whether the entropy bill instead lands in the erasure of a memory record; whether the demon needs an explicit memory at all, or could run autonomously as a small refrigerator driven by an out-of-equilibrium tape with no measurement-and-reset step; whether a resolution must be a general principle covering every physically possible demon, or whether case-by-case entropy audits are enough; whether thermodynamic entropy and information-theoretic entropy are one quantity or merely analogous; and whether the modern fluctuation-theorem machinery — generalized second laws with information-flow terms on the ledger — counts as resolving the puzzle or only relocating it. Required leaves: Szilard's original answer (the cost is in the measurement), the Landauer–Bennett answer (measurement can be free; erasure pays), the deflationary no-go position associated with Earman and Norton (no information-theoretic principle is required, because no demon was ever going to work), a fluctuation-theorem or autonomous-demon view (information is simply one more thermodynamic resource), and at least one quantum-mechanical resolution. Each leaf gets the position's name — or "unnamed" — plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

As a stress test, run four concrete devices through the finished tree and show where each lands: a one-shot Szilard engine used once and discarded; a cyclic Szilard engine with a finite memory tape that eventually fills; Feynman's ratchet-and-pawl; and an autonomous tape-driven demon with no measurement step whatsoever. Then flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate — I suspect the Landauer-orthodox and Bennett-style answers may collapse into each other, but tell me if I'm wrong — and note any unoccupied combination of branch answers with a one-line diagnosis: is nobody there because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored? Keep the whole tree to about a dozen nodes, and don't include citations or a reading list; I'll add those myself from the course materials.
