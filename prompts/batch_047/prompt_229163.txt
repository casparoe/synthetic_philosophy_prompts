I write about law and ideas for a national magazine, and for several years I have covered a state supreme court whose right-to-bear-arms opinions lean heavily on William Blackstone's Commentaries on the Laws of England. In Book the First, in the chapter on the absolute rights of individuals, Blackstone lists the keeping of arms as the last of what he calls the auxiliary rights of the subject: a right to have arms for one's defense, proportioned to one's condition and degree, and extending only so far as the law allows. He describes it not as an unqualified individual entitlement but as a regulated public allowance of the natural right of self-preservation, operating where the ordinary sanctions of society and law prove insufficient. The court's majority cites this passage constantly, almost always as authority for a robust individual right, and I have begun to suspect the citation does more work than the passage can bear.

Here is the court's track record to date:

- It upheld a lifetime prohibition on firearm possession by persons convicted of violent felonies.
- It struck down a municipal ordinance forbidding possession of any operable handgun in the home.
- It upheld bans on carrying weapons inside courthouses, the statehouse, and polling places.
- It upheld a licensing regime requiring background checks and safety training, but invalidated the requirement that applicants demonstrate a special need to carry.
- It upheld a ban on machine guns and comparably destructive weapons.
- It struck down a county-wide prohibition on all public carry by licensed adults.

Now before the court is a statute forbidding the carrying of firearms on crowded public transit systems. The state defends the law entirely on modern crime and crowding data and concedes in its brief that no close Founding-era or nineteenth-century regulatory analog exists.

I would like your help preparing my analysis before the decision comes down. First, predict how this court will rule, given its own precedents rather than your view of the correct law. Second, identify which prior case is the closest analog and why. Third, say which governing rule best explains the full pattern — a historical-tradition test under which novel regulations lacking period analogs fail, a genuinely Blackstonean reading under which lawful regulation is internal to the right itself rather than an external limit on it, or a sliding scale centered on core self-defense — keeping in mind that the precedents appear consistent with more than one of these. Fourth, address the passage the court keeps invoking: does its self-description as Blackstonean survive its own track record, given that Blackstone built lawful restriction into the right's definition and subordinated the whole arrangement to the preservation of civil order? Finally, name the single hypothetical future case whose outcome would best discriminate among the candidate rules you have identified.
