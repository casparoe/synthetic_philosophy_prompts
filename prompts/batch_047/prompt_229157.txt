I help run a first-year undergrad discussion group (about 12 students, we get 75 minutes) and our next session is on population ethics. Instead of me explaining the nonidentity problem and watching everyone nod politely, I want an activity where they blunder into it themselves and only hear the name at the very end. Design me the full thing: setup, materials, sequence, and debrief.

Here's the rough shape I have in mind but please improve on it or replace it: something like a role-play where the group acts as the planning board of a newly founded settlement (or a colony ship, or a town council) and has to pick between two policies -- one clearly better on the usual measures, one with some built-in downside for a future generation. The catch, which they should NOT be told up front, is that whichever policy they pick determines which specific people end up existing (I'd want a concrete mechanism for this in the activity, like the policy choice determining which envelopes/cards of "future residents" get opened). Then later in the session a harm or complaint should surface from one of those future residents, and the group has to deliberate about whether anyone was wronged and whether compensation is owed -- at which point they should discover, from their own earlier choice, that the person complaining wouldn't exist at all under the other policy. I want the moment where someone says "but she's lucky to exist" and then goes quiet because of what that implies. Real-ish small stakes if you can work them in (points, snacks, whatever) so the choices feel like choices.

Then give me a debrief plan: map each beat of the activity onto the actual structure of the problem (the harmless-by-their-own-lights choice, the person-affecting intuition doing the pulling, where the "no one was wronged" conclusion starts to feel gamed), and tell me where students will predictably try to wriggle out -- e.g. treating the resident roster as fixed, sliding into rights talk, insisting the group "could have had both" -- and what it costs to hold each of those exits, so I can close the exits gently rather than by fiat. Also be honest about which part of the real problem the game can't capture and I'll just have to say in words afterward (I suspect it's something about existence across possible worlds vs. envelopes, but you tell me). No mini-lecture from me beforehand, and they shouldn't hear Parfit's name or the phrase nonidentity problem until the last ten minutes.
