I am a philosophy professor and serve, somewhat to my own surprise, as the faculty chair of my university's student conduct board. Following a rash of plagiarism cases this year, the dean of students has circulated a draft policy that would recast our entire sanctioning framework along the lines Gregg Caruso defends for criminal justice: a public health-quarantine approach under which sanctions are justified only as minimally restrictive incapacitation and rehabilitation, never as deserved suffering or censure. Under the draft, a repeat plagiarist would receive mandatory supervised study sessions and restricted course enrollment, explicitly framed as protective measures rather than punishment, and all language of desert, blame, and earning a penalty would be purged from our guidelines. At our last meeting, a colleague objected on broadly Dennettian grounds: she argued that a community which stops treating students as genuinely deserving of blame will erode the incentive structure that makes responsibility practices function at all, and that quarantine talk merely euphemizes what remains coercive confinement-like treatment while abandoning the only vocabulary in which accountability makes sense. The debate has split the board, and I have been asked to break the impasse in writing.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before our next meeting, roughly 800 words, recommending one of three courses: adopt the dean's draft, reject it, or adopt a specified modification. The recommendation must be concrete enough to act on -- name which sanctions and which specific bits of language survive under your proposal. Whatever you recommend, engage seriously with the strongest point on the losing side: if you side against the dean, explain why blame and deserved censure could be legitimate without presupposing libertarian free will, rather than simply asserting that people deserve what they get; if you side against my Dennettian colleague, explain why forward-looking incapacitation is not merely retribution in disguise when applied to a chronic plagiarist who, unlike a carrier of disease, poses no danger to anyone who takes reasonable care with their own citations. Finally, address a wrinkle specific to our setting: university sanctions are supposed to be educative, and both camps claim that mission for themselves -- Caruso-style measures as habit- and skill-building, desert-based censure as moral instruction in the norms of scholarship. Say which claim you find more plausible for our context and what follows for how sanctions ought to be described to the students subjected to them.
