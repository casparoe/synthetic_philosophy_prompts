I'm TAing an undergrad philosophy of language seminar this semester and its driving me a bit crazy how the students treat the difference between indicative and counterfactual conditionals as basically a verb-endings thing, so I want a dialogue I can hand out that shows the strongest version of each side before we get into the textbook. Write a dialogue (roughly 500-700 words) between two grad students, Priya and Marcus, arguing in a pub after the seminar. Priya defends the deflationary line: there's really just one conditional, and the indicative/counterfactual contrast is pragmatic -- a matter of which information gets held fixed when we evaluate the claim, not a difference in what the sentences literally say. Marcus holds that these are two genuinely different conditionals with different truth conditions, and that no purely pragmatic story can explain the felt contrast or the cases where one is assertable and the other plainly isn't.

Give them concrete material to fight over rather than abstractions. They should work through a murder-mystery pair with the structure of the old Oswald/Kennedy example but fresh dressing: an indicative like "If the butler didn't poison the count, someone else did" versus a counterfactual like "If the butler hadn't poisoned the count, someone else would have," where accepting the first but rejecting the second seems natural. They should also grapple with a card-game case where two observers with different information about the same hand can each felicitously assert an indicative conditional with apparently clashing conclusions, while the corresponding counterfactuals can't both be said -- Marcus will think this is grist for his mill or a problem for it, and I want to see which. Beyond those two set pieces, have them argue about at least two of: "might" counterfactuals (like "If he'd bet on red, he might have won" and whether that's compatible with "if he'd bet on red, he wouldn't have won"), whether indicative conditionals are even truth-apt at all or just have assertability conditions, sequences where adding more to the antecedent flips your verdict on the counterfactual, or counterfactuals whose antecedents are impossible.

Two ground rules, because my students are good at spotting bad-faith philosophy. First, no strawmen: each character must concede at least one specific point that genuinely costs their own view something, and they must respond to what the other actually said rather than delivering alternating mini-lectures. Second, no name-dropping -- neither character may mention any philosophers or named theories, since my students haven't met them yet and I want the arguments to stand on the cases. The dialogue shouldn't end in a tidy both-sides-have-a-point handshake, nor in one side conceding everything: end it with the two of them pinning down the single smallest sentence pair or scenario where their two views make different predictions, the thing they'd actually go and test against their own intuitions.
