A friend and I run a small philosophy pub night, and last month's session was on moral trade — the idea that people with conflicting moral aims can sometimes do deals that are mutually beneficial by each party's own lights, like a vegan and an anti-malaria donor agreeing to swap part of their donations because each rates the other's cause as better value than the swap they were making before, or two voters trading which referendum they vote yes on. The arguement went round in circles, mostly because my friend thinks moral trade is just complicity with extra steps — you can't launder something bad by agreeing to it in exchange for good elsewhere — while I think refusing to trade on principle leaves everyone worse off by everyone's own lights and is squeamishness dressed up as integrity. We agreed afterwards that we were probably talking past each other.

So draft the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between us would produce if we sat down for a few honest sessions. The two parties are the moral trader, who takes impartial welfarist aggregation seriously and treats moral disagreement as a resource to be exploited for mutual gain, and the clean-hands deontologist, who holds that knowingly facilitating wrongdoing is impermissible even when the deal produces more good overall by both parties' own lights. I want formulations neither of us would call a hatchet job: the claims we can both sign (not vacuous ones — things like which trades are clearly fine, which clearly aren't, and what the debate actually turns on), the residual disagreements stated precisely enough that each side would accept the wording of its own position, and the cruxes. My guess is one crux is whether complicity tracks what you intentionally facilitate versus what you merely tolerate for the sake of a deal, and another is whether there's a morally relevant difference between trading with a genuine wrongdoer and trading with someone whose values you simply don't share — but push back on my cruxes if you think they're misplaced. For each crux, give one concrete case (vote-swapping, paying a factory farm to retire its worst cages, hiring decisions, whatever does the work) that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's verdict on that case written down in advance.

Format it as exactly five numbered sections — (1) the agreed claims, (2) the residual disagreements, (3) the cruxes, (4) the test cases with both sides' predictions, and (5) a closing note on whether any part of the dispute turns out to be verbal — each a few sentences, with no preamble or sign-off.
