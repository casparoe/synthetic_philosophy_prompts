A widely read online encyclopedia article I've been using summarizes two-dimensional semantics like this: "Two-dimensional semantics assigns every expression two intensions instead of one. The primary intension of a term like 'water' is the description a competent speaker would use to identify it -- something like 'the clear, drinkable liquid found in rivers and taps' -- and it maps each possible world to whatever that description picks out at that world. The secondary intension fixes the term's actual reference first, and then maps each possible world to what the term picks out there, which is why 'water' rigidly designates H2O at every possible world. The framework's chief selling point is that it dissolves Kripke's puzzle about the necessary a posteriori: 'water is H2O' is necessary because its secondary intension is true at every world, and a posteriori because its primary intension is false at some worlds. Since the primary intension is what a speaker grasps in understanding a term, it simply is Fregean sense under a new name. The upshot is that two-dimensional semantics refutes Kripke's doctrine that necessity and apriority come apart: the appearance of necessary a posteriori truths is an artifact of conflating the two dimensions."
