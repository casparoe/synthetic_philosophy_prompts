My aunt is genuinly the best person I know — she's run the church-hall food pantry for twenty years (long after she stopped actually believing in any of it), drives elderly neighbors to appointments, remembers everyone's birthdays. At a family thing last week my cousin, who's very devout, told me gently but firmly that none of this "counts" — that good deeds without saving faith are worthless in God's eyes, and that my aunt's leaving the church basically cancels out everything she does.

A friend then pointed me to George Eliot's essay "Evangelical Teaching: Dr. Cumming," where (as I read it) Eliot goes after a popular preacher who claims to read off people's spiritual worth and eternal fate from whether they hold the correct doctrines. Eliot wants to flip the whole thing around: instead of judging people's conduct by their creed, you should judge a creed by the kind of character and conduct it produces — and a religion that teaches that a cruel believer outranks a loving unbeliever has, for her, convicted itself.

Here's my problem: I think Eliot's move is obviously right, but my cousin would say it just begs the question — if God exists and really did set the terms of salvation, then "judging the creed by its fruits" is just substituting my human moral sense for divine revelation, which is the one thing you can't do. Can Eliot's way of evaluating a religion from the outside actually work, or does the believer's framework make it impossible to get that argument off the ground? And practically speaking, is there any version of this I could say to my cousin that isn't just us talking past each other?
