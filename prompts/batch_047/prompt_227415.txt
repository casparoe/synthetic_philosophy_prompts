im putting together the final exam for my upper-level undergrad philosophy of mathematics course and i want one essay question on the epistemics of probabilistic heuristics in number theory. here's the terrain i want the question to force students into, so you know what it's testing:

Number theorists constantly justify conjectures by treating some arithmetic property as a random coin flip. E.g. the crude argument for twin primes: treat "n is prime" and "n+2 is prime" as independent events with probabilities around 1/log n each, and you get the right order of magnitude but the wrong constant — Hardy and Littlewood got the right constant precisely by noticing the independence assumption is false (n being odd forces n+2 to be odd, and similar congruence effects at every small prime) and correcting for it. But in other places independence-style heuristics fail catastrophically rather than just by a constant factor: the Mertens conjecture was heuristically plausible and numerically confirmed to enormous bounds, and it's false; same vibe with the li(x) vs pi(x) comparison, which flips eventually even though it looks settled for as far as you can compute.

So the question should make students say something general: when does a presumption-of-independence heuristic actually confer justification on a conjecture, and what does the difference between the twin-prime case (heuristic fixable) and the Mertens case (heuristic misleading) tell us? I explicitly do NOT want an answer that just recites Cramér's model or summarizes those two stories — the question should push them to articulate a criterion (something like: the heuristic justifies in proportion to whether the possible sources of dependence have been enumerated and excluded, and finite numerical evidence can't substitute for that because dependence can be oscillatory and deferred past any computable bound) and to apply it, maybe to a case of their own choosing. 90 minutes, 20 marks.

Deliverables:
1. The exam question itself, phrased the way i'd put it on the paper, including any setup the students need stated (assume they know the twin prime and Mertens stories from lecture).
2. A model answer of about 500 words, at the level of your best student.
3. A marking scheme as a markdown table with exactly these columns: | Point | Marks | What full marks requires | How candidates typically blow it | — five rows, one per must-hit point, marks summing to 20, and the last column should name the specific wrong move (e.g. treating numerical evidence up to 10^15 as strong confirmation, or saying the twin prime correction "proves" the heuristic method is sound).

Don't put any of this in citations or references, students wont need them.
