Two colleagues of mine have been arguing for months and I want you to draft the document their dispute should have produced. One is a committed longtermist of a broadly utilitarian bent: she holds that the astronomical number of possible future persons means that actions affecting the far future — extinction-risk reduction, shaping the values of advanced AI, and so on — dominate the moral calculus, and she accepts the "fanatical" conclusion that even tiny probabilities of affecting vast stakes ought to swamp near-term considerations. The other thinks that taking the simulation hypothesis seriously undermines exactly this fanaticism. His argument, roughly, is this: if there is a substantial probability that we are simulated, then our estimates of the number of reachable future minds, of the physical resources available to be turned into value, and of the causal levers available to us are all hostage to the simulators' purposes and compute budget; the expected-value calculation that licenses fanaticism therefore rests on treating one cosmological scenario (base reality, bountiful and manipulable) as near-certain when our own reasoning gives us reason to doubt it. She replies that the simulation hypothesis cuts both ways — a simulation large enough to contain us might contain an astronomical future too, simulators might reward or be indifferent to our longtermist efforts, and in any case "maybe none of it is real" is not an argument one can selectively deploy against far-future stakes while continuing to care about the simulated people in front of one.

Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between them. Specifically, I want:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims: what both accept about the simulation argument itself (what it establishes and what it does not), about expected-value reasoning under moral and empirical uncertainty, about the structure of the fanaticism worry, and about which considerations are common ground rather than contested.
2. A disagreements table giving the precise points of divergence, each row phrased so that each side would accept the wording of its own position. I suspect genuine cruxes include: whether simulation uncertainty is symmetrical (does it shrink expected far-future value, or merely redistribute it?), whether the longtermist's probability assignments over cosmological scenarios are doing illegitimate work, and whether discounting for "being simulated" is principled or is motivated reasoning against an unwelcome conclusion. But do not take my guesses as definitive — identify the cruxes yourself.
3. For each crux, what each party would accept as bearing on it — an argument, a thought experiment, or a formal result about decision-making under this kind of uncertainty — together with each side's prediction about how it would resolve.
4. A closing note on which parts of their dispute, if any, you conclude are verbal rather than substantive, and why.

Keep the statement neutral: neither side should come off as the winner, and formulations should be ones both could sign. Please write in British English.
