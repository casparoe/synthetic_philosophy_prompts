I work as a software engineer, mostly on systems that allocate scarce resources -- compute schedulers, inventory optimization, that kind of thing -- and I just finished Francis Spufford's Red Plenty, the 2010 semi-fictional account of the Soviet attempt, under Khrushchev, to run the economy on mathematics. A line I keep coming back to, from my own professional life: the dream Spufford's characters are chasing is essentially my day job. Kantorovich invents what we now call linear programming in the late 1930s, and the book's reformers imagine an economy steered by shadow prices computed on the new Soviet computers. If my team can schedule a datacenter with millions of competing demands, it is hard for me to feel, at a gut level, that an economy is a categorically different problem. The book made that gut feeling very uncomfortable, and I'd like help articulating exactly why.

So: read Red Plenty through the lens of the socialist calculation debate -- Mises's original claim that rational economic calculation is impossible without market prices for producer goods, Hayek's distinct argument that the relevant knowledge is dispersed, local, and partly tacit and therefore can't be centralized in principle, and Lange's trial-and-error market socialism, which the book's cyberneticists sometimes seem to be reinventing with better hardware. A few things I specifically want from the reading:

First, keep the three objections separate and locate the novel's failures on the right one. Much of what goes wrong for Spufford's planners looks political rather than epistemic -- the mathematics works, the scientists are brilliant, and the reforms are strangled by the party, by ministries defending their turf, by targets set for prestige. Does the novel therefore quietly side against Mises and Hayek, suggesting the calculation problem was solvable and only the incentive and power problem was fatal? Or is that a misreading -- is Spufford dramatizing the Hayekian point that the political pathologies are themselves consequences of trying to substitute commands for prices?

Second, does the book notice the distinction between static optimization and discovery? A solver finds the optimum of a problem someone has already specified. Spufford's planners can in principle compute the best allocation given known technologies and known demands, but the capitalist economies they are chasing keep generating new products, new processes, and new wants. Does the novel treat this as the deep problem -- and if so, which scenes or character arcs do the work?

Third, a question from my own bias: has the novel's verdict been overtaken? Modern retail and logistics firms run internal planned economies the size of small nations, with real-time data Kantorovich could only dream of. If a Hayekian reads Red Plenty as an elegy for a doomed idea, and a modern engineer reads it as a prototype that shipped fifty years too early, which reading does the text actually support?

Please don't flatten the novel into an illustration of an economics paper -- Spufford is plainly doing something more ambivalent, and the utopian longing in the book is rendered with real sympathy, as is the human cost. Where the novel resists the Hayekian reading, say so. And if you can, end with what the book shows about this debate that the journal articles don't: I suspect it is something about what it felt like to be an intelligent, rational person inside a rational dream that was failing, but I'd like that argued rather than asserted.
