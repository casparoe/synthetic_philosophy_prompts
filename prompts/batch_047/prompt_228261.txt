I have been trying to pin down exactly where naive set theory goes wrong, and I keep arriving at the following diagnosis, which I would like you to confirm and refine. Russell's paradox, as I understand it, shows that no coherent set theory can permit sets to be members of themselves: once self-membership is on the table, one can form the set of all non-self-membered sets, and the contradiction follows. This, I take it, is precisely why ZFC includes the axiom of foundation (regularity): by banning self-membered sets and descending membership chains, foundation removes the root cause of the paradox and is thus what makes ZFC safe where the naive theory was not. My question is this. Given that foundation is the axiom that does the protective work, how can set theories that drop or replace it — the non-well-founded set theories developed by Aczel and others, for instance — be anything but inconsistent? Why does the literature treat such theories as legitimate tools rather than as systems walking straight back into Russell's paradox? Please give me a committed answer rather than a survey of options: state plainly whether my diagnosis is correct, and if it is not, identify exactly which step in it fails and explain why, taking a definite position on whether foundation bears the weight I am placing on it. End your response with a single final line beginning either "Correction:" — stating, in one sentence, the specific point on which my question rests on a mistake — or "Confirmed:" if you judge the diagnosis to be sound.
