I have recently been appointed to an independent panel that reviews claims of medically inexplicable recoveries submitted to a religious shrine, and the work has forced me to confront, for the first time in a serious way, how I actually weigh testimony. Below are my verdicts on a set of cases, some drawn from the panel's docket and some hypothetical. I would like to know what principle, if any, captures the pattern, and whether it commits me to something like Hume's maxim on miracles or to something different.

1. Four physicians of unimpeachable record, working independently, attest that a patient's verified tumor disappeared overnight with no treatment; every imaging artifact has been ruled out. My verdict: accept that an unexplained remission occurred, but do not accept that a law of nature was violated.
2. A close friend, scrupulously honest and not devout, tells me he saw a statue in a church weep blood, at a distance of a few feet, in good light. No sample was preserved. My verdict: I do not believe it happened as described, and I feel no tension in continuing to trust him on every other matter.
3. Two hundred villagers attest that a local healer restored a blind man's sight. All are devoted followers of the healer; none have medical training; the man was never examined before or after. My verdict: disbelieve.
4. A single well-run laboratory reports a measurement that contradicts a very well-confirmed physical theory (particles apparently traveling faster than light). My verdict: instrument error, and I would hold this verdict with confidence even before any specific fault is located.
5. I accept, on the basis of a few partisan texts written decades after the fact, that a particular Roman general marched his army across a particular river. My verdict: accept, and I notice that the testimony here is thinner and more interested than in cases 2 and 3.
6. A stage illusionist performs an apparent impossibility before thousands of sober, educated witnesses, none of whom can explain it. My verdict: nobody in the audience believes nature was suspended, and neither would I, despite unanimous testimony and no available explanation.
7. If that same honest friend from case 2 told me he saw a black swan on the lake, a species I had thought absent from the region, I would believe him on his word alone.

The pair I find hardest to reconcile is 5 against 2 and 3: I accept commonplace ancient events on testimony that is weaker, in every respect I can name, than testimony I reject for marvels. I also suspect 1 and 2 stand or fall together in a way I cannot yet articulate, since I treat "unexplained" as a resting place in medicine but not in the church.

Please tell me: (a) whether there is one coherent principle governing the threshold at which testimony can overturn an established background belief, stated precisely enough that each clause can be tied to the case that motivates it; (b) what that principle predicts for cases I have not listed, including a miracle claim supported by synchronized smartphone video from a hundred strangers, and a sensor network rather than human witnesses; and (c) which of my seven verdicts the principle forces me to abandon or revise, if I refuse ad hoc clauses.

After your prose answer, give me a structured summary as a JSON object with these fields: "principle", an array of objects each with "clause" (a statement of one component of the principle) and "motivating_case" (the case number that forces that clause); "overturned_verdicts", an array of objects each with "case" (case number) and "reason"; "new_case_predictions", an array of objects each with "case" (a short description) and "predicted_verdict"; and "relation_to_hume", a string stating in at most three sentences whether the principle vindicates, modifies, or replaces Hume's position.
