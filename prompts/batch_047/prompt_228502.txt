For more than half a century philosophers and cognitive scientists have argued about whether the brain is a computer, and the debate seems to me to have stalled rather than progressed. The computationalists point to the predictive and explanatory success of information-processing models in neuroscience and to the apparent naturalness of describing neurons as encoding, transforming and transmitting signals. The critics reply that computation is too cheap to be explanatory: if a sufficiently ingenious mapping makes a wall or a thunderstorm implement any computation you like, then calling the brain a computer either says nothing or merely records a choice of modelling vocabulary rather than a discovery about what the brain is doing. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is really a disguised disagreement about the concept of computation itself — about whether being a computing system is an observer-independent property of a physical mechanism or always relative to a description — and that both sides quietly presuppose their preferred answer rather than argue for it. Is that the right diagnosis, or is something else doing the work: a hidden empirical question about what neural coding actually is, a difference about what a scientific explanation must deliver, or a purely verbal dispute about the word computer? Please give a diagnosis with reasons, engaging what each side actually argues, and say what would genuinely move the debate forward on your diagnosis — a conceptual distinction, a formal result about implementation, a class of experiments — or argue, if you think so, that no such resolution should be expected and why. Please answer in British English.
