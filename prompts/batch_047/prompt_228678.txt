I'm putting together the syllabus for a graduate seminar on rational choice, and I keep running into the same frustration with how textbooks handle Parfit's hitchhiker case and the surrounding cluster of puzzles about rational irrationality. The case, briefly: a man is stranded in the desert and will die without a ride; a driver stops who is an excellent judge of character, and who will take him to the city only if she is confident he will pay her on arrival; once safely in the city, he has every reason not to pay, and the driver knows this. Intuitively the best thing he could do is somehow make himself into someone who would pay -- but at the moment of payment, standard rationality seems to tell him to keep the money. Parfit used this and related cases to argue that being rational can sometimes require making oneself irrational, or at least that a theory of rationality can be indirectly self-defeating. I've discussed this with colleagues and we disagree about almost everything, including what a theory of rationality is even supposed to do with such cases.

Acting as a specialist in decision theory and practical reason, write the desiderata that an adequate theory of rational choice in the vicinity of this case must meet -- a specification I can then use to evaluate the main contenders (evidential and causal decision theory, resolute-choice views of the McClennen sort, sophisticated/dynamic-consistency views, and dispositions-based or rational-irrationality views of the Parfitian sort). Take your time and think carefully; before finalizing the list, brainstorm the tricky borderline cases a good specification must handle, then keep only what earns its place.

Please structure your response in the following sections, in this order:

1. Preliminaries and purpose (roughly 150 words): state what the theory is supposed to be adequate *for* (normative guidance for agents? prediction? a constitutive account of practical reason?), since the desiderata shift with the purpose.

2. The desiderata (the core of the response, roughly 600-800 words): each requirement with the case or consideration motivating it. Cover, at minimum: what the theory must say the hitchhiker should do and why; how it must handle the moment of payment (the decision the theory seemingly can't endorse even when it endorses setting up the situation); whether it must deliver verdicts for cases where being disposed to act irrationally is itself instrumentally valuable (e.g., threats, promises, toxin puzzles of the Kavka sort, deterrence); what it must say about self-effacing theories -- whether a true theory of rationality is allowed to recommend that agents not follow or even not know it; and which structural constraints (dynamic consistency, separability, independence of irrelevant alternatives) are genuine requirements versus camp-specific prejudices.

3. Negotiable versus non-negotiable (roughly 150 words): mark which desiderata you take to be hard constraints and which are desiderata only for particular camps.

4. Joint satisfiability (roughly 150-200 words): this is the part I care most about. If some subset of your desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied -- say, requiring both that rationality always guides action at the moment of choice and that it be instrumentally optimal across the hitchhiker case -- prove or argue for the incompatibility rather than merely gesturing at it.

5. Brief scorecard (roughly 200 words, no more): the main contenders against the list, one short paragraph each. Keep the weight on the criteria, not the scoring.

Avoid summarizing the literature at length; I know the standard positions and want the specification, not a survey.
