I am writing the case-study section of a term paper for a metaphysics seminar on verbal disputes and metaontology, and rather than reusing the stock examples (holes, tables and their arrangements of simples, statue-and-lump), I want to build the section around the reclassification of Pluto. The background, as I understand it: Pluto was identified as the ninth planet after Clyde Tombaugh's discovery in 1930; in 2005 astronomers announced Eris, a Kuiper Belt object comparable to Pluto in size, which made the status quo untenable; and in 2006 the International Astronomical Union, meeting in Prague, adopted a definition under which a planet must orbit the Sun, be massive enough for its own gravity to pull it into rough hydrostatic equilibrium, and have cleared the neighborhood around its orbit. Pluto fails the third condition and was reclassified as a dwarf planet. The decision drew sustained public outrage and open dissent from some planetary scientists, Alan Stern among them, who have defended a rival geophysical definition on which any gravitationally rounded body that is not a star counts as a planet, a definition that would promote the Moon and over a hundred other bodies to planetary status. Both camps, notably, agree on essentially every empirical fact about Pluto: its mass, orbit, geology, and neighborhood.

Please analyze this episode through the lens of the literature on verbal disputes and metaontology, and let the analysis run in both directions. On the side of the theory illuminating the case: is the dynamical-versus-geophysical dispute a merely verbal disagreement over the meaning of the word planet, and if so, does that make it idle — or is it better understood as a substantive question about which concept of planethood science ought to employ, a question of conceptual choice on which something genuinely turns even though no mind-independent fact does? Consider also what the third criterion is doing: does clearing the neighborhood smuggle a status judgment into what presents itself as a factual definition, and does it matter that the criterion is itself vague, since Earth shares its orbital zone with thousands of asteroids and has not cleared anything in a strict sense? On the side of the case testing the theory: the dispute was settled, after a fashion, by a vote of a professional body. Does that support a deflationary metaontology, on which questions about what exists in a domain are fixed by our linguistic conventions rather than by the world — or does the widespread sense that the vote left something unresolved cut the other way? Finally, address the metaontological question the episode raises most directly: is there a fact of the matter about how many planets the solar system contains, independent of our classificatory decisions? Say what a quantifier-variantist — someone who holds that existential questions have answers only relative to a choice of language — would say about that question, what a realist about natural kinds would say, and which of the two positions the episode, taken as a whole, fits better.
