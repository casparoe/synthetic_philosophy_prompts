This spring I found myself in a situation that sent me into the peer disagreement literature in earnest. I sit on my department's hiring committee, and after campus visits my two most trusted colleagues and I -- all of us with full access to the same files, the same interviews, the same job talks -- split cleanly on the top candidate. What rattled me was not the disagreement itself but that learning of it did not move my confidence at all, and I could not tell whether that made me reasonable or pigheaded. So I read my way into the conciliationism versus steadfastness debate, expecting it to settle the question for me. Instead I found what looks to me like a stalled dialectic: the conciliationists press the thought that another person's judgment is evidence you must weigh like any other, the steadfasters reply that conciliationism is self-defeating because its defenders do not split the difference with the steadfasters, the conciliationists answer that the view applies only to first-order questions or only after bracketing the dispute about the view itself, and around it goes. The same handful of toy cases -- the restaurant-check arithmetic, the pilots disagreeing about whether the runway is clear -- gets recycled, and as far as I can tell nobody has changed sides in years.

Here is my working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis rather than a conviction: the two camps are not actually disagreeing about what rationality requires of you when you learn a peer disagrees. They are disagreeing about what kind of question that is. The conciliationists seem to be answering a question about what the total evidence supports, treated from a third-person standpoint, where your own reasoning and your peer's are just two instruments with track records. The steadfasters seem to be answering a first-person question about what it is to actually believe something rather than to manage a portfolio of credences -- and from inside that standpoint, your own reasoning simply is what assessing evidence looks like, so it cannot be demoted to one instrument among others without ceasing to be yours. If that is right, the debate is stalled because each side keeps scoring points on the other's formulation of the question, and the self-defeat charge and the instrument analogy just talk past each other. A rival hypothesis I cannot rule out is that there is a buried empirical question doing the real work -- about how people in fact process higher-order evidence and whether first-order reasoning and peer testimony genuinely are separable channels -- and that the armchair standoff persists because neither side will admit the question is partly psychological.

What I want is a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement, not a verdict on who is right. Is the impasse best understood as two questions under one name, as a dispute about which cases should count as data (contrived symmetry cases versus the messy asymmetric disagreements, like my committee, that the view is supposed to govern), as bedrock intuitions about the nature of belief that admit no further argument, or as a substantive question with a hidden empirical or formal component? Whichever diagnosis you give, support it from what the two sides actually say in the standard moves -- the self-defeat objection, the independence bracketing maneuver, the track-record reasoning -- rather than from a survey of positions. And then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a distinction that dissolves it, an experiment or formal result that could in principle settle it, a reframing that lets both camps keep what they care about, or a reason to think no progress is available because the disagreement is, at bottom, about what epistemic rationality is for.
