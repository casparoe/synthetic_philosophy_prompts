I'm in my school's ethics club, and last week I presented an argument I built out of the classic bathtub cases. Everyone agreed each step looked reasonable, but the conclusion is so extreme that I must have gone wrong somewhere, and nobody could agree on where. Here it is. (1) If two courses of conduct share the same motive, the same outcome, and the same foresight, and differ only in whether the outcome results from acting or from refraining, then they are morally on a par. (2) The case of Smith, who drowns his young cousin in the bath for an inheritance, and the case of Jones, who intends to drown his cousin for an inheritance but finds the boy already slipping under and simply watches, ready to push if needed, differ only in that one acts and the other refrains, while motive, outcome, and foresight are identical. (3) So killing and letting die, considered just as such, are morally on a par. (4) If the bare distinction between doing and allowing carries no moral weight in itself, then whenever someone knowingly allows a death they could have prevented at trivial cost, their conduct is on a par with killing. (5) By not donating to effective charities, I knowingly allow deaths I could prevent at trivial cost to myself. (6) So my failure to donate is morally on a par with killing. (7) But by the same reasoning, every hour I spend on homework or hobbies instead of saving lives is on a par with murder, and since no one can spend every hour saving lives, morality demands the impossible. (8) Morality cannot demand the impossible. (9) So either my not donating is not wrong at all, or killing is not especially wrong either -- and both options are absurd.

What unsettles me is that the argument feels strongest exactly where it should feel weakest. I would very much like to keep (2)'s verdict about Smith and Jones -- those two really do seem equally monstrous -- and I would also like to keep the idea that I'm doing something wrong when I ignore easy chances to save lives. So I'd rather not solve this by declaring the doing/allowing distinction sacred, and I'd rather not solve it by declaring charitable giving entirely optional. But one of the steps has to go, or several do.

Please tell me which premise or inference I should reject, and be specific: if the flaw is a slide between two different claims that sound alike, name the two claims and show me which step trades on confusing them. If the argument is actually valid and I just have to swallow a premise, tell me which premise is cheapest to give up and what I lose by giving it up -- for instance, what a defender of (1) would have to say about Smith and Jones, or what someone who rejects (4) has to say about a person who watches a child drown in shallow water to avoid getting wet. I'd also like to know whether the step involving morality demanding the impossible does any real work, or whether the argument was already finished -- or already broken -- before I got there.
