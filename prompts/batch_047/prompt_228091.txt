I'm in an upper-level undergrad course on decision theory and multi-agent systems (cross-listed with the AI safety program here). For the final our prof lets study groups submit practice exams and says the best ones actually get used, so my group wants ours to be good.

One of the weirder topics this term was safe Pareto improvements in delegated game playing. Quick recap of how the course defined it, so the question matches our terminology: imagine each player in a one-shot game delegates their move to a program, all the programs follow the same default algorithm, and delegates can tell when they're up against another delegate of their own type. Switching off the default counts as a SAFE Pareto improvement if, no matter what the other delegates' default algorithm would have made them do, everyone ends up at least as well off as under default play and at least one player strictly better off. So both delegates switching to cooperate in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma is safe, since (C,C) beats (D,D) for both regardless of what the opponent's default would have done.

Here's my problem: every practice question my group has drafted is answerable by just reciting that definition plus the prisoner's dilemma example. I want one where you actually have to understand the machinery. The thing I keep noticing is people conflating "the outcome we'd switch to Pareto-dominates the default outcome" with "switching to it is safe" -- those come apart whenever there's uncertainty about what the other side's default would do, and I'm pretty sure that's the crux of the whole topic. There's also the bit where the target of a safe deviation doesn't have to be a Nash equilibrium at all, which breaks people's brains a little.

So: write me one exam question on safe Pareto improvements, answerable in about 45 minutes, that tests whether a student understands WHY safety is defined by quantifying over everything the other delegates might have defaulted to, rather than by checking that the target outcome is an equilibrium or that it beats whatever outcome actually happens. Ideally it makes them construct their own example(s) where Pareto-better-than-default and safe come apart, instead of just analyzing a game you hand them.
