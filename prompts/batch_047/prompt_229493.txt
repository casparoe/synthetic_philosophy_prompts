I'm a high school junior writing an ethics paper on what makes promises binding, and I've been polling a friend on cases to figure out the principle behind her judgments. Her verdicts so far: (1) a promise to return a borrowed book binds; (2) a promise made at gunpoint does not; (3) a promise to help a friend cheat on an exam does not bind even though it was made freely; (4) a promise to pick someone up at the airport binds, and more strongly than the book promise, because the person skipped making other arrangements on the strength of it; (5) a casual promise to wear a particular sweater tomorrow binds only weakly, since nobody's plans depend on it.

Here's the new case I can't predict her answer to: a teenager freely promised her grandfather, shortly before he died, that she would keep his small orchard running. Nobody relies on the orchard, it's a financial drain, and the grandfather obviously can't be disappointed now. Does the promise bind?

I'm less interested in the verdict itself than in the reasoning. Please give me a table with these columns: "Candidate rule" (a principle that generates all five of her verdicts), "How it fits verdicts 1–5", "Its verdict on the orchard promise", and "A further case that would test it against the other rules". I want at least two rules that genuinely diverge on the orchard case — for instance, one on which binding force comes from the promisee's reliance and one on which it comes from the promisor's free commitment — and then, after the table, a short paragraph saying which rule you'd bet on as hers, why, and what that implies about whether deathbed promises bind.
