Two classmates in my philosophy elective got into an argument after we read a short excerpt introducing the idea of the self as a center of narrative gravity -- roughly, the claim that what we call the self is not a thing in the brain but a fictional character the brain generates by spinning a story about who is doing and feeling everything, the way a physical object has a center of gravity that is a useful abstraction rather than a part you could point to. I stayed out of it, but I kept notes, and I'd like a verdict.

Mara's opening position: if the self is fictional, then it doesn't exist, full stop. Sherlock Holmes is a fictional character, and there is no Sherlock Holmes. So the view really says there is no me, which she found both obviously false (she was, she pointed out, sitting right there feeling annoyed) and destructive, because moral responsibility needs someone to be responsible, and a fictional character can't be punished or praised for anything.

Daniel replied that she was conflating two senses of "fictional." A center of gravity is an abstraction, not a physical object, but no physicist says centers of gravity don't exist -- calculations that ignore them fail. Likewise, the self could be real in the only way selves could be real: as a stable pattern that predicts and explains behavior. He added that her "I feel annoyed, therefore I exist" move begs the question, since the whole point of the view is to explain what that feeling of being a someone amounts to without positing an inner observer.

Mara's comeback: there's an asymmetry he's ignoring. A center of gravity is defined over physical parts that exist independently of the abstraction. But if the self is generated by the narrative, then the narrative exists prior to the self, and something has to be doing the narrating -- so either there's a real self underneath telling the story (which refutes the view) or the story tells itself, which she said is incoherent, since stories are essentially told to or by someone.

Daniel's counter: the brain does the narrating, and the brain isn't a self; asking "who is the story told to?" assumes a little person inside, which is exactly what the theory is trying to dissolve. But he conceded -- and this is where they got stuck -- that he couldn't explain why the narrative feels like anything to anyone without smuggling the self back in, and Mara conceded she couldn't say what her "real self" would be made of if not the story, just that it had to be there.

So: who is right about which specific points?
