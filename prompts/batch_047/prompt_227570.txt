I'm preparing reading materials for a graduate seminar on metaethics, and I want to get my own head straight before I foist anything on the students. Every treatment of the Frege–Geach problem I've read seems to presuppose a different sense of what the problem even is: some authors treat it as a problem about negation specifically, others as a problem about embedding generally, others as a problem about explaining why moral inference patterns like ordinary inference, and some as a problem about why we don't just "reason ourselves into contradiction" when we combine expressivist commitments across contexts. I no longer trust that these are one problem or that a solution to one transfers to the others.

Acting as a specialist in metaethics who is neutral between cognitivism and expressivism, write the desiderata that any adequate expressivist semantics for normative language must meet. I want the full specification, not a sales pitch for one family of solutions: the constructions the semantics must handle (negation, disjunction, conditionals with normative antecedents, normative predicates under attitude verbs and modals, mixed normative-descriptive sentences, quantification over normative claims), the inference patterns it must validate or explain away (including why modus ponens on normative premises doesn't commit an equivocation, and why inconsistency between normative judgments behaves like disagreement rather than mere difference of taste), and the data from ordinary practice it must accommodate (e.g., that we call moral claims true, believe them, retract them, and treat them as revisable in light of argument). For each desideratum, state the case or consideration that motivates it, and be explicit about which are genuine constraints that even a non-expressivist should accept and which are arguably cognitivist prejudices that an expressivist may legitimately refuse. If some subset of the desiderata looks jointly unsatisfiable on purely structural grounds -- that is, not because expressivists haven't been clever enough yet, but because the requirements pull against each other -- say so, because that's the part I care about most.

Then, briefly, assess the main families of response against the list: simple attitude-expressivism plus a logic of attitudes, Gibbard-style possible-worlds treatments in terms of ruling out combinations of plans or norms, "being for"-style approaches that make inconsistency primitive, hybrid views that give normative sentences both descriptive and expressive content, and minimalist or deflationary moves that try to earn truth-aptness cheaply. Before writing the final list, take a moment to brainstorm candidate desiderata freely, then prune and organize them so the final output distinguishes non-negotiable requirements from camp-specific preferences. I care much more about the quality of the criteria than the scorecard; I'll grade the contenders myself in seminar.
