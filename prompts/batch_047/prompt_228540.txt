I'm on my county's criminal justice reform commission and next month we vote on a proposal that's splitting us down the middle. The proposal would strip all the language about offenders "deserving" their sentences out of our sentencing framework and rebuild it on a public-health model: you lock someone up only for as long as they're a demonstrated danger to others, roughly the way you'd quarantine someone with a contagious disease, and the savings go into prevention and rehabilitation. The member pushing it argues that nobody has the kind of free will that would make anyone truly deserve punishment, so desert-based sentencing is just vengeance wearing a lab coat. The other half says that the moment you stop punishing people for what they did and start detaining them for what they might do, you lose any principled limit on how long you can hold someone, and you're halfway to locking people up for crimes they haven't commited yet.

Act as a professional philosopher who specializes in free will and the ethics of punishment, and think carefully -- I'd suggest brainstorming the strongest version of each side before you commit to anything. Then draft our commission a decision memo: recommend adopt, reject, or adopt with modifications, with reasons concrete enough that the losing side could actually respond to them. Two things the memo really needs to engage with: (a) whether defending desert actually requires the deep metaphysical kind of free will, or whether some more modest notion of responsibility can carry the weight, and (b) whether the quarantine-style model genuinely avoids the proportionality and dignity objections, or just shoves them somewhere less visible.
