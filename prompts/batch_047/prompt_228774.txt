I've been chewing on s-risks for a while -- roughly, risks of futures that contain astronomical amounts of suffering, on a scale that could make extinction look mild by comparison -- and I keep stress-testing my intuitions against each other. Here are four cases with my actual verdicts. What I want from you is the principle, if there is one, that all four verdicts are approximations of.

Case 1. A button painlessly ends all human life tomorrow. Pressing it forecloses a future where, two centuries from now, self-replicating probes have spread through the galaxy and the dominant form of sentience is engineered organisms living in unrelieved agony, trillions of them, for millions of years. Verdict: press it, easily. Extinction beats that.

Case 2. Same button, but the future it forecloses is one where trillions of beings live genuinely wonderful lives and, as an unavoidable side effect, a few billion live lives bad enough that they'd rather not have been born. Verdict: don't press. Some suffering, even a lot in absolute terms, doesn't outweigh a mostly-great future.

Case 3. Humanity can lock in 5% of world GDP for a decade to cut in half a 1-in-10,000 risk that we accidentally lock in a stable totalitarian system that eventually runs vast numbers of suffering digital minds. Verdict: obviously worth it. A small probability of an astronomically bad outcome deserves real sacrifice.

Case 4. Same setup, but the probability is 1 in 10^20, and the outcome at stake is proportionally worse -- suffering spread across the whole reachable cosmos, say 10^18 times as much of it as in Case 3. Verdict: not worth it. I just refuse to let a probability that tiny, however bad the stakes, run my decisions, even though I realize the expected-suffering math here is *bigger* than in Case 3, not smaller.

So: Cases 1 and 2 seem to need some threshold at which existence stops being worth it, and Cases 3 and 4 seem to need some threshold at which small probabilities stop mattering. But I can't see why either threshold would exist, and Case 4 in particular looks indefensible by the same lights that make Case 3 look obvious. If there's one principle (expected value with a cap? a lexical badness threshold plus probability discounting? something about what's 'stakeable' in a decision?) that delivers all four, lay it out precisely, clause by clause, and say which case motivates each clause. If no tolerably simple principle saves all four, tell me which verdict to surrender -- I care most about keeping 1 and 2 -- and what it costs. Then, whatever principle we land on, tell me what it predicts for two cases I haven't listed: (a) a 1-in-1,000 chance of a merely bad outcome, like a miserable century for Earth and nothing cosmic, and (b) a future where the extreme joy and the extreme suffering happen in the *same* minds rather than in separable populations, so theres no clean ledger of good lives vs bad lives.
