I'm a novelist at work on a book whose narrator is an inveterate liar, and in the course of researching how philosophers think about truth I fell down a rabbit hole and ended up hosting a small interview series for our local literary society. My next guest is a philosopher who defends a deflationary view of truth — roughly, the idea that saying a claim is true adds nothing beyond the claim itself, so that truth is a thin logical device rather than a deep property connecting words to the world. The literary society's audience is sharp but not specialist, and I confess my own sympathies run the other way: as someone who spends her days trying to make sentences answerable to the world, the notion that truth is metaphysically weightless sits badly with me, though I recognize that unease is not an argument. I don't want a conversation in which my guest simply restates the view; I want to see where it creaks.

I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher of language who knows the deflationist literature inside out — Ramsey-style redundancy views, disquotationalism, Horwich's minimalism, and the standard objections raised against them by correspondence sympathizers and by figures like Dummett. Think carefully, and before drafting anything, briefly brainstorm the four or five pressure points where a deflationist is most likely to be genuinely uncomfortable rather than merely rehearsed. Then write me a sequence of eight to ten interview questions, ordered so that each one builds on the last, covering at least the following: first, how the view handles generalizations about truth, such as my saying that everything in my grandfather's diary is true without being able to recite every entry — the worry that this seems to require an infinite conjunction rather than genuine understanding; second, the objection that truth functions as a norm of assertion and inquiry, something we aim at, and that a mere disquotational device can't play that role; third, the role truth seems to play in logical inference, where we need to say that a conclusion follows because the premises are true, not just assert the premises; and fourth, how the deflationist proposes to treat the liar paradox without smuggling in a substantive theory through the back door. Because I'm a novelist, I'd also like one question — but only one, and placed wherever it fits best — about truth in fiction, and whether the deflationist has anything illuminating to say about what it means for a sentence to be true of Anna Karenina. For every question, add the follow-up I'd want ready given the answer a sophisticated deflationist is most likely to give, and flag for me which of the questions you judge most likely to produce a genuine concession rather than a prepared reply.
