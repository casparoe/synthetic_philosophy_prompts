Hi! I'm a junior in high school and I'm doing my civics final project on the ethics of voting, and I keep getting stuck on something that seems wierd to me.

So in the US (and basically every democracy I've heard of) it's a serious crime to buy or sell a vote. If some billionaire offered me $500 to vote for his candidate and I happily took the deal, we'd both be in legal trouble, even though we're two consenting adults and nobody got hurt. Ok fine. But here's what confuses me:

- It's totally legal for the same billionaire to spend millions on ads, super PACs, lobbyists etc. to influence how I vote.
- It's completely legal for me to vote for purely selfish reasons, like picking whoever promises to cut my taxes even if I think it'd be bad for everyone else.
- My vote is secret, so even if I did sell it, nobody could ever check whether I held up my end of the deal. I read that this is actually part of why secret ballots were introduced in the first place -- to make bought votes unenforceable.
- Nobody has to vote at all. There's no legal duty.

In class we read a bit of John Stuart Mill's Considerations on Representative Government, where he argues (this is my paraphrase, not an exact quote lol) that your vote isn't your private property to do whatever you want with -- it's more like being on a jury, a duty you owe the public, so you're supposed to use it for the common good and not just for yourself. And apparently he even argued AGAINST secret ballots, on the grounds that if voting is a public duty then people should have to answer publicly for how they use it. Which is the opposite of what we actually do.

So my questions:

1. What does the ban on vote-selling actually presuppose, philosophically? Does it basically commit us to Mill's picture -- a vote is a trust or a duty, not property? Or could the ban make sense on other grounds too, like the rich would just buy elections, or some things shouldn't be for sale at all? Which of those does the practice actually NEED, and which just happen to fit it?

2. Are the pieces of the practice even consistent with each other? If the worry behind the ban is that money shouldn't decide elections, why is buying ads fine but buying votes a crime? And if the worry is Mill's one, that you're supposed to vote for the common good, then why is selfish voting perfectly legal and basically unenforceable? It kind of feels like the law presupposes one theory here and a different one there.

3. If the ban really does rest on Mill's trust/duty picture, isn't our secret ballot strange, since Mill thought that same picture implies public voting? Did we take half his theory and drop the other half, or can you coherently keep secret ballot + vote-selling ban while rejecting the publicity part?

4. What would have to change if we dumped Mill's picture entirely and said a vote is just a private choice you make for your own reasons, full stop? Would vote-selling have to become legal in principle, or are there arguments against it that don't lean on the duty idea at all?

One more thing: please be careful to separate what's basically settled (what the law actually is, what Mill actually argued -- and please tell me if I've got him wrong) from what's genuinely contested among philosophers who work on this, like whether there really is a duty to vote well or whether vote markets would actually be bad. I've noticed some summaries present one side as the obvious consensus and I don't want to put a contested take in my project as if everyone agrees on it.
