So I'm a PhD student in sociology, working on the memory studies side of things, and I'm trying to stress-test the framing of a dissertation chapter before I sink another six months into it. The case I keep coming back to is the Enola Gay controversy at the Smithsonian in 1994-95. Quick recap in case your memory of it is fuzzy: for the 50th anniversary of the Hiroshima bombing, the National Air and Space Museum planned an exhibit built around the restored fuselage of the Enola Gay, but the script also included material on the destruction on the ground, photographs of victims, and the historiographical debate over whether dropping the bomb was actually necessary to end the war. Veterans' organizations and a bunch of members of Congress attacked the script as revisionist and as treating the aircrew like war criminals, historians and curators defended it as standard practice, and in the end the museum cancelled the exhibit and replaced it with basically just the fuselage and a bare technical plaque. The museum's director resigned over it.

Here's what I want you to do: analyze this episode as a conflict between collective memory and disciplinary history, using the classic distinction (Halbwachs and the tradition after him) between memory as present-oriented, identity-constituting, and carried by living groups, versus history as an evidence-disciplined reconstruction that owes its allegiance to the record rather than to any community. Be disciplined about the mapping: say who exactly counts as the memory-bearing group here (and notice that there might be more than one), what the museum as an institution is supposed to be in this framework, and what "giving way" would even mean for an exhibit, which isn't a belief or a claim but a physical arrangement of objects and text.

Then run the analysis in both directions. First: which features of the episode does the memory-vs-history framework explain well? I'm thinking of things like why the fight intensified around an anniversary, why veterans objected specifically to victim photographs and casualty estimates rather than to the plane itself, and why "the men who flew the mission" functioned rhetorically as a trump card. Second: where does the framework miss or misdescribe things? A few candidate anomalies I'd want addressed: the historians weren't unified either; the state's involvement (Congress threatening funding) doesn't obviously fit a bottom-up model of group memory; and the final "solution" of displaying the artifact with almost no interpretation arguably satisfied neither memory nor history. Third: what does the episode show about the framework itself? Does it confirm the claim that commemorative pressure reliably beats historiography when the two collide in public institutions? Or does it show the dichotomy is too crude, e.g., because the museum was never a neutral arena where two things called memory and history met, but was itself constitutive of the national memory it supposedly just displayed?

End with a verdict: in this case, when the community's memory and the historians' record conflicted, which one should have given way, and does your answer generalize or is it hostage to specifics of this episode (e.g., that the memory-bearers were dwindling in number, that it was a federally funded museum, that the war in question is foundational to the national self-image)? Don't split the difference by default -- if you think the record should always win, or that memory has legitimate veto rights in some domains, defend it. Also flag anywhere the theory just doesn't have an answer rather than forcing it to pronounce.
