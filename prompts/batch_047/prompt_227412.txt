So I'm in this reading group on the quest for the historical Jesus, and we've hit a wall I want a diagnosis of. We spent weeks on the classic criteria of authenticity -- multiple attestation, dissimilarity, embarrassment, coherence -- the idea being that you can sift the gospels, find the bits the early church wouldn't have invented, and build a defensible picture of the actual man on top of those kernels. Then a few of us read the newer critics influenced by memory studies, and now the group is split in a way that feels different from normal scholarly disagreement. The critics say the criteria rest on a broken picture of how memory works: they treat the traditions like recordings with later churchy accretions you can strip off, when really every remembered saying is already an interpretation shaped by the community's present needs, so there's no uninterpreted kernel to recover. They also point out that dissimilarity, applied strictly, delivers a Jesus alien to both Judaism and the church, i.e. someone no real human could actually have been. The defenders reply that criteria were never meant as proof-machines, just probabilistic heuristics, and that giving them up means giving up on saying anything about Jesus at all, which surely goes too far. Meanwhile -- and this is the part that bugs me -- both sides have been making these same moves for over a decade now, and meanwhile the criteria-users among themselves can't agree either: same tools, same sources, and you get an apocalyptic prophet from one scholar and a countercultural wisdom teacher from another.

My question isn't which side is right. I want to know what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is there a genuinely empirical question buried here about how memory works that cognitive science could in principle settle, or are the memory people and the criteria people using the same psychology to draw different methodological morals? Is it a dispute about what "authentic" should even mean -- a saying Jesus uttered vs. a tradition that preserves his impact -- in which case it's partly verbal? Or is it a standoff between priors about how much confidence historical reasoning under these source conditions can ever justify, where nobody has a non-question-begging argument for their threshold? Give me your best diagnosis with actual reasons, and say what progress would even look like given that diagnosis -- a new method, a new distinction, a controlled comparison, or is this just what the evidential situation permits. And be straight with me about your own confidence: what's the strongest consideration on the other side, and what specific argument or finding, if someone produced it, would make you change your diagnosis?
