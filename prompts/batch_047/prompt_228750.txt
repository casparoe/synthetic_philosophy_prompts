I'm a philosophy professor (ethics and political philosophy mostly, but I've co-taught jurisprudence with the law faculty for years, so I'm properly fluent in private law -- torts, unjust enrichment, trusts, the lot). I want to stress-test the philosophical case for reparations for historical injustice -- slavery, colonial expropriation, that cluster -- by forcing it into the vocabulary of private law and seeing what falls out the bottom.

So here's what I'd like. Take the strongest version of the reparations argument as philosophers usually make it (backward-looking, grounded in the wrong itself rather than in present-day disadvantage, owed to descendants of victims by the descendants or institutional successors of perpetrators) and restate it as precisely as you can using only private law concepts: duty and breach, causation, standing, unjust enrichment and tracing, limitation periods, corporate personality and successor liability, the trust as a device for holding obligations across generations. Where you need a fictional mechanism to make a claim expressible, invent one explicitly and flag it as an invention -- e.g. something like "treat the state as a continuing legal person analogous to a company that's changed ownership three times" -- rather than quietly smuggling it in.

Then the part I actually care about: a residue report. Which claims translate cleanly (I'd guess unjust enrichment with tracing does a lot of work?), which only translate with distortion, and which have no private law counterpart at all. I'm particularly interested in what happens to (a) wrongs that were perfectly lawful when committed, (b) the idea that the obligation persists when both original victims and original wrongdoers are long dead, and (c) the expressive or acknowledgement function of reparations, which doesn't look anything like damages. For each bit of residue, I want your verdict: does private law's failure to express it show that private law is the wrong tool (or is missing a concept it ought to have), or does it show that the philosophical claim was trading on vagueness and the translation exercise has caught it out?

One more constraint: don't let the translation cheat by leaning on public law or criminal law notions -- no punishment, no constitutional duties. Private law only, otherwise the exercise is too easy.

Please write the answer in British English.
