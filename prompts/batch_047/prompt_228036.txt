In our pub philosophy group last week, Dan and Ola got into a proper argument over the old sea battle puzzle, and I've been asked to settle it even though I only half followed it. Here's the exchange as best I remember:

(1) Ola's opening move was the classic one: "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is neither true nor false right now. If it were already true, then tomorrow would be fixed in advance and we're stuck with fatalism; so bivalence must fail for claims about contingent future events, end of story.

(2) Dan said this just moves the bump under the rug. Come tomorrow evening, the sentence will be true (say the battle happens) — but then "this sentence will be true in 24 hours" was true today all along, so the future was as fixed as ever and she's gained exactly nothing.

(3) Ola said that conflates two things. A sentence becoming true when the event happens is completely unspooky — what makes it true is the battle itself, and the battle is still open today. The fatalist argument needs the truth to be settled in advance, and that's the bit she's denying.

(4) Dan came back with: fine, but then she's committed to saying the sentence's being uttered today doesn't fix anything even if it turns out true, which sounds like the old Ockhamist trick of calling tomorrow's truth a "soft fact" about today — and nobody, in his view, has ever stated the soft/hard fact distinction cleanly, so she'd have to defend it before it can rescue her.

(5) Ola said she doesn't need any of that machinery, and here's her new idea: predictions are like bets. A bet isn't true or false while the race is still running — it's on, and later it gets settled. The mistake is treating a prediction as a standing claim that already has a truth value from the moment it's uttered. Really a prediction is a commitment that waits on the world, so the fatalist argument never gets going because there's no truth-bearer of the kind it needs.

(6) Dan's last shot: that's a bait-and-switch. Whatever we say about the speech act, there's still the plain proposition that there will be a sea battle tomorrow, sitting there independent of anyone's utterance — and the fatalist argument runs on that, not on her bets.

Can you go through it move by move and say who's right at each step, and why? And also who comes out ahead overall, and what you think both of them are missing.
