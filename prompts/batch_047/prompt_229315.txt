In my political theory seminar we just finished The Prince, and the session on Machiavelli's treatment of fortune stuck with me. His picture, as I understood it, runs roughly like this: fortune is like a flooding river that sweeps everything away when nobody has built dikes and channels in calmer weather; it governs perhaps half of human affairs while leaving the other half to us; and — this is the part I keep turning over — men prosper when their characteristic way of acting suits the temper of the times, so that an impetuous ruler thrives while circumstances reward impetuosity and is ruined when the times change and he cannot change his own nature. On that view, what fortune rewards is not any one style of action but the fit between style and circumstance, and since people are mostly incapable of switching styles, success and failure both look somewhat luck-driven. Meanwhile, my roommate, with whom I run a tiny side business, has lately taken to deploying the line 'fortune favors the bold' whenever I urge caution — most recently to justify staking most of our savings on an aggressive expansion — and when I push back he has started claiming Machiavelli as an ally, since, as he puts it, Machiavelli says fortune sides with the impetuous.

When I press him, the slogan seems to slide between several different claims without his noticing. Sometimes he means something statistical: bold moves have high variance, so boldness buys you exposure to extreme upside that caution forgoes. Sometimes he means something causal and social: acting decisively itself changes how investors, competitors, and employees behave, so fortune 'rewards' boldness because boldness partially manufactures its own luck. Sometimes he seems to mean something almost normative, that boldness is the sort of thing that deserves to be rewarded. And once, when a bet of his paid off, he used it in a way that struck me as vacuous — 'fortune' just being the name for whatever happens to bold people who succeed. What I would like is a careful disambiguation. Lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses 'fortune favors the bold' could be expressing — separating at least the probabilistic reading, the causal reading, the desert-based reading, and any empty or survivorship-skewed readings I haven't thought of — and for each one tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or simply false, and what kind of evidence would even bear on it.

Then the harder question: which, if any, of these readings does Machiavelli's actual discussion in that chapter support? My own suspicion is that his position is close to the opposite of the slogan taken unconditionally — he seems to say that the cautious prosper in cautious times and the impetuous in impetuous ones, and that most ruin comes from the inability to adapt — so that what he praises is not boldness but a kind of flexibility or fit, and the famous river metaphor points at preparedness rather than daring. But my roommate insists the chapter's ending, where fortune is described as letting itself be mastered by the forceful rather than the cold and cautious, vindicates him. Tell me which reading of the slogan my roommate's argument for the expansion actually needs in order to go through, whether that reading is one Machiavelli would accept, and whether the disagreement between us is ultimately about the slogan, about the text, or about our business.
