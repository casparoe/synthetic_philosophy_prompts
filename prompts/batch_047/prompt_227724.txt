I teach a third-year module on decision theory, and every year the same thing happens: I put Pascal's mugging on the board (the mugger who promises, in exchange for your wallet, to use powers you can't verify to save an astronomically huge number of lives) and the seminar splits into five camps who then procede to talk past each other for an hour because they're all describing their discomfort differently. So for next term I want a proper instrument: a "which response to fanaticism do you actually hold?" quiz that sorts people by what they'd do, not by which label they've happened to hear of.

Spec:

- Eight to ten questions. Each must be a concrete scenario with a decision in it, never "do you think utility is bounded?". Work in at least: a mugger variant where the tiny probability is stated but unverifiable, a public-spending variant (cheap programme, minuscule chance of averting catastrophe, real money diverted from things that definitely help), a St Petersburg-flavoured gamble, one case with a genuinely infinite payoff rather than a merely enormous one, and one case where the agent can't even get a grip on how small the probability is.
- Two to four answer options per question, phrased as actions or gut verdicts, not as theoretical commitments.
- A scoring key mapping answer patterns onto at least these positions: strict expected utility maximisation (the pay-the-mugger fanatics), de minimis views (small enough chances get treated as zero), bounded utility, lexical thresholds (sufficiently improbable outcomes can't outweigh solid certain goods), risk-weighted expected utility (you treat probabilities non-linearly, so tiny chances of huge prizes get downweighted relative to their expectation), and refusal to assign credences at all under deep uncertainty. Flag any pair your quiz can't tell apart — I have a feeling bounded utility and steeply-diminishing marginal value only come apart on very strange cases, so either find the strange case or own up to it.
- Build it from the logical space, not from a list of names: first the diagnostic questions and the answer-combinations they allow, then locate the actual positions in that space, then note which combinations nobody occupies and diagnose briefly why not (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored?).
- One design constraint I really care about: students constantly conflate discounting the probability of the payoff with discounting the credibility of the person making the offer. I want at least one question pair that seperates the two and catches them at it.
- Audience is bright undergrads with one term of decision theory behind them. Whole thing, key included, must fit on two sides of A4.

Don't just reproduce whatever taxonomy is floating around online — the questions are the product here. And write the whole thing in British English, please.
