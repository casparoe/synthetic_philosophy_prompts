I cover science and medicine for a general readership, and I'm writing a long piece about where medicine draws the line between treatment and enhancement. In reporting it I've run into a conceptual problem I can't solve, and I'd like your help reconstructing the philosophical commitments underneath a set of practices that otherwise seem unremarkable.

Here are the practices. Health insurers and national health systems will pay for growth hormone in a child whose pituitary gland fails, but generally not for a child who is simply at the bottom of the normal height distribution; they pay for breast reconstruction after mastectomy but not for cosmetic augmentation; they pay for stimulants given to a child diagnosed with ADHD but not, officially, for the same stimulants taken by a healthy student before exams. Drug regulators, meanwhile, approve medicines for indications, meaning for named diseases, and the entire apparatus of coding and reimbursement assumes that every legitimate medical act answers to a diagnosis. In psychiatry, the diagnostic manual adds a further clause: a cluster of traits counts as a disorder only if it produces clinically significant distress or impairment in the person's life, so that unusual but harmless and undistressing traits are explicitly excluded.

What puzzles me is that the classic scientific statement about disease seems to deny that any such line can be drawn in nature. In An Introduction to the Study of Experimental Medicine, Claude Bernard argues that health and disease are not two essentially different conditions or rival principles struggling over the body; he holds that disease involves the very same physiological mechanisms as health, differing from it only in degree and circumstance, so that medicine has no scientific warrant for treating the pathological as a separate order of reality. Bernard's continuity thesis is usually read as a founding document of a naturalistic, value-free conception of disease. But the coverage practices I describe appear to need a robust distinction between the normal and the pathological, and the psychiatric clause appears to build a judgment about what harms a person or spoils her life directly into the definition of disorder, which looks normative through and through.

So here is what I'd like. First, reconstruct the implicit theory: what conception of health and disease does the therapy/enhancement boundary, as actually administered, presuppose? Does it presuppose a naturalistic benchmark like statistically normal species functioning, or a normative judgment about which lives go badly, or some hybrid? Second, be precise about the strength of the commitment: which features of the practice strictly require a particular view, and which are merely consistent with several rival views, so that the practice is philosophically underdetermined? In particular, could a thoroughgoing Bernard-style naturalist run the same insurance system, and could a thoroughgoing normativist? Third, tell me whether the combined practice is coherent: is there a tension between a reimbursement system that wants a value-free, diagnosis-driven criterion of medical necessity and a psychiatric manual that writes distress and impairment into the definition of disorder itself? Finally, say concretely what would have to change in these practices if one dropped the presupposed view: what would coverage decisions look like under a pure continuity thesis of the Bernard sort, and what would they look like under a fully normative account on which a condition is a disease just in case it is bad for the person who has it?
