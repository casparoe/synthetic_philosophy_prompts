I'm drafting a short position statement for a graduate epistemology reading group and want it audited for internal consistency before I circulate it. Here are my commitments:

(1) Rational outright belief is closed under known conjunction: if it is rational for me to believe p and rational for me to believe q, and I notice the connection, then it is rational for me to believe p and q together.

(2) Fallibilism: rational belief does not require certainty; I can rationally believe propositions while acknowledging, in general terms, that I might be wrong about them.

(3) The preface verdict: the historian who has carefully checked every claim in her book is rational in believing each individual claim and also rational in believing, on general grounds of her own fallibility, that at least one of them is false.

(4) The consistency norm: it is irrational knowingly to hold a set of beliefs that cannot all be true together.

(5) The Lockean thesis: sufficiently high credence short of certainty — say, anything above 0.99 — suffices for rational outright belief.

(6) The lottery verdict: in a fair lottery with a million tickets, it is not rational to believe outright, of any particular ticket, that it will lose, no matter how high the probability that it will.

Do these six hang together? I have a nagging sense that some pair or triple of them is doing incompatible work, but I haven't managed to locate the problem, and I don't want to abandon any particular commitment unless forced. Rank the tensions you find by severity rather than treating everything as equally bad, distinguish genuine conflicts from merely apparent ones, and identify the least costly repair. Format: exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion — the genuine tensions in descending order of severity, then any apparent tension that dissolves on inspection, and finally the single revision that restores the most consistency at the lowest price, with a statement of what that price is.
