Working on a short paper for a seminar on blame and I've been told to pin down where I actually stand before I write it. Here's my position, roughly:

1. You lose your standing to blame someone when you're a hypocrite about that very norm -- a tax cheat can't legitimately call out tax cheating. But it's norm-specific: being a tax cheat doesn't cost you standing to condemn littering or lying. Moral imperfection in general can't disqualify people from blaming, or nobody could blame anyone.
2. The reason hypocrisy costs you standing is that if you don't accept the norm as binding on you, your blaming others for violating it is insincere. You don't actually stand behind the norm, so you can't properly invoke it against someone else.
3. Concrete case: a lifelong heavy smoker who genuinely believes smoking is terrible, is ashamed of the habit, has tried to quit a dozen times and keeps failing, still lacks standing to lecture their teenage kid for starting. Practice what you preach.
4. That said, the hypocrite's criticism can still be completely accurate and the blamed person can still have every reason to change. Losing standing doesn't make the content of the blame false, and it's a mistake to dismiss a criticism just because of who said it.
5. Blame isn't just a private judgment -- it's an address, something you do to someone, that makes a claim on them. That's part of why the standing question even arises.

A few of these feel like they might grind against each other but I can't tell which ones actually do and which just look like they do. Audit the set: which pairs are genuinely in tension, rank them by how bad the problem is, and for each one tell me what I'd have to give up to fix it. Also flag any apparent tensions that dissolve once you're careful. Don't just tell me "it's complicated" -- I want to know which commitment is the weak link if one has to go.
