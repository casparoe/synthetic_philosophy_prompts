Longtime lurker here, posting this because it came up in a thread on priors and I want to sanity-check my reading. I recently reread Hume's essay 'Of Miracles' (from the Enquiry) and his argument as I understand it goes roughly: a miracle is a violation of a law of nature, laws of nature are established by uniform experience, so the evidence for the law will always outweigh any testimony, meaning testimony can never make it rational to believe a miracle report.

Neat result, but it seems to prove too much? On that logic nobody could ever rationally accept eyewitness reports of something genuinely unprecendented — the first meteorite falls, say, or radioactivity — because prior experience would always count against the report. But clearly we can and did accept such things, and were right to.

So my question: roughly how many independent eyewitnesses would Hume's argument actually demand before a rational person should accept, e.g., a resurrection? Presumably there's some threshold of witnesses where the likelihood ratio swamps even a very low prior and the balance tips. And is Hume's maxim just an informal anticipation of Bayesian updating with extreme priors — i.e., something like Cromwell's rule applied to testimony — or is there anything in it that doesn't translate into the Bayesian frame? If it's the former, is the only real disagreement between Hume and the believer a disagreement about the size of the prior?
