I'm preparing the final exam for a graduate seminar on AI safety and the philosophy of machine learning. I need one essay question on low-impact AI that cannot be answered by summarizing the standard impact-measure proposals (relative reachability, attainable utility preservation, and their cousins). The question should force candidates to engage with what I take to be the real crux: every impact measure is a deviation-from-a-baseline measure, so it has two free parameters -- a baseline state and a metric on deviations -- and every concrete choice of those parameters either smuggles in a partial specification of value (which is what the approach was supposed to avoid) or produces verdicts that any reasonable value function would reject.

To make the question concrete and un-Googleable, please build it around the following scenario, which candidates must reason about directly rather than by citing the literature: a warehouse robot is tasked with stacking crates in Bay A. A meteor will strike Bay B in one hour regardless of anything the robot does, destroying an identical, unoccupied warehouse wing. The robot has a forklift maneuver that would deflect the meteor but crush a child's bicycle left on the floor, and a second maneuver that would restore the bicycle by repainting it with paint that happens to be carcinogenic. Candidates should be asked: (a) which of these actions count as "high-impact" under a state-deviation measure with the natural baseline, and under a measure whose baseline is the inaction rollout; (b) whether the meteor case shows that some baselines classify *not* acting as the high-impact option, and whether that is a defect of the measure or a correct verdict; (c) whether the repaint maneuver exposes a general "offsetting" problem -- that an agent can cancel measured impact by performing a second, independently harmful action -- and whether the fix (penalize each action's impact separately rather than net deviation) merely relocates the problem, since impact so defined is not additive and the decomposition into separate actions is itself a choice; (d) what all of this implies for the hope that "avoid side effects" can be defined with substantially less than a full specification of human value.

Deliverables: the exam question as it would appear on the paper (phrased as a single prompt the candidate reads, answerable in 75 minutes); a model answer of at most 700 words; and a marking scheme of exactly five must-hit points totaling 25 marks, where each point names the specific move a top answer must make (for instance: recognizing that the meteor baseline makes inaction "impactful" and stating what that reveals, rather than just describing it), the marks it is worth, and a one-line note on how candidates typically get that point wrong. The marking scheme should make explicit what separates an answer that merely defines an impact measure correctly from one that understands why the two free parameters are a problem. Keep the entire response under 1,200 words.
