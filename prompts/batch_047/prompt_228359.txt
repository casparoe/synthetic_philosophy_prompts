For a comparative politics seminar I'm taking, I've been arguing with a classmate about whether Sweden or Norway is the more liberal society, and we've found the standard democracy and freedom indices oddly unhelpful: both countries cluster near the top, the differences are within the margin of aggregation, and the indices seem to flatten exactly the distinctions we care about. My classmate insists Norway is plainly more liberal; I suspect the answer depends almost entirely on what one means by liberal, and that the Nordic model scrambles the usual intuitions, since both countries combine remarkably high personal autonomy with remarkably muscular states -- punitive drug enforcement traditions, state alcohol monopolies, high marginal taxes, extensive data collection by trusted bureaucracies -- alongside, in recent years, conspicuous divergences such as Sweden's comparatively hands-off pandemic response and Norway's more visible public debate about decriminalizing drug possession.

To force the question to be tractable, consider two rival operationalizations of liberalism, which I'd like you to treat as live competitors rather than a false dichotomy. Under what you might call permissive liberalism, a society is liberal insofar as it refrains from prohibiting, surveilling, and punishing: the state gets out of the way of speech, worship, intoxication, sexuality, association, and exit. Under enabling liberalism, a society is liberal insofar as it equips individuals to actually author their own lives: the state subsidizes childcare, education, healthcare, and geographic and occupational mobility, accepting taxation and regulation as the price of real rather than formal freedom. By the first metric, Nordic paternalism looks illiberal; by the second, it looks like liberalism fulfilled.

Here is what I'd like from you. First, state your credence -- a number -- that a careful, honest analyst applying whichever operationalization you yourself would defend would, as of today, judge Norway the more liberal country overall. Then decompose it: your probability that permissive rather than enabling liberalism is the better operationalization (or your weighting of the two); your dimension-by-dimension judgments (speech and association, religion and the church-state settlement, drugs and lifestyle regulation, immigration and integration, surveillance, family and education policy, economic liberty), each with a rough probability that Norway leads Sweden on that dimension; and how these terms combine into your headline number. Finally, tell me which single sub-question your final credence is most sensitive to, what piece of evidence or institutional change would move it most, and -- since my classmate and I want to actually settle this rather than talk past each other -- what a decisive resolution procedure would even look like: is there a dataset, natural experiment, or foreseeable policy divergence that would force one of us to concede?
