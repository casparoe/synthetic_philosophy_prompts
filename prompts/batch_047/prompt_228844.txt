So I've been a pretty committed eliminativist about beliefs and desires for a couple of years now -- the Churchland-style version, where folk psychology is a theory, and a bad one, and its posits go the way of phlogiston and the crystal spheres once neuroscience matures. Churchland's 1981 paper on eliminative materialism and the propositional attitudes is basically my bible here, especially the parts where he argues that folk psychology hasn't changed in thousands of years, explains almost nothing new, and sits totally disconnected from every other science -- compared to, say, how biology got absorbed into chemistry. He says that kind of stagnation and isolation is what failure looks like for a theory, not venerability.

What I want to know is: what would count AGAINST this view? I keep asking people this and getting lame answers, so let me be specific about what I'm after. I want the evidence and arguments that should actually move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be, and I want them separated into at least these buckets:

- Defenders' own concession conditions. What have the Churchlands themselves (or Dennett-style critics, or Ramsey/Stich/Garfield types) said would count as folk psychology being vindicated? E.g. if neuroscience ends up finding discrete, content-individuated states that map tolerably onto belief/desire, is that a win for folk psychology or a reductive success story I can still claim? Where exactly is the line between "folk psychology got reduced" and "folk psychology got eliminated," and is my view actually risking anything on that line, or have I quietly set it up so that any neuroscientific outcome counts as confirming me?

- Things that arguably already happened and that I might be cheating about. Cognitive science still uses belief/desire attribution constantly and it seems to work; developmental psych and the whole theory-of-mind literature treat it as a real capacity that develops and can break (autism, etc.); LLMs, frankly, seem to make the intentional stance pay off in a weird new way. Do these count as predictive successes of folk psychology that a genuinely failing theory shouldn't be capable of, or are they the equivalents of caloric theory's decent thermodynamic predictions -- real but destined to be re-explained away? I want someone to actually take a position on which analogy is right rather than just gesturing.

- The self-refutation argument. The Broad-style complaint (and Stich wrestled with this too) that the eliminativist can't even state the view without believing things. I have a canned answer (you can assert the sentence without positing belief-states; theories don't have to be stated in their own vocabulary) but I want to know if my canned answer actually works or just changes the subject.

- Empirical defeaters that nobody's produced yet but could. What neuroscientific or computational result, if it came in tomorrow, would make me say "ok, beliefs exist"? Be concrete -- "we find the belief-box in the brain" is not an answer, but something like convergent evidence that propositional content does real causal-explanatory work in a mature theory might be.

And then the honest meta-question: has eliminativism been qualified into unfalsifiability? It seems to me like every apparent failure (no neural correlates of specific beliefs found, content looks irreducibly holistic and context-sensitive, folk psychology keeps not being replaced) gets absorbed as "mature neuroscience just isn't here yet." If the view can only be confirmed by a future science that doesn't exist, and can never be disconfirmed by the science we have, is that a problem -- or is that just what it looks like to bet on a research program early? Feel free to tell me the view is well-insulated and that this is fine, or that it's well-insulated and that's damning, but tell me which.

If it helps: what I would personally find most persuasive is either (a) a real case where folk-psychological explanation does explanatory work that no future neuroscience could absorb, or (b) a convincing demonstration that the stagnation argument proves too much -- i.e., it would have eliminated some theory we now rightly accept.
