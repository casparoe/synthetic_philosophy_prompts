I run a small community-garden nonprofit in a mid-sized American city, and next month I have ten minutes at a city council hearing to argue that our municipal mosquito program should stop truck-based fogging with broad-spectrum adulticides and rely instead on larvicide and source reduction, which kill far fewer non-target insects. I have no academic training, but over the past year of reading on my own I've become persuaded by a style of reasoning built on scale: even if the odds that any individual insect experiences something bad when fogged are quite low, the number of insects affected is so enormous that the expected amount of suffering could still dwarf what we'd casually accept elsewhere. I did my own back-of-envelope math for our metro area and came up with a rule of thumb I've been calling my "crossover rule": if you grant even a one-in-five-hundred chance that insects undergo something genuinely aversive when killed by insecticide, then the expected suffering per fogged mile exceeds what the county would ever tolerate inflicting on, say, the pets and songbirds in those same neighborhoods for the sake of a marginally more pleasant summer evening. That's the core of my pitch: not that insects definitely suffer, but that the precautionary arithmetic favors the low-kill methods even under heavy discounting.

The audience I'm facing is specific, and I want to be prepared for their actual vocabulary rather than philosophy-seminar vocabulary: two council members who mostly care about cost and complaints, the county extension entomologist who I suspect thinks the whole framing is sentimental nonsense, a public-health official who will (fairly) bring up West Nile virus, and a handful of residents worried about their backyards. Please prepare me a prep sheet, in American English, covering the following: the objections I am most likely to get, ordered by how likely and how damaging each is, phrased the way each of these people would actually phrase it; which objections have real force that I should concede up front rather than fight (I assume the disease-risk point and the uncertainty in my kill estimates are on that list); a reply to each objection that I can deliver in under ninety seconds, with a note on where each reply is weakest; and the question I am privately dreading, which is some version of: if your whole argument is multiplying a huge number by a tiny probability, what stops the same trick from making mowing a lawn or driving at night a moral catastrophe -- and for that matter, doesn't your own arithmetic sometimes favor killing insects quickly rather than leaving them to whatever nature does to them? If my crossover rule has an obvious hole, I'd rather hear it from you than from the entomologist.
