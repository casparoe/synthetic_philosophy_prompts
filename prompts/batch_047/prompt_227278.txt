**The Escalator: a spectrum argument and an attempted escape**

1. Start with a familiar setup. Life A contains one extraordinarily intense moment of joy -- say, a single afternoon of complete, overwhelming happiness -- and is otherwise ordinary. Life B is just like A except the peak afternoon is very slightly less intense, and in exchange B contains one additional day of mild contentment. Intuitively, B is at least as good as A; arguably better. Now iterate: Life C trades a further sliver of peak intensity for another mild day, and so on, down a long sequence. At the end we reach Life Z: no peak at all, just an unbroken flatline of mild contentment, every day the same, nothing ever rising above "fine." Yet Life Z seems clearly *worse* than Life A. A whole life with one transcendent afternoon beats a life of permanent mildness. But if each step in the sequence is an improvement or at least a tie, transitivity forces Z ≥ A. Contradiction. Something has to give: either transitivity of "at least as good as," or one of the steps, or our judgment about the endpoints.

2. The standard escapes, as I understand them: (a) deny transitivity and accept that betterness can cycle or break (this seems to be Temkin's line in his work on intransitivity); (b) insist there is a threshold somewhere -- some step where the trade is genuinely a loss -- even if we can't say which; (c) go vague: adjacent pairs are neither determinately better nor determinately worse, so the chain never gets going; (d) attack the endpoints and bite the bullet that Z really is at least as good as A.

3. Here's what I take to be the problem with (c), and this is the first place I want checked. If betterness is vague along the spectrum, then somewhere between A and Z there must be a region where "x is at least as good as y" is indeterminate. But now compare the two ends of that indeterminate region: they differ only by a handful of tiny steps, so by the original intuition they too should be roughly on a par. The indeterminacy has to *accumulate* smoothly from "determinately comparable" to "determinately ordered the other way," and there is no principled place for that accumulation to happen either -- it's a sorites about determinacy itself, not just about betterness. So vagueness doesn't dissolve the paradox; it relocates it one level up. Conclusion: escape (c) fails, and anyone who wants it owes us an account of higher-order vagueness in comparative value, which seems even more mysterious than first-order vagueness.

4. Now my own wrinkle. Run the spectrum in two dimensions. Dimension one, as before: peak intensity traded against number of mild days. Dimension two: total length. Consider not a line of lives but a grid, where from any life you can move right (slightly less peak, one more mild day) or down (everything the same, but the whole life is repeated -- every experience happens twice). Moving right always seems at least as good. Moving down also seems at least as good -- surely duplicating a good life doesn't make it worse; that would be a bizarre kind of anti-natalism about one's own past. But now consider paths across the grid. Go all the way right, then all the way down: you get a very long flatline life, duplicated. Go all the way down, then all the way right: a duplicated A, eroded into flatness. Both endpoints are reached from A by steps each of which seems fine, and both seem clearly worse than A -- but worse, by tweaking the ratios I think you can construct *cycles*: grid lives G1, G2, G3 where each is arguably better than the next and the last is arguably better than the first, with no single step you can point to as the weak link. If that's right, the problem isn't just that transitivity forces one unwelcome conclusion; it's that "better than" over lives is actively cyclic once you have two independent dimensions of trade-off, and threshold views (b) become hopeless because there is no consistent place to put all the thresholds at once.

5. My tentative conclusion: "all things considered better than" really is intransitive over lives, and that's fine, because the concept's job is to guide *choice*, and choice only ever requires pairwise rankings over small menus. Cycles only bite when someone -- a god, a benevolent planner, a population ethicist -- tries to rank the whole space of lives at once, and maybe the lesson is that no such global ranking exists rather than that our local judgments are defective. In short: intransitivity isn't a paradox about value, it's a proof that global welfarism is false.
