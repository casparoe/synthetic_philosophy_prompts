I am setting the final exam for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the historiography of the First World War, and I need help designing the central essay question. Last year I asked a straightforward question — was Germany primarily responsible for the outbreak of the war? — and received forty nearly identical answers: a dutiful summary of the Fischer thesis, a paragraph on its conservative critics, a gesture toward Christopher Clark's The Sleepwalkers, done. Almost no one said what kind of claim a responsibility-attribution even is, and that is the failure I want this year's question to make impossible. Students in the seminar have studied Fischer's Griff nach der Weltmacht and the controversy it provoked, the July Crisis and the 'blank cheque' to Austria-Hungary, the war-guilt clause of the Versailles settlement, and the more recent diffusionist historiography, so the question can assume that material — but it must be unanswerable by reciting it.

Here is the device I want the question built around. Ask students to suppose that in 2030, in a newly opened archive, historians discover a memorandum circulated among the German leadership in late July 1914, authenticated beyond dispute, in which the drafters estimate that honouring the commitment to Austria-Hungary carries roughly a 40 per cent chance of escalating into a continent-wide war, observe that Germany's relative military position will have improved within a decade, and recommend proceeding on the ground that the present diplomatic alignment is unlikely to recur. The exam question should ask students to assess what this discovery would and would not change about the responsibility debate.

Concretely, please deliver: (i) the exam question itself, phrased as it would appear on the paper, for a three-hour exam in which this essay is worth 60 of 100 marks; (ii) a model answer of roughly 1,000–1,200 words; and (iii) a marking scheme detailed enough to be used by a teaching assistant who has not taken the course. The model answer must show that the memorandum bears differently on three claims that students and historians routinely conflate under the heading of 'war guilt': a causal claim about whose actions were necessary conditions of the war's outbreak; a counterfactual claim about whose choices, if removed, would have averted or postponed it; and a moral or quasi-legal claim about who acted culpably given what they knew, believed, and intended. It should also press on two harder points: whether knowingly running a 40 per cent risk of general war is better described as intending war or as gambling on peace, and what hangs on that distinction; and whether a finding about a handful of decision-makers can ground a judgment about 'Germany' at all, or whether talk of national responsibility is an inheritance from the Versailles settlement that the Fischer controversy never properly examined. The marking scheme should allocate the 60 marks across these conceptual moves rather than across coverage, name the specific errors that should cost marks (treating the memorandum as settling the debate by itself; treating mobilization timetables as self-standing proof of aggressive intent; answering a causal question when the question asked a moral one), and state explicitly what distinguishes an answer in the top band from a merely competent one.
