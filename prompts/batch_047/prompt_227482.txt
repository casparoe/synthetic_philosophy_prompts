I chair the five-member credentialing board of a regional hospital, and I need to decide how to run a vote at next week's meeting. The case concerns a surgeon applying for operating privileges at our facility. Our bylaws state that privileges may be granted only if the board finds both that the applicant's technical record meets our standards and that the applicant has performed a sufficient volume of comparable procedures within the last three years. The bylaws specify that decisions are made by majority vote, but they are silent on how the vote is to be structured.

We discussed the application at length last week, and every member's individual view is settled:

- On the technical record: Members One, Two, and Three find it satisfactory; Members Four and Five do not.
- On case volume: Members One, Four, and Five find it sufficient; Members Two and Three do not.

So Member One alone believes both conditions are met. Each of the other four believes the application fails on at least one condition, though they disagree about which.

This leaves me with two defensible ways to run the vote, and they produce opposite outcomes. If I put each condition to a separate vote, both pass three-to-two, and under the bylaws privileges must then be granted -- even though four of the five members individually judge that the application should be denied. If instead I put the single question "shall privileges be granted?" to a direct vote, it fails one-to-four -- even though majorities of the board have endorsed each of the two conditions the bylaws require.

Two further complications. First, Member Four argues that under the first procedure the board would be endorsing an outright contradiction: we would be on record as finding that every required condition is satisfied, while a large majority of us holds that the application fails. Member Two replies that the second procedure is the problematic one, since it lets a minority veto what the majority has affirmed on every question actually put to it. Second, whatever we decide, the board must issue a short written rationale, and the applicant can appeal to the hospital administration on the ground that our decision was arbitrary. I am unsure what rationale we could honestly issue under either procedure, since no single line of reasoning commands a majority in support of either outcome.

My questions: Which procedure should I adopt, and why? Can either outcome properly be described as what "the board" believes or finds, and if not, what exactly is the board saying when it votes? Each member's individual set of judgments is perfectly consistent, so if the collective result looks inconsistent, where does the inconsistency actually reside -- in the members, in the procedure, or in the idea that the board is the kind of thing that can hold a view at all? Does the requirement of a written rationale, or the prospect of an appeal for arbitrariness, tip the balance toward one procedure? And should I propose amending the bylaws -- for instance, to require a single up-or-down vote with stated reasons, or a supermajority -- or is the underlying problem one that no voting rule can fix? Finally, does any of this turn on the fact that the decision affects a person's livelihood and patients' safety, rather than being, say, an abstract question of policy?
