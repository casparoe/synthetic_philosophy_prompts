At a dinner with two old colleagues from my physics program, I watched them fall into a surprisingly heated dispute about a pair of hypothetical devices, and they eventually turned to me to settle it. I couldn't, so I'm asking you.

The setup: Imagine two objects. (a) A delicate glass vase is permanently encased in a transparent polymer shell engineered so that if the vase is struck, dropped, or shaken, the shell absorbs and distributes the force, and the glass inside never breaks. (b) A copper wire is connected to a "guardian" device that monitors it continuously and, the instant any current begins to flow, sends a pulse that temporarily alters the wire's structure so that it stops conducting -- and restores it afterward.

Round one. Priya argued that neither object retains its disposition. Being fragile, she said, just means it would break if struck; being conductive just means it would conduct if a voltage were applied. For the encased vase and the guarded wire, those conditionals are simply false, and the two cases are perfectly symmetric, so any verdict must treat them alike.

Round two. Marcus replied that this proves too much. The vase is obviously still fragile -- its molecular structure is completely unchanged, and nobody who knew its composition would hesitate to insure it as fragile goods. If a theory of dispositions forces us to call it non-fragile, so much the worse for the theory; and by parity, the wire is still conductive, since the copper itself is untouched.

Round three. Priya conceded the vase but held her ground on the wire. The difference, she said, is that the guardian is wired into the same circuit as the copper; you cannot apply a voltage to "the wire alone" without the guardian acting, so the conductive structure is, in the only sense that matters, absent. The shell, by contrast, leaves the vase's own response intact -- it just prevents the response from culminating in breakage.

Round four. Marcus answered that this makes dispositions unacceptably extrinsic: on her view, whether a wire is conductive depends on a gadget sitting across the room, and if I unplugged the guardian for an afternoon the wire would gain and lose conductivity without anything happening to it. He insisted the right line is the one he drew in round two: both objects keep their dispositions, because in both cases the intrinsic basis is undisturbed, and only the route from stimulus to manifestation is interfered with.

Round five. Priya closed by saying his round-two argument had quietly shifted: he first defended the vase by appeal to structure, but then lumped the wire in with it, when in fact the guardian case is one where the wire's very response is removed at the moment of stimulus, not merely blocked from producing its usual upshot -- so the symmetry he needs fails in his favor nowhere.

Please adjudicate. Assess each move -- is Priya's opening symmetry claim sound, does Marcus's structural argument establish what he thinks it does, does Priya's round-three distinction withstand Marcus's round-four charge of extrinsicality, and is her final diagnosis of the asymmetry between finks and masks correct? Conclude with an overall verdict: who is right about what, who is more right in total, and whether there is a commitment (for instance, about what disposition ascriptions are really claims about) that both parties share and that is doing the damage.
