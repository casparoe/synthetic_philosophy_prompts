I'm a retired chemist (spectroscopy, forty years of it) and since hanging up my lab coat I've been going to a philosophy reading group at the community college. Right now we're on Bayesian confirmation theory, and we've hit the so-called problem of old evidence: if some piece of evidence was already known before the theory came along, then your probability for it is 1, and conditionalizing on it can't budge your probability for the theory. So on the standard picture, old evidence can't confirm anything, which seems crazy to me.

When I objected, the guy who runs the group waved it off with an analogy. A theory that accomodates old data, he said, is like a tailor-made suit: of course it fits, it was cut to fit, so the fit proves nothing. What would impress you is an off-the-rack suit fitting — that's your novel prediction. He also threw in that leaning on old evidence is like counting the same dollar twice. And he used all this to draw two conclusions: first, that the Bayesian result isn't a bug in the framework but actually tracks good scientific practice, and second, that when I said that back in my lab we routinely took a model to be supported by spectra we already had sitting in the drawer, that was at best weak tea, confirmation-wise.

Something nags at me though. A tailored suit is cut to the customer's measurements, but nobody cut our models to fit those old spectra — the spectra were just there, and the model still had to match them or be thrown out. And I have my own analogy from the bench: testing a new model against old data is like calibrating an instrument against a known standard, which is obviously worthwhile, we did it all the time.

So: how far does the tailor analogy actually hold? Give me the correspondence table — who is the tailor, what are the measurements, what's the fit, who's the customer, what plays the role of the off-the-rack suit — and then lay out where the mapping breaks. Seperate the disanalogies that matter for his two conclusions from the ones that are just noise. Does his conclusion that old evidence confirms weakly or not at all survive once the disanalogies are on the table? Then do the same audit on my calibration analogy — does it do better, or is it smuggling in its own assumptions (for instance, calibration tells you about the instrument, not about the world — does that matter here)? And if neither analogy survives, tell me what a better one would look like, or whether this whole debate is just a place where analogies can't carry the weight.
