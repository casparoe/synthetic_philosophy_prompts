So there's this move that comes up constantly in identity threads -- someone says the self is an illusion, and to back it up they reach for King Milinda's chariot. Basically: a person is like a chariot, and "chariot" is just a label we slap on a pile of parts, so there's no extra thing the word refers to. I've seen it used to argue for full-on Parfit stuff -- teletransportation is fine, identity isn't what matters, death is no big deal, the whole package.

The actual passage from The Questions of King Milinda (Rhys Davids translation) that everyone is gesturing at goes like this, king speaking to Nagasena after getting cross-examined about whether the pole or the axle or the wheels is the chariot:

"I have spoken no untruth, reverend Sir. It is on account of its having all these things--the pole, and the axle, the wheels, and the framework, the ropes, the yoke, the spokes, and the goad--that it comes under the generally understood term, the designation in common use, of 'chariot.'"

Here's my question: how far does this analogy actually hold up? Give me the explicit mapping -- what plays the role of the parts, the label, the king, Nagasena, the guy who needs to get somewhere in the chariot -- and then where it breaks. Some stuff I'm suspicious about: chariots are built for a purpose and don't care about anything, so of course nothing turns on whether "chariot" picks out something real, whereas with persons there's supposedly something at stake (prudential concern, responsibility, fear of death). Also, notice the king's answer kind of wins the exchange -- he does say what the chariot is. Nagasena taunted him with "there is no such thing as a chariot" and then accepted the conventional-designation answer. So does the passage support "chariots don't exist" or "chariots exist as conventions," and does that difference do any work when you port it to persons?

At the end tell me whether the Parfit-style conclusions people bolt onto this actually survive the audit, or whether the chariot was carrying them the whole time.
