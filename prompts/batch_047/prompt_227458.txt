I have recently taken over as the moderator of a mid-sized online history discussion community, and I find myself having to make judgment calls about which fringe historical claims get a hearing and which get removed. I would like to stop adjudicating case by case and instead figure out what principle, if any, my actual judgments add up to. Here are my verdicts so far, given honestly:

Plato's Atlantis as the record of a real Bronze Age civilization: no, that is not history. The ancient astronaut hypothesis for the pyramids: no. Fomenko's new chronology, on which centuries of the Middle Ages were fabricated and dynasties are duplicates of one another: no, and I feel no obligation to read the books first. Graham Hancock's lost ice-age civilization that supposedly taught agriculture to the survivors: no. The Bosnian pyramids: no.

But then: the old consensus that the Clovis people were the first Americans was overturned by Monte Verde, and I regard that revision as entirely legitimate science even though the excavators were dismissed by the establishment for years. Pre-Columbian contact between Polynesia and South America, on the strength of the sweet potato and some contested genetic evidence, strikes me as a live hypothesis worth taking seriously despite being a minority position. And I have to admit that both Troy and the Norse settlement at L'Anse aux Meadows were fringe claims until they weren't, which makes me nervous about the confidence of my "no"s above.

My tentative principle has been: defer to the credentialed consensus unless the challenger produces extraordinary physical evidence. But I can see it does not capture my verdicts. It would have ruled against Monte Verde for most of its history, and it gives me no principled way to take the sweet potato contact seriously while dismissing Hancock, since both currently lack the smoking gun.

So here is what I want from you. State the most precise principle (or small set of candidate principles) that captures as many of my verdicts as possible, and for each clause of it tell me which case motivates it. Be honest about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and tell me which one you think I should give up if forced. Then apply the resulting account to three cases I have not listed: the Younger Dryas impact hypothesis, the claim that Homo naledi deliberately buried its dead, and the Voynich manuscript. Finally, tell me whether there is any principled difference between a claim being unfalsifiable and a claim being falsifiable but currently unevidenced, and where that distinction does real work in my list.
