For several years I have followed the debate between illusionists about phenomenal consciousness (Frankish, Dennett, and their successors) and phenomenal realists (Chalmers and most of the field), and I have watched it calcify in real time. Both sides accept the same cognitive science: brains build introspective models, people make phenomenal judgments, and a complete functional account of those judgments is in principle achievable. The illusionist concludes that nothing beyond this remains to be explained — phenomenal consciousness is simply what the brain's introspective models represent, and the representation is inaccurate. The realist replies that the seeming itself, the fact that there is something it is like to be me, is the one datum no functional account touches, and that the illusionist has explained away the evidence rather than explained it. A decade of exchanges has moved no one.

I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. What kind of disagreement is this, exactly? Some candidate structures: (a) it is verbal, because the two sides use the term phenomenal consciousness differently, one treating it as a theoretical posit and the other as a pre-theoretical given; (b) it is about standards of evidence, because the sides disagree over whether first-person seemings count as data or merely as events to be predicted; (c) it is about which question is the real one, the meta-problem of why we judge there to be a hard problem, or the hard problem itself; (d) it is bedrock, a clash of starting intuitions neither side can defend without begging the question.

My own hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that the stall comes from (b): the realist counts the seeming of redness as evidence, while the illusionist counts only the report and the disposition to report, so no shared observation can exist between them. If that is right, new neuroscience will not help, because the dispute concerns what would even constitute data. But I may be wrong, and I would rather have the structure of the impasse mapped than be told who is winning.

Here is a proposed crux to test your diagnosis: imagine a mature science that predicts with perfect accuracy every phenomenal judgment anyone ever makes, including the judgment that the prediction has left something out. Does the realist position survive that scenario, and does the illusionist position? If each side can redescribe the scenario in terms favorable to itself, does that show the disagreement is bedrock, or only that the scenario is underdescribed?

The points should address, in order: what kind of disagreement this is; what each side actually counts as evidence; whether my proposed crux discriminates between the positions; what would count as progress given your diagnosis, whether a distinction, a formal result, an agreed experiment, or a change of question; and, if you conclude that no progress should be expected, why not.
