I work in theoretical biology, building evolutionary models of social behavior; my working vocabulary is replicator dynamics, evolutionarily stable strategies, asymmetric contests, costly signaling, and assortment. I have been reading about David Lewis's account of convention in his book Convention, and I want to know how much of it survives translation into my framework.

As I understand it, the account runs roughly as follows. A convention is a regularity in the behavior of members of a population facing some recurring situation, where it is common knowledge among them that nearly everyone conforms to the regularity, that nearly everyone expects the others to conform, and that nearly everyone prefers to conform conditional on the others doing so; and where the situation admits at least one alternative regularity that could equally have served. The underlying problem is thus one of coordination with multiple solutions, and the convention is arbitrary in the sense that another would have done. Conventions get started and sustained through precedent and salience rather than through enforceable agreement.

Here is what I would like. First, restate each component of this account in evolutionary game theory vocabulary, as faithfully as you can: the recurring situation and its multiplicity of solutions; conformity as a preference conditional on others' conformity; the expectation and common-knowledge conditions; arbitrariness; precedent and salience. Be explicit about what each component becomes -- for instance, whether common knowledge turns into a claim about public signals, correlated encounters, or assortment, or simply drops out of the model -- rather than giving me a loose paraphrase.

Second, a test case. Behavioral ecologists describe the resolution of territorial contests by owner-intruder asymmetries -- the incumbent almost always wins, and selection favors individuals who escalate when owner and retreat when intruder -- as a convention, and it is standardly modeled as a correlated equilibrium built on an observable role asymmetry. Would this count as a convention on your translation of Lewis, given that the animals presumably hold no expectations about one another, let alone common knowledge? Use this case to show me where the translation has bite, and whether my field has been using the word too loosely or Lewis too strictly.

Third, the residue report. Which parts of the account translate cleanly, which translate only under distortion, and which have no expression in my framework at all? The candidates I have in mind include the epistemic conditions (do replicator dynamics need anything like common knowledge, or does the epistemic layer matter only for agents who deliberate?); the distinction Lewis wants between a convention and a mere behavioral regularity (can an ESS fail to be a convention, and can my framework even state the difference?); and whatever room Lewis leaves for agents to reflect on their conventions -- to keep one, abandon one, or coordinate on switching to another -- which seems to have no counterpart in a population of replicators. For each piece of residue, give me your verdict on whether it marks something my framework is missing (a notion of norm, of shared expectation, of deliberation among equilibria) or something Lewis's rational-choice scaffolding added without earning its keep. In other words: does the evolutionary reconstruction show that convention was never the epistemically loaded phenomenon Lewis took it to be, or does it instead reveal what his account can see and mine cannot?
