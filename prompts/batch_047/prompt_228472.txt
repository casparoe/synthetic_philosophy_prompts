I keep running into the same puzzle whenever someone gives me a probability about a particular case, so let me lay out my gut reactions and see if there's a principle underneath.

If an insurance company charges me more for car insurance because I'm a 23 year old man, that seems fine -- they're not claiming to know anything about me, they're just pricing a pool. If a doctor tells me my specific cancer has a 40% five-year survival rate and I ask "but what about MY case, given my exact genetic markers and lifestyle," and she says "well, conditional on those, 70%," that also seems fine -- she just used a narrower class. But if she keeps drilling down -- my age, my markers, my smoking history, my bloodwork last Tuesday -- at some point the reference class has like three people in it, and my gut says that number is now meaningless, even though each step felt like an improvement. That's the first tension: narrowing feels epistemically better at every step but the endpoint feels worse.

Second: I think there's no fact of the matter about "the probability this coin lands heads on the next flip" beyond 0 or 1, but I do think there's a fact about the probability that a randomly selected atom of uranium-238 decays in the next hour, even though that's just as physically determined. I have no good reason for this asymmetry and it might just be physics chauvinism.

Third: if a courtroom hears that DNA matches occur in the general population at 1 in a million, I think that base rate should matter a lot. But if someone tells me the base rate of accountants among people who wear tweed is high, and uses it to argue this specific tweed-wearer is probably an accountant, that feels gross even though the statistical structure is identical.

Fourth: I keep wanting to say the true probability of a single case is the frequency in the most specific relevant reference class, but "relevant" is doing all the work there and I can't cash it out.

So: is there one principle about how to pick the reference class for a single-case probability that delivers all these verdicts, or do I have to drop some? Which ones would a coherent view force me to abandon, and what does it say about a case I haven't listed -- like whether an AI weather model's "70% chance of rain tomorrow" is even the same kind of claim as the insurance one?
