I've used Zorn's lemma in the standard applications — every vector space has a basis, every nontrivial ring has a maximal ideal, every filter extends to an ultrafilter — and the usual gloss ("keep adjoining elements until you can't; whatever you end up holding is maximal") feels like an ordinary induction that one has simply allowed to run transfinitely. Yet the lemma is equivalent to the axiom of choice, so something in that gloss must be doing illicit work, and I'd like to be able to feel where. Please explain, at a gut level rather than at the level of a formal proof: (a) exactly which step of the naive transfinite-induction story smuggles in choice — is it the need to select one strict extension at each stage, the assumption that the process must terminate, or something subtler? (b) why the conclusion delivers only the existence of a maximal element and never a recipe for reaching one, and what goes wrong when the poset is explicitly definable; (c) why the hypothesis has to be that every chain has an upper bound, rather than the weaker and seemingly sufficient condition that every non-maximal element has something strictly above it — ideally with a concrete poset satisfying the weaker condition but possessing no maximal element. The test of a good answer is whether it would let me look at a new poset and predict in advance whether a Zorn-style argument is available, rather than merely restating that the lemma is equivalent to choice.
