I teach an upper-level undergraduate course in the philosophy of physics, and in two weeks I have a single ninety-minute session on the hole argument. The students are comfortable with the basic machinery of general relativity at the level of smooth manifolds, tensor fields, and diffeomorphisms, but most have never encountered the metaphysics of spacetime before. I understand the argument myself; what I want is a plan for how to teach it, not a presentation of the argument to me.

Please plan the session with the following structure.

First, the dependency order. What has to be in place before the argument can even be stated, and in what sequence? I suspect the notion of a diffeomorphism as something more than a coordinate change has to come before anything else, and that the class needs to feel the pull of manifold substantivalism as a default reading of the formalism before the argument can have any bite -- but tell me if I have the order wrong, and justify each placement by what would go wrong if a later element were introduced earlier.

Second, the carrying construction. I want one concrete setup to build the whole session around: presumably two models that agree everywhere outside some compact region and differ inside it. Say what this example must have and must lack. In particular, should the region be literally empty of matter, or does that invite a confusion? And should I use a spacetime with a well-posed initial value problem, or does that smuggle in the very determinism framing I want to control?

Third, the misconception map, with the point in the sequence where each should be headed off. I expect at least these: that the argument is just the trivial point that physics is coordinate-independent; that it is merely a puzzle about determinism with no metaphysical stakes; that it straightforwardly shows spacetime points do not exist; and that general covariance by itself generates or by itself dissolves the problem. Correct me where my predictions are off, add misconceptions I have missed, and tell me at which exact step of the session each one will naturally form, so that I defuse it there rather than after it has set.

Fourth, one question I can put to the class at the end whose satisfactory answer would show the argument has landed as an argument -- that they can identify which premise a given response denies -- rather than as a slogan about substantivalism.

Finally, tell me what I should openly decline to cover at this level, and say so to the students explicitly.
