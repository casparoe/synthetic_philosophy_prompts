You are grading a short-answer response from an introductory philosophy exam. Here is the question the students were given:

"Plato's Euthyphro poses a dilemma: is the pious loved by the gods because it is pious, or is it pious because the gods love it? Consider the following secular analogue, aimed at the view that rightness is constituted by community endorsement: is conduct right because the community endorses it, or does the community endorse it because it is right? State the original dilemma, transpose it onto this target, and assess whether the transposition preserves the force of the original."

Here is the student's answer:

"The Euthyphro dilemma asks whether the pious is loved by the gods because it is pious, or whether it is pious because the gods love it. In its modern form: is an action right because God commands it, or does God command it because it is right? If the first, morality is arbitrary, since God could have commanded cruelty and cruelty would then be right. If the second, rightness is independent of God, and Socrates concludes that the gods are therefore irrelevant to morality and theism stands refuted.

Transposed: is conduct right because the community endorses it, or does the community endorse it because it is right? Take the second horn first: if the community endorses conduct because it is right, then rightness just is whatever the community endorses, which makes morality arbitrary and hostage to majority whim; endorsement could flip tomorrow and slavery could become right. On the first horn, conduct is right because it is endorsed, so the community's verdict tracks an independent standard, and consensus ethics comes out true but trivial: it tells us rightness exists but adds nothing of its own.

Since both horns are unacceptable, the dilemma disproves consensus ethics, just as it disproves divine command theory. As Aquinas's resolution shows, the only escape is to locate the standard in the nature of the authority itself."

Please assign a grade out of 10 and justify it with point-by-point comments: for each component of the answer (statement of the original dilemma, accuracy of the transposition, handling of each horn, the conclusion drawn, and the closing attribution), say what is correct and what is wrong or imprecise. In particular, flag any subtle errors that a hasty grader would let slide. Finally, indicate which of your criticisms rest on settled matters (the structure of the dilemma, what the student actually wrote) and which touch on genuinely contested interpretive questions (for example, whether the dilemma even touches sophisticated modern divine command theories), rather than presenting the contested as settled.
