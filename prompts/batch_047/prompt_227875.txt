In my epistemology seminar we keep running into a defense of conspiracy theorists that leans on an analogy: the conspiracy theorist is like a detective or investigative journalist. Both refuse to take the official account at face value, both expect the powerful to have interests in lying, and both treat apparent coincidences as clues; and since history has vindicated real conspiracies -- my classmates cite Watergate and MKUltra -- dismissing conspiracy belief as irrational is said to be mere deference to authority. Our professor dismisses this as the "just asking questions" move but hasn't said why it fails. Please audit the analogy: lay out the correspondence mapping explicitly (the detective's suspicion, evidence-gathering, and standard of proof -- onto what in the conspiracy theorist's practice?); identify where the mapping breaks, especially in how each party responds when expected evidence fails to appear and in what institutions exist to correct each when they err; separate the disanalogies that bear on the intended conclusion from the superficial ones; and say whether anything in the vicinity of the conclusion -- some claim about the epistemic respectability of suspicion toward official narratives -- survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy only works by borrowing the detective's credentials without his discipline.
