I am a PhD student in philosophy working on the foundations of AI agency, and I keep running into the same analogy in both seminar discussions and informal write-ups. The distinction between approval-directed and goal-directed agents is routinely explained by analogy to managing people: a goal-directed agent is like a contractor handed a written specification who then disappears to optimize it, whereas an approval-directed agent is like a junior employee who, at each step, considers which action the manager would most endorse and does that. The analogy is then typically put to argumentative work: because the "employee" keeps the "manager" in the loop, approval-direction is said to be inherently safer—it allegedly avoids Goodhart-style specification gaming, preserves corrigibility, and prevents the agent from pursuing a frozen objective past the point where any principal would want it stopped.

I would like a careful audit of this analogy. Specifically: (1) give me the explicit correspondence table—what plays the role of the manager, the employee, the act of asking, the endorsement itself, and the employment relationship—and note where the mapping is only approximate even on a charitable reading (for instance, the approval signal in any realistic implementation is produced by a learned model of the approver, not by the approver directly); (2) identify the features of human employees that the analogy quietly imports but that an artificial agent would lack—shared background values, independent judgment that can push back on a boss, an intrinsic rather than merely instrumental interest in genuinely pleasing, and a limited ability to reshape the manager's beliefs and preferences; (3) assess whether the disanalogies undermine the safety conclusions the analogy is used to support—in particular, whether optimizing for predicted approval is itself just goal-directedness toward an approval objective (with Goodhart and manipulation risks of its own), or whether there is a structural difference that survives this objection; and (4) say whether a better analogy exists for what approval-direction actually is, and what conclusions it would license instead.

Please write your answer in American English. I am less interested in a survey of positions than in a sharp judgment about how much argumentative weight this particular analogy can bear.
