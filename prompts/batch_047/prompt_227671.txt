I accept what is usually called the procreation asymmetry: we have a moral reason not to bring into existence someone whose life would foreseeably be bad, but no corresponding moral reason to bring into existence someone whose life would foreseeably be good; creating a happy child is morally optional in a way that creating a miserable one is not. I take this to be the ordinary, widely shared intuition, and I currently hold that it survives the standard attempts to reduce it to absurdity. What I want is a map of what would actually count against it.

Please tell me which considerations would genuinely threaten the asymmetry, as opposed to merely making it awkward to state. I am interested in at least the following fronts, and any others you take to matter:

- Cases designed to force an unwelcome verdict. Would the asymmetry, consistently applied, commit me to saying it would be wrong to create even a mildly unhappy but worth-living life, or indifferent about preventing wonderful lives at negligible cost? If so, does that count as a refutation or as a consequence I should simply accept?
- Formal pressure from the non-identity problem. Since creating a bad life wrongs no one who would otherwise have existed, can the asymmetry be stated without appeal to a person-affecting principle that itself collapses under scrutiny? What would have to be true about value -- impersonal versus person-affecting -- for the asymmetry to be coherently defensible, and would discovering that that picture of value is untenable be a defeater of the right strength?
- Arguments that the asymmetry proves too much or too little. If the same reasoning that supports it also supports, say, Benatar-style conclusions that it is always a harm to be brought into existence, does the asymmetry stand or fall with those stronger claims? Conversely, if the intuition is preserved only by an ad hoc restriction, does the need for the restriction itself count against it?
- Empirical and psychological findings. Suppose it were shown that the intuition tracks framing effects or scope neglect rather than any stable moral commitment. How damaging would that be, given that moral intuitions generally have messy etiologies?

For each candidate defeater, say whether it would be fatal, would force a retreat to a weaker or more qualified asymmetry, or would merely be uncomfortable; say what defenders of the asymmetry themselves would concede counts against the view, as opposed to what only its critics regard as counting; and assess whether the view, once qualified to survive the obvious objections, has become unfalsifiable -- and if it has, whether that is a defect for a position of this kind or simply what a fundamental normative commitment looks like. Rank the defeaters from most to least threatening. Note also which single result, argument, or finding you would expect to change my mind fastest, given that I am more moved by formal collapse into an unacceptable rival position than by counterintuitive case verdicts.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- the single most serious threat to the asymmetry and whether it is fatal or survivable -- and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Write in British English.
