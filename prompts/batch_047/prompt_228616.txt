I'm a philosophy prof and I teach a small seminar on concept learning, and for a few years now I've been making homemade Bongard-style puzzles for it (six boxes on the left that all fit some rule, six on the right that don't). My colleague D in the psych department has extremely strong opinions about what counts as *solving* one versus gaming it, and I've started keeping a record of her rulings because I can't figure out her consistency. Here's her track record so far, where in each case someone proposed a rule that really did sort all twelve boxes correctly:

1. Boxes with three circles vs. four circles. Proposed: "exactly three circles." Verdict: yes, obviously, that's the concept.
2. A dot inside a closed squiggle vs. outside it. Proposed: "the dot is inside the closed curve." Verdict: yes.
3. The intended solution was "contains exactly one triangle," but a student proposed "the total number of line-endpoints in the box is prime," and annoyingly it also fit all twelve. Verdict: absolutely not, that's numerology, not a solution. She said this even after admitting it sorts every box.
4. Proposed: "either two squares, or a triangle with a dot in it." Fit all twelve. Verdict: rejected instantly, "you can't just glue two rules together."
5. Proposed: "the shapes get bigger left to right." Verdict: accepted without hesitating, even when I pointed out it's about relations between objects and not any one object.
6. Proposed: "the total amount of black ink in the box falls between two values" (a student actually measured pixel counts; it fit). Verdict: rejected, she laughed.
7. Proposed: "contains a shape with an odd number of sides" (triangles and pentagons vs. squares and hexagons). Verdict: accepted, but grudgingly — she said it was "a bit iffy" because you have to count.

New puzzle I've made for next week. Left boxes contain 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, and 13 dots respectively (one box per count). Right boxes contain 4, 6, 7, 9, 10, and 12. Dot size and placement are randomized, and I checked there's no spatial pattern doing the work — the only clean rule I can find is that the number of dots is a Fibonacci number. That's what I intended, for what it's worth.

So: predict her verdict on this one, with reasons. Is Fibonacci a solution or is it the same sin as the prime-number-of-line-endpoints case? Tell me what rule you think she's actually been running across cases 1–7 (something like "the rule has to be perceptually natural"? or "it has to be the simplest rule that fits"? or something else), and what rival rules fit her record just as well. Which single earlier verdict does the most work for your prediction? And if her record genuinely underdetermines the new case, design the one follow-up puzzle that would settle which rule she's running.

If you want to connect any of this to grue or rule-following or whatever, go ahead briefly, but mostly I want the prediction and the reverse-engineering, not a lit reveiw.
