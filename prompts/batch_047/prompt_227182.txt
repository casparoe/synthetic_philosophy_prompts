I sit on the ethics advisory panel for a public-private consortium that's about to fund the first industrial operation on the Moon: a plant in a permanently shadowed crater near the south pole that would mine water ice and crack it into rocket fuel. A big chunk of the planetary science community wants us to block it. Their case: these crater floors have been cold-trapping volatiles for billions of years, basicly a frozen archive of comet impacts and solar wind history you can't get anywhere else in the solar system, and the lander's exhaust plumes alone will scatter contamination over neighbouring craters before anyone digs a single scoop. Once disturbed it's gone, no remediation, no do-overs.

The company's response is honestly not stupid. The Moon has no life, no ecosystems, nobody's drinking water. Preserving grey dust because a small professional group finds it scientifically interesting is, they say, an aesthetic preference dressed up as a moral duty, and a preference the rest of humanity gets billed for. They also point out that if we say no, a less scrupulous operator with no ethics panel at all will do it anyway within the decade, so we'd achieve nothing except feeling good about ourselves.

Our charter says the panel must weigh "the interests of humanity as a whole, including future generations," a phrase nobody ever defined. One panel member says future generations have an interest in the archive -- in knowing the early history of their own solar system -- and we're about to shred it to save a fuel bill. Another says future generations have an interest in cheap, routine access to space, and a permanent fueling station is exactly how they get that, so blocking it would be the real betrayal of them.

Draft the panel's decision memo: approve, reject, or approve with conditions. I need an actual bottom line I can table at next month's meeting, with the reasoning laid out, and it has to engage seriously with the strongest argument on whichever side loses -- not just restate the winner. If you recommend conditions, make them things the company could actually comply with and we could actually check, not "minimize impact" boilerplate. And say something about who even has standing to make this call, given that no one owns the Moon and our panel certainly doesn't represent humanity.
