I work as a software engineer at a mid-sized company, and I recently came across the idea of the "unilateralist's curse" (from a paper by Nick Bostrom and co-authors, via some writing about existential risk). As I understand it, the idea is this: suppose a group of people can each, acting alone, initiate some action, and each person initiates it just in case their private estimate of the action's value exceeds some threshold. Then, as the group grows, the action ends up being initiated whenever the single most optimistic member judges it worthwhile — and since that person is selected precisely for having the most extreme estimate, their judgment is likely to be an overestimate, so the group systematically over-acts, even if everyone is individually rational and well-intentioned. This struck me as immediately relevant to my day job. Our release process lets any of about thirty engineers unilaterally halt a deployment if they judge it too risky, and I've been tasked with proposing a redesign of that policy.

My thinking so far is that the curse shows our current setup is statistically unsound: the decision to halt a release is effectively being made by whichever engineer happens to be the most alarmed outlier on a given day, which means we halt releases far more often than the true risk warrants. The natural fix, it seems to me, is to require a quorum — say, three or five engineers must agree before a halt takes effect — with the quorum size chosen to be large enough to neutralize the selection effect. What I'd like help with is the calibration question: how do I reason about the right quorum size to fully cancel out the curse, given the number of engineers and the rough distribution of their risk assessments? And does the curse apply symmetrically, so that we'd also need a quorum for unilateral *inaction* — e.g., an engineer who privately decides a risky-looking release is fine and stays silent — or does the asymmetry between acting and refraining break the parallel?

One request: please don't just survey the considerations and leave the design choice to me. Give me a committed recommendation — what policy you would actually implement for a team like ours, and why — and if any step in my reasoning above is wrong or rests on a misreading of what the unilateralist's curse actually establishes, say so directly and correct it before answering.
