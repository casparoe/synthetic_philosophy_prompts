So I'm a novelist, deep in a historical novel set in Italy around 1503 (my protagonist is a cautious little functionary in the orbit of the Papal court, which is the whole point of him), and my editor keeps writing FORTUNE FAVORS THE BOLD in the margins. She means it as an instruction: make him act, make him gamble, readers love a decisive hero, boring characters don't sell. I've heard this line a million times and it always felt like a greeting card, but then for research I've been reading The Prince and chapter 25 ambushed me. Machiavelli writes: "I believe also that he will be successful who directs his actions according to the spirit of the times, and that he whose actions do not accord with the times will not be successful." And then later: "I conclude therefore that, fortune being changeful and mankind steadfast in their ways, so long as the two are in agreement men are successful, but unsuccessful when they fall out." Which... is not the greeting card. He seems to be saying boldness isn't favored at all, fitness is, and the impetuous guy gets wrecked the moment the weather changes. But then my editor would say look at his Julius II example, the man charged in and it worked, so fortune does favor the bold. So now I can't tell if the chapter supports the slogan, refutes it, or just ignores it.

For each reading: is it a platitude, is it substantive and defensible, is it just false? And crucially, which reading does the Machiavelli passage commit him to, because as I read it his view is weirdly closer to "fortune favors whoever happens to match the moment, and most people can't change their charachter, so luck dominates" -- which would make it almost an anti-boldness passage except for Julius. Then tell me which reading my editor needs for her margin note to be good advice about fiction (as opposed to about ruling Bologna), because if the slogan only means "characters who do nothing are dull" that's different from "make him reckless and it'll work out."
