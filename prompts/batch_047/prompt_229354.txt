I run a small epistemology reading group (we're mostly grad students and a couple of interested undergrads), and after our session on the lottery and preface paradoxes half the room insisted they had "a view" but couldn't say which one. So I want to build a one-page flowchart handout called "Where do you actually stand on the lottery and preface?" that starts from someone who has just heard both paradoxes and walks them, question by question, to a named position. Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of famous names: first figure out what the diagnostic questions are, then which combinations of answers they generate, and only then slot actual positions in as leaves. Every node should be a single question a reader can answer without already knowing the label of where they end up (so no "do you accept closure under conjunction?" -- phrase it as a concrete verdict about the modest author or the ticket holder), the tree should be at most four questions deep, and I'm aiming for somewhere between 8 and 12 leaves.

The leaves have to include at least: the Kyburg-style view that rational acceptance isn't closed under conjunction, so the modest author rationally believes each claim in her book and also that the book contains an error; the threshold/Lockean view that outright belief is just credence above some cutoff, with the paradoxes as the honest price; the eliminativist view that outright belief is a folk-psychological mistake and only credences exist; the view that belief constitutively aims at knowledge, so neither lottery propositions nor the preface confession were ever genuine candidates for belief in the first place; and the genuinely heretical view that a rational agent may knowingly hold a jointly inconsistent set of beliefs, full stop. If the logical space forces other leaves (stability-style accounts, contextualism about "accept", two-notions views that seperate belief from acceptance), add them. For every unoccupied combination of answers, give a one-line diagnosis of why nobody sits there: incoherent, unmotivated, or merely unexplored. And flag explicitly any pair of leaves your questions can't pull apart, rather than papering over it.

Two more things I care about. First, treat the two paradoxes jointly: I want at least one leaf occupied by a position that resolves them asymmetrically, and the handout should say which leaves are committed to calling the modest author irrational. Second, end the handout with a three-case stress test the reader can aim at their own leaf: (a) the archivist who has checked each of a million records and believes of each that it's accurate, while believing at least one record in the archive is wrong; (b) the two-author preface, where each co-author believes her own chapters are error-free but is sure the other author's chapters contain a slip somewhere; and (c) a third case of your own design, one you'd pick specifically because it splits whatever leaves your tree couldn't separate. If a leaf flunks its own stress test, say so.
