i keep running into the same move in biology writing and in arguments with my labmates. someone points out that we constantly talk about organs and traits as if they were designed -- the heart is FOR pumping blood, the liver's job is detox, camoflage exists in order to hide the animal -- and a creationist-friendly reader could take that literally. the standard comeback is an analogy: natural selection plays the role of the designer. selection "wants" what an engineer wants, it "solves problems," and so function-talk is totally fine because there's a real process standing in for the intentional designer, just minus the intentions. this analogy gets used to settle things -- like, once you accept selection-as-designer, supposedly there's no remaining puzzle about why it's legitimate to say a trait has a function or that a heart is malfunctioning.

i want an audit of this analogy. give me the actual correspondence table: what plays the role of the designer's intentions, the blueprint, the manufacturing step, the artifact's proper use, the repairman's manual that says how the thing is supposed to work. then where it breaks. a few places i suspect it breaks but i'm not sure: designers have foresight and selection has none, so "solutions" get attributed to problems that were never posed; artifacts get built to spec in one step while traits get co-opted from other uses mid-history (feathers, the panda's thumb kind of cases), which seems to mess with the idea of a fixed intended function; and with an artifact there's a fact about what it's for even if it's never been used, whereas a trait that was never selected for anything seems functionless -- does the analogy even cover that case or does it quietly assume the etiological reading?

also: two camps seem to both claim this analogy. one says a trait's function is whatever past selection acted on (so a mutation with no history has no function yet), the other says function is just what the trait currently does in the system regardless of history (so a brand-new mutation doing useful work already has a function). does the designer analogy actually favor either of these, or does it paper over the disagreement by being loose in exactly the spot where they differ? and at the end, tell me whether the "function-talk is unproblematic" conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was smuggling it in. finally, say what would change your verdict -- which consideration, if it turned out differently than you assume, would flip your answer, and where you think your reasoning is most likely to have gone wrong.
