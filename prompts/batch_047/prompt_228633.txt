I'm a graduate student in philosophy of cognitive science, and for the past year my reading group has been circling the question of whether large language models understand language, or anything at all. The debate has calcified into two camps that talk past each other with remarkable consistency. One side, drawing on the "stochastic parrots" critique and on Bender and Koller's octopus thought experiment, argues that a system trained only to model the statistical shape of text has access to linguistic form but not to meaning, and so cannot understand, full stop; for them, understanding requires a causal grip on the world that text prediction cannot supply. The other side points out that these models answer questions, resolve ambiguities, follow novel instructions, translate, summarize, and correct their own mistakes, and asks what more we could possibly want; if using words correctly in indefinitely many novel contexts isn't understanding, they say, then the word has been defined so as to be undetectable. Both sides are by now familiar with each other's best arguments, and neither has moved. My own suspicion is that the dispute is largely verbal — a fight over the word "understanding" — but a colleague insists it's a substantive disagreement about what kind of evidence could ever settle questions about mentality.

While preparing for this week's session I went back to Turing's "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950) and was struck by how early in the paper he anticipates something like our impasse. Right after posing his opening question, he writes:

"If the meaning of the words 'machine' and 'think' are to be found by examining how they are commonly used it is difficult to escape the conclusion that the meaning and the answer to the question, 'Can machines think?' is to be sought in a statistical survey such as a Gallup poll. But this is absurd. Instead of attempting such a definition I shall replace the question by another, which is closely related to it and is expressed in relatively unambiguous words."

Turing's move was to refuse a definitional inquiry into "think" and substitute an operationalized question in its place. What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict. Please act as a philosopher of mind with expertise in artificial intelligence, and think carefully through the following before composing your answer: Is the stalled LLM-understanding debate best understood as exactly the kind of dispute Turing tried to dissolve — one that can only be "settled" by a Gallup poll about usage, since each side has simply fixed on a different conception of understanding, one causal-worldly and one behavioral? Or is there a genuine substantive disagreement underneath: about which cases count as data, about whether behavioral competence is evidence of understanding or merely consistent with its absence, or about bedrock intuitions concerning what meaning requires? Consider also a third possibility my advisor floated — that the two camps are answering different questions under a single heading, one about whether these systems meet the standards we actually use to attribute understanding to each other, the other about whether any purely textual training regime could in principle produce it. Engage the Turing passage directly: tell me whether his substitution strategy actually succeeds here, or whether the LLM case reveals a way in which the operational question and the "meaningless" original can come apart — i.e., a system could pass his test while the deeper question remains open.

Conclude by telling me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress. If the dispute is verbal, say what a productive reformulation of the question would look like. If it's substantive, say what kind of consideration — a conceptual distinction, a finding about the models' internal representations, an experiment, a formal result — would actually move one side, and be honest if your diagnosis implies that no such consideration exists and the debate should be expected to continue indefinitely.
