Last week I watched two friends argue after a meeting of our housing cooperative, and I've gone back and forth on it enough times that I'd like a careful referee.

The background: at the meeting, Dana sharply criticized Marcus for repeatedly blowing past deadlines on a maintenance project he had volunteered for, telling him in front of everyone that it showed disregard for other people's time. Several people at the meeting had pointed out, somewhat gleefully, that Dana herself had missed a string of deadlines the previous spring. Dana said that was beside the point. Afterward, two friends of mine who had been there disagreed about who was right, and their exchange went roughly as follows.

Elena's opening position was that Dana had no business delivering that criticism. Someone who is guilty of the very same fault cannot complain when others commit it; whatever the truth of the charge, blame coming from her is an impersonation of moral authority she hasn't earned, and Marcus would have been within his rights to say so to her face.

Sam replied that this conflates the critic with the criticism. If only the blameless may blame, then no one may blame anyone, and the cooperative's deadlines become unenforceable by unanimous hypocrisy. Dana's past failures make her a hypocrite, perhaps, but hypocrisy is a flaw in her character, not in her assessment of Marcus, which everyone present knew to be accurate.

Elena answered that Sam was missing what hypocrisy actually does to a speech act. The problem isn't a purity requirement. It's that Dana was holding Marcus to a standard she has shown, by her own conduct, that she does not accept for herself — and a person who rejects a standard in her own case lacks any entitlement to demand that others answer for violating it. The unfairness is in the blaming itself, not just in Dana's soul.

Sam then offered a test: suppose Dana had begun by acknowledging her own missed deadlines and said, I was just as bad last spring, and we both need to do better. On Elena's view, Sam claimed, this confession shouldn't matter, since the underlying inconsistency in her conduct is unchanged. Yet intuitively it changes everything — which suggests the real defect is not hypocrisy as such but the pretense of standing on higher ground, a defect that can be cured by honesty. So Marcus's lateness still warranted criticism, and Dana's only mistake was presentational.

Elena's final move was to deny that the cure works. Even a confessed hypocrite, she said, faces the question of why Marcus should answer to her in particular. When someone says you're one to talk, they aren't merely deflecting; they are challenging the blamer's right to press the complaint at all, the way a court asks whether a plaintiff has standing to sue. Marcus's conduct is still criticizable — by the people his lateness actually burdened, or by Dana speaking as a fellow offender rather than as a judge — but Dana pitched her blame from above, and that posture was not hers to adopt.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: assess each of the five moves above on its own terms, saying what it gets right and where, if anywhere, it goes wrong. Then say who is more right overall and on which specific point. Finally, if there is a distinction both of them are missing that would clarify the dispute — for instance, among the truth of what was said, the act of saying it, and the entitlement to be the one who says it — explain whether drawing it vindicates one side, both, or neither.
