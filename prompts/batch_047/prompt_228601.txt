Here's the essay question I set:

"Do filmmakers and historical novelists owe anything morally to the real dead people they dramatise? If the dead cannot be harmed, does it follow that 'anything goes' in their portrayal? Discuss with reference to the posthumous harm debate and at least two normative ethical frameworks."

And here's the student's answer, verbatim:

---
Legally and morally, dramatists owe nothing to the dead themselves, because the dead no longer exist as subjects who can hold interests. Parfit's work on the non-identity problem showed that we cannot harm people who do not exist at the time of the act, and this applies a fortiori to the dead. The law bears this out: defamation claims cannot be brought on behalf of the deceased, so a biopic can say what it likes about someone once they are gone. Since the law tracks morality in this area, the only genuine obligations run to the living: the feelings of descendants, and the epistemic interests of audiences who might come away with false beliefs about historical events.

A utilitarian would reach much the same conclusion. Harm requires experience, the dead experience nothing, so dramatists should weigh only effects on the living. A utilitarian might still condemn fabrications that mislead audiences about matters of public importance, since false beliefs about the past can do real damage downstream. A Kantian, by contrast, grounds duty in respect for rational nature; since the dead are no longer rational agents, a Kantian must be indifferent to how they are portrayed, caring only about what dramatising them does to the living. So on both major frameworks the answer converges: anything goes with respect to the dead themselves, with constraints arising only from the interests of the living.

There is one wrinkle: promises and dying wishes. If someone extracts a promise about how they will be portrayed, breaking it seems wrong even though no one is harmed by the breach, which suggests our moral practice already treats the dead as considerable. But this can be explained away: the wrongness lies in damaging the institution of promising, which matters to the living who rely on it.

Conclusion: artistic licence is a moral permission, not merely an aesthetic convention, and the dead are owed accuracy only as a means to serving the audience's interest in truth.
---
