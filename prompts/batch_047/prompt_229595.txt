I recently joined an informal ethics discussion group, and last month we spent three sessions on assisted dying. We kept agreeing on conclusions while disagreeing wildly about reasons, and I came away unsure whether my own judgments have any consistent principle behind them or are just a pile of intuitions. So I want to lay out my verdicts on a set of cases and ask what principle, if any, captures them. My verdicts: A woman with terminal pancreatic cancer, weeks to live, whose pain cannot be fully controlled even with good palliative care, asks for help dying — clearly permissible, maybe even something her doctors ought to offer. A man in his fifties with severe depression that has resisted every treatment over twenty years, lucid and consistent in his request — not permissible; his wish to die strikes me as a symptom of the very condition in question. A woman who, upon being diagnosed with early Alzheimer's, wrote a detailed directive asking to be euthanized once she no longer recognizes her children; that point has arrived, and she now spends her days apparently content, listening to music, frightened only when her children are strangers in the room — I say the directive should not be carried out, whatever she would have wanted then. A man with locked-in syndrome after a stroke, fully competent, communicating by eye movements, who has requested death steadily for two years — permissible. A healthy seventy-eight-year-old who says her life is complete, she has seen her grandchildren grow up, and she is simply done — I genuinely cannot decide. And a twenty-five-year-old, six months after a spinal cord injury left him quadriplegic, who wants to die — I say no, at least not yet, though I admit this sits awkwardly next to the locked-in case.

Two pairs of these verdicts bother me. First, the depressed man and the locked-in man: both are competent, both have endured their condition for years, both request consistently — if my answer differs, the difference has to be about the source of the suffering rather than its severity or the competence of the request, and I am not sure that is defensible, since it seems to assume psychiatric suffering is less real or less truly the patient's own. Second, the Alzheimer's directive: in every other medical context I would insist that a person's settled prior wishes about her own body be honored — I would certainly want my own refusal-of-treatment directive respected — yet here I let her current, demented contentment override her former, fully competent self's considered wish. Either I am being inconsistent, or there is some distinction between directives that refuse treatment and directives that request death, or between what a past self can demand and what a present self can suffer, that I cannot articulate.

What I want is this: state the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that best captures my pattern of verdicts, with the case that motivates each element of it; tell me honestly which of my verdicts each candidate cannot accommodate and would force me to abandon; and say what the best principle implies for the two cases I flagged as unstable — the completed-life woman and the recently injured young man. I am not asking for a summary of the standard positions in the literature (sanctity of life, autonomy, the doctrine of double effect, and so on) so much as for a diagnosis of my own verdicts and the least revisionary coherent position in their neighborhood. If no single principle fits and the honest answer is that some verdict has to go, say which one and why. Please write your answer as continuous prose, the way you would write a long letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
