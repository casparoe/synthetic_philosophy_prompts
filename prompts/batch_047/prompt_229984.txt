I am preparing a handout for a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and I need a counterexample built to order. In the fourth chapter of Part I of A Treatise on Probability (1921), Keynes renames the classical principle of insufficient reason the principle of indifference, exhibits the contradictions it generates (the family of puzzles that includes Bertrand's), and then offers a diagnosis: the trouble arises only when we apply the principle to alternatives that can be further subdivided into sub-alternatives of the same form. His proposed repair is a restriction — apply the principle only to alternatives that are, relative to everything we know, indivisible — and he presents the restriction as dissolving the paradoxes.

Construct a thought experiment that puts this repair under maximum pressure: a scenario in which the principle of indifference yields two contradictory probability assignments for the same event, even though Keynes's restriction is fully respected — that is, the alternatives under each of the two descriptions are genuinely atomic given what the agent in the scenario knows, so there is no hidden subdivision for Keynes's fix to exploit. Constraints: (1) realistic — a calibration laboratory, a brewery, a hospital dosing protocol, a quality-control line — not abstract geometry and not a lottery designed by a paradox-monger; (2) the scenario itself in one paragraph; (3) the two descriptions must be ones a competent practitioner would actually reach for, so neither parameterization may be gerrymandered or obviously less natural than the other; (4) no cheating by stipulation — the two assignments must be derived from the principle applied to the case as described, not asserted; (5) the agent's ignorance must be symmetric under both descriptions without any appeal to an oracle who knows the true parameterization.

After the scenario, demonstrate that it meets the specifications: state each description, derive the probability it delivers, and show the contradiction. Then argue, point by point, that Keynes's restriction does not block the case — that the alternatives under each description really are indivisible relative to the agent's knowledge, and that any attempt to subdivide them either is epistemically unavailable to the agent or merely regenerates the same ambiguity one level down. Identify which single feature of the case is doing the work and show that the contradiction disappears if that feature is removed. Finally, steelman the defense: how would someone committed to Keynes's restriction attack the case — most plausibly by denying that both descriptions are equally natural, or by insisting that one of them smuggles in a subdivision — and does the case survive? If you come to believe that no realistic case can satisfy all five constraints at once, abandon the construction, say so plainly, and explain what that impossibility would show about whether Keynes's restriction succeeds.
