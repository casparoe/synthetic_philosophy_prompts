So I'm a PhD student working on early modern philosophy of mathematics, and my dissertation chapter is on Hobbes's claim that geometry is the one genuinely demonstrable science, and that it's demonstrable precisely because we make its objects ourselves. Roughly his line (in De Corpore and scattered through Leviathan) is that the geometer can know his conclusions with certainty because the definitions of geometrical figures are really recipes for generating them -- a circle, say, is what you get when a line moves around one of its own fixed endpoints -- so unlike in natural philosophy, where we only ever guess at the causes of things we didn't make, in geometry we are the cause, and knowledge of causes is supposed to be the whole of science.

Here's what I need. Construct a thought experiment that puts real pressure on this "we know what we generate" criterion, in the harder of the two directions: a case where someone has genuinely constructed or generated the geometrical object in question, so Hobbes's criterion says they have demonstrative knowledge, but where any reasonable judge says they don't actually know the relevant geometrical fact. Not the easier direction (knowing a theorem you didn't construct -- Hobbes has resources there, like learning the generation from someone else).

Constraints:

- One scenario, one paragraph max, and it has to be set in an ordinary 17th-century-plausible practice -- a surveyor, a shipwright, a navigator, a draftsman -- no computers, no demons, no skeptical hypnosis.
- The failure of knowledge can't come from the person just having made a mistake in their construction. They construct the figure exactly as intended, every step correct. The problem has to be structural -- something about what generating an object does and doesn't put you in a position to know.
- The case has to be for testing whether Hobbes's criterion is sufficient for knowledge, not just necessary, so don't smuggle in some extra defect that any epistemologist would flag (like the guy not believing the conclusion).

After the scenario: state exactly what Hobbes's criterion predicts and why, state the intuitive verdict and why, and identify which feature of the case is doing the work -- i.e., is the gap between construction and knowledge a gap between making something and knowing its causes, or between knowing causes and knowing consequences, or what? Then tell me what happens to the case if that feature is removed, and how someone defending Hobbes would try to show the case doesn't count (e.g. by insisting the construction wasn't really the generation of that figure after all). Bonus if you can say whether the same trick threatens his parallel claim that we know civil philosophy because, so to speak, we make the commonwealth ourselves.
