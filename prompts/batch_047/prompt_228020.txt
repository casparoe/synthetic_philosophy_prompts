I keep chewing on Kavka's toxin puzzle and want to pin down what I actually think by going case by case. My verdicts:

- Intending to raise my hand in ten seconds: easy, just do it.
- Intending to drink a gross smoothie tomorrow for $10, paid after I drink it: fine, no problem.
- The original setup: an eccentric billionare wires me $1M at midnight tonight if and only if I sincerely intend, at midnight, to drink a toxin tomorrow afternoon that will make me horribly ill for a day. Money is mine to keep no matter what I do after midnight. My gut says I can't form the intention, because I already know that come tomorrow, drinking is just pointlessly making myself sick.
- Same setup but the money only arrives after I actually drink: trivially fine, and the intending is no problem either.
- Same as the original but I've hired a hypnotist to make me forget the whole deal after midnight: now I think I *can* intend tonight, which is weird, since the hypnotist doesn't change what I'll want tomorrow, only what I'll remember.
- Promising a friend I'll drink it: this feels like it genuinely gives me a reason tomorrow that the bare deal doesn't, even though the billionaire is only scanning my intention, not my promises.

Is there one principle about when an intention can actually be formed that delivers all of these? Something about reasons for intending vs. reasons for acting, or about whether an intention has to survive contact with future-you? Tell me which of my verdicts the best version of the principle has to overturn, and what it predicts for cases I didn't list -- like trying to intend to fall asleep, or intending to one-box on Newcomb when you suspect the predictor is fallible.

Answer in prose, then end with a table with columns: Case | My verdict | What the principle says | Saved or overturned.
