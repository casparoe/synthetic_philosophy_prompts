So I've been obsessing over this idea I picked up from a pop-science book about self-replication and logic, and I want a short story built around it. Here's the concept, as I understand it: for anything to make a copy of itself, the information it carries has to do double duty. It has to be read once as instructions -- a recipe that gets followed to build the thing's parts -- and once as plain data that just gets duplicated symbol by symbol and handed to the offspring. If you only do the first, the recipe builds something but the offspring recieves no recipe of its own; if you only do the second, you get endless copies of a text that does nothing. Real cells apparently pull this off (the same DNA gets both transcribed/translated, i.e. interpreted, and replicated, i.e. copied verbatim), and the same trick is supposedly at the heart of Gödel's self-referential sentences and of those quine programs that print their own source code. I find the double-use move really elegant and I'd love to see it dramatized rather than explained.

Write me a short story (roughly 900-1300 words) set in a medieval scriptorium -- or some analogous pre-modern institution, feel free to move it -- where texts are copied by hand, and where one strange old manuscript is not just text but behaves like a machine: reading it makes the reader perform operations on other texts (snip out a passage, swap two sentences, append a fixed closing line, that kind of thing). The plot: a young scribe is tasked with producing a working copy of this manuscript and discovers that the naive approaches fail -- copying only the "instructions" produces a dead book that does nothing when read, while trying to make the book contain a full description of itself explodes into regress (the description would need to describe the description...). The resolution should be the double-use trick, discovered in-story: the scribe realizes the original gets read twice in two different modes, once as commands to be obeyed and once as mute symbols to be traced, and that this is the whole secret. No character should mention Gödel, DNA, or computers; keep it fully in-world, so the insight lands as the scribe's own discovery.

Format: give me the story first, no headings inside it, then one section titled "Author's note" (around 200-250 words) where you step outside the fiction and spell out how the story's mechanics map onto the actual logic of self-reproduction -- which element played the role of the interpreted program, which played the role of the passively copied data, and where exactly the regress got blocked. Also flag one deliberate simplification you made, if any.
