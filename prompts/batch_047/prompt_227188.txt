I've been reading about the Mencius–Xunzi dispute over human nature, and I'm stuck on identifying the actual crux. On the surface it looks like a clean empirical disagreement: Mencius holds that human nature is good, in that we are born with incipient moral dispositions (the famous sprouts) that need only be protected and extended; Xunzi holds that human nature is bad, in that our spontaneous inclinations produce strife, and that goodness is wei — conscious artifice built on top of nature through ritual, study, and teachers. But the further I read, the more the two seem to converge on nearly everything downstream: both think ordinary people can become good, both think this requires sustained effort and the right environment, and both treat bad upbringings as capable of ruining almost anyone.

When two positions disagree in their slogans but converge in practice, I start to suspect the dispute is definitional or normative rather than factual. And indeed both sides seem to be making moves about what deserves the label nature: Xunzi reserves the term for whatever operates spontaneously, without learning or effort, so artifice is not-nature essentially by definition; Mencius instead identifies our nature with whichever tendencies, when fully developed, are distinctive of human beings — so the sprouts count as nature, and the fact that they are easily stunted doesn't count against the claim.

Here is what I'd like explained:

1. Is there a genuine disagreement here that could in principle be settled by evidence about human psychology, or is it purely a fight over which partitioning of our dispositions receives the honorific? If it is partly empirical, what would a discriminating observation look like — what does each side predict that the other doesn't?

2. Does Mencius's thought experiment about the child at the well — the spontaneous alarm and distress anyone supposedly feels on seeing a child about to fall in, unmediated by calculation of advantage — actually do the work he needs? It seems to me Xunzi has absorptive moves available: he could grant the reaction while denying that a raw reflex constitutes virtue, or attribute it to early conditioning rather than birth. Which reading is more textually and philosophically defensible, and does Mencius have a response?

3. Each view comes with a different model of moral failure: Mencius treats badness as the destruction or stunting of something already present (the Ox Mountain image of a deforested hillside that once was lush), Xunzi as the failure to impose form on recalcitrant material, like straightening warped wood. Do these models cash out differently — for instance, in what moral education should feel like from the inside, or in what each expects from someone raised entirely outside a ritual tradition?

4. As a sanity check on my own framing: is it fair to gloss Mencius as claiming the moral objective is latent in the agent and education is amplification of an internal signal, and Xunzi as claiming the objective is external to the agent and education is training against an imported standard? Or does that mapping distort one or both?

Two requests about how you answer. First, don't flatten the dispute into a modern nature-vs-nurture debate if that's anachronistic; I want the crux as it actually stood between the two texts, even if it's stranger than the modernized version. Second, if any premise above is confused — if the claimed convergence on practice is overstated, if the empirical-versus-definitional question is malformed as applied to Warring States moral psychology, or if one of my sub-questions dissolves on inspection — say so plainly and explain why, rather than answering on my terms out of politeness.
