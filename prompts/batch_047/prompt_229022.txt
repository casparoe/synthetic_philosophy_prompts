I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am drafting a column on the rivalry among the leading AI laboratories. Every executive I interview insists they would prefer a world in which all labs moved more slowly and spent more on safety testing, yet each also insists that slowing down unilaterally simply hands the market to a rival. My instinct is to call this a race to the bottom, but when I tried to write the paragraph, I realized I could not state what makes some competitive spirals destructive and others benign. So let me report my settled verdicts on familiar cases, and ask what principle, if any, underlies them.

Spectators standing up at a concert: a trap. Everyone ends up uncomfortable, and nobody sees better than when all were seated. Doping in professional cycling: a trap. I believe nearly every rider would vote for a reliably enforced ban, yet abstaining alone is career suicide. Overfishing a shared stock: a trap. The inflation of educational credentials, where jobs that once required a secondary diploma now require a master's degree: a trap; everyone pays more tuition to stand in the same place. Social-media platforms tuning their feeds for maximal engagement: a trap; each platform's incentive makes the shared attentional environment worse.

But price competition among supermarkets, which has the same formal shape (each firm must cut margins because its rivals will), I call healthy. And here is the pair I cannot reconcile: I feel that elite athletes training ever longer hours is healthy, that it has raised the level of sport and given us better races, yet structurally it seems identical to doping — if every marathoner trained half as much, the races would be just as exciting and the athletes' bodies less broken.

Is there a principle that delivers these verdicts, or close? I suspect it must turn on some combination of: whether what is gained is purely positional; whether the costs fall on the competitors themselves or on third parties; and whether the participants would welcome a binding collective agreement. But I have not managed to assemble a formulation that both condemns doping and blesses training, or that blesses supermarket price wars and condemns tax competition between nations — that last case is one where I genuinely cannot settle my own verdict, since slashing corporate taxes to attract firms looks to one colleague like a race to the bottom and to another like healthy discipline on bloated governments.

Please give me the best principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause does which work; tell me honestly which of my reported verdicts it forces me to surrender; and then apply it to the case I actually have to write about: AI laboratories racing to release ever more capable systems, each convinced that unilateral caution is merely unilateral defeat.
