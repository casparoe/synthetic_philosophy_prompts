I TA a graduate seminar cross-listed between philosophy and computer science (the course is basically Minds and Machines but this year it's turned into a seminar on interpretability research in neural networks -- probes, sparse autoencoders, activation patching, the works). The room is split: the philosophers suspect the whole field is sophisticated curve-fitting plus storytelling, and the ML students think the philosophers are being precious about the word "representation." For the final I want one essay question that shows who actually understood the underlying issue and who just memorized the week's vocabulary.

Draft me a question on realism about interpretability: when, if ever, does an interpretability result warrant the claim that a network itself represents some concept or has some feature, as opposed to that claim being a useful fiction for the researchers looking at it? The question should be built around two hypothetical results the candidates have to evaluate, stipulated in the question so that no knowledge of any actual paper is required. Something like: (1) researchers take a transformer with completely random, untrained weights, train a linear probe on its activations, and find they can decode some linguistic property of the input (say, whether a sentence is in the past tense) well above chance; (2) a seperate team finds that the inventory of features their sparse autoencoder extracts from a *trained* model changes substantially depending on how they set the sparsity penalty and dictionary size. The crux I want the question to force: can the student articulate what would distinguish information merely being decodable from activations (perhaps thanks to the probe's own learning capacity) from the network actually using that information downstream, and can they say whether there is any fact of the matter about which features a network has if the feature inventory depends on the researcher's hyperparameter choices. Candidates should have to take a position on at least one of the two hypotheticals and defend it, not just survey options.

Constraints: answerable in two hours by someone who has done a unit on representation in classical cognitive science, so they can bring in ideas about causal role, content, misrepresentation, etc., but the question must not hinge on any specific named theory or author. 25 marks total. Deliverables: the question itself, a model answer of 600-800 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points with the marks each is worth, plus for each point a one-line note on the characteristic way a mediocre answer fumbles it (e.g., treating high probe accuracy as by itself settling that the network represents the property, or saying "it's all just matrix multiplication" as if that observation settles the content question).
