I am a PhD student in formal epistemology and recently overheard two colleagues dispute a variant of the Sleeping Beauty problem. On Sunday, Beauty learns that a fair coin will be tossed. If it lands heads, she will be awakened once on Monday; if tails, she will be awakened on Monday and Tuesday, with her memory erased between awakenings. Ana says that, upon awakening, Beauty should assign probability 1/3 to heads because there are three indistinguishable awakening-events, only one of which follows heads. Ben says the probability remains 1/2: awakening was guaranteed, so Beauty receives no evidence against heads. Ana responds that, over repeated trials, only one-third of all awakening records would follow heads, and that a bet offered at every awakening should therefore be evaluated using 1/3. Ben replies that this samples awakenings rather than coin tosses: selecting uniformly from awakening records gives tails twice the weight because tails produces twice as many records. Ana answers that Beauty’s problem is precisely self-locating—she is asking which awakening she is having, not merely how the coin landed. Ben counters that even if the probability of the centered proposition “this awakening follows heads” is 1/3, the objective chance of heads remains 1/2, and the betting argument changes the stakes by offering two bets after tails but only one after heads. Please adjudicate their disagreement point by point: identify where each is right, whether they are conflating different probabilities, and which position is more defensible overall.
