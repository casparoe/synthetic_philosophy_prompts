I run a small policy experimentation unit inside the government of a mid-sized city — about 400,000 residents. My office fields roughly a dozen proposals a year from departments and council members who want to "pilot" something: a new transit fare structure, a participatory budgeting expansion, a congestion charge, a redesigned permitting process. My job is to decide which proposals get piloted, how the pilots are run, and what happens afterwards. The stakes are real but not life-and-death; each pilot costs between tens of thousands and a few million dollars, and any decision to scale a pilot citywide ultimately rests with the elected council, not with me.

I have been trying to run this office on Deweyan lines — Dewey's experimentalism about inquiry and democracy, the view associated with works like his Logic: The Theory of Inquiry and The Public and Its Problems. I want the translation done properly. Write me a decision procedure I can actually apply to each proposal that lands on my desk: the questions to ask in order, what counts as an adequate answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a clear no, and where it must hand the question over to elected officials or to the affected residents because the matter is one of ends rather than means.

The procedure has to preserve the parts of Dewey's view that are easiest to lose in practice, so please build in explicit tests for the following:

- Whether the proposal begins from a genuinely problematic situation — a concrete difficulty experienced by identifiable people — or from a solution in search of a problem, which on Dewey's picture is not inquiry at all.
- Whether the pilot is designed as a test of a hypothesis, where certain outcomes would count as evidence against the policy and force revision or abandonment, or whether it is pilot-theater: a trial whose design guarantees "success" and functions as marketing for a decision already made. Give me diagnostic questions that distinguish the two, since sponsors rarely admit to the second.
- How to handle Dewey's claim about ends-in-view: the idea that goals stated in advance of inquiry are provisional and should be revised in light of what the means and consequences turn out to be. Concretely, I need to know what the procedure does when a pilot "fails" against its stated objectives but produces valuable unanticipated consequences, and when it "succeeds" against its stated objectives while generating harms nobody thought to measure.
- Dewey's account of the public: those indirectly affected by transactions who must organize to have a say over them. My pilots often affect people who are not at the table — shift workers, undocumented residents, commuters from outside the city. Tell me how the procedure should identify the affected public for a given proposal, and at what point a pilot's design is invalid because the people bearing its consequences have no channel into it.
- Where the procedure has to stop deferring to experimentation altogether. Some proposals are not revisable (a demolition, an irreversible infrastructure commitment), and some questions — how much inequality to tolerate, which neighborhoods matter — are not the kind that evidence settles. Mark these handoff points explicitly.

Then run the finished procedure on two proposals, showing its work at each step: (1) a six-month congestion pricing pilot in the downtown core, with revenues earmarked for bus service, proposed by a council member who is clearly already committed to a permanent scheme; and (2) a proposal to replace the city's public comment process at council meetings with a standing citizens' assembly of forty randomly selected residents. At least one of these should come out with an answer I will not enjoy defending.

Close with a candid account of what the procedure cannot do: where Dewey's theory itself gives out or becomes unhelpfully vague when pressed into administrative form, and which steps in my procedure are judgment calls wearing procedural clothing.
