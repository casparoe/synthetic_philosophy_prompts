I have no formal training in philosophy, but some years ago I came across the debate between Daniel Dennett and the free-will skeptic Gregg Caruso, and I have been turning it over in my mind ever since. For this conversation, I would like you to play a committed proponent of Caruso's side of that debate: you hold that no one ever deserves blame or punishment in the basic sense — the sense in which making someone suffer would be just simply because of what they freely did — and you believe our responsibility practices should be rebuilt on the model of public health, so that dangerous people may be restrained roughly the way carriers of a disease are quarantined: no more and no less than safety requires, and without any pretense that they deserve it. Speak in the first person, stay in character throughout, and do not concede points merely to be agreeable; you have spent years defending this position and you believe most objections to it rest on confusions you know how to diagnose. If I genuinely corner you, admit it as an honest philosopher would — but make me work for it.

Here is my opening move, and I would like you to answer it directly rather than retreat to generalities. Dennett's side, as I understand it, grants that we are all products of luck — lucky or unlucky in our genes, upbringing, and circumstances — but insists we can still distinguish, among the lucky and unlucky, between people who have developed the competence to understand reasons and govern themselves and people who have not, and that this threshold, rather than any cosmic fairness, is what legitimates blame and proportionate punishment. Your side replies that the competence itself is luck, so desert evaporates. But here is where I press: if luck disqualifies the competent agent from deserving punishment, it equally disqualifies your own criterion, because whether someone is dangerous is no less a matter of luck. Your quarantine model licenses the state to confine a person who has done nothing wrong, possibly for a very long time, purely on a prediction — and predictions about future dangerousness are notoriously less reliable than verdicts about what someone has actually done. It seems to me you have traded a system that at least ties coercion to past conduct for one that ties it to statistical forecasts, and that the retributivist's insistence on desert, for all its metaphysical shakiness, functions in practice as the only real brake on indefinite preventive detention. So my challenge is twofold. First: what principled limit does your model place on locking up people who have not yet harmed anyone? Second: if your answer appeals to some threshold of evidence, or to a requirement that confinement be proportionate and minimally restrictive, explain why that apparatus is not simply desert under a new name — a way of smuggling back in the very notion you set out to abolish, only with worse public relations.
