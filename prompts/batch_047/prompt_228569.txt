Right so I just spent about three hours in the pub arguing with my mate Dan, who has read Turchin and will not shut up about it. His position, roughly: history is not just one thing after another, societies go through secular cycles of a few centuries (integrative phases, disintegrative phases), and you can actually model and predict political instability from a handful of measurable variables -- things like elite overproduction (too many credentialed people chasing too few elite positions, so the losers turn against the system), mass immiseration, and declining state fiscal health. He even wheeled out Ibn Khaldun's thing about group solidarity decaying over generations. My position is that this is astrology with regression lines: tiny datasets, curve-fitting, retrodictions dressed up as predictions, and no account of the fact that people who read the predictions then behave differently.

We both realised, eventually, that we were arguing past each other because we'd never agreed on what a theory of macrohistory would even have to DO to count as good. So that's what I want from you. Not a verdict on cliodynamics itself -- the specification.

Purpose first: I mean a theory good enough to be decision-relevant, i.e. one whose outputs a government or an investor could act on with a horizon of, say, five to thirty years. Not a theory good enough to be interesting at a dinner party. Feel free to tell me if that purpose is itself incoherent, that's part of what I'm paying for.

Format -- use these sections, in this order:

**The floor** (~250 words): the non-negotiable desiderata. What any quantitative theory of large-scale historical dynamics must deliver to even count as a theory rather than a vibe -- with the consideration or case behind each one. Things I'm already chewing on: the prediction/retrodiction distinction and why postdicting the fall of Rome proves almost nothing; how to do inference when your n is a few dozen states and the data from 14th-century fiscal records isn't commensurable with modern statistics; whether the theory must name mechanisms measurable in advance rather than cycles inferred from the shape of the curve; what would actually falsify it. Add whatever I'm missing.

**The hard ones** (~250 words): the desiderata I'm unsure are even jointly satisfiable. Specifically: does the reflexivity problem (societies that learn the theory change their behaviour, a sort of Goodhart's law for macrohistory) knock out prediction entirely or only certain kinds? And can a theory both respect contingency (the assassination, the plague arriving in year X) and still predict? If some pair of requirements can't be met together, say which and which one you'd drop.

**Camp preferences** (~150 words): which demands are really just prejudices of one side -- e.g. Dan's demand that the theory be mathematical, my demand that it treat human agency as irreducible.

**The knife** (~100 words): the single requirement that excludes the most contenders, and what it does to structural-demographic theory specifically.

Stay neutral between me and Dan until the last section. And be honest where the spec itself is the interesting result.
