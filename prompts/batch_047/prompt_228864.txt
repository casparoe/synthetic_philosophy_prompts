Last semester I took a course on the ethics of blame, and my roommate -- who did not take the course -- produced an argument over dinner that has been bothering me ever since, because each step sounds right to me and the conclusion cannot be. Her argument: (1) A person has standing to blame another for a wrong only if their blame is not hypocritical; blame issued from a position one refuses to occupy oneself is morally compromised in a way that mere mistaken blame is not. (2) One's blame of another for wrong W is hypocritical whenever one does not hold oneself to the standard one's blame invokes -- that is, whenever one would not accept, or would not issue, the same blame directed at oneself for wrongdoing that is relevantly similar to W. (3) People are systematically more lenient toward their own wrongdoing than toward others': we excuse in ourselves what we condemn in others, we remember our own lapses as constrained by circumstances and others' lapses as revealing character, and this asymmetry is a well-documented feature of ordinary moral psychology rather than a defect of unusually bad blamers. (4) So for virtually every actual instance of blame, the blamer would not treat relevantly similar wrongdoing of their own the same way, and their blame is therefore hypocritical by (2). (5) Therefore virtually no one ever has standing to blame anyone for anything. (6) But a practice in which no one may blame anyone is not morality as we know it; it is the abolition of moral criticism. (7) Since that is absurd, standing to blame must not actually be hostage to hypocrisy -- which means, she concluded triumphantly, that the whole philosophical literature treating hypocrisy as a threat to standing is confused, and a hypocrite's blame is just as authoritative as anyone's.

I refuse to follow her to (7), since it seems to let the most flagrant "do as I say" meddler off the hook entirely, but I also cannot accept (5), and I cannot find the weak joint. My suspicion is that something shifts between (2) and (4) -- that "relevantly similar" does different work in the definition of hypocrisy than in the psychological generalization, or that "would not accept the same blame" conflates what a person would in fact feel with what they are committed to accepting -- but I have not been able to make this precise enough to convince her. Alternatively, perhaps the problem is upstream: maybe (1) conflates a defect in the blame with a defect in the blamer's entitlement to issue it, and those come apart. Please tell me which premise or step you would reject and why, explain why that step looked plausible in the first place, and say what it costs to reject it compared with the alternatives -- in particular, what you give up about the intuition that the hypocrite's blame really is defective, if you deny (1), or what you commit yourself to about how similar wrongs must be, if you deny (2). I'd most like to preserve both ordinary blaming practices and the thought that hypocrisy genuinely matters. Finally, please indicate how confident you are in your diagnosis overall, and single out the specific point in your answer where you are least sure you have it right.
