I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about responsibility when an autonomous system hurts someone, so let me just dump my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and you tell me what principle, if any, is doing the work.

1. A self-driving taxi brakes too late on a weird input -- someone pushing a bike sideways through a crosswalk at night -- and kills them. The investigation finds the software did what it was designed to do and testing met or exceeded industry norms. My verdict: nobody is blameworthy, but the company should pay out, and that feels like actual justice rather than a consolation prize.

2. A trading firm's model falls into a feedback loop with two other firms' models and helps cause a flash crash that wipes out a lot of retail savings. Every engineer followed best practices; no individual screwed up. My verdict: the firm is blameworthy, not merely on the hook financially. Somebody should have set things up so this couldn't happen, and "no individual did anything wrong" doesn't dissolve that.

3. A drone operator accepts the system's target identification, it turns out to be a wedding, people die. My verdict: the operator is blameworthy for just deferring, even though overriding correct systems 99 times out of 100 is how you get other people killed.

4. A radiologist accepts the system's "benign" read without double-checking, because she's spot-checked hundreds of its calls over two years and it's been more reliable than her own eyes. Patient dies. My verdict: she's not blameworthy. And yes, I know this looks structurally identical to case 3. That's the pair I can't reconcile.

5. An automatic lawnmower wanders into the neighbor's flowerbed and mulches it. My verdict: totally on the owner, zero gap, boring case.

6. A hospital deploys a triage chatbot; it tells someone with early stroke symptoms to rest at home and they die. My verdict: the gap feels real here but oddly I want to blame the procurement decision years earlier, not anyone at the hospital now.

My tentative hypothesis is that responsibility attaches to whoever made the last meaningful choice about the system's risk envelope -- the decision about what it was allowed to do and how sure we had to be -- rather than whoever was nearest to the harm or whose finger was on which button. That gets cases 2, 5, and maybe 6, but it seems to collapse cases 3 and 4 together, and it makes case 1 come out with the designers blameworthy, which I'm not sure I want.

So: is there a principle (mine repaired, or a different one) that captures most of these verdicts? Which of my verdicts does it force me to give up, and can you talk me into giving it up? And what does your principle say about the case I keep flip-flopping on: an autonomous research vessel, no human involvement for months, surfaces through sea ice and wrecks a fishing boat?
