Here's something that's been bugging me since a constitutional law seminar. Lots of democracies let a constitution bind future majorities in ways ordinary legislation never could: amendment rules requiring supermajorities, germany's eternity clause that puts certain principles beyond amendment altogether, independent central banks that elected governments aren't supposed to touch, debt brakes written into constitutions. Yet many of the same legal systems also run the opposite principle -- no parliament can bind its successors, so any ordinary law can be repealed later by simple majority. So the combined practice seems to be: the present can't tie the hands of the future, except that it definitely can, provided it uses the right procedure and calls the result a constitution.

People usually defend this with the Ulysses analogy: like Ulysses having himself tied to the mast before the sirens, a society ties its own hands while calm and sober so it can't do something rash when tempted later. But the analogy seems off to me in at least one important way. Ulysses binds *himself* -- same guy, same ears, he knows exactly what the temptation will be -- whereas a constitutional assembly binds people who mostly weren't born yet, facing circumstances nobody predicted. That looks less like self-binding and more like binding somebody else, which is normally the very thing we think no generation is allowed to do to the next.

So: what view of a political community across time does the practice of constitutional entrenchment actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory -- about whether "the people" is one continuing agent like Ulysses, and about when an earlier majority counts as more rational or more authoritative than a later one. Which rival views (say, that each generation is a distinct agent bindable only by its own consent) does the practice quietly rule out? Is the practice even internally consistent, given that it pairs entrenchment with the no-binding-successors doctrine? And what would concretely have to change in constitutional design if we dropped the continuing-agent picture and took the distinct-generations view seriously? If the practice is underdetermined and several different theories would license it, say so rather than picking one for me.
