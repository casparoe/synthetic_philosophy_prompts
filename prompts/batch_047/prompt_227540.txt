In foreign-policy circles, the dominant analogy for US–China relations is the so-called Thucydides trap, popularized by the political scientist Graham Allison: when a rising power threatens to displace an established one, the resulting fear makes war the historical default, as it supposedly did between Athens and Sparta. The framing leans on Thucydides' own methodological claim near the start of the History of the Peloponnesian War (Book 1), where he writes that his work is meant to be useful because events resembling those he records will recur in the future insofar as human nature stays the same. Audit this analogy for me. First, give the correspondence mapping explicitly, in prose: what plays the role of Athens, of Sparta, of the Spartans' fear, of the immediate quarrels over Corcyra and Potidaea that actually triggered the war. Then separate the disanalogies that matter from the ones that don't: nuclear deterrence, trade interdependence, the difference between a two-bloc Greek world and today's many-power system, and the selection problem in Allison's case list of the last five centuries. Finally, test the analogy against Thucydides' own criterion: he licenses inference from the past only where human nature, not structure, is doing the work, and the trap framing assumes exactly the reverse, so say whether the analogy survives its own source's test, whether the alarmist conclusion it is used to support stands once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether some other historical comparison would carry the argument better or whether the whole genre of great-power analogy should be retired here.
