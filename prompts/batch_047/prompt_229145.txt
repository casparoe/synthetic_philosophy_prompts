In my upper-division philosophy of science seminar I regularly defend evolutionary psychology against the charge that it traffics in just-so stories, and I had long assumed the charge was more of a rhetorical posture than an argument. Last term, however, a student reconstructed it in a form I have not been able to answer cleanly, and I would like a diagnosis before I teach the unit again. Her argument ran as follows:

(1) A hypothesis is scientific only if evidence bears on it asymmetrically: there must be possible observations that would count for it and possible observations that would count against it, and one must be able to say in advance, at least roughly, which are which.

(2) Adaptationist hypotheses about human cognition -- that we possess a dedicated cheater-detection module, that pregnancy sickness is an adaptation protecting the fetus from dietary toxins, that men and women differ in jealousy because the ancestral costs of infidelity were asymmetric -- are confirmed when observed behavior fits the hypothesis's retrodictions.

(3) But for any observed behavior whatsoever, some adaptationist accommodation can be constructed after the fact. If the predicted trait appears, it is an adaptation. If it fails to appear, the cost of the trait exceeded the benefit, or the relevant selection pressure was absent. If the opposite of the prediction appears, the environment of evolutionary adaptedness must have differed from what was assumed, or the trait is a byproduct of some other adaptation, or a developmental constraint blocked the optimum. Nothing in the method forbids any of these moves.

(4) Therefore no possible observation counts against an adaptationist hypothesis; such hypotheses can only be confirmed, never disconfirmed.

(5) By (1), adaptationist hypotheses in evolutionary psychology are not scientific.

Her conclusion in (5) is already more than I want to grant, but the real trouble is that the argument appears to prove too much. Phylogenetic reconstruction, historical geology, and much of cosmology also infer past processes from present traces, and in each case a sufficiently clever theorist can accommodate anomalous traces by revising auxiliary assumptions about initial conditions or background processes. If (3) and (4) are sound as applied to evolutionary psychology, parallel versions seem available for these fields -- and I am not prepared to conclude that inferring the tree of life from molecular sequences is unscientific. Nor do I want to win cheaply by pointing to cases where adaptationist reasoning got there first, as Fisher's sex-ratio argument is sometimes said to have done, since the student's premise (1) demands advance asymmetry and that case arguably supplies it.

Which step should I reject? I am most reluctant to give up (1), since some version of it seems required to distinguish empirical inquiry from unfalsifiable systematizing. If the flaw lies in (3) or (4), explain what an adaptationist hypothesis would have to look like for an observation genuinely to count against it, and whether actual research programs in evolutionary psychology meet that standard or merely gesture at it. And tell me what rejecting your chosen step costs -- what other commitments, in evolutionary biology or in the historical sciences generally, I would have to revise or abandon along with it.
