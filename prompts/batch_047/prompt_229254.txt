I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and right now I'm deep into a long feature on suffering-focused ethics. The view that's gotten under my skin is tranquilism, the position associated with Lukas Gloor: roughly, the claim that what matters, full stop, is the absence of craving and suffering. Pleasure that isn't the relief of some craving adds literally nothing of value; a stretch of experience completely free of craving is already perfect, whether it feels blissful or just flat and neutral; and apparently it follows that an empty universe is, in the only sense that matters, exactly as good as one full of serene, uncraving minds. Defenders insist it's not negative utilitarianism and not just rebranded Epicurean ataraxia. I've half-converted, honestly, which is a terrible thing to do mid-feature, so I want to stress-test it before I embarass myself in print.

Here's some texture from reporting. I interviewed a meditation teacher who swears advanced practitioners sometimes sit in states with no wanting whatsoever but a rich positive tone, and who say afterwards those states were worth having for their own sake, not as relief of anything. Suppose contemplative neuroscience eventually corroborates the phenomenology. Does that dent tranquilism, or does the committed tranquilist just shrug and redescribe the reports? More generally: what would actually count against this view? I don't want the lazy "but pleasure seems good!" rejoinder, I want the real map. Which intuitions or cases would a sincere tranquilist concede as trouble, versus dismiss as confusion? Are there findings -- in psychology, in how craving and pleasure dissociate in the brain, in what people choose when they reflect calmly -- that would genuinely wound it? Does it risk collapsing into a rival, like antifrustrationism or a preference-based view, in a way that would make the disagreement merely verbal?

Also: does tranquilism predict anything its rivals don't, or has it been insulated by clever redescription, where anything you point to as valuable gets relabeled as craving-relief after the fact? If it's unfalsifiable, is that a defect or just what every axiology looks like under pressure? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which one you'd lead with if you were trying to talk me out of it over coffee.
