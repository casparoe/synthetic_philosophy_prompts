I'm putting together a grad seminar on panpsychism and ethics and I want to run one session backwards: before we look at any actual panpsychist moral theory, I want the spec. Here's the setup. Suppose someone defends what I'll call the thermodynamic criterion: rudimentary sentience is present in fundamental physical processes generally, but suffering specifically tracks irreversible entropy production — so a superconductor carries on in painless equilibrium while a burning log, or a star, is a rolling catastrophe of morally relevant experience. Don't try to refute this particular criterion (its inventor can take care of themselves). Instead, write the desiderata list: what ANY theory that grounds moral patiency in fundamental physics would have to deliver before the question of its truth even gets going. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and mark whether it's non-negotiable or just one camp's preference.

Here's what I think it has to handle — tell me what I'm missing. It must get the boring cases right: an awake human in pain has status; a human under general anaesthesia presumably doesn't, though the theory had better say why without special pleading. It must not make moral status depend on our choice of coarse-graining, since physics descriptions are level-relative and moral status presumably isn't. It must distinguish having some status from how much status, and mere sentience from valenced experience — a criterion can find feeling everywhere and still find nothing good or bad anywhere. It must survive the combination problem: if micro-experiences exist, do they compose into subjects, and where does one subject end and the next begin? It must have something to say about aggregation across the cosmos, because if the criterion is right then the sun's moral weight outweighs the Earth's by a genuinely horrifying margin and someone has to say whether that's a result or a reductio. And it must be consistent with something like causal closure without rendering experience so epiphenomenal that we could never know about it — which would be an odd position for a theory argued for on experiential grounds.

The part I care most about: which pairs of desiderata look jointly unsatisfiable, and which single requirement knocks out the most candiates people actually defend. If the list itself is a result — no theory could meet all of it — say so plainly rather than quietly relaxing something. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases, not on a scorecard; a couple of sentences each on how the thermodynamic criterion and a more standard integration-or-complexity-based panpsychism fare against the list is plenty.

British English throughout, please. End with a final line of the form \answer{...} naming the one requirement you'd keep if you could only keep one.
