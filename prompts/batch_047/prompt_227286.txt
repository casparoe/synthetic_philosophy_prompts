Alright, so some background first. I spent forty years doing condensed matter physics, retired about three years ago, and since then I've fallen down the philosophy of science rabbit hole, which my old labmates find hilarious. Last week over beers one of them (a chemist, very much of the "just compute it" school) got into an argument with me about Milton Friedman's old essay on the methodology of economics from the fifties, the one where he defends wildly unrealistic assumptions on the grounds that it doesn't matter if the assumptions are false so long as the theory predicts well. My friend thought this was obviously right and also obviously what we did all our careers, frictionless this, infinite lattice that, spherical cows all the way down. I'm not so sure, and I want an essay that thrashes this out properly.

Here's the specific wrinkle I want the essay to engage with, because I think the usual back-and-forth about Friedman misses it. It seems to me there's a difference between what I'd call an idle idealization and a load-bearing one. An idle idealization is one where, if you relaxed it, the model's output would drift a bit but its basic behavior would survive: neglecting air resistance for a falling cannonball, treating the gas molecules as point masses. A load-bearing idealization is one where the phenomenon you're predicting only exists in the model because of the false assumption, so that removing the assumption doesn't perturb the result, it destroys it. My suspicion is that Friedman's as-if defense only really works for the idle kind, and that in economics (and honestly in some of my own field, if I'm being honest about things like mean-field theory) a lot of the idealizations are load-bearing, which makes the predictive-success defense start to look fishy in a way I can feel but can't quite articulate. Write me an essay that takes this distinction seriously: is it a real distinction or does it collapse under pressure (maybe everything is load-bearing once you look closely enough, or maybe load-bearingness is itself just a matter of degree and I'm inventing a false binary)? Does predictive success actually license indifference to false assumptions in the load-bearing case, or does it just license indifference to whether the theory is true, which is a seperate and weaker claim? And what happens to Friedman's position if you bring in the explanatory side of science, the fact that we don't just want predictions but want to understand why things happen, which is roughly where I remember Cartwright's old complaint about the laws of physics being relevant?

The essay should weigh both sides fairly, including the strongest instrumentalist reading of Friedman and the strongest realist pushback, but I don't want one of those on-the-one-hand-on-the-other-hand shrug endings. Reach a verdict and defend it, even if the verdict is that my distinction doesn't survive scrutiny. One format request: please write it as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. I want to read it like a letter from a very patient friend, not a report.
