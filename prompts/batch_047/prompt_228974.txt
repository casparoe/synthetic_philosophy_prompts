I am a PhD student in philosophy of technology writing a dissertation chapter on when AI systems may justifiably frustrate, refuse, or override their users. Please analyze the Boeing 737 MAX MCAS episode as a case study in AI paternalism.

Use only the broadly established public outline of the episode: MCAS was flight-control software intended to improve handling in particular conditions; it relied on angle-of-attack information, received erroneous sensor data, repeatedly commanded nose-down trim, and contributed to two fatal crashes in 2018–2019. The crews encountered behavior they did not expect, while the system’s operation had not been made sufficiently salient in training and documentation. Design and certification practices also relied on assumptions about how quickly and reliably pilots would recognize and counter unwanted commands. If a more specific detail is disputed or unnecessary, do not let the analysis depend on it.

For the purposes of the analysis, treat MCAS as an assistant-like automated system, but remain alert to the disanalogy: it was neither conversational nor obviously capable of beliefs, intentions, or judgment in the way discussions of AI assistants often assume.

Please structure the substantive analysis around the following questions:

1. **Mapping the case.** Identify the relevant agents and decision points: pilots, designers, Boeing, airlines, regulators, and passengers. At each point, specify the apparent options, the information available, and whether the relevant intervention was informational, advisory, friction-based, a refusal, or an override.

2. **Paternalism toward whom?** Distinguish at least four interpretations of the case:
   - *user-directed paternalism:* constraining pilots for their own good;
   - *third-party protection:* constraining pilots chiefly to protect passengers;
   - *epistemic paternalism:* privileging the system’s model or sensor inputs over the operator’s judgment;
   - *architectural paternalism:* embedding substantive safety judgments in system design rather than presenting them as contestable recommendations.

   Explain which interpretation best fits MCAS and which features it leaves unexplained.

3. **Override and refusal.** Analyze what a defensibly paternalistic assistant should have done in the situation MCAS faced: notify the pilots, explain its basis, seek confirmation, limit repeated intervention, defer to the pilots, or refuse to act on suspect data. Consider how the answer changes with confidence, urgency, reversibility, the operator’s competence, and the danger to non-consenting third parties.

4. **Opacity and contestability.** Assess how the moral analysis changes when an automated judgment is hidden, difficult to interrupt, or described to users only after it has already shaped their options. Is concealment an aggravating feature, or can temporary opacity sometimes be justified in high-stakes systems?

5. **Where the theory breaks.** State plainly what standard accounts of paternalism cannot explain here. In particular, consider distributed agency, corporate and regulatory responsibility, the fact that pilots were not exactly the system’s “users” in the ordinary consumer sense, and the fact that protecting passengers may be regulation rather than paternalism at all.

6. **What the case shows about AI assistants.** Derive principles for contemporary assistants that can refuse or override requests—for example, systems controlling vehicles, medical devices, finances, or hazardous equipment. Which principles transfer from MCAS, and which depend on features absent from ordinary chat-based assistants?

The chapter’s working hypothesis is that legitimate override requires more than the system being right: it also matters whether the user can understand, contest, and recover from the intervention. Test that hypothesis against the episode rather than assuming it.
