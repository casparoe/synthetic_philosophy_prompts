A friend and I argued until late last night about the following setup, and neither of us could put a name to the thing we were circling. Imagine a robot that refuses to act on any claim unless its built-in proof-checker has certified a proof of that claim from the robot's fixed axioms. Now suppose we want the robot to reason about itself: to make plans, it needs to accept that whatever its checker will certify tomorrow is true. So we hand it a blanket rule: anything my checker certifies is true. My friend insists the result is just Gödel's incompleteness theorems all over again -- a sufficiently powerful system limping along, unable to affirm its own reliability. But the way I understand the failure, it is worse than limping: once the blanket rule is in place, the checker can certify anything at all, including claims the original axioms ruled out. The system does not merely fail to gain self-trust; the self-trust mechanism detonates the whole thing.

Is there an established name for this specific collapse -- self-trust, added as a general rule, making a proof-driven agent trivial -- and for the theorem underlying it? I also want to know how exactly it relates to Gödel's second incompleteness theorem (my friend's candidate), whether it genuinely applies only to idealized logical machines or also bears on bounded, fallible reasoners like ourselves, and what the standard workarounds look like -- I dimly gather that people try things like trusting only short proofs, or only weaker versions of oneself.

Please structure the answer in four parts, in this order: "The name, if there is one" (one short paragraph), "Why this is not just Gödel" (about two paragraphs), "Does it touch ordinary human self-trust?" (one or two paragraphs), and "Known escape routes and their costs" (a brief list, one or two sentences per item).
