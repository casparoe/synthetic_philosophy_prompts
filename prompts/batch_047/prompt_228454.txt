Is there a name for this pattern in AI takeoff arguments? I keep noticing a version of it that doesn’t seem to be just “there’s a bottleneck.”

Example 1: suppose an AI lab gets a model that can produce promising new training algorithms in a few hours. But testing each one still takes weeks, so actual capabilities improve slowly. Then the model automates most of the testing pipeline, and now the limiting step is getting enough reliable evaluation data. Once that’s solved, the constraint moves again to safely integrating the new methods into a huge training run. Each obstacle is real, but each one is shorter-lived than the last because the improved system is also better at diagnosing and removing the next obstacle.

Example 2: a model might rapidly become much better at chip design, but the visible effect is delayed by fabrication and validation. People call that slow takeoff. Yet during the delay, the model is also improving simulation, verification, supply-chain planning, and the design of the next chip iteration. So by the time the first physical bottleneck is cleared, several later bottlenecks have already been partly cleared too. What looked like a flat period can turn into a sudden stack of improvements arriving close together.

The phenomenon I’m asking about isn’t merely a weakest-link process, because solving one link changes the next link. It also isn’t ordinary recursive self-improvement, since the AI may be recursively improving while the end-to-end pace still looks gated from the outside. And it isn’t exactly a simple delay, because the delay can be used to compress future delays.

Does this have an established name? If not, coin one and define it carefully. I’m especially interested in the boundaries: what has to be true for this pattern to count, how it differs from Amdahl-style fixed bottlenecks and from a normal production ramp-up, and what observations would tell us whether an apparently slow takeoff was storing up acceleration or just genuinely slow.
