I am a PhD student in political theory working on institutional epistemology, and I am trying to assess an analogy that comes up repeatedly in informal discussions of futarchy.

Proponents often say that a prediction market is like a thermometer: many people possess dispersed fragments of information, market incentives aggregate those fragments, and the resulting price gives a reading of what will happen if a particular policy is adopted. The analogy is then extended in a more ambitious direction. A legislature choosing policies is said to resemble a homeowner adjusting a heating system: instead of voting on whether a proposed intervention will work, the government can connect policy selection to the thermometer. If the conditional market indicates that a policy will produce better outcomes, the policy is adopted; otherwise, it is rejected. On this picture, futarchy is essentially a thermostat governed by a high-quality thermometer.

Act as an expert in philosophy of social science and the political economy of prediction markets. Think carefully, begin by brainstorming the possible correspondence relations, and then audit the analogy systematically. Specifically:

1. Construct an explicit correspondence table identifying what plays the roles of the thermometer, temperature, thermostat, heating system, homeowner, desired indoor climate, calibration, and feedback loop.
2. Distinguish the respects in which conditional prediction markets plausibly do aggregate information from the respects in which policy selection is unlike temperature control. Consider, for example, strategic manipulation, liquidity, correlated beliefs, selection into market participation, ambiguity about the welfare variable being predicted, and the possibility that traders anticipate both the market price and the institution’s response to that price.
3. Determine whether the analogy assumes that the measured quantity is causally independent of the measuring and decision procedure, and explain where that assumption fails in conditional policy markets.
4. Separate the disanalogies that undermine the analogy’s central argumentative use from those that are merely superficial.
5. Assess whether the thermometer-to-thermostat argument supports replacing democratic judgment with market-based selection, or only the weaker conclusion that market prices can be one evidential input into policy evaluation.
6. If another analogy captures the relevant institutional relation better, propose it and explain why.

Please make the reasoning precise enough to help me decide what work, if any, the analogy can legitimately do in an argument for futarchy.
