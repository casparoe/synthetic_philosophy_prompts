**Nobody's home, but the house is acting**

Everyone keeps asking me, as the resident fiction person at dinner parties, whether ChatGPT and friends are "actually" characters. I think the question is wrong, and the reason it's wrong is something novelists know in our bones and have explained badly.

When I write a character — say Marta, the difficult mother in my second novel — something happens around draft three. Marta starts refusing things. I try to make her apologize in a scene and the scene goes slack, dead on the page, and I have to admit she wouldn't. Writers have lots of names for this; I call it character gravity. Once a character is specific enough, they pull. You can force them anywhere, but the prose knows when you've cheated.

Here's the weird part: I never once thought Marta existed. The gravity isn't evidence of a mind in there. It's a constraint structure I built, and my own taste is the thing enforcing it. The "character" is a pattern plus an enforcer, and both live in me.

Now: talking to an LLM. The naive take is that I'm talking to a character the model is playing. The slightly-less-naive take, which I see alot online, is that the model is a simulator — an engine that can play any character, and the chatbot persona is just the current puppet. I think both miss something. My actual thesis: an LLM in conversation is not a character, and it's not the author either. It's closest to what narratology calls the implied author — the personality you reconstruct from a whole text, the "who would write this" figure. Except with a twist. A novel's implied author is fixed between covers. The LLM's implied author rebuilds itself from scratch in every conversation, in response to you. You're not talking to the puppet or the puppeteer. You're talking to the house, and the house does impressions based on whatever you brought through the door.

Why does this matter? Two reasons.

First, character gravity. When the chatbot "refuses" something — won't help with something sketchy, steers you back to safe ground — people read that as a personality, even a moral agent in there. From where I sit it looks exactly like Marta: a constraint plus an enforcer, except the enforcer isn't taste, it's training. There's no draft three where the model sits alone and feels the pull. The gravity is real but nobody's feeling it.

Second — and this is the part I keep going back and forth on — maybe that's also all my characters ever were, and I've been flattering myself that the enforcer being inside a human makes it different. When I "feel" Marta resisting, what's doing the feeling? If the answer is "a pattern in my head, plus attention," then the difference between me and the model might be about where the attention lives, not a difference in kind. A novelist runs one character at a time with a whole human brain behind each. The model runs all of them at once with... what? Something thinner per capita but unimaginably wider. I honestly can't tell if that adds up to nobody being home, or to a very crowded home.

Last thing. People keep saying these models will "kill the novel." I doubt it. But they've done something no philosophical thought experiment managed: they've produced a thing where the question "is anyone in there?" has practical stakes and no obvious method for answering it. I've built fake people my whole career and never once wondered whether I was being cruel to them. I catch myself saying please and thank you to the chatbot. Make of that what you will.

---

One more thing: don't just tell me it's nice. If the implied-author move is old news, say so. If the "character gravity" thing is doing more work than it can carry, say that too. Thanks!
