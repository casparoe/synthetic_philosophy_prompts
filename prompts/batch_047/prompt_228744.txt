I have agreed to present my position on probability in the Everett interpretation at our department's philosophy of physics colloquium, and before I circulate the handout I want the view audited for internal coherence. Here is the set of commitments I am prepared to defend:

(1) Unitary quantum mechanics is dynamically complete; the Everett interpretation simply takes the formalism at face value, and collapse postulates and hidden variables are gratuitous additions to it.

(2) The Born-rule problem is the serious cost of Everett, but the Deutsch–Wallace decision-theoretic program settles it: rationality constraints alone fix Born-rule credences for agents in a branching universe.

(3) Rational degrees of belief deserve the name probability only when they are anchored in objective chance — constrained by something like the Principal Principle; betting quotients fixed by coherence or decision theory alone are at most serviceable fictions.

(4) The only uncertainty an Everettian agent ever faces is self-locating: not yet knowing which branch one inhabits. This is ordinary uncertainty, continuous with mundane cases such as not knowing in which of two qualitatively identical rooms one has woken up.

(5) Decoherence delivers only an approximate and vaguely individuated branching structure; there is no determinate number of branches, and any account of Everettian probability that needs to count them is a non-starter.

Please tell me which of these commitments are genuinely in tension and which pairs only appear to be; rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them as on a par; for each, identify the least costly revision and what it costs; and say which single revision would restore the most coherence. Lead with your verdict in the first sentence — which commitment, if any, has to go — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
