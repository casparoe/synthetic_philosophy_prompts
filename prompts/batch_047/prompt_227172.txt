I got roped into a volunteer wetland restoration group after my apartment flooded twice in three years, and now I’m stuck in these meetings where people talk about plants like they’re both museum pieces and Lego kits. The county rule, roughly, is that eelgrass and rush seeds have to come from within 40 km and match herbarium specimens from before 1960, unless the climate resilience clause kicks in, in which case we may use southern stock if it is functionally equivalent. But last month the board rejected a heat-tolerant southern strain because genetic tests suggested old hybrid ancestry, while also approving a local-looking line that everyone admits probably hybridized too, just earlier and harder to detect. So I keep asking: what are we treating a native species or local ecotype as, exactly?

What picture of natural kinds does this practice presuppose? Is it assuming nature really has joints there and the pre-1960 samples track them, or is native just a messy historical lineage label we use because courts, grants, and volunteers need bright lines? Does the hybrid anxiety only make sense on something like an essence view, or can a homeostatic cluster kind view, a lineage view, or a bluntly pragmatic view make sense of excluding some hybrids and forgiving others? Please separate what the policy strictly commits us to from what it merely vibes with, point out any place where the genetics rule, the 40 km rule, and the warming clause seem to presuppose different ontologies, and say what would concretely change if we admitted the kinds are partly made by management goals rather than discovered. Also tell me if the practice is definately incoherent or just underdetermined in a way I’m overreading because I’m annoyed at the board.
