Last weekend I was at my friend Dev's place, he has that Escher print on his wall with the two hands drawing each other, and it kicked off an argument between him and my other friend Maya (she's a philosophy grad student, he's a software engineer) that went in circles for alot of the evening. At the end they demanded I referee, and I honestly couldn't do it, so I'm outsourcing.

Maya's position was that genuinely total self-modification is impossible. Whenever something changes itself, she said, the change has to be carried out according to something that isn't itself changed in that act. Escher's real hand hovers above the two drawn ones: the picture can tangle its levels all it wants, but the level doing the drawing stays inviolate. She claimed the same holds for any self-rewriting program — even if 100% of the code gets overwritten, the interpreter executing the rewrite isn't itself rewritten, so there's always an untouchable level, and people who fantasize about fully self-transforming systems (AIs rewriting their own goals, constitutions that can amend even their own amendment rules) are kidding themselves.

Dev pushed back that she's conflating the system with its medium. The CPU staying fixed is like the paper staying fixed — of course the physics underneath is stable, but that's not a rule *of* the system, it's just the substrate, and within the system literally every rule can be up for grabs. He said he's actually written a small program where the module that does the rewriting is itself rewritten on every cycle, so nothing in the software is exempt. Maya's counter was that at the moment of the swap there still has to be some fixed procedure that decides which new rewriter replaces the old one, and even if that procedure is used exactly once, it's functioning as an inviolate level for that instant — otherwise the change is arbitrary and basically uncaused. Dev's last move, which I'm not sure was totally fair, was to say this proves too much: if "the inviolate level" just means whatever state the system happens to be in while the change happens, then her thesis is trivially true of every process ever and explains nothing. Causal explanation only needs temporal ordering, he said, not something exempt from change, and she's confusing "unchanged during the act" with "unchangeable in principle."

Can you go through their exchange move by move, say who's right about each specific claim, and then who's more right overall? And if they're both missing something — some third way of carving up what's going on — I'd like to hear that too.
