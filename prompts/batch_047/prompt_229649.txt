I write a column on science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about consciousness that hinges on how seriously to take epiphenomenalism -- specifically the precise thesis that phenomenal properties (what it's like to see red, to feel pain) are real but have no causal influence on any physical event, including the neural events underlying our reports and judgments about them. For the piece I invented a thought experiment I call the Detached Dial. Imagine a perfected neuroscience that lets us intervene on a subject's brain in two independent channels: we can vary what it's like for her (say, slowly shifting the phenomenal character of her color experience along a spectrum) while holding her verbal reports, introspective judgments, and all downstream behavior completely fixed; or we can vary her reports while holding the phenomenal character fixed. Suppose the first intervention works: her inner world changes dramatically and she never notices, never says anything different, never becomes curious. My intuition is that such a result would not merely support epiphenomenalism but would expose something incoherent in our concept of knowing our own experience; a colleague's intuition is that it would just be a sad empirical finding about us.

Give me your credence that epiphenomenalism, as defined above, is true -- a number, not a hedge -- and derive it in the open rather than asserting it. Decompose the question into the sub-questions it depends on: roughly, whether the causal closure of the physical is as secure as it's usually taken to be; whether systematic causal overdetermination of our phenomenal judgments is a live option or a degenerate one; whether making true judgments about one's own qualia requires those qualia to cause the judgments, or whether some non-causal relation (identity, constitution, acquaintance) could ground them; and whether the Detached Dial is even coherent, i.e., whether it smuggles in dualist assumptions or merely makes vivid what epiphenomenalism already predicts. Assign a rough probability to each sub-question, show how they combine into your final number, and tell me which single sub-question that number is most sensitive to -- and what argument or finding, if any, would move it most.

Mine is about 0.15; my colleague's is about 0.6. Tell me where our disagreement most likely lives in your decomposition. Throughout, be careful to separate what is actually settled (e.g., what epiphenomenalism logically entails, what closure arguments do and don't assume) from what is genuinely contested among philosophers who work on this, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
