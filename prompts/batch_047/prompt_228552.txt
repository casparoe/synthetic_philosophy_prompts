I'm a PhD student in sociology (my dissertation is on volunteering and civic engagement, so this is adjacent to my stuff but not exactly my lane), and I somehow ended up as the student rep on my city's community-grants panel. We have one big grant left this cycle and two finalists, and the panel chair asked each of us to write a one-page decision memo before the meeting. I want mine to actually be good, so I'd like you to help me draft it. Please take on the role of a careful philosopher-social scientist hybrid who writes institutional decision memos for a living, and before giving me the polished memo, do a short brainstorm of the considerations on both sides, including the strongest argument for whichever option we end up recommending against.

Here's the case. Finalist A is a church-affiliated group running a food bank and a visitation program for isolated elderly people. Their track record is genuinely impressive -- low volunteer turnover, people show up for years, clients report high trust in the staff, and their volunteers also do a lot of informal helping that never shows up in the paperwork. Finalist B is a secular nonprofit with a similar mission; their numbers are fine but flatter -- decent outcomes, more volunteer churn, less of the extra informal stuff. Several panel members want to fund A on the straightforward grounds that the money should go where it demonstrably produces more community trust and reliability. The catch, raised by one skeptical member, is that the church's program grew out of caring for its own congregation first and mostly recruits through the parish, and there's some reason to think tight-knit religious groups generate intense loyalty and helping mainly *within* the group, with outsiders benefiting as a kind of spillover -- so the very mechanism that makes A effective might be one a public funder can't fully endorse, and might even be weakened by our standard grant conditions, which require open enrollment and non-preferential service to all residents regardless of affiliation. A counter-argument floating around is that a healthy secular society should be able to generate the same trust and commitment without the religious scaffolding, and public money should bet on that rather than on in-group bonds.

So: draft the memo -- recommend funding A, funding B, or funding A with conditions (like the open-enrollment requirement), and give reasons the losing side could actually respond to rather than a strawman. One thing I specifically want the memo to grapple with: what would it even mean for a program to "make people more moral" in a way a city government is allowed to care about -- is generalized trustworthiness toward strangers the right metric, or is deep commitment to a particular community also a legitimate public good, and does it change things if the mechanism only works because members share beliefs the city itself can't endorse? Keep it honest about what we don't know empirically, and end with a clear bottom-line recomendation.
