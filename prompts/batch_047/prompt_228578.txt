At a meeting of my workplace union last week, we were debating whether to refuse to cross the picket line of the cafeteria workers, who are employed by a contractor and are on strike over wages. Honoring their line would put us in breach of the no-strike clause in our own contract, and several members were reluctant. At that point our shop steward invoked the old labor slogan 'an injury to one is an injury to all,' said it settled the question, and the room went quiet in a way I found unsatisfying. The slogan obviously did something rhetorically, but when I pressed him afterward on what exactly it claims, he got impatient, as if asking for precision were itself a betrayal of solidarity.

I suspect the slogan compresses several quite different theses, and I want them separated before the next meeting. It might be a causal-empirical claim: tolerating an injury to one worker measurably weakens the bargaining position of every worker, so the injury literally propagates. It might be a normative claim: members owe one another a duty of solidarity such that an injury to one *should be treated as* an injury to all. It might be a prudential reciprocity claim: I protect you now so that you protect me later, essentially an insurance arrangement. It might be a constitutive claim about what union membership is: to join is precisely to adopt the standpoint that fellow members' injuries count as one's own, so that refusing to act is not breaking a rule but defecting from the identity. Or it might be a purely mobilizing utterance with no propositional content at all, closer to a cheer than a thesis. There may be readings I am missing.

Please lay out the distinct claims the slogan could be making, as a numbered list. For each, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false on its face, and -- this is the part I most need -- whether that reading, if true, would actually support the steward's conclusion that we are obliged to honor the cafeteria workers' picket line even at the cost of breaching our own contract. Note also which reading the steward most likely meant, given how he used it, and which reading a skeptic in the room would find hardest to dismiss. Keep the whole answer under 600 words.
