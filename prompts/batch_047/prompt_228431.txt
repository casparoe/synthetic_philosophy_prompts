So next Thursday I'm doing my second-year seminar talk for my department's logic and religion reading group, and I'm already nervous. Quick background: I'm a philosophy PhD student working on Buddhist logic, and my thesis in the talk is roughly this -- the tetralemma refutations in Nāgārjuna (you know, the "it's not x, not not-x, not both, not neither" pattern) don't actually commit him to rejecting bivalence or exploding into contradictions. My claim is that the four corners are being *declined* rather than *denied* -- like, Nāgārjuna is refusing to make any assertion about the topic because every assertion would presuppose the svabhāva-laden conceptual scheme he thinks is broken, so it's more of an illocutionary move than a truth-functional one. I lean on the Indian distinction between prasajya and paryudasa negation for this, and I argue that once you read the negations as prasajya all the way down, you get emptiness as a kind of global anti-realism about concepts, not a logical heresy. The upshot I push is that the modern paraconsistent/dialetheist readings (where Nāgārjuna accepts some corners of the tetralemma as true contradictions) are well-meaning but anachronistic, and ironically LESS charitable than the quietist reading because they attribute an unnecessary logical scandal to him.

The audience is what's killing me. It's a mixed bag: about half are formal logic people who know their way around paraconsistent logic, LP, four-valued stuff like Belnap's semantics, and who are kind of in love with the dialetheist reading of Madhyamaka because it's a fun case study for them. The other half are Buddhism/Sanskrit people who are deeply suspicious of anyone "analytic-izing" Nāgārjuna, and some of them think any reading that gives him a *position* (even mine) already misunderstands the point of prasaṅga method.

What I need is a prep sheet, not a pep talk. Give me the objections I should expect, roughly in the order they'll come and phrased the way each faction would actually phrase them (logic people talk different than the Sanskritists).
