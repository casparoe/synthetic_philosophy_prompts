I work at a company whose customer-service chatbot, I've come to realize, is treated by many users as a person — and this has made me take seriously Dennett's argument that AI systems that convincingly pass as human are a kind of counterfeit person, analogous to counterfeit currency, whose creation should be treated as a serious offense, with mandatory disclosure and provenance marking, rather than regulated as ordinary software. Next month I will defend this position at an industry panel on AI governance. The audience: mostly machine-learning researchers and product leads who regard chatbots as mere interfaces, a couple of policy scholars sympathetic to regulation but skeptical of enforcement, and one philosophy-trained researcher who knows Dennett's work well. Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections in the order they are likely to come, phrased as this audience would phrase them — including the one rooted in Dennett's own intentional stance, to the effect that if a system reliably passes, his philosophy of mind leaves no principled line between the real and the counterfeit. For each objection, say what I should concede and what I should hold, and give a committed reply rather than a menu of options. End with the objection I am hoping nobody asks, and whether it has a good answer.
