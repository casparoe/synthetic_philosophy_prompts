I have no academic background in political philosophy, but I have followed debates about Scotland, Catalonia, and Quebec and have been trying to write down my own position on secession. I would like to know whether the following commitments can consistently be held together:

1. A territorially concentrated community can have a moral right to become independent after a clear, informed, and durable majority votes for independence, even if it has not suffered serious oppression.

2. The rest of the existing state should not have an equal vote on whether that community may leave, although it may legitimately negotiate over debts, assets, borders, citizenship, defense arrangements, and other consequences of separation.

3. If a province votes to leave a country, then a contiguous district within that province that clearly votes to remain should ordinarily be allowed to remain with the original country. For example, if 55 percent of a province votes for independence but 70 percent of its northern district votes against it, the northern district should not automatically be carried into the new state.

4. Not every group that can draw a boundary around itself has a right to secede. A wealthy street, suburb, or arbitrary collection of municipalities should not be able to leave merely because a local majority wants lower taxes or different regulations.

5. There is an important difference between having a moral right to secede and having a unilateral legal right to do so. Even where independence is morally permissible, constitutional procedures and negotiations may appropriately govern how it occurs.

6. The borders, electorate, and question for a secession referendum should be fixed before the vote rather than revised afterward to produce a desired result.

7. Minorities within a seceding territory deserve strong protections, but an individual who dislikes the referendum result does not thereby acquire a right to remove his or her house and land from the new state.

Please audit this set rather than simply explaining secession in general. Which commitments are in genuine tension, which apparent tensions dissolve once distinctions are made, and how severe is each problem? In particular, I am unsure whether my views about counter-secession can be reconciled with my claim that not every majority-drawn territory may secede, and whether negotiated procedures can matter without giving the existing state a practical veto. For each serious tension, identify the cheapest revision that would restore consistency and what that revision would cost. Finally, if one commitment had to be modified to make the overall position as coherent as possible, which should it be?
