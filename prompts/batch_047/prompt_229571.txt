I write a magazine column on science and ideas, and I'm currently finishing a long essay on whether history can be made scientific. The piece leans heavily on Carl Hempel's 1942 paper The Function of General Laws in History, and in particular on the stretch where he argues that explaining an action by citing the agent's reasons works only because it tacitly presupposes an empirical generalisation about how rational agents characteristically behave in such circumstances. My editor, before signing off, has asked me to submit the essay's philosophical commitments to a consistency audit, and I'd like you to act as the auditor. Here is the line the essay takes, stated as five commitments:

(1) Hempel's covering-law model states the correct ideal of explanation in history: a genuine explanation must at least tacitly subsume its explanandum under general laws, and where the laws are unstated the result is what he calls an explanation sketch rather than something of a different kind. (2) The best narrative histories — thick accounts of revolutionary crowds, diplomatic breakdowns, court politics — explain their events fully and completely without ever stating a law, and demanding laws of them would add nothing of explanatory value. (3) With William Dray, I hold that explaining an action by reconstructing what the agent had good reason to do is a distinct and self-sufficient mode of explanation: the principle of action involved is normative (it says what a reasonable person would do), not an empirical hypothesis about what people in fact do. (4) Nonetheless, Hempel's analysis of reason-explanation is correct: such explanations succeed only because they quietly rely on a contingent regularity about the conduct of rational agents. (5) Singular causal claims in history — that a drought caused a migration, that an assassination triggered a war — are explanatory on their own, just as they are in everyday life, without needing any law-backing to earn their keep.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture on the philosophy of history. Go through the five commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and which are mutually supporting. Where a tension is genuine, say precisely what would have to give and what that concession would cost the essay's overall argument; where it is merely apparent, show how it dissolves. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and end by identifying the single cheapest revision to the set. Write in British English throughout. After your prose assessment, summarise the audit in a table with the following columns: "Pair" (the commitment numbers involved), "Status" (genuine tension, apparent tension, or mutually supporting), "Severity" (high, medium, or low, with brief justification in a word or two), "What must give" (which commitment, or which part of it), and "Cheapest fix and its cost" (one sentence each).
