I host a small philosophy podcast and have somehow landed an interview with a philosopher whose recent book defends what I'll call the raw-feel view: the claim that what makes pain bad, full stop, is its distinctive felt quality -- the hurting itself. On her view, anything that reduces that felt intensity (even by one iota) reduces the badness by exactly that much, and nothing else about the person's psychology matters to how bad the pain is.

I have no academic training, but I've been thinking about pain asymbolia ever since I read about it: patients, sometimes after certain brain lesions or surgical interventions like cingulotomy, who still report feeling pain and can describe its location and intensity, yet say it no longer bothers them. They don't pull their hand away, don't ask for relief, and seem oddly cheerful about it. Morphine patients sometimes describe something similar -- the pain is still there, but it has stopped mattering to them.

Here's the thought experiment I've been turning over, which I want the interview to build toward. Imagine a drug, call it Apathyl, that works perfectly and reversibly: it leaves the felt quality of pain completely intact -- same burning, same throbbing, same intensity -- but eliminates all concern about it. On my guest's view, it seems to follow that giving someone Apathyl instead of a standard painkiller does them just as much good, since the hurting continues untouched either way... no, wait, that's backwards: on her view Apathyl does no good at all, because the felt quality is unchanged. So it follows that a hospital that switched all its patients to Apathyl would be leaving all suffering exactly where it was, and a doctor who offered only Apathyl to a patient begging for relief would have nothing to answer for. I find this conclusion very hard to accept, and I suspect it's where the raw-feel view is weakest.

Write me a sequence of eight to ten interview questions. I don't want a balanced tour of the issues; I want the sequence itself to take a side. Commit to one diagnosis of where her view is most exposed -- the Apathyl case, the asymbolia evidence, or something else you judge stronger -- and build the whole sequence around pressing that single point, with each question anticipating her most likely reply and cutting it off, so she can't retreat to vague talk of further research. Include at least one question that lets her state her position in its strongest form early, so the later pressure doesn't seem unfair. End with a question about what finding or case would make her abandon the view. Briefly explain, at the end, why you chose the line of attack you did over the alternatives, and flag anywhere the questions lean on a contested reading of what asymbolia patients actually experience rather than something established.
