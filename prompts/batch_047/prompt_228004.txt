I teach a third-year undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of AI safety (13 students, one 110-minute session, they can all fit around tables in groups of 4 or 5). Next week we're doing impact measures, and I've assigned the Armstrong and Levinstein paper "Low Impact Artificial Intelligences". The claim I want the whole session organised around is this passage, where they state the core problem:

"The first challenge is, of course, to actually define low impact. Any action (or inaction) has repercussions that percolate through the future light-cone, changing things subtly but irreversibly. It is hard to capture the intuitive human idea of 'a small change'."

Most of my students read that and nod along like it's obviously true, and I want them to *earn* the nod. So: design me a classroom activity, not a discussion, that makes them run face-first into this problem before we ever talk about it. My rough idea is something like this, feel free to improve it or replace it: you give each group a simple toy world (a grid on paper with tokens, or a set of labelled objects on a table, something cheap), one group writes down an explicit "impact rule" for what counts as a big change to that world, and then another group plays a powerful AI with a silly goal (maximise paperclips, get all the red tokens in one corner, whatever) and tries to achieve it while technically satisfying the written rule. My hunch is that the rule-writers will discover their measure is either so strict that the AI can't do anything at all, or has loopholes you could drive a truck through, or only works because they smuggled in their own sense of which changes *matter* — which is exactly the point that "low impact" can't be specified without already having solved the value problem. But I haven't run anything like this before and I want someone to actually think through the mechanics.

Please answer as if you were someone who's both deep in the AI safety literature and has run a lot of classroom games and simulations — you know how groups actually behave, not how the lesson plan says they should. Think carefully about incentive structure and about what will predictably go wrong. Maybe sketch two or three candidate designs quickly for yourself first, then commit to one and give me the full version of that one.

Format it like this, in this order:

**The idea in one paragraph** — what the activity is and what it should make students feel, ~100 words.

**Setup and materials** — what I need to prepare, what the toy world actually is, roles, group sizes. ~200 words.

**Rules and scoring** — the actual game mechanics, precise enough that I could hand them to students on a sheet. Include how the "AI" group wins, and how rule-writers win or lose, so both sides have real motivation. ~300 words.

**Timeline** — minute-by-minute for the 110 minutes, including when groups swap roles and when the second round happens. ~150 words.

**The debrief** — how I map what just happened onto the Armstrong-Levinstein passage and onto the standard moves in this literature (penalising changes across many variables, trying to offset or "undo" impacts, whitelisting acceptable changes). I want them to see that the loopholes they exploited are basically the offsetting problem, and that their instinct for which loopholes "don't count" was doing normative work. ~250 words.

**What will go wrong** — the two or three most likely ways this flops in practice (e.g. a group that just refuses to engage, or a rule so vague the game stalls) and what to do about each. ~150 words.

**What the game can't teach** — be honest about which features of the real problem the toy world hides (unlimited action space, no genuine uncertainty, students can see each other's intentions). ~100 words.

One constraint: no technology, no printing costs beyond a couple of A4 sheets, and it has to work with students who are smart but have zero technical background. Also the debrief has to land even if one group's game totally collapsed — that's data too, not a failure I need to rescue.
