Last weekend, over a long dinner, a close friend and I got into a heated argument about whether political decisions should be left to democratic majorities or handed over to those with relevant expertise. Midway through, he deployed an analogy I keep turning over in my head: he said that trusting voters to set policy is like letting airline passengers vote on how to fly the plane. Nobody on board, he pointed out, would put the landing to a show of hands; we defer to the pilot because she has certified competence and everyone else does not. Governing, he argued, is far more complex than aviation, so if deference is rational in the cockpit it should be rational in the state — and he took this to license a fairly strong epistocratic conclusion, something like weighting or restricting political influence by demonstrated knowledge. I pushed back with the thought that passengers on a plane all share one goal — arriving safely — whereas citizens disagree about where the country should go in the first place, and also about whose arrival counts. But I'll concede I was improvising, and he had a rejoinder: he said even the destination of a flight involves trade-offs passengers are incompetent to weigh, like fuel burn against speed against turbulence, and we still don't vote on those.

I'd like you to act as a political philosopher with expertise in democratic theory and the epistocracy debate, and to audit this analogy rigorously on my behalf. Please begin by brainstorming freely — list every correspondence the analogy relies on and every disanalogy you can think of, including ones that initially seem damning but may not survive scrutiny — and then organize your analysis. I want the mapping made explicit (a table of correspondences is welcome: pilot, passengers, destination, flight manual, turbulence, and so forth, against their supposed political counterparts), followed by a careful separation of the disanalogies that actually matter for my friend's intended conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. In particular, please examine what the analogy quietly imports that politics lacks: a single agreed-upon objective, certification standards for competence that are independent of the people being governed, the fact that passengers consent to the flight and can choose airlines, and the difference between expertise about means and authority over ends. I'm also interested in the reverse direction — whether there's anything the plane case has that politics lacks and that might actually strengthen his argument, since I don't want to win this by selective auditing.

Conclude with a verdict on what, if anything, the analogy can carry: does my friend's strong epistocratic conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or does the analogy at most support a weaker claim (for instance, insulated technocratic institutions within a fundamentally democratic framework)? If the pilot-passenger comparison fails, please propose the best available replacement analogy for each side of the dispute — one a fair-minded epistocrat could use, and one a fair-minded democrat could use — and briefly say what each of those can and cannot establish. I'd like to bring something genuinely rigorous to the rematch, not just better rhetoric, so please reason carefully and flag any point where you think the honest answer is that intuitions simply diverge.
