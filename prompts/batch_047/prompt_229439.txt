Bit of context first. I'm German, grew up with the full memory-culture programme — school trip to a former concentration camp, Stolpersteine on my street, the lot — and last summer I visited my girlfriend's family in rural Georgia (the US one). At a barbecue her uncle defended the Confederate battle flag on his truck with "it's heritage, not hate". I pushed back a bit and got absolutly nowhere, mostly, I realised afterwards, because I couldn't work out which claim I was even disagreeing with.

Since then I keep noticing the same move in different national costumes. Japanese politicians describe Yasukuni Shrine visits as honouring their war dead rather than endorsing what those dead fought for. Turkish relatives of mine insist that recognising the Armenian genocide would mean calling their grandparents murderers, so it can't be right. And Germans themselves say "Nie wieder" while loudly disagreeing about whether that means never again genocide or never again war, which turn out to pull in opposite directions the moment someone asks about sending weapons somewhere. Same grammar, different fillings: my ancestors, my grief, my identity — not the bad thing.

So here's what I want. Take "heritage not hate" and lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims it could be making. Some candidates I can half-see myself: a claim about the speaker's own motives (I don't mean anything hateful by it); a semantic claim about what the flag means, full stop; a historical claim about what the Confederacy was actually for; a claim about who gets to fix a symbol's meaning (the people displaying it? the people it was used against? historians? majority vote?); a causal claim about what displaying it does to bystanders; maybe a normative claim that sincere non-hateful motives license the display regardless of how it's received. For each reading: state it precisely, say whether it's a platitude, defensible, or false, and what evidence would settle it. Then tell me which reading the uncle most likely meant, and which reading his argument actually needs — because I suspect the slogan gets its power from sliding between a reading that's true and a reading that would justify the flag, which aren't the same one.

Then the part I really care about: run each reading through a transplant test. If a Japanese official says "filial piety, not militarism" about Yasukuni, or my Turkish cousin says "our grandparents, not genocide", does each precisification of the slogan work equally well there, better, or worse? If a given reading only works in the American case, that's diagnostic of something — tell me what. And say what Germany's approach (banning the symbols outright, mostly) amounts to as an implicit verdict on which reading of the slogan is right — or whether banning symbols is answering a different question entirely.

Menu format with verdicts please, not one long essay, and write it in British English.
