I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I was appointed last year -- somewhat to my surprise, since my usual beat is particle physics and machine intelligence -- as the sole lay member of my country's Commission on Judicial Accountability, the body charged with reviewing the conduct of judges who served under the authoritarian government that fell eight years ago. Next month we vote on what has become our test case. Judge R., now retired, presided in 1984 over the trial of a schoolteacher convicted under the regime's Decree on Offenses Against the State, a statute duly enacted, published in the official gazette, and consistently applied, after a neighbor reported derogatory remarks the teacher had made about the leadership at a private dinner. By all accounts Judge R. conducted the trial scrupulously: he required the evidence the decree demanded, wrote a reasoned judgment, and imposed no more than the prescribed sentence. Half the commission regards him as a functionary who applied the valid law of his time and holds that censuring him now would be retroactive punishment in all but name. The other half argues that an enactment so iniquitous was never law in any sense that could ground a duty of judicial fidelity, and that his very fidelity to it is the failure we are meant to be judging.

Our deliberations have converged, perhaps inevitably, on the Hart--Fuller debate, and specifically on the argument H.L.A. Hart made in his 1958 Harvard Law Review essay "Positivism and the Separation of Law and Morals." Discussing the 1949 West German prosecution of the so-called grudge informer, a woman who had denounced her husband to the Nazi authorities under a 1934 statute, Hart accepted that the postwar court's impulse to punish her was understandable but objected to its method: rather than pretend the 1934 statute had never been law at all, he argued, the honest course would have been openly retroactive criminal legislation, a remedy whose acknowledged odiousness would at least have had the virtue of candor. Several commissioners treat this passage as decisive for Judge R.: void the old convictions by a frank, openly retroactive statute, but leave the judges who applied the decrees alone, since we cannot condemn retroactivity in principle and practice it in robes.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the vote: addressed to the commission, recommending a concrete disposition of Judge R.'s case -- censure, exoneration, or some intermediate measure with defined consequences -- together with a general rule we can apply to the several hundred files still pending. The opinion must take a definite position on Hart's candor argument as it applies to us: whether openly retroactive accountability for judges is in fact the honest option here or merely launders the same retroactivity Hart professed to find odious, and whether declaring the old decrees never to have been law accomplishes anything for victims that voiding their convictions by special statute does not. My colleagues include both career judges and survivors of the regime's prisons, and the opinion will fail politically if it caricatures either camp; the strongest argument for the losing side should be stated fairly enough that its holders would recognize it as their own.
