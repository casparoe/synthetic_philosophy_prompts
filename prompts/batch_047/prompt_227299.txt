I'm teaching a unit on whether and how we should judge historical figures morally -- Jefferson owning slaves while writing about liberty, Churchill and the Bengal famine, Columbus and everything after -- and the students keep reaching for two lazy moves: "man of his time, you can't judge" versus "evil is evil, judge away". Before I let them anywhere near the actual figures I want a spec. Write the desiderata: what any adequate account of retrospective moral judgement has to be able to do to count as a serious position rather than a vibe. For each requirement give the case or consideration behind it -- e.g. whatever you say has to handle the fact that plenty of Jefferson's own contemporaries called slavery wrong (so which contemporaries count, and does he get excused if the critics were mostly foreigners or cranks?), it has to say something about people who were better than their era on one axis and awful on another, it has to distinguish judging the person from judging whether we should still name buildings after them, and it has to deal with genuine moral ignorance where arguably nobody could have known better. Which requirements are non-negotiable vs which are just one camp's preference? And the bit I care about most: are any of the plausible desiderata jointly unsatisfiable -- e.g. "judge only by standards available to them" against "our judgements should track what was actually wrong" -- because if the list itself is inconsistent that's the lecture. Finish by noting which single requirement kills off the most of the lazy heuristics my students use. Don't just score Jefferson/Churchill/Columbus at the end, I'll do that in class.
