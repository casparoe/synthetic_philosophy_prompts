French laïcité is usually presented as strict state neutrality toward religion, but the practice is selective in ways that are hard to square with a single principle: conspicuous religious symbols are banned in public schools and for public employees, yet Christmas, Easter Monday, the Ascension, the Assumption, and All Saints are national public holidays; the state is forbidden from funding religion, yet it owns and maintains thousands of pre-1905 church buildings at public expense; and one region, Alsace-Moselle, operates under a concordat in which the state pays clergy salaries. What theory of religious neutrality does this pattern actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view -- is it that religion is a private matter to be kept out of shared civic space, that historically established religion is culture rather than belief and therefore exempt, that neutrality means equal distance only among currently visible religious minorities, or something else? Say which parts of the practice genuinely require the theory you reconstruct and which merely fit it, whether the different elements presuppose consistent things, and what concretely would have to change if France instead adopted the Anglo-American model of neutrality as even-handed accommodation.
