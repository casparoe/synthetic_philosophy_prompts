I am an undergraduate taking a seminar on the history and measurement of political violence, and for my term paper I am assessing whether the decline-of-violence thesis — the claim, associated above all with Steven Pinker's The Better Angels of Our Nature, that rates of violent death have fallen over the course of history — can be stated as a genuinely testable historical hypothesis rather than an unfalsifiable narrative about progress.

A friend who has read the book summarized it for me as follows, and before I build the paper around it I want to know how much of the summary I can rely on:

"The book argues that violence of every kind — warfare, homicide, judicial torture, slavery, domestic abuse — has declined dramatically and more or less continuously across human history. Hunter-gatherer societies were astonishingly violent by our standards, the medieval world was far more violent than the modern one, and even the catastrophes of the twentieth century, once you adjust for the growth of world population, were less deadly than the routine practices of earlier ages. The main engine of the decline, it says, is the modern state: once governments monopolized legitimate force, private violence stopped paying and withered. The world wars, on this view, were statistical flukes — rare draws from the extreme tail of the distribution of war sizes, which tell us nothing about the underlying trend. It follows that we should expect violence to keep falling as states consolidate and commerce spreads, and that most contemporary anxiety about living in a dangerous world is really just statistical illiteracy: people mistake vivid news coverage for an actual increase in danger."

I am inclined to accept the broad picture, but I distrust both the "fluke" claim and the forecast. Could you audit the summary sentence by sentence — which claims a careful quantitative historian or historical sociologist would accept, which they would want heavily qualified, and which are simply wrong or conflate things the thesis itself keeps apart? In particular, I would like you to address four points. First, whether the population-adjustment move is as straightforward as my friend presents it, and what comparing death rates across wildly different societies actually requires one to assume about what counts as violence and against which baseline. Second, whether the state-monopoly explanation is the thesis's actual causal story or a flattening of it, and what rival or supplementary mechanisms are in play. Third, whether treating the world wars as tail events that carry no information about the trend is a fair rendering of the statistical argument or a misreading of it, and what critics of that argument have said. Fourth, whether the thesis, properly stated, licenses any prediction about the future at all — that is, whether my friend has illegitimately converted a retrodiction into a law.

Finally, for the purposes of the paper: given the corrected version of the thesis that survives your audit, what would count as evidence against it? If nothing would, I want to say so plainly; if something would, I need to know what.
