I'm running a 75-minute intro-to-philosophy discussion section (about 16 undergrads, most of whom have never read any Plato) and I want a structured activity that makes them feel the fork in the Euthyphro dilemma before I ever say the words "Euthyphro dilemma." Roughly: is something good because God commands it, or does God command it because it's good?

Here's the vibe I'm after. In round one I want some student stuck playing a god who gets to just declare things right and wrong, and everyone else has to live under the declarations -- and I want the room to start squirming and asking "but WHY is that wrong?" and discover that the god has nothing to say except "because I said so," which feels gross. Then in round two the god has to give reasons for every command, and I want the class to notice on their own that the reasons are now doing all the work and the god has been demoted to a messenger. If there's a clean way to also get the polytheist version in there (two gods who disagree, so "the gods command it" stops even being an answer), that would be great, since that's closer to what Socrates is actually poking at in the dialogue.

Please give me: concrete roles and rules, a rough timeline with how long each round runs, what I as facilitator should and shouldn't say during (important: the dilemma needs to come out of the mechanics, not out of me hinting), a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the two horns, what will predictably go wrong when I run this (e.g. the god-player going tyrant-mode for laughs, or someone immediately jumping to "God's nature is good" as the dodge), and an honest note about which part of the real philosophical problem the game can't reproduce -- I'm guessing the activity makes the arbitrariness horn feel bad but can't really recreate what the dilemma costs a sincere believer, and I'd have to supply that in discussion.
