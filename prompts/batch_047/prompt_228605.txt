Before I retired, I spent four decades as an experimental physicist, and in my retirement I have been working my way through the philosophy of science -- slowly, and with the handicap of a man who trusts his laboratory instincts more than his armchair ones. I find I have accumulated a small set of convictions about thought experiments, and I would like to know whether they can all be true at once, or whether I am quietly contradicting myself. Here is where I have landed:

(1) I am an empiricist in the plain scientist's sense: genuine knowledge of how the physical world behaves must ultimately trace back to observation and experiment, never to pure reflection alone.

(2) Galileo's argument about falling bodies -- imagining a heavy stone and a light one tied together, and deriving that the combination must fall both faster and slower than the heavy stone alone -- strikes me as yielding real, certain knowledge, without anyone having to drop anything from a tower.

(3) I hold that thought experiments have no magical source of content: they can only ever repackage empirical knowledge we already possess, drawing out consequences that were implicit in it all along.

(4) When I turn to the thought experiments beloved of philosophers -- trolley problems, twin earths, brains in vats -- I find myself dismissing them as verbal games that elicit intuitions about words rather than knowledge about anything.

(5) I believe Maxwell's demon taught physics something substantive -- the connection between information and thermodynamic entropy -- even though no demon was ever constructed, and that this lesson was later vindicated in real physics rather than merely in argument.

I cannot myself see clearly where the fault lines run. (2) and (3) seem friendly enough on the surface, but I have a nagging suspicion that (3) quietly robs (2) of what I want from it. Likewise, I am not confident that my scorn in (4) is consistent with my admiration in (2), since both cases involve a man in an armchair imagining things. But perhaps there is a principled difference I have not articulated. I do not want a general lecture on the epistemology of thought experiments; I want an audit of these five commitments specifically.
