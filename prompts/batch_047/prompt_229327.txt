My grandfather died last month and at the wake my cousin, who did one philosophy module in college, told me I shouldn't dread my own death either because Epicurus settled all this two thousand years ago, and he read me this from his phone: "Death, therefore, the most awful of evils, is nothing to us, seeing that, when we are, death is not come, and, when death is come, we are not." (Apparently it's from a letter Epicurus wrote to some guy Menoeceus.)

I get the surface trick but it feels like the argument is sliding between several different claims and I can't pin down which one it's actually making. Can you lay out, as a list I can throw back at him at christmas, the different precise theses the slogan could be expressing -- from ones that are basically trivial (there's no such thing as the *experience* of being dead, ok, granted) to really strong ones (nothing that happens after I die can bear on my wellbeing at all, so even posthumous betrayal or the failure of my lifes projects can't harm me). I suspect there are also distinct claims tangled together about (a) whether death can be *bad for* someone, (b) whether it's rational to *fear* it, and (c) whether a harm has to occur at a time when its victim exists -- and I'd like those teased apart, since "no point dreading it" doesn't obviously follow from "no point minding it" or however that goes.

For each reading: is it defensible, and what hidden extra premises does it need? (e.g. does it quietly assume that only felt experiences can be good or bad for you, and how plausible is that?) And then the actual payoff: which of these readings, if any, would really deliver what my cousin wanted -- that dreading my own future death is *confused*, not just that being dead won't hurt? I'm especially curious where the standard counter-moves land on your menu: the idea that death is bad because it deprives you of future goods you'd otherwise have had, and that symmetry point about why we don't mind the eternity before we were born but do mind the one after. Do those refute the strong readings, or just the trivial ones?
