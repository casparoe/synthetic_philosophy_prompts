My friend and I just got out of an argument about It’s a Wonderful Life and we’re both too stubborn to let it go. During the bank-run scene, George tells the depositors that their money isn’t sitting in the Building and Loan as a pile of cash, but is tied up in one another’s houses and businesses. My friend thinks this scene accidentally reveals that money is basically a collective hallucination: everybody stops believing in the bank, so the money “stops existing.” I said that’s too crude. A bank note or a bank balance is socially constructed, sure, but so are marriages, borders, debts, and the office of mayor; that doesn’t make them fake. It means their reality depends on institutions, expectations, and people continuing to treat them in certain ways. She answered that this sounds like a fancy way of admitting she was right.

Read the film through that dispute, but don’t simply pick my side. Distinguish at least three different things the movie is showing: physical tokens like dollar bills, bookkeeping claims such as deposits and loans, and the broader social status of money that depends on trust and collective acceptance. How do the bank run, Potter’s behavior, George’s explanation to the depositors, and the final basket-of-money scene each illuminate a different part of the social construction of money? In particular, is the ending supposed to restore the bank because the depositors now have enough currency, or because George has enough social trust? Also, where does the film oversimplify or sentimentalize the issue—say, by treating personal decency as a substitute for institutional safeguards? I’m looking for a reading that works as actual film criticism, not just philosophy with the movie pasted on, so mention what the film adds to the debate that an abstract discussion of money doesn’t.
