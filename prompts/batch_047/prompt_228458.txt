I am persuaded by William James's defence, in his 1896 essay 'The Will to Believe', of the lawfulness of passional belief where evidence cannot decide, and I would like my commitment stress-tested. The load-bearing move, as I read it, is his account of the forced option:

"But if I say, 'Either accept this truth or go without it,' I put on you a forced option, for there is no standing place outside of the alternative. Every dilemma based on a complete logical disjunction, with no possibility of not choosing, is an option of this forced kind."

James restricts his permission to options that are living, momentous, and forced in this sense, and that cannot be settled on intellectual grounds. What would count against the view? I want a map of its exposure, not a rehearsal of Clifford's rival position. In particular: does the criterion of forcedness survive the objection that suspension of judgement appears to be precisely the standing place outside the alternative that James denies exists — so that the 'go without it' formulation must already presuppose that withholding belief forfeits the good at stake, which was the point needing proof? Would it count decisively against him if the goods he invokes (religious salvation, mutual trust, moral strenuousness) proved attainable under agnostic commitment, or could he retreat to the self-fulfilling cases, where belief is itself a causal condition of the evidence or the good? Which concessions could James himself accept, given his restriction to intellectually undecidable options, and which would force him to abandon the essay's central contention? Finally, does a thesis hedged by four conditions risk anything at all: if a critic showed that no actual option satisfies all four, would that refute James or merely empty his thesis of application? Distinguish defeaters that would be decisive from those that would be merely awkward.
