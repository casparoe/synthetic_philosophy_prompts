Next month I am presenting a paper to my history department's historiography colloquium defending a position I call asymmetric presentism. The thesis: the long-running dispute over whether historians may judge the past by present-day standards dissolves once we separate its objects. Past institutions, laws, and social arrangements should be judged by the standards of our own time, because many of them (or their direct descendants) still structure lives now, and historiography that refuses to evaluate them is not neutral but merely quiet about its verdicts. Individual past actors, by contrast, should be assessed only against the normative resources actually available to them: the standards they could plausibly have known, the live options their position afforded, and the costs that dissent would have imposed on them specifically. This is not a blanket acquittal of individuals -- someone can be condemned even by the lights of their own time and place -- but it means the felt impasse between contextualists and moralists comes from treating judging the past as a single activity when it is two.

The audience is about fifteen people, and I know the likely objectors. A senior intellectual historian whose whole method is reconstructing the conceptual world of a period and who regards moral vocabulary in historical writing as an occupational vice. A philosopher of history who is a moral realist and will press on whether available standards can be identified without smuggling in our own judgment about which standards were better. A public historian who works on contested commemoration cases and cares less about the theory than about what it licenses her to write on a plaque. Two graduate students writing on individual collaborators with an unjust regime, for whom my actors-clause is either a lifeline or an evasion. And a chair who dislikes frameworks and will ask why this is anything more than common sense with a label.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections in the order they are most likely to come, attributed to the person most likely to raise each and phrased the way that person would actually phrase it, in their professional vocabulary. For each objection: how damaging it is to the thesis as stated, what I should concede up front and in what words, what I must hold, and a reply deliverable in about ninety seconds. Identify which objections have genuine force against the position rather than against a caricature of it, and flag any where the honest move is to narrow the thesis rather than defend it as stated. I suspect my weakest point is the boundary between person and office -- a magistrate applying an unjust statute is both an individual with limited options and a functioning part of an institution my framework condemns -- so tell me how that probe will most likely be posed and whether my best answer survives it. Finally, give me the question I am hoping nobody asks, and my best honest answer to it if someone does.
