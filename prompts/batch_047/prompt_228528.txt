So I'm on a little advisory group for a constitutional reform commission in a mid-sized European country (won't say which, it's not public yet lol) and we're looking hard at replacing or supplementing the elected parliament with an allotted chamber. In a late-night reading group someone dug up this bit from Aristotle's Politics (Book VI, part 2, Jowett translation), where he's listing the characteristic features of democracy:

"the election of officers by all out of all; and that all should rule over each, and each in his turn over all; that the appointment to all offices, or to all but those which require experience and skill, should be made by lot; that no property qualification should be required for offices, or only a very low one; that a man should not hold the same office twice, or not often, or in the case of few except military offices: that the tenure of all offices, or of as many as possible, should be brief"

The thing I keep chewing on: Aristotle bundles four distinct mechanisms into one package -- selection by lot, no repeat terms, short tenure, and near-universal eligibility -- but he also carves out an exception for offices that "require experience and skill." I want to stress-test whether that carve-out is doing honest work or whether it's just an aristocratic reflex he couldn't shake.

Here's the task. Take these seven offices and sort them into three tiers: (A) where you'd happily switch from career-elected/appointed officials to Aristotle's package (lot + short tenure + no repeats), (B) where you'd keep professional selection but add some element of the package (say, term limits without the lot, or a citizen oversight layer), and (C) where the whole package would be a disaster:

1. The national legislature
2. The central bank's rate-setting board
3. Appellate judges
4. A city's annual budgeting council
5. Audit officials who scrutinize the accounts of other officials (Aristotle actually seems to think judging accounts is exactly the kind of thing ordinary citizens should do -- see the bits right after the passage)
6. The head of government
7. A constitutional review body

For each one, in a sentence or three: which tier and why, AND whether it falls inside or outside Aristotle's own "experience and skill" exception -- and where you disagree with his placement, say so explicitly.

Then the part I actually care most about: tell me whether you think the package can be unbundled. My hunch is that the lot and the short tenure solve completely different problems -- one is about who gets in (descriptive representativeness? breaking the career-politician pipeline?), the other about what incentives they face once in (re-election motives, party discipline) -- and that reforms fail when they expect one mechanism to do the other's job. Am I right that these come apart, or does Aristotle's instinct to bundle them track something real, e.g. that rotation only equalizes power if anyone really can hold any office? Grade my hunch out of 10 and correct it where it's wrong.
