I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have been turning a puzzle over in my mind for months and cannot settle it, so I would like to watch two competent people fight it out properly.

The puzzle is this. I have often heard it said, as a principle attributed to Leibniz, that no two distinct things can share absolutely all their properties -- that if there is literally no way to tell A and B apart, even in principle, then A and B are one and the same thing. I have also encountered a famous objection, due to the philosopher Max Black: imagine a universe that contains nothing whatsoever except two perfectly round, perfectly homogeneous metal spheres, exactly alike in size, composition, temperature, and every other intrinsic feature, floating some fixed distance apart in otherwise empty space. There seem to be two spheres, yet every property of the one seems to be a property of the other. So the principle seems false. But the reply I keep coming back to is that the sphere-describer cheats: he says "two" spheres without being able to point to anything that makes them two.

Please write a dialogue between two characters: one who defends the principle that perfectly indiscernible things must be identical, and one who defends the two-sphere universe as a genuine counterexample. I want them to engage seriously with the specific sticking points I have been unable to resolve on my own:

First, whether relational properties break the symmetry. Each sphere is a certain distance from the other, and each is not identical to the other -- so doesn't each have properties the other lacks, like "being one meter away from a sphere" read in the right way? Or does that covertly presuppose the distinctness it is meant to establish?

Second, whether "being this very one" is a property at all. The defender of the counterexample will want to say the spheres differ in which one they are. Is that a genuine respect in which things can differ, or is it just assigning them different names and mistaking the names for a difference?

Third, the swap test I invented while walking my dog: take the two-sphere universe and now imagine the two spheres exchanged, each exactly where the other was. Is that swapped universe a different possible state of affairs from the original, or the very same state of affairs described twice? It seems to me that whoever answers this question has implicitly already taken a side on the whole dispute, and I want the characters to notice this and fight about it.

Fourth, whether the defender of the principle saves it only by watering it down -- restricting it to qualitative properties, for instance -- and whether that restriction is a principled refinement or an ad hoc retreat that concedes the original point.

Both characters should be equally sharp and equally well prepared; please do not give either one the last word by fiat. Let each respond to what the other actually said rather than restating their opening position, and end the dialogue at the point of deepest disagreement, with each character stating crisply what they take the remaining disagreement to be about -- or, if they conclude the disagreement has become merely verbal, having them argue about that too.
