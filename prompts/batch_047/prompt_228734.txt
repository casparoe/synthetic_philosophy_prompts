I'm in the third year of an undergraduate philosophy degree, and last week in our seminar on distributive ethics a classmate shut down a discussion of total utilitarianism with a single move: maximizing aggregate welfare, he said, implies that resources should be funneled to a "utility monster" — a being, from the thought experiment associated with Robert Nozick, who derives more pleasure from any good than everyone else combined — and since that conclusion is absurd, any aggregative maximizing theory is thereby refuted. The rest of the table nodded as though a theorem had been proved. I suspect the move does much less work than he thinks, but when I tried to push back I found I couldn't say precisely what the objection even claims, and I now have to write a short response paper on it within the fortnight.

Could you disambiguate the utility monster objection for me? Lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing, as a menu I can work from: for instance, whether it is (a) a merely modal point about a metaphysically possible creature, and whether the bare possibility of a counterintuitive consequence refutes a moral theory at all, or only its claim to capture all of morality; (b) an objection specifically to summing welfare across persons, as opposed to maximization as such — and hence whether a prioritarian or sufficientarian view that still maximizes something escapes it, or merely relocates it; (c) an argument that trades on the monster's pleasure being of a different kind or alien intensity, and whether it survives if the monster enjoys exactly what we enjoy, only more efficiently per unit of resource; (d) a disguised complaint about the intelligibility of interpersonal welfare comparisons rather than about aggregation; or (e) a reductio of total utilitarianism in particular, leaving average, critical-level, or person-affecting variants untouched. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, defensible, or implausible, and — most importantly for my paper — whether it actually supports my classmate's sweeping conclusion that aggregative consequentialism is "refuted," or only forces a revision he never bothered to distinguish from the refutation.

Finally, please make the verdicts appropriately self-critical rather than tidy. Indicate where the assessment is most likely to go wrong — for example, how much evidential weight intuitions about physically impossible creatures should carry against a systematic theory, or whether the objection covertly presupposes the distributive intuitions it is meant to establish — and state explicitly what argument or consideration would change your verdict about which reading is strongest. I want to be able to tell whether the utility monster is a genuine constraint on moral theorizing or a vivid image doing argumentative work it has not earned.
