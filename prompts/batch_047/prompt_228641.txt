I'm in an undergraduate seminar on moral progress, and much of what we've read leans on the expanding-circle metaphor associated with Peter Singer: moral development, both across history and within a person's life, is pictured as a circle of concern that begins around the self and pushes outward to family, tribe, nation, all humanity, animals, and perhaps future generations or artificial minds. In our discussions the metaphor does real argumentative work. My classmates take it to support three claims: (1) there is a single axis along which moral progress occurs, so apparent disagreements about, say, animal welfare versus global poverty are really disagreements about how far out the circle should reach; (2) the historical trajectory so far is evidence that further expansion is likely and desirable, so the burden of proof falls on whoever wants to stop at the current boundary; and (3) resisting a proposed extension (to insects, or to future digital minds) means repeating the mistake of everyone in history who resisted a previous one. Please audit the analogy: state explicitly what maps onto what (center, radius, circumference, the act of expanding), identify where the mapping breaks, and distinguish the disanalogies that matter for claims (1)–(3) from the ones that don't. In particular, say which features of the geometric source the metaphor quietly imports that the moral target may lack — I'm thinking of uniformity in all directions, monotonic growth, a single shared metric of 'distance' between beings, and the implication that everything inside the boundary is equally included. Then tell me whether (1)–(3) survive once those imports are made explicit, and if the circle can't carry that weight, what image, if any, would do better.
