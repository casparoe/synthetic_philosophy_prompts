I run a workshop for science-fiction writers, and a recurring problem in our critique sessions is that a story's plot commits to a picture of time the author never consciously chose. One writer has a character travel to 1066 and speak with people who are, by any ordinary standard, long dead; another has a prophet whose predictions are fixed truths in advance; a third has a villain erase the protagonist's childhood so that those events 'never were,' and treats this as different from the childhood merely being forgotten. In discussion, the group keeps reaching for labels like 'block universe' and 'presentism' without agreeing on what feature of a story would settle which label applies, and the usual encyclopedia summaries don't help because they classify philosophers' stated doctrines rather than the implicit commitments of a fictional world.

Design a decision tree, presented in words, that sorts a fictional setting by the temporal ontology its plot presupposes. The tree should have at most seven yes/no or multiple-choice questions, each keyed to something observable in a story's plot or narration (what a time machine can visit, whether prophecies can fail, what happens to characters after they die within the story, whether altering the past rewrites or branches the timeline, and so on), phrased so that a writer can answer them from their own manuscript without any metaphysics vocabulary. Each internal node should be labeled with the question that separates its children, not with a doctrine name.

The leaves must include presentism (only the present exists), eternalism (past, present, and future all equally exist), the growing block (past and present exist, the future does not), the moving spotlight (all times exist but one is objectively privileged as now), and a shrinking or eroding variant (the future exists but the past has ceased to). At least one logically available combination of answers should lead to a node nobody seems to occupy; mark it and diagnose briefly whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive for storytelling, or simply unexplored. If two of the listed positions cannot be separated by any question a manuscript could answer, say so explicitly rather than forcing a split, and explain what extra fact about a story would distinguish them.

Finally, test the tree on these two capsule summaries and show its path through the questions for each: (1) a historian visits ancient Rome, participates in events the history books already record, and realizes on returning that her own interventions were in the books all along; (2) a woman watches her deceased husband through a device that shows the past as it was, and the narrator insists the husband is not merely recorded but still there, living those days, while nothing whatsoever of next year exists to be viewed. If the tree gives a result that seems wrong for one of these, revise the tree once and note what you changed.
