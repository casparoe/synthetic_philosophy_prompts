Think it through carefully, and before committing to an answer, brainstorm two or three rival diagnoses of what's going on and then tell me which one you actually buy and why. Also, British English throughout please.

The setup, so we're on the same page: the Principal Principle (Lewis's version) says, roughly, that if you know the objective chance of some event is 0.7, and you know nothing "inadmissible" (no crystal-ball info about how it actually turns out), then your credence should be 0.7 as well. Chance constrains rational belief. Fine. The fight is about what this principle even IS.

Camp one (my flatmate) says it's a norm connecting two things we already understand seperately: there's objective chance out there in the world, there's rational credence in here, and the principle tells you to line the second up with the first. On this reading the hard question is why you should defer — what gives some physical magnitude authority over my beliefs? Camp two (my supervisor, annoyingly) says the principle isn't a bridge between two independent things at all; it's partly constitutive of what chance is. Chance is just whatever plays the chance-role in something like the Principal Principle, so the "why defer?" question dissolves rather than gets answered.

The reason it's stalled, I think, is that both camps have a favourite embarrassment for the other side and nobody concedes anything. Camp two's problem is the undermining business (Lewis's Big Bad Bug): chance distributions can assign positive probability to futures in which the chances themselves come out different, so naive deference is incoherent and you have to retreat to a modified principle, which on the constitutive reading looks like changing the subject mid-sentence — if chance was defined by the old role, the new role defines a new thing. Camp one's problem is that after forty years nobody's produced a non-circular justification for why rational agents must defer, and every attempt seems to quietly assume something chance-like already. So we go round in circles: "your view can't even state the principle consistently" versus "your view can't justify it".

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: this isn't a factual disagreement at all, it's a disagreement about what a theory of chance is FOR. Camp one thinks chance is fundamentally something physics delivers and epistemology has to cope with; camp two thinks chance is fundamentally an epistemic-cum-pragmatic posit and physics just fills the slot. If that's right, no argument will move anyone because the sides are grading theories by different criteria.

So: what kind of disagreement is this actually? Verbal? A clash about which data count (intuitions about cases vs. the role of chance in statistical mechanics)? Bedrock starting points neither side can argue for? Or is there a hidden substantive question — formal or empirical — that's doing the work, which neither side has stated cleanly? Give me your diagnosis with reasons grounded in what each camp actually says, and then tell me what progress would look like given your diagnosis: a distinction that would help, a case that would break the tie, a formal result that would matter, or an honest argument that no progress should be expected because the question is ill-posed. I want the diagnosis, not a survey of the literature and not a verdict on who wins.
