ok so my reading group (long-running ACX-adjacent thing, we meet monthly, the mood lately is best described as "everyone read the consciousness papers and now dinner is weird") does a satire night every year and this year I want the target to be illusionism about phenomenal consciousness.

Write me a pamphlet/FAQ titled something like "So You've Learned Your Experiences Are Illusory: A Guide for the Newly Unphenomenal." Premise: illusionism is correct, this has just been announced to the public, and some well-funded institute has put out a reassuring pamphlet to help everyone adjust. Deadpan the whole way through — the pamphlet sincerely believes it is being comforting, and the horror is that by its own lights there is nobody home to be comforted, which it keeps almost noticing. Around 600 words.

The humor has to come from the actual position, not a strawman. The pamphlet should keep insisting that of course nobody is denying that things *seem* to you — seeming is precisely what's on offer, it's the only thing on offer — and then tie itself in knots the moment it has to say what seeming amounts to without quietly letting phenomenality back in. Greatest hits to work in: a grief section ("you do not miss your mother; you represent yourself as missing her, which functionally..."), a section explaining why it's still worth taking an aspirin, testimonials from satisfied members who couldn't tell the difference, and a brisk footnote dismissing the objection that an illusion of phenomenality would itself have to seem like something *to* someone, addressed in the literature, no further elaboration provided.

Hard consistency constraint: the pamphlet may never, not even once, actually attribute an experience to anyone. No slipping into "you'll feel relieved to learn" — every reassurance has to be phrased in the currency the view permits (representations, judgments, dispositions, introspective self-models). The moments where it *almost* slips, catches itself, and rewrites the sentence mid-clause should be where the reader laughs. Someone deep in the illusionism literature should feel the satire is fair — it's exaggerating real commitments, not inventing ones.

Don't state the question it's circling, but by the end a reader should be left holding it: what would it even take for something to seem like something without seeming like anything? Also no winking at the reader, no scare quotes around the whole thing, the institute definately believes every word it prints.
