So I've been drafting an essay for my blog and I'm stuck on whether the middle actually works. It's about the old Hofstadter-style complaint about machine translation -- you know, the argument that even when a translation engine nails a tricky sentence, there's nobody home: it moves words around without grasping what anyone is talking about. My take is that both sides of that fight are arguing about the wrong variable. Here's the draft, please tear into it:

---

**Looking for understanding in the wrong place**

Everyone wants to know whether machine translation systems "understand" the sentences they translate. Critics like Hofstadter spent years showing off examples where a translation engine produced fluent output while clearly missing what the sentence was about -- translating the words, not the scene. Fans of the technology reply that modern systems now handle even these traps, resolving ambiguous pronouns, preserving tone, catching puns. I think both sides are making a measurement error. They keep pointing the instrument at the translation itself, when understanding doesn't live there.

Here's the picture I want to push. Think of every text as carrying a certain amount of what I'll call *semantic friction*: the quantity of world-model you have to spend in order to process it correctly. A sentence like "the trophy doesn't fit in the suitcase because it's too small" has high friction -- you can't get through it without paying some price in physical reasoning about trophies, suitcases, and fitting. A sentence like "the meeting is at three" has nearly zero friction. You could translate it correctly with a dictionary and no mind at all.

Now the key observation: translation is the *lowest-friction way to consume a sentence's meaning*. When you translate, you hand the meaning right back out again, in another language, before it can do any work in you. A translator -- human or machine -- is a pipe. And here's the uncomfortable part: a pipe is exactly the one place where understanding is hardest to detect, because a good pipe and a smart pipe produce identical output. Demanding that a translation system "show its understanding" in the translation is like demanding a mirror show off its eyesight.

So where should we look instead? I propose the *leftover test*. Understanding is what's left over after the translation is delivered. Take the translated sentence and ask the system to do something with it that the original speaker would consider trivial: answer a question about it, notice a contradiction with the previous paragraph, refuse to translate further because the text has started lying to it, laugh at the right moment. A system that understands has residue -- the sentence changed its state in ways that outlast the output. A system that merely converts has none; once the translation leaves, nothing about it is different.

This reframes the whole debate. Hofstadter-style critics were right that translation quality is no evidence of understanding, but for a deeper reason than they gave: it's *constitutively* no evidence, because translation is the one linguistic act designed to launder understanding away. And the fans are wrong too, but symmetrically: the fact that modern systems pass the old pun-and-pronoun traps shows nothing, since those traps were always friction tests administered at the one spot where friction is invisible. Both sides kept checking the pipe for wetness. The wetness was downstream.

One more wrinkle before I stop. The leftover test has a nasty consequence nobody wants to own: plenty of *human* translators fail it. A skilled simultaneous interpreter in a booth will tell you they often couldn't summarize the speech they just flawlessly rendered -- the meaning passed through without sticking. If understanding is residue, then the interpreter didn't understand the speech in the relevant sense, even while doing the job perfectly. I'd call that a feature of the view: it shows understanding comes in degrees and is bound to tasks, not a light that's either on or off. But I can already hear someone saying I've just redefined "understanding" so that translating well counts as not understanding, and that a definition with that consequence is a reductio, not a discovery.
