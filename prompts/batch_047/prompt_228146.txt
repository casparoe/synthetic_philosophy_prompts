For a graduate seminar on naturalizing mental content, write a specification for teleosemantics rather than a survey of the literature. State the desiderata that any adequate teleosemantic theory of representation must satisfy, number them, and give the philosophical motivation and a concrete test case for each.

Organize the specification around problems such as the following:

- A neural mechanism in a frog fires in response to small dark moving objects and causes snapping. Across the frog’s evolutionary history, snapping at flies usually contributed to survival, but snapping at seeds and shadows did not. Must the theory assign the representation the content fly, small dark moving object, food, or something else? Explain what an adequate theory should say about proximal versus distal content and about disjunctive interpretations.
- An animal possesses a danger detector selected because false alarms were cheap and missed dangers were catastrophic. The mechanism therefore produces many inaccurate responses while remaining adaptive. A satisfactory theory must explain how it can misrepresent without treating every adaptive false alarm as a representation of danger.
- Two lineages inherit physically indistinguishable heat-sensitive mechanisms. In one lineage, the mechanism was selected because warm rocks indicate shelter; in the other, because body heat indicates prey. Their present causes overlap substantially. Should selection history assign them different contents despite their identical present operations?
- An animal learns within its lifetime to use the position of a particular star for navigation, although neither the animal nor the mechanism has an evolutionary history of representing that star. Must teleosemantics account for learned and developmentally acquired contents, or may it treat them as merely derived from evolutionarily established contents?
- A perfect molecular duplicate of an organism is produced accidentally and therefore has neither ancestors nor a selection history. Its internal states guide behavior just as the original organism’s states do. Decide whether a theory of content may allow the duplicate to represent anything, and state whether this consequence should count as a discovery or as a reductio.
- Ordinary thought includes contents such as same individual as before, enough food, predator absent, and permissible action. Do not let the specification apply only to simple stimulus detectors: identify what teleosemantics would need in order to cover relational, negative, modal, and normatively governed contents.
- Explain how the theory should distinguish representational error from malfunction, absence of a function, a change of function, and successful behavior guided by a nonrepresentational mechanism.

For every desideratum, indicate whether it is a non-negotiable requirement for any theory claiming to naturalize content, a commitment distinctive of one teleosemantic camp, or merely a methodological preference. Identify any desiderata that appear to be jointly unsatisfiable—for example, fully determinate content, purely historical selection, adequate treatment of learned representations, and a strong distinction between content and use. If such an inconsistency exists, present that as a substantive result rather than resolving it by stipulation.

Conclude with a brief scorecard for four approaches, each evaluated against your own list: a producer-selection account, on which content derives from what a mechanism was selected to detect; a consumer- or success-based account, on which content derives from the condition under which downstream use historically succeeded; an informational account supplemented by biological function; and a learning- or use-based account that allows individual history to generate new contents. Define each approach in one or two sentences before scoring it, do not provide citations, and do not use the scorecard as a substitute for developing the desiderata themselves.
