I am a monetary economist, fluent in the modern taxonomy of my field: commodity versus credit theories of money, the chartalist or state account, the quantity theory and the equation of exchange, the canonical functions of money, and current debates around Modern Monetary Theory. I have been reading George Berkeley's The Querist, first published in 1735 and addressed to the economic condition of Ireland — a series of numbered queries written by a man better known for his idealist metaphysics and his critique of abstract ideas — and I am struck by how much of it reads like a heterodox monetary theory avant la lettre. Three representative queries: "Whether money is to be considered as having an intrinsic value, or as being a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, as is variously suggested by writers? And whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?" (Q. 23); "Whether money be not only so far useful, as it stirreth up industry, enabling men mutually to participate the fruits of each other's labour?" (Q. 5); and "Whether, therefore, less money swiftly circulating, be not, in effect, equivalent to more money slowly circulating?" (Q. 22). Berkeley adds that the four elements and human labour are the true source of wealth, that a nation's wealth is proportioned to its industry and its industry to the circulation of credit, whatever the marks or tokens in which that credit is transferred, that the real end of men is power, that appetites are manufactured by fashion, and that a legislature may therefore properly concern itself with managing taste.

Please restate this position in the vocabulary of my discipline, as faithfully as you can, and then report what did not survive the translation. Distinguish three grades. First, claims that translate cleanly: is the ticket-or-counter doctrine simply the credit or claim-check theory of money, and is Q. 22 just the velocity term of the quantity theory? Second, claims that translate only with distortion — name the distortion in each case, for instance in Berkeley's labour-command measure of value, in his apparent indifference to inflation risk in a chronically demand-deficient economy, or in his neglect of the medium-of-exchange versus unit-of-account distinction. Third, claims that have no expression at all in modern monetary vocabulary: I have in mind especially the possibility that his monetary nominalism is his metaphysical nominalism — the rejection of abstract ideas and of unperceived substance — applied to coin, and his picture of preferences as manufactured and governable rather than sovereign.

Finally, take a position on the residue: does what fails to translate mark a genuine loss in the modern framework, or evidence that Berkeley's package was doing moral and rhetorical rather than analytical work? Do not dissolve the tension by flattening Berkeley into an early chartalist; I want the friction preserved.
