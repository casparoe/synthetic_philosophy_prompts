I'm a PhD student working on the theory of practical reason, and I'm designing a seminar exercise for a reading group that has just finished the literature on incommensurability and hard choices. I'd like you to write a dialogue between two equally capable philosophers. The first defends the view that in some hard choices the options are genuinely "on a par" — neither better than the other, nor exactly equally good, but related by a sui generis evaluative relation — and that this parity is what makes it possible for us to create reasons through our commitments rather than merely respond to reasons already there. The second holds that so-called hard choices are hard only epistemically: in every such case one option really is better (or the two are exactly tied), and our sense of incommensurability is just a confession of our limited information and limited imaginative access to what each life would be like from the inside.

To keep the dialogue from rehearsing the standard career-versus-family examples, set it in the following situation. The two characters are advisors to a hospital ethics board that must choose between funding a palliative-care wing, which will reliably relieve a moderate amount of suffering for many identifiable patients, and a speculative research program in neuropathic pain, which will probably accomplish nothing but would, if it succeeds, transform the lives of a far larger number of future sufferers. Midway through the conversation, have one character introduce a thought experiment: a benevolent oracle that can tell the board, with certainty, exactly how much suffering each option would in fact prevent. The parity theorist should have to explain whether the choice remains hard once every empirical uncertainty is removed, and if so, in what the remaining hardness consists; the epistemicist should have to explain why the oracle case doesn't simply vindicate their view, given how natural it is to imagine the board still agonizing even after hearing the oracle's answer.

Make sure the characters genuinely engage each other rather than trade set pieces: the epistemicist should press the small-improvement worry (if the two options were on a par, shouldn't sweetening one slightly make it clearly better, and doesn't it seem like it wouldn't?), and the parity theorist should press back on whether the epistemicist can make sense of regret, resolution, and the phenomenology of "throwing oneself into" a choice if every hard choice is really just ignorance in disguise. Each should concede at least one point to the other before the dialogue ends, though neither should capitulate on their core position.
