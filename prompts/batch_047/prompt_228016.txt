I'm finalizing my position on fakes and originals for a seminar paper and want to know if the package hangs together before I commit to it in writing. Here's where I've landed:

1. Aesthetic value lives in the perceptible object, so a truly perfect copy of a painting — one no expert could distinguish, right materials, right craquelure, aged canvas, everything — is aesthetically worth exactly as much as the original.
2. Even so, forging a painting is a serious moral wrong, and not just because of the fraud/paperwork side.
3. When a celebrated work gets exposed as a fake (think of the Vermeer forgeries that fooled everyone for years), something of real value is genuinely lost — the loss is in the artwork itself, not just in our heads or its price tag.
4. The special pull of standing in front of the original isn't snobbery or market psychology, it's a real kind of value.
5. Whether a canvas is authentic is a historical fact, not an aesthetic property — you can't see provenance.
6. To perceive a painting properly at all you have to see it under the right categories — as a cubist work, as a devotional icon, as a satire — and getting those categories right depends on knowing actual facts about where, when, and why the thing was made.

Which of these genuinely conflict, which only look like they do, and what's the cheapest way out? Please rank the tensions by how much damage each does to the overall position, and for each one say definately which commitment has to move and what it costs. I don't have a favorite candidate for what to drop, so don't pull punches.
