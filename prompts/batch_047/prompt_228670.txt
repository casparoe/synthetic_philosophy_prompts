I'm a TA grading short-answer responses for an introductory course in logic and philosophy. The exam question was: "In your own words, reconstruct the puzzle Lewis Carroll poses in his dialogue about Achilles and the Tortoise, and state what it is standardly taken to show about the justification of deductive inference. Be precise about what exactly the Tortoise refuses to accept, and why simply adding further premises does not resolve the difficulty." Here is the student's answer:

"Carroll imagines Achilles trying to get the Tortoise to accept the conclusion Z of a Euclidean proof from premises A and B. The Tortoise accepts A and B but refuses to accept Z until Achilles writes down an additional premise C stating that if A and B are true, then Z is true. When C is granted, the Tortoise still withholds Z and demands a further premise licensing the step from A, B, and C to Z, and so on without end. Carroll's point is that deduction falls into an infinite regress: because the required premises can never all be written down, modus ponens is in fact invalid, and logical reasoning can never genuinely compel assent. The puzzle is thus a version of Agrippa's trilemma — every demand for justification ends in regress, circularity, or dogmatic assertion — and it shows that deductive inference provides no real justification for its conclusions. The regress could have been avoided, however, if Achilles had treated the rule of inference as simply one more premise from the outset, since then every step would be fully explicit."
