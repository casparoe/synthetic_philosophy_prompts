I sit on the small-grants committee for my university's center for experimental philosophy, and we're split down the middle on a proposal I need to write the decision memo for. A grad student is asking for $12,000 to run what she calls a field test of superrational reasoning, inspired by the lottery games Hofstadter ran in his old Scientific American column. Her design: an online lottery, open to the general public, advertised through puzzle and rationality forums. Entry costs $2, one entry per person. After entries close, one entrant is drawn at random and wins a prize equal to $50,000 divided by the total number of entries. So if fifty people enter, the winner gets $1,000. If fifty thousand enter, the winner gets a dollar. The grant money is there to cover the payout if entry numbers stay low, since the center loses money whenever fewer than about 160 people enter and profits after that.

Her pitch is explicitly philosophical. She says the player base she's recruiting is about as close as you can get to the ideal case for the symmetry argument: people who all know the rules, know that everyone else knows them, and know that everyone is facing the identical decision. If the "we're all in the same epistemic situation, so we should all land on the same choice" style of reasoning (she calls it superrationality, she mentions the Platonia dilemma by name) has any real pull, entries should stay low, either because everyone abstains or because everyone does the mixed-strategy thing where you enter only with some small probability so the expected crowd stays tiny and the prize stays huge. If it has no pull, the lure wins, entries flood in, and the prize collapses to nothing. Either way, she says, we learn whether that family of arguments describes anything real about how reflective people actually choose, as opposed to being a just-so story philosophers tell each other.

The objections flying around the committee: (1) this is a re-run of a stunt from the 80s dressed up as research; (2) no possible outcome discriminates between her hypotheses -- if few people enter, that could be superrational restraint, ordinary expected-value math, or nobody seeing the ad, and if many enter, a superrationalist can just say the crowd wasn't really like-minded; (3) the economics only work in the center's favor if participants reason badly, so we're effectively running a fundraiser that profits from confusion, and several members think that fails some basic duty to participants even though it's legal and the entry fee is two bucks; (4) her plan to post the running entry total on the site "for transparency" seems to half of us like it destroys the very coordination problem she's studying, and to the other half like the only thing making the study fair.

Draft the committee's memo: fund, don't fund, or fund with conditions. Give a clear bottom line up front, then the reasoning. The memo has to actually engage with the symmetry argument she's testing rather than just gesturing at it -- including whether any result would count against it, and what design changes (pre-registration, a control arm, different disclosure) would make the experiment answer a real question. Give the strongest version of whichever side loses, because this memo is going to be quoted back at us either way. And take an actual position on the transparency fight, since I suspect that's where the whole thing turns.
