I'm a philosophy professor, and somehow half my job is running votes: hiring committee shortlists, picking which colloquium speakers to invite, curriculum proposals, grad admissions waitlists. A while back I actually sat down and read up on the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem (partly out of guilt, since I teach social choice every few years and always hand-wave the proof), and it's been bugging me ever since. As I understand it: once there are three or more options, any deterministic rule for picking a single winner that isn't a dictatorship and lets every option possibly win can be gamed by someone voting insincerely. Fine. Depressing, but fine. The thing is, I still have to run these votes next semester, and the theorem doesn't come with a "so here's what to do about it" section.

So: turn what the theorem actually implies -- and just as importantly, what it does NOT imply -- into a practical procedure I can follow when I'm setting up a departmental vote. Roughly: given the kind of decision, the number of options, what I know about how people's preferences line up, and how much I care about sincere voting versus other goods (speed, legitimacy, the losers not feeling robbed), which rule should I use? Or should I restructure the choice itself instead -- narrow the field first, vote on things one at a time, pair options off?

Some constraints on the procedure:

- It should start from the theorem's actual content and scope conditions. It only bites, as I understand it, when preferences are unrestricted, so I want an early step that checks whether something like single-peakedness plausibly holds in the case at hand (hiring votes often feel nearly one-dimensional -- research quality -- until someone mentions teachign coverage and suddenly we're in two dimensions and I'm doomed). The procedure should say what to do when the theorem genuinely applies versus when a restriction plausibly saves me.
- It should distinguish "the theorem says strategic voting is possible" from "strategic voting is likely and worth designing around." I suspect a lot of people overread it as "everyone will lie all the time," and I don't think that's what it says.
- It should go through the escape hatches I keep hearing about and say whether each one genuinely escapes: sequential elimination with an agenda, approval voting (voters aren't submitting rankings, so is it even under the theorem's jurisdiction?), randomization, deliberation before the vote, just... refusing to have more than two options on the table.
- Include three worked applications: (a) a hiring committee ranking four candidates for one job; (b) a faculty meeting choosing among five curriculum proposals with known factions; (c) at least one case where the procedure gives me an answer I won't like -- e.g., it tells me a rule I'd pick on other grounds is fine despite being manipulable, or it tells me to shrink the agenda when I'd rather not.
- Be explicit about what the theorem can't settle. As far as I know it says nothing about which rule's manipulability is worst in practice, nothing about legitimacy, nothing about when strategic voting is even bad. Mark clearly where the procedure runs out and I'm back to judgment.

Write it as an actual checklist or flowchart-in-words I could tape next to my office door, not an essay. Casual tone is fine; I'm going to be following this thing at 4:45pm in a stuffy seminar room and I need it to work then.
