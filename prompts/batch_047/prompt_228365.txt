I co-teach a second-year political philosophy seminar (about 16 students, 75-minute slot) and we're about to hit social choice. I want them to run headfirst into Sen's liberal paradox -- the result that even a tiny minimal-liberalism condition (each person decisive over their own purely personal sphere, like what they read in their own room) conflicts with the weak Pareto principle once you allow unrestricted preferences. The thing is, when I just state it on the board they nod politely and move on, so I want them to *generate the contradiction themselves* in a game before they ever hear Sen's name. Sen's own illustration with the prude and the lewd arguing over who should read a scandalous novel is a great intuition pump -- both prefer that the *other* person reads it rather than themselves reading it, in opposite directions, and suddenly Pareto and individual rights collide -- but I'd like something the students actually play rather than a story I tell.

So: design me a runnable in-class game. It should use pairs or small groups, some genuine (small) stakes -- maybe candy, or loser-does-whiteboard-duty, something with actual mild preferences so the rankings feel real and not assigned. Crucially, the rules should be set up so that if everyone sincerely follows "you decide over your own personal business" plus "if everyone prefers X to Y, the group goes with X," the group's collective verdict cycles or deadlocks, and the students notice the cycle on their own before I say anything. I need: setup and materials, exact rules, a timeline for the 75 minutes (including how long to let them stew before debriefing), and a debrief script that maps each moment of the game onto the conditions of Sen's theorem (unrestricted domain, minimal liberalism, weak Pareto, collective acyclicity) and gets them to see which condition they'd rather drop and what that costs.

Also tell me honestly: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (students treating it as a bargaining problem, faking preferences to win, missing that the cycle is structural rather than a bug in that particular group, etc.) and how to handle each failure. And be frank about which feature of the theorem a one-shot game can't reproduce -- my worry is that a single played instance shows a *problem* but not an *impossibility*, since the theorem says no rule can satisfy all the conditions across *every* possible preference profile. How do I close that gap in the debrief without just lecturing the proof at them?
