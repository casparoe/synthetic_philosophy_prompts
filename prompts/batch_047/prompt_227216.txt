Exam question: "Set out the fatalist argument Aristotle considers in De Interpretatione 9 (the sea battle). What exactly does he conclude, and how do two later responses to the problem try to block the argument? Is either response successful?"

Student's answer:

"Aristotle's argument is this. Suppose it is true today that there will be a sea battle tomorrow. Then it was also true a thousand years ago. But truths about the past are necessary — nothing can be done about them now — so the sea battle's occurrence is already fixed and unavoidable. The same reasoning applies if it is true that there won't be one, so everything that happens happens of necessity, and deliberation is pointless.

Aristotle concludes that the law of excluded middle fails for future contingents: 'there will be a sea battle tomorrow' is neither true nor false today, although the disjunction 'there will be one or there won't be' is true.

Łukasiewicz formalized this with a three-valued logic in which future contingents take a third truth-value. Ockham instead kept bivalence and distinguished hard facts about the past from soft ones: that it was true yesterday that there would be a battle today is a soft fact, still partly up to us, so the step from past truth to necessity fails.

I think Ockham's solution fails because the hard/soft distinction is ad hoc. Łukasiewicz's logic captures Aristotle's position exactly, though the price is giving up excluded middle, which most people find too high."
