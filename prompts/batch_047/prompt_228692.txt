I'm a postdoc and in six weeks I'm giving a colloquium talk in a joint philosophy–physics series, defending a modal argument for the ideality of time that I'm building out of Gödel's 1949 essay A Remark About the Relationship Between Relativity Theory and Idealistic Philosophy. The passage I want my whole talk to hang on is the closing stretch where Gödel reasons that in his rotating cosmological solutions you can't consistently define an objective earlier-than/later-than ordering across the whole spacetime, so the lapse of time there can't mean what we take it to mean — and that since our world's actual physics doesn't rule such configurations out, whether time is real here looks like a contingent accident of initial conditions rather than something grounded in the nature of things, which he takes to suggest that time as we experience it is something ideal. (That's my paraphrase, not the exact wording — I'll read the real passage aloud in the talk.) My claim is stronger than a mere exegesis: I want to argue the modal step still works even granting that Gödel universes are causally and observationally nothing like ours, because the argument only needs the laws to permit them, not for us to live in one.

Here's my problem: the room will not be philosophers of time. It's mostly philosophers of physics who've internalized the standard rejoinders (there's a guy who'll definitely bring up Malament-style results on time in Gödel spacetime, someone else whose whole thing is that modal arguments from spacetime structure prove too much), plus two general relativists from down the hall who are polite but allergic to metaphysics, plus one very senior Kantian who thinks this was all settled by the First Antinomy. Nobody in the room except me has actually read the essay recently. A colleague who heard my practice run said my replies sounded rehearsed for the wrong audience, like I was answering a LessWrong comment section instead of physicists, which stung but is probly fair.

So draft my Q&A prep sheet. Give me the eight objections this specific crowd will raise — not the generic philosophy-of-time seminar ones — ranked by (likelihood × damage), each phrased the way that person would actually phrase it (the relativists will talk about energy conditions and physical realizability, the Kantian will talk about the form of inner sense, etc.), each with a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and a candid note on where that reply is softest. Flag which objections I should concede ground on versus hold the line. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and tell me honestly whether my core modal step — from the laws permit time-less-in-this-sense worlds to time here is ideal — can survive a hostile room or whether I should downgrade the thesis to something weaker before I print the handout.
