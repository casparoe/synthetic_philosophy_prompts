In June 1945, a committee of scientists at the Manhattan Project's Metallurgical Laboratory in Chicago, chaired by James Franck, submitted a report to the Secretary of War urging that the atomic bomb not be used on Japan without warning. The following passage comes from the Summary section of the Franck Report (Report of the Committee on Political and Social Problems, Manhattan Project "Metallurgical Laboratory," University of Chicago, June 11, 1945):

"Much more favorable conditions for the eventual achievement of such an agreement could be created if nuclear bombs were first revealed to the world by a demonstration in an appropriately selected uninhabited area.

In case chances for the establishment of an effective international control of nuclear weapons should have to be considered slight at the present time, then not only the use of these weapons against Japan, but even their early demonstration may be contrary to the interests of this country. A postponement of such a demonstration will have in this case the advantage of delaying the beginning of the nuclear armaments race as long as possible."

What I want is an analysis of the decision to bomb Hiroshima and Nagasaki through the lens of just war theory, using this passage as the pivot -- not a general survey of the debate, which I have read before.

Work through the following, in whatever order you find natural, in roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words (the passage does not count toward this):

First, diagnose the passage itself. The committee's case against an unannounced attack is built entirely from prudential and forward-looking considerations -- arms races, international agreement, strategic advantage. Nowhere in the report's reasoning does the immunity of Japanese civilians appear as an independent constraint. Does this omission mean the scientists were reasoning as pure consequentialists, or can their demonstration proposal be reconstructed as a jus in bello argument in disguise? Reconstruct the strongest just-war version of their position and note what is lost or gained in the translation.

Second, run the actual decision through the standard criteria. Take discrimination, proportionality, and last resort in turn. For last resort, do not treat it as a slogan: the genuinely available menu included the demonstration the Franck committee proposed, continued blockade and conventional bombing, modification of the unconditional-surrender demand to preserve the Emperor, and waiting for the announced Soviet entry into the war. How does a theory developed for binary choices -- fight or not, target this or that -- handle a decision whose real structure is a choice among differently-priced waiting strategies? Can "last resort" even be applied when each alternative is a probability rather than a certainty?

Third, use Nagasaki as a stress test. Whatever one says about Hiroshima, the second bomb came three days later, before Japan's leadership had realistically absorbed the first. Does just war theory have the resources to condemn or excuse a second application of the same act within a single ongoing decision, or does it silently assume that each bombing is a fresh case? Say where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.

Finally, let the case bite back at the theory. If the strongest moral argument available to insiders in 1945 was the Franck Report's prudential one, and if just war theory struggles to rank the waiting strategies above, is that evidence that the theory is the wrong tool for decisions about weapons of mass coercion -- or does the doctrine of supreme emergency, which you should define before using, show that the theory already anticipated cases like this? Take a position on at least this last question and defend it.
