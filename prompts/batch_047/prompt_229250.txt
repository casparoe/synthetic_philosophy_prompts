So Kavka's toxin puzzle, in the version I know: an eccentric billionaire offers you a fortune if, at midnight tonight, you genuinely *intend* to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow afternoon. The toxin won't kill you, it'll just make you miserably ill for a day. The money lands in your account before noon, and nothing whatsoever hinges on whether you actually drink -- only on whether you really intended, at midnight, to do it. Assume the billionaire has some way of checking the intention.

I want to convince my reading group that this puzzle isn't really about the metaphysics of intention, it's about the payout structure. So construct two thought experiments to order.

Case 1: a fully realistic version of the puzzle. No brain scanners, no mind-reading tech. The intention-check has to be something that could plausibly exist today -- a best friend who can always tell when you're bluffing, a signed commitment filed with a lawyer who knows you, whatever works -- but it must be genuinely checkable, and the check can be fallible-but-good rather than perfect. Show that the classic structure survives under this constraint: it's desirable (maybe even rational) to intend at midnight, yet irrational to actually drink at noon. And say where the fallibility of the checker matters: would the puzzle even get going with a *perfect* checker, or does the realistic version quietly change what's hard about it?

Case 2: change exactly ONE feature of the payout/verification setup so that it becomes rational to drink at noon too -- i.e., the agent who genuinely intended at midnight also has a real reason at noon to follow through (for instance, maybe the money only fully clears after a later review of whether the midnight intention was genuine, so backing out would itself be evidence you never meant it). Keep everything else fixed: same toxin, same stakes, same checker.

After both cases: walk through what three positions say about Case 1 -- (a) the view that rational action requires reasons at the time of action, (b) a resolute-choice view where it can be rational to follow through on rationally formed plans even when you lack a fresh reason, and (c) Kavka's own claim that you can't even *form* the intention once you know you'll have no reason to act. Then: which single structural feature is doing all the work in Case 2? What happens if you remove it -- does the case collapse back into Case 1? And how would a defender of the "you only ever need reasons to intend, never reasons to act" camp attack Case 2 as rigged, or as no longer about intention at all?

One hard constraint: no stipulating the verdicts. If you just write "suppose the agent finds it rational to drink," you've cheated -- the rationality has to fall out of the setup you built.
