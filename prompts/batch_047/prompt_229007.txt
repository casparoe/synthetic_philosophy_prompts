Last night I got into it with a friend (she's a philosophy PhD student, I'm an ML engineer) at a bar until basically closing time, and we left it unresolved. The fight was about Searle's Chinese Room and whether anything about a mind is special in principle. Her big claim was that the intentionality of our thoughts -- their being about things -- is somehow intrinsic or "original," while anything a machine has is at best derived, borrowed from whoever designed or uses it. I kept saying this is just an artifact of where you draw the system boundary. She pulled out Dennett's vending machine example, the "two-bitser" I think it's called: a machine built to accept US quarters, so its internal state means quarter. But ship the same machine somewhere else where a different coin of the same size and weight is legal tender, and now it happily sorts those -- so does its state mean quarter, or the other coin, or "whatever coin-shaped thing triggers me"? Dennett's point as I understood it is that even the derived intentionality of artifacts isn't really fixed by the designer's intentions, and that this is supposed to soften us up for the idea that our own aboutness is just as interpretation-dependent, which Searle would hate.

Here's what I actually want. Restate this whole dispute -- original vs derived intentionality, the two-bitser case, Searle's insistence that syntax isn't enough for semantics -- in the vocabulary I use every day: training objectives, loss landscapes, reward functions, distribution shift, frozen weights vs fine-tuning, function approximation, the gap between intended behavior and learned behavior. Like, is "derived intentionality" just... a model's behavior being interpretable relative to the objective it was trained on, and the two-bitser case a story about deployment distribution shift making the "real" function underdetermined? Is Searle's intrinsic intentionality the claim that there's something like a ground-truth label that isn't just relative to some interpreter or loss? Do the mapping as faithfully as you can, don't sand off the edges to make me feel smart.

Then, and this is the part I care most about, give me the residue report. Which pieces of the debate translate cleanly into ML vocabulary, which pieces you had to fudge, and which parts have no counterpart at all. And be honest about the diagnosis: when something doesn't survive translation, is that because my framework is missing a concept it genuinely needs (my friend's view, obviously), or because the philosophers are treating as a deep fact something that from my side looks like an artifact of picking a description level -- like arguing about what a model "really" computes when multiple interpretations of the same weights are available? I lost the argument last night, or at least didn't win it, and I'd like to know whether that's because she was right or becuase we were talking past each other in two vocabularies.
