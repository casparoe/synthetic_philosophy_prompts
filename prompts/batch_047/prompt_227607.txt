Next month I will present a paper at a decision theory workshop defending the following thesis: Derek Parfit's treatment of the hitchhiker case—his celebrated instance of rational irrationality, in which it is rational to make oneself into the kind of person who will later act irrationally—rests on an equivocation that dissolves once the case is redescribed with its information structure made explicit. My argument, in outline: (i) the case generates its paradox only if the driver can reliably detect the passenger's disposition at the moment of the rescue decision; (ii) given such detection, the passenger's later refusal to pay is arguably not irrational at all on any theory that evaluates acts relative to the agent's standing commitments rather than momentary preferences alone, so nothing genuinely irrational was ever adopted; (iii) where detection is absent or imperfect, the rational-irrationality maneuver collapses into an ordinary expected-utility calculation about signaling, and the supposed category between rational and irrational action vanishes. I conclude that rational irrationality is not a stable category: every alleged instance is either rationality wearing a mask or a bet whose payoffs have been misdescribed.

The audience: two committed causal decision theorists sympathetic to resolute-choice approaches in the tradition of McClennen, who will resist any claim that commitment dissolves paradox rather than merely relocating it; one Parfit scholar who knows Reasons and Persons and the surrounding literature—including the connections to Kavka's toxin puzzle and Gauthier's constrained maximization—thoroughly; and several graduate students who will side with whichever camp seems technically sharper.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections in roughly the order I should expect them, phrased as each audience member would actually phrase them: the resolute-choice sympathizers will press formal points about dynamic consistency and whether my step (ii) simply smuggles in their own machinery while denying it; the Parfit scholar will press textual and dialectical points about what Parfit actually took rational irrationality to be and whether my argument targets a construction of my own. For each objection, state what I should concede outright, what I should hold, and the strongest reply I can deliver in about two minutes, together with a note on where that reply is weakest. Identify the single objection most dangerous to my thesis and explain why, and name the point in my own argument that I should prepare to defend at length rather than hope goes unexamined. Finally, flag any place where my outline appears to misdescribe Parfit's actual position, since a misattribution caught in the room would damage me more than any substantive objection.
