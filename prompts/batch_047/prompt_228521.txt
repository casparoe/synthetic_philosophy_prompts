I retired from chemistry a couple of years back and have been reading philosophy since, and I keep running into akrasia — weakness of will — and this idea of hyperbolic discounting, where a cost or reward that's far off weighs less than it should, so your preferences flip as the moment approaches. Fine as theory, but here's my actual problem: I make plans (a daily walk, finishing a book manuscript, a savings rule) and then I revise them, and afterwards I can never tell whether I revised because I genuinely learnt something new or because the imminent effort loomed large and I simply caved. Write me a procedure I can run in the moment, when I'm about to abandon a plan — the checks in order, what counts as passing each one, and a default rule for when I honestly can't tell. It needs to be usable by someone who doesn't know the theory, it should mark which steps are really judgement calls dressed up as procedure, and it should say what it can't settle. Then run it on three of my real cases: pulling out of a conference talk I'm due to give in six weeks, moving my morning walk to the afternoon on a rainy day, and quitting a Spanish course three weeks in because the homework feels pointless. At least one of them should come out as stick-with-the-plan even though that's not the answer I want.
