I've recently retired (biochemist by trade, forty years of it) and have been auditing an undergraduate moral philosophy module at my local university, mostly for something to do on Tuesday afternoons. The convenor, an old squash partner of mine, has roped me into helping with the marking for a take-home essay, which I'm rather enjoying, though I feel a bit of a fraud.

Here's the essay question, exactly as set:

"Egalitarians hold that it is bad in itself if some people are worse off than others through no fault of their own. Explain the 'levelling down' objection to this view. How does prioritarianism differ from egalitarianism as a principle of distribution, and does moving from equality to priority genuinely escape the objection, or does the same problem resurface in a different form?"

And here is the student's answer, pasted in full:

***

Egalitarianism, understood as a telic view, holds that inequality is bad in itself, quite apart from its effects on anyone's life. On this view, if some people are worse off than others through no fault of theirs, that is a bad-making feature of the situation, one that could in principle be outweighed but never disappears.

The levelling down objection, due to Larry Temkin's influential work on inequality, runs as follows. Suppose equality is what matters. Then we can always improve a situation in one respect by bringing the better off down to the level of the worse off: for instance, by taking from the sighted so that they are no better off than the blind, even though this helps nobody. The egalitarian must say such a change is in one way good, since it produces more equality. But it seems absurd to say a change that benefits no one and harms some is good in any respect at all. So equality, as an intrinsic value, stands condemned, and we should look elsewhere.

Prioritarianism is the natural alternative. On this view, what matters is not relative standing but absolute welfare, with benefits counting for more the worse off the recipient is. Prioritarianism thus directs us to maximise the position of the worst-off person or group, since a unit of benefit to them carries the greatest weight. Crucially, this escapes levelling down. The view is essentially person-affecting: a change can only be better if it is better for someone. Levelling down is better for no one, so the prioritarian calls it simply bad, with no awkward concession that it is good in one respect. The egalitarian, by contrast, is stuck with that concession because her principle attaches value to a pattern rather than to people's lives.

Does the problem resurface? I think not, at least not in the same form. A critic might complain that the weights prioritarianism assigns are arbitrary, and there is something in this. But arbitrariness in the weighting function is a different and lesser vice than commending pointless harm, so the move from equality to priority earns its keep.

***
