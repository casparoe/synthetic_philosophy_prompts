I recently started a research assistantship in a lab that runs ensemble weather forecasts, and it has made me rewatch Jurassic Park with fresh eyes. At a department social last week I mentioned this to a colleague in the physics PhD program, and we ended up in a long argument about what the film is actually claiming. Her position is that Ian Malcolm's chaos-theory speeches amount to an argument that the park was impossible in principle, and that the film endorses this; my position is that Malcolm is only making a point about the limits of prediction, and that a deterministic universe is perfectly compatible with everything he says and everything that happens on screen. I'd like you to settle this by reading the film closely through the philosophy of chaos and determinism.

Specifically: distinguish carefully between sensitive dependence on initial conditions, which is an epistemic limitation on predictors like us, and genuine indeterminism in the physical world, and ask which of these the film's events actually illustrate. Does anything in the plot require the universe to be non-deterministic, or would Laplace's demon have foreseen the whole disaster, Nedry's sabotage, the frog DNA, the storm and all? Relatedly, does the film overreach when it moves from the claim that complex systems are unpredictable to the claim that they are uncontrollable? Our ensemble forecasts are unpredictable in detail yet we still act on them, so I'm suspicious of Malcolm's slide from one to the other. And consider a complication my colleague raised: arguably the park failed for boring reasons -- underinvestment, one disgruntled employee, no redundancy in critical systems -- rather than for anything specific to chaos. If that's right, is Malcolm's framing a misdiagnosis that happens to sound deep, and does the film itself seem to notice (say, in how it stages Hammond's arc)?

Please be faithful in both directions: don't flatten the film into a textbook illustration, but don't let the film's rhetoric distort the philosophy either. Where Malcolm's argument genuinely anticipates something real about complex systems and control, say so; where the film trades on a conflation, name it precisely. I'd love to end up with a reading I could actually bring back to the lab and to my colleague -- ideally one that says what a disaster film can show about determinism and predictability that a philosophy paper can't.
