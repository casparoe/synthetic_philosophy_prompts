My flatmate is an econ grad student and has been constructing this argument at me all weekend, mostly over breakfast, and I can't find the loose brick. It starts from Hume's essay Of Money — specifically the bit where Hume concedes that even though the absolute amount of money in a country makes no difference to anyone's real happiness, an *increasing* supply is another matter: when new money enters a nation it piles up in a few hands first (his example is merchants receiving silver returns for goods shipped to Cadiz), those people hire extra workmen and place extra orders before anyone thinks to demand higher prices or wages, and only as the money slowly disperses through the whole population do prices catch up to it. So there's a window, between the money arriving and prices rising, where the new money genuinly makes people more industrious and output is really, physically higher. Fine so far. Then he goes:

(1) Inside that window the extra production is real — more cloth woven, more ships fitted out, not just bigger numbers on price tags. (2) Prices only catch up because each round of new money eventually spreads through everyone; so as long as fresh money keeps being injected at the top before the previous round has fully dispersed, the window never actually closes. (3) A central bank can inject money continuously, forever, at essentially zero cost to itself. (4) Therefore a central bank can hold an economy permanently inside Hume's window — output permanently above where it would otherwise sit, paid for with nothing but rising prices, which on Hume's own neutrality point don't touch real welfare anyway. (5) He says he's delighted to bite this bullet, and that every government in history has simply lacked the nerve.

I refuse to accept (4)–(5); it's a free lunch and nothing is a free lunch. But each step looks locally fine, and I can't name what slipped in or where. Which step do I reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost me? If I'm forced, I'd rather give up the no-free-lunch principle than (1), since Hume's mechanism seems to describe something that actually happened historically — but I'd like to know if there's a cheaper exit.
