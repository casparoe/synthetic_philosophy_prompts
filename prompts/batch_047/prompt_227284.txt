I'm preparing a position paper for a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind and, before I commit to it, I'd like an audit of whether my views can coherently be held together. I've become convinced that teleosemantics is the right framework for naturalizing intentionality, but people in the seminar keep pulling me in different directions, and I suspect I may be trying to have things both ways. Here is where I currently stand: (1) Mental content must be naturalized, and the most promising strategy grounds representational content in biological function — specifically, in what a mechanism was selected for doing, where selection is a matter of actual evolutionary history. (2) Within teleosemantics, I favor the consumer-based version: what a representation means is fixed not by what produces it but by what the downstream systems that use it were designed to do with it; on this picture the frog's snap mechanism represents food rather than a small dark moving thing, because it is the usefulness of catching flies to the consuming systems that explains why the mechanism was retained. (3) I have the strong intuition that a Swampman — a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a person, formed by sheer cosmic accident with no evolutionary or learning history whatsoever — would have beliefs and desires with determinate content from the very first moment of its existence, and I take this intuition seriously enough that I want any theory I accept to vindicate it. (4) Any acceptable theory of content must leave room for genuine misrepresentation: it must be possible for a token representation to be false, and the theory must not collapse into treating every token as veridical of whatever disjunctive condition happens to trigger it. (5) Content determinacy is achievable in principle; in particular, the standard indeterminacy challenges — why fly rather than fly-or-fly-shaped-shadow, why the distal stimulus rather than the proximal retinal pattern — are questions a sufficiently careful teleosemantics can and should answer without remainder. (6) Learned concepts have content too: a creature that acquires a representation of a particular predator during its own lifetime, through individual learning rather than selection, genuinely represents that predator, and the theory of content should extend to such cases.

What I'd like is a consistency check on this particular package, not a general defense or critique of teleosemantics. Go through the six commitments and tell me which of them genuinely pull against each other and which only appear to. For each real tension, say how severe it is — I'd like the tensions ranked rather than treated as equally grave — and identify which commitment would be cheapest to surrender or weaken, along with what that concession would cost the overall view. For any apparent conflict that dissolves on closer inspection, show why it dissolves, because I don't want to revise anything unnecessarily. If it turns out that one single revision would clean up most of the problems, I'd like to know which one it is.
