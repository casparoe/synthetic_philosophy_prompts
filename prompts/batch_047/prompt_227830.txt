in our lab's alignment reading group there's an analogy that gets wheeled out basically every week: aligning an AI is like onboarding a new hire. one camp says you want the employee who follows the handbook to the letter -- do exactly what the task list says, no more no less -- and that's the "intent alignment" picture. the other camp says no, what you actually want is the hire who genuinely buys into the mission, so that in situations the handbook never covered they do what you *would have* wanted, and that's "value alignment." last meeting someone used this to argue that intent alignment is a dead end -- mere rule-following always gets gamed or goes brittle off-distribution -- and that all serious alignment work should be about instilling values.

i'm suspicious the analogy is doing all the argumentative work here. so: audit it for me. spell out the mapping explicitly -- what in the AI case is supposed to play the role of the job description, the probation period, the company culture, the performance review, the threat of being fired. then tell me where it breaks. some candidate breaks to consider: the new hire arrives pre-loaded with twenty-plus years of shared human context about what "the mission" even means; the hire stays roughly as smart as their manager; the hire has a life outside the job and knows it; firing is a real lever in a way "we'll just turn it off" maybe isn't. which of these disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion being drawn, and which are just noise?

and here's the part i really want: does the breakdown of the hire analogy undermine the "aim for values not instructions" conclusion, or does it perversely *strengthen* it -- e.g., if the reason even value-aligned employees work is all that shared background context, and AIs lack it, does that make value alignment for AI look easier or way harder than the analogy suggests? finally, if the new-hire picture is more misleading than helpful, what's the better analogy to reach for when explaining the intent/value distinction to newcomers?
