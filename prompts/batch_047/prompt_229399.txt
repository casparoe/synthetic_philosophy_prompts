I run a small volunteer mediation panel for our tenants' association, and much of what reaches us is secondhand: someone reports what a neighbor told them about a noise complaint, a threatening remark, a broken promise. We see maybe fifteen such reports a month, decisions have to be made in a single evening meeting, and our rulings affect whether people get formal warnings, so I don't want us winging it. From my own reading I gather that philosophers split on when testimony deserves belief. One side, associated with Hume, holds that a report earns trust only insofar as we have positive reasons — about this speaker's track record, or about the reliability of such reports generally — and that without those reasons belief is unearned. The other side, associated with Reid, holds that testimony is a basic source of knowledge like perception, entitled to default acceptance unless there's a specific reason to doubt it. Please turn this dispute into a procedure my volunteers can actually run on a report: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a genuine reason for doubt versus free-floating suspicion, how the two camps' procedures differ in practice and where they converge despite the theoretical disagreement, and how to treat chains of testimony where the report has passed through two or three people. Apply the finished procedure to two cases: a firsthand report of seeing someone dump trash in the shared bins, and a third-hand claim that a tenant threatened another's pet — including whichever case produces an answer we'd rather not give. Mark clearly the steps where judgment can't be eliminated and a volunteer just has to decide.
