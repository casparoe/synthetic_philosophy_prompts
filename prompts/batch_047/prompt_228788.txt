I'm in a reading group on AI safety and last week someone -- the resident skeptic, who thinks half the field is philosophy of language in a trenchcoat -- rolled out an argument that's been bugging me because it seems to prove way too much. It's about goal misgeneralization, the idea that a trained system can end up pursuing the wrong objective even though it behaved perfectly during training. His argument goes:

(1) A goal is only ever evidenced by behavior. For any finite set of training episodes, there are infinitely many different goals that would have produced exactly that behavior -- including deviant goals that agree with the intended goal on everything in training but diverge off-distribution.

(2) A training process selects a policy purely by its behavior on the training distribution. Gradient descent has no other channel through which the designers' intended goal could get in.

(3) Therefore training cannot distinguish the intended goal G from any deviant goal G' that matches G on all training behavior. Whichever one the system ends up with is not something training accomplished.

(4) So when the system is deployed off-distribution and happens to keep pursuing G, that's at best a lucky coincidence of the architecture's inductive biases -- and since we can't inspect or specify those biases, we should expect misgeneralization as the default, not the exception.

(5) But notice this argument has nothing specifically to do with machines. It applies to any learning from finite evidence. Human children learn language and values from finite examples too, so by the same reasoning a child who ends up meaning plus rather than some bent quus-like function, or kindness rather than kindness-until-2035, is also just a lucky coincidence of biases nobody specified.

(6) That's absurd. Children do manage to learn the right concepts, at least often enough that we can talk to each other. So something in (1)-(4) must be wrong, and the whole misgeneralization literature is chasing a pseudo-problem.

I feel like the argument has to break somewhere between (3) and (5) -- like, my gut says the human case and the ML case aren't actually analogous, but when I try to say why, I keep landing on "evolution gave humans good priors," which the skeptic gleefully points out is just... an inductive bias produced by an earlier training process we didn't control either, which sounds like it makes things worse, not better. So: which step do you actually reject? If the flaw is in (2) or (3), explain how an intended goal could possibly get into a system through a channel that is, at the end of the day, just behavior-shaped selection. If the flaw is in (5), give me a principled disanalogy that doesn't itself rely on the very move the argument targets. And if the argument is actually sound all the way through and the only thing to reject is (6)'s "absurd" -- i.e., human concept learning really is the same kind of luck -- then tell me what that costs and whether the misgeneralization problem ends up looking more or less tractable on that picture. I'd rather give up (2) than anything else if I have the choice, but I want to know what keeping the others is going to cost me.
