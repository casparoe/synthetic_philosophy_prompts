I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm drafting a column on insect sentience. Before I file it I want to stress-test my own positions, because I suspect I'm trying to have things both ways somewhere. Here's where I've landed:

(1) My credences: something like 5% that bees are sentient, well under 1% for fruit flies. Low but not negligible.

(2) The scale argument works: there are so many insects that even these tiny probabilities multiply out to enormous expected moral weight, which is why I think insect welfare deserves serious research funding, arguably more than most vertebrate welfare causes.

(3) The empirical evidence everyone's excited about (nociception, the bee ball-rolling stuff, motivational trade-offs) doesn't move me much above my prior, because every one of those behaviors has a plausible reflexive explanation that doesn't require felt experience.

(4) In other domains I roll my eyes at arguments that multiply tiny probabilities by astronomical stakes. I think that's basically a mugging, and that any decision theory that takes those arguments seriously has gone wrong.

(5) Despite (2), I don't think anyone is obligated to change their actual behavior much yet -- driving, pest control, farming practices can mostly carry on while the science catches up.

So: which of these are genuinely in tension and which just look like it? I'd like the tensions ranked by severity, with what the cheapest fix would be for each, and if there's one commitment whose removal tidies up the most, tell me which. I'm especially unsure whether (3) quietly undermines (1) and (2), but don't let me steer you.
