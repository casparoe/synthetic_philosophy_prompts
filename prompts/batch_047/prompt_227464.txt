I recently got into an argument with a colleague about the unilateralist's curse, and I want to settle it with a well-built thought experiment. For context: the curse is the structural problem that arises whenever several people each have the individual power to launch some irreversible initiative — publishing a sensitive document, releasing a piece of dual-use software, starting a project that will be hard to stop — and the initiative goes ahead as soon as even one person judges it worthwhile. Since everyone's private estimate of the initiative's value contains some error, the person who acts will tend to be the one whose estimate errs on the optimistic side, so such initiatives get launched more often than the group's average judgment would justify. My colleague insists this is really just a story about overconfidence and bias: calibrate people properly, warn them about the phenomenon, and the curse evaporates. My hunch is that it is a selection effect rather than a bias, and can therefore survive perfect individual calibration.

Build me a case that settles this: a scenario in which every agent is individually well-calibrated (their estimates of such initiatives are, on average, neither too high nor too low), everyone knows about the curse and sincerely tries to correct for it, nobody acts maliciously or irrationally, and yet a net-harmful initiative still gets launched. Constraints: a realistic contemporary setting (a research lab, an open-source project, a newsroom, a grant panel — your choice), no hidden agendas and no one withholding information out of spite, and the scenario itself should be a single paragraph. The bad outcome must be generated by the structure of the setup, not stipulated into it; a case that simply declares the agents miscalibrated, or has someone ignore a warning they had every reason to heed, would concede the argument to my colleague. One thing to watch: awareness of the curse only protects you if you can estimate how many others are quietly deliberating about the same initiative, so make sure your scenario gives a realistic reason why that number is unknown to the person who ends up acting.

After the scenario, show the work. Demonstrate that each agent's calibration survives intact (their errors are symmetric and cancel out across cases, even though the error of whoever acts does not). Walk through the deliberation of the person who acts and show that it is locally rational given what they know. Explain precisely why general awareness of the phenomenon fails to help them — the agent cannot tell ex ante that they are the optimistic tail of the distribution rather than a moderate voice. Identify the single load-bearing feature of the case and say whether removing it collapses the scenario back into an ordinary overconfidence story. Close by describing what institutional change would actually dissolve the curse in your setting (deliberation requirements, cooling-off periods, pooled estimates) and what that fix costs, so I can judge whether the cure is worse than the disease.
