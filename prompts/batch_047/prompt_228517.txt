An epistemologist I know has been giving me her snap verdicts on a run of cases about knowing how, and I want to figure out what she's committed to before next week's case. Her calls so far: a jazz guitarist who plays superbly but, when asked to explain what he is doing, gives garbled and demonstrably false descriptions — knows how to play; a musicologist who can describe correct guitar technique in exhaustive, accurate detail but freezes with an instrument in her hands — does not know how to play; a man with dense amnesia who sincerely insists he has never touched a piano, yet plays competently the moment he is seated at one — knows how to play; and a chef with a freshly broken wrist who cannot cook for the next six weeks — still knows how to cook while the cast is on. Next week's case: a concert violinist develops a neurological condition that permanently scrambles fine motor control in her fingers, so that under no conditions can she play again, though her ear and judgment are unimpaired and her real-time instructions let a talented student produce performances indistinguishable from her own old recordings. Predict her verdict, and tell me which rule she appears to be running: that knowing how is nothing over and above the retained capacity to perform, that it is a species of propositional knowledge held in a practical rather than articulable form, that it is the capacity to produce successful performance whether through one's own body or another's, or some fourth rule that also fits her record. If more than one of these fits her calls so far, say which you take her to be actually using and why, and describe the single further case whose verdict would settle the question.
