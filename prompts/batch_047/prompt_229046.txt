During my office's annual giving campaign last month, a coworker invoked Singer's drowning-child thought experiment to argue that the rest of us were approaching charity all wrong. His pitch ran roughly as follows. If you walked past a shallow pond where a child was drowning, and you could save her at the cost of ruining a five-hundred-dollar pair of shoes, refusing would be monstrous. But effective charities can save a child's life for a comparable sum. So declining to donate, while continuing to spend on concert tickets and restaurant meals, is morally indistinguishable from standing at the pond's edge scrolling on your phone. Distance, invisibility, and the fact that plenty of other well-paid people could also give are, he insisted, morally irrelevant. He concluded that people at our income level are obligated to give vastly more than we do — perhaps until further giving would cost us as much as the rescue would — and he said the pond case settles it. I felt the pull of the argument, but I also suspect it is doing more work than it legitimately can.

Could you audit the analogy for me? Start by laying out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the pond, the drowning child, the wading, and the ruined shoes in his version — and then identify where the mapping strains. The candidate disanalogies I keep circling are these. In the pond case, rescue is direct and nearly certain, whereas donations are mediated by institutions I cannot observe and save lives only probabilistically; I am the sole bystander at the pond, whereas there are millions of non-donors, and I genuinely do not know whether their failure raises, lowers, or leaves unchanged my own obligation, or whether my donation simply crowds in or crowds out government funding; the pond is a one-time emergency with an identifiable victim, whereas global poverty is chronic and the beneficiaries are statistical; and the cost at the pond is bounded — one pair of shoes, and the episode is over — whereas the charities can absorb effectively unlimited funds, so the analogy seems to convert "save this one child" into "keep wading back in forever." Which of these disanalogies actually bear on my coworker's conclusion, and which are distractions from it?

If the demanding conclusion survives the audit only in a weakened form, I would like to know what the strongest surviving version looks like — and whether there is a better analogy for that weaker claim than the pond, since at the moment my coworker's rhetoric seems to be carried almost entirely by the image of the drowning child, and I want to know how much weight that image can actually bear.
