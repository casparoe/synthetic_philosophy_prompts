So I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm writing a piece on what, at bottom, Marx thought was wrong with capitalism. I've interveiwed two scholars who barely agree on anything. One is an exploitation-first reader: the core injustice is that workers are made to hand over unpaid labor, and this would still be a scandal even in a perfectly pleasant, stimulating workplace. The other is an alienation-first reader: the core wrong is that wage labor estranges people from what they make, how they make it, each other, and their own human capacities, and exploitation only matters as one symptom of that deeper fracture. They keep talking past each other, so for my piece I want the document a good-faith adversarial collaboration between them would produce.

Give me: first, a numbered list of claims both could actually sign -- about what Marx says across the early and late writings, about what capitalism structurally involves, about what would or wouldn't count as fixing the problem. Second, the exact points of disagreement, each phrased in wording its own side would accept, no strawmen. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions where settling one would genuinely move at least one of them. I suspect one crux is whether alienation without exploitation is possible (say, fulfilling self-directed work inside a market economy) and another is whether exploitation without alienation is (miserable, meaningless work where the worker keeps the full product). For each crux, name a case or argument both would accept as bearing on it, and write down in advance what each side predicts about it.

Two more things. If part of their fight is really verbal -- two senses of exploitation or of alienation doing the damage -- say which part and why, and commit to that call rather than hedging. And don't give me a menu of ways to draft this: make the definite choices, take a position on which crux is the real load-bearing one, and give your reasons. I can push back myself; what I can't use is a shrug.

Hard limit: 850 words, total. Agreed-claims section first, then the disagreements, then the cruxes with predictions.
