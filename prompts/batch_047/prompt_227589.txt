I write a science-and-ideas column for a British weekly and I am deep into a long essay tracing the idea of subsidiarity across federal systems -- the United States, Germany, Switzerland, and the EU. For the American section I have been reading the less-celebrated Federalist papers rather than the usual Numbers 10 and 51, and I have become genuinely stuck on a passage in Number 32, where Hamilton defends the states' power of taxation. Here is the passage: "This exclusive delegation, or rather this alienation, of State sovereignty, would only exist in three cases: where the Constitution in express terms granted an exclusive authority to the Union; where it granted in one instance an authority to the Union, and in another prohibited the States from exercising the like authority; and where it granted an authority to the Union, to which a similar authority in the States would be absolutely and totally CONTRADICTORY and REPUGNANT. I use these terms to distinguish this last case from another which might appear to resemble it, but which would, in fact, be essentially different; I mean where the exercise of a concurrent jurisdiction might be productive of occasional interferences in the POLICY of any branch of administration, but would not imply any direct contradiction or repugnancy in point of constitutional authority." Later in the same essay he writes: "The restriction in question amounts to what lawyers call a NEGATIVE PREGNANT that is, a NEGATION of one thing, and an AFFIRMANCE of another; a negation of the authority of the States to impose taxes on imports and exports, and an affirmance of their authority to impose them on all other articles."

Could you help me understand this passage properly? Specifically: first, reconstruct Hamilton's three-case taxonomy of "alienated" state sovereignty in your own words, and explain why he insists on distinguishing his third case (constitutional repugnancy, as with the uniform rule of naturalisation) from mere policy friction between concurrent jurisdictions -- what work is that distinction doing for him against the Anti-Federalist worry he is answering? Second, explain the "negative pregnant" argument: why does Hamilton think the Constitution's express prohibition on state import duties is itself evidence that the states retain all their other taxing powers, and what is the inferential structure of that move? Third, the interpretive question that matters for my essay: does Hamilton's scheme anticipate the modern European architecture of subsidiarity and catalogued competences -- the EU's division into exclusive, shared, and supporting competences, or German concurrent legislation in which Land authority recedes only so far as the federation has legislated, or the Swiss model of residual cantonal powers -- or is it a structurally different idea altogether, namely residual concurrent powers held by the states with federal pre-emption operating only at the point of actual conflict? I would like a considered answer to that third question, not a shrug in both directions.

Please write in British English. End your response with a final line beginning "Verdict:" followed by one sentence stating, in a form I can lift into my notes, whether Hamilton's scheme in Number 32 is best described as subsidiarity avant la lettre or as something else, naming in a phrase what that something else is.
