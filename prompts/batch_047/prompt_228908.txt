So here's the situation: my mom was recently diagnosed with early-stage dementia. She's mostly fine right now, but things will get worse, and my sister and I keep circling the same fight -- when my mom loses more of her memory, and her personality shifts (she's already gotten weirdly irritable, which is NOT like her), will she still be her? I promised her years ago I'd never put her in a home, and I don't know if a promise made to the-person-she-was is owed to the-person-she'll-be, or if that's even a coherent thing to say.

Down a rabbit hole I found out philosophers have an actual fight about how things persist through time: endurantism (things are wholly present at each moment of their existence) vs. perdurantism (things persist by having temporal parts, like how a road extends through space by having spatial parts). I want to see the disagreement actually play out on my case rather than read another dry overview.

Write a dialogue between a competent endurantist and a competent perdurantist (give them names) who are both friends of mine and have come along to visit my mom with me. During the visit and on the drive home they debate which view makes better sense of what's happening to her. Some things I'd like covered, though feel free to go beyond them:

- Whether the perdurantist's "she's a four-dimensional worm of person-stages" picture is comforting, horrifying, or neither, and whether it secretly dissolves my question rather than answering it
- The temporary intrinsics problem (how can the very same thing have contradictory properties at different times) and whether the endurantist's answer actually helps with my mom or just relocates the puzzle
- Whether perdurantism's talk of temporal parts makes my promise-keeping question easier or just different, since the "mom" I promised and the "mom" now are supposedly different stages of the same worm
- What each view says about whether it's ok that my feelings toward her are changing

Neither of them should be a strawman -- both should have thought about this seriously, and they should respond to each other's actual arguments instead of delivering alternating lectures. It's fine (good, even) if they end up disagreeing about which view is more humane rather than one of them winning definately. Keep the tone like a real conversation between friends, around 800-1000 words.
