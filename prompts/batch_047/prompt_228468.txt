A position I keep returning to, and one that I think is fairly standard in rationalist-adjacent circles, is that Bernard Williams's integrity objection to consequentialism — the business about the chemist George, Jim and the Indians, and the famous complaint that the husband who stops to calculate whether saving his wife is permissible has one thought too many — rests on a confusion between a criterion of rightness and a decision procedure. On this view, consequentialism tells you what makes an action right; it does not instruct anyone to deliberate consequentially in the moment. A sophisticated consequentialist, the reply goes, has excellent consequentialist reasons to cultivate exactly the dispositions Williams valorises: loving partiality, project loyalty, the refusal to run the arithmetic before rescuing one's spouse. The person with one thought too many is simply a bad consequentialist, and Williams has at most refuted a straw version of the theory that no careful defender ever held. The integrity objection, properly understood, is practical advice about moral psychology dressed up as a refutation.

I want to stress-test this rather than have it applauded. What would actually count against it? Please map the position's exposure honestly, and in particular:

1. Which objections would its defenders themselves have to concede as damaging, as opposed to objections only its critics find forceful? I am suspicious that the criterion/decision-procedure move is one consequentialists wave through in their own ranks but would never accept from a rival — say, a deontologist who replies to every counterintuitive verdict by distinguishing the criterion of duty from the deliberative process of the virtuous agent. If the move is available to everyone, does it protect anyone?

2. Is the position falsifiable in any interesting sense? If every apparent counterexample — the man who lets his wife drown after a correct calculation, the agent whose projects are ground to dust by aggregation — can be reabsorbed as a case of suboptimal deliberative policy, has the theory bought immunity at the price of saying nothing? Distinguish, where you can, between consequences that would be decisive defeaters and consequences that would merely be awkward, and be willing to say if you think the position has been insulated past the point where 'awkward' is even a category available to it.

3. There is a variant of the thought experiment I have never seen discussed and would like you to take seriously rather than dismiss: suppose the deliberation is costless. Imagine an agent with instantaneous, perfect, non-conscious computation of expected consequences — the calculation happens in no time and crowds out no thought, so the wife is saved in exactly the same motion, with the rescue now issuing from the calculation rather than from love. Williams's point, I take it, is meant to survive this amendment; the complaint was never that deliberation is slow. But if that is right, then the criterion/decision-procedure reply cannot be the whole story, since in this variant the criterion and the procedure coincide and the objection is supposed still to bite. Does this show the reply misses Williams's real target — something about the justificatory structure of partial concern, not the psychology of it — or does it instead expose that Williams's intuition itself depends on smuggling in the phenomenology of hesitation?

4. Finally: what would the position have to look like for you to regard it as having collapsed into a rival? If the sophisticated consequentialist is permitted to be, in the moment, indistinguishable from a virtue ethicist or a deontologist, in what does the disagreement consist — and is there any possible world in which the two theories issue different verdicts, or have we located a dispute with no empirical or normative content?

The outcome I would personally find most persuasive is not a verdict that Williams wins, but a demonstration that the reply is doing real work somewhere I haven't seen, or conversely that it is pure protective belt. I would rather be told the position is well insulated and that this is a problem than be given a balanced both-sides tour.
