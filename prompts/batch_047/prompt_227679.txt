A colleague of mine has an argument that seems to prove that Bongard problems — the visual puzzles in which six figures on the left share some rule that the six figures on the right violate, and the solver must find the rule — do not actually have correct answers. I can't accept the conclusion, but I can't find the hole either. Here it is:

(1) For any finite set of labeled examples, there are infinitely many mutually incompatible concepts that classify all of them correctly. This seems to be a plain fact of logic: finite data never entail a general rule.

(2) If two incompatible concepts both classify all observed examples correctly, then those examples give a learner exactly the same reason to accept the one as to accept the other. The data cannot favor what they cannot distinguish.

(3) A Bongard problem presents only its twelve labeled figures — six positive, six negative — and nothing else.

(4) Therefore, by (1)–(3), the twelve figures of a Bongard problem give the learner exactly the same reason to accept each of infinitely many incompatible rules.

(5) If the evidence gives equal reason to accept each of infinitely many incompatible rules, then selecting any one of them is arbitrary; there is no fact of the matter as to which is the rule.

(6) Therefore, Bongard problems have no correct answer. The so-called intended solution is merely the designer's arbitrary selection from an infinite menu, and the fact that most human solvers converge on it is a coincidence of shared taste or shared training, not a case of successfully learning a rule that was really there.

The conclusion strikes me as absurd — people plainly do solve these problems, and their agreement would be a staggering coincidence if there were nothing to be right about. I'd most like to keep (1), since it looks like a theorem, and I refuse to accept (6). So please tell me:
