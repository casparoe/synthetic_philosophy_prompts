Next month I'm presenting at a philosophy of science colloquium, defending a proposal in the literature on verisimilitude, and I want to be ready for the question period. My thesis: the notion of truthlikeness is not dead, but it only becomes well-defined once you relativize it to a question. Popper's original definition failed on the Tichý–Miller trivialization result, and I'll grant that. The later approaches I'll engage with are the likeness program (Oddie, Niiniluoto), which measures distance between a theory's allowed worlds and the actual world, and the content program. My move is to argue that the notorious language-dependence problem (Miller's argument that comparative truthlikeness judgments flip under re-axiomatization of the language) is not an objection to the likeness program but a discovery: truthlikeness is genuinely relative to a partition of logical space supplied by a research question, and the metric over that partition is fixed by what the scientists asking that question treat as relevantly similar. Scientific progress is then real but always progress-toward-truth-on-a-question, never toward the truth simpliciter.

The audience: mostly general philosophers of science with realist sympathies, plus two formal epistemologists who will have actually read the technical literature, and one senior philosopher who still thinks Popper's definition was unfairly maligned. No one there works on verisimilitude full-time.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections this particular audience will raise, roughly ordered by likelihood and by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that person would actually phrase it. For each, tell me what I should concede up front, what my reply is, and where that reply is softest. I especially want the objection from the formal epistemologists about whether my partition-relativity just collapses truthlikeness into usefulness, and the objection I'm hoping nobody asks. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you'd write a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
