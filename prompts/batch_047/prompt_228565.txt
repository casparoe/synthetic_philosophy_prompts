Hey, so I'm in a second-year course on philosophy of history and we're doing the whole contingency vs. inevitability thing -- Cleopatra's nose, for want of a nail, chaos theory applied to history, all that. I want to pressure-test it on a case I think I know decently well: the fall of the Berlin Wall in November 1989.

The story as I understand it: the East German regime was already wobbling badly. Hungary had opened its border with Austria earlier that year and East Germans were fleeing west through there and via the Prague embassy, there were the huge Monday demonstrations in Leipzig, and Gorbachev had made it pretty clear the Soviets weren't going to send tanks in like in '53 or '68. The politburo decided to loosen the travel rules to take the pressure off, and then at a press conference on November 9th Günter Schabowski, who apparently hadn't been properly briefed and was reading off notes he didn't fully understand, said the new rules applied immediately. Crowds massed at the Bornholmer Straße crossing, the officer in charge couldn't get clear orders from anyone and just opened the gate, and by morning the Wall was functionally finished.

Here's the analysis I actually want. My prof keeps pushing a distinction between trigger-contingency (the specific spark was accidental, could easily have been different) and outcome-contingency (the thing that ultimately resulted could have been otherwise). Apply that distinction carefully to this case: which parts of the episode were contingent in which sense? My gut says the timing and the wierdly peaceful manner were highly trigger-contingent but the collapse of the GDR itself was close to overdetermined -- push back on me if you think that's wrong.

Second: chaos theory talks about sensitive dependence on initial conditions, but it also has attractors, states a system keeps falling into no matter how you perturb it. If you had to classify "East German regime collapse by 1991" as either a butterfly or an attractor, which is it, and what does the Schabowski press conference specifically do to your answer?

Third, and this is the direction I find hardest: what does the episode show about the contingency framework itself? A worry I have is that contingency stories always seem to explain when and how something happened but never whether it happened, which would make the whole for-want-of-a-nail style of history way less revisionary than it sounds. Does this case confirm that worry or cut against it? And be honest about where the chaos metaphor just breaks -- we can't rerun November 1989 with a properly briefed Schabowski, so in what sense is any of this even testable?
