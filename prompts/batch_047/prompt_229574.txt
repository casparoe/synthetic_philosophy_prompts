For my school's literature-and-philosophy elective, each of us has to close the semester by presenting a short poem that engages one of the texts we read, and I have chosen the butterfly dream passage from the Zhuangzi. What struck me, and what I want the poem to capture, is that the passage is usually misread as a Chinese anticipation of Descartes -- as if Zhuangzi were raising dream skepticism in order to doubt the external world. But as I understand it, the passage ends very differently: the dreamer wakes, is briefly unsure whether he is Zhou who dreamed of being a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming it is Zhou, and the resolution is not doubt but the observation that there is nevertheless a real distinction between Zhou and the butterfly -- and that this shifting between identities is itself what the text calls the transformation of things. So the point is almost the opposite of Cartesian: the uncertainty is not a threat to knowledge but a picture of how identities pass into one another while remaining distinct.

Please write me a villanelle in which this structure does the work. The two alternating refrains should be the two hypotheses -- Zhuang Zhou dreaming he is a butterfly, and a butterfly dreaming it is Zhuang Zhou -- phrased in your own words rather than borrowed from any published translation. I want the form of the villanelle to mirror the argument: the two refrains trade places across the tercets the way the two identities trade places in the dream, and when they finally meet in the closing quatrain, their side-by-side placement should enact the distinction-within-transformation rather than a skeptical stalemate. The middle tercets should not just repeat the puzzle but develop it -- perhaps touching on how the butterfly in the dream is perfectly content in its butterfly-ness and does not know it is Zhuang Zhou, which is what makes the waking disorientation so strange.

Two constraints. First, resist every temptation toward the Cartesian reading: no imagery of deception, evil demons, unreliable senses, or the world dissolving as unreal, since that is precisely the misreading I want to argue against in my presentation. Second, the poem should work as a poem for a listener who has never read the passage -- concrete imagery of the garden, the fluttering, the waking -- so that the philosophical point emerges from the scene and the returning lines rather than from abstract statements about identity. Roughly nineteen lines, of course, given the form, and I would be glad of a title that nods to the transformation of things.
