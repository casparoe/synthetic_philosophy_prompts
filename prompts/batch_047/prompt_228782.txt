I spent forty years as an experimental condensed-matter physicist before retiring, and in the last few years I have been reading moral philosophy with the same itch to systematize that I once brought to phase transitions. I would like help reconstructing the implicit theory of a friend and former colleague -- call her a chemist -- from her verdicts on cases where one person's contribution to a collective harm seems to make no difference. Her four verdicts, with her stated reasons, are these:

(1) She votes in every election, including municipal races she concedes are foregone conclusions, and she maintains that voting is obligatory even though no ballot of hers has ever changed, or plausibly could change, an outcome. She calls non-voters free-riders.

(2) She has not bought industrially farmed meat in fifteen years. Her reasoning is not expressive: she argues that retailers reorder in discrete units, so each purchase carries some small probability of being the marginal one that triggers another order, and given the number of animals per order, the expected cost of buying is not trivial. She reached this view on her own, though I gather philosophers have a similar threshold or expected-impact argument.

(3) She makes no effort whatsoever to reduce her household electricity consumption. The grid, she says, does not register her kilowatt-hours, and she regards guilt on this score as superstition.

(4) She flies several times a year to visit her grandchildren, buys no offsets, and loses no sleep over it: the aircraft departs whether or not she is seated in it, and fleet and route decisions are made far above the weight class of a single passenger.

Now the new case. Her retirement savings sit in a broad index fund with holdings in both fossil-fuel companies and industrial meat producers. A screened fund excluding those sectors is available at essentially identical fees and historical performance. I want to know whether she will conclude -- or, better, what she should conclude to remain consistent with herself -- that she is obligated to switch. Note the wrinkle that interests me most: in case (2) she boycotts the products of an industry whose shares she profits from in the new case.

Please do the following. First, state the two or three candidate principles, each consistent with all four verdicts, that best reconstruct the rule she is running -- stated precisely enough that they yield verdicts rather than slogans. I expect one of them will be a threshold expected-impact principle and another something closer to a prohibition on participating in or profiting from a wrongful practice, but formulate them properly and say how each accommodates the vote in case (1). Second, state what each candidate principle predicts about the pension switch, and where they diverge. Third, give me your best prediction of what she will say, with your reasons, and identify which of the four verdicts is the closest analog to the pension case and why. Fourth, if the four verdicts genuinely underdetermine the answer, say so honestly, and state what further commitment each resolution would amount to. Fifth, propose one further case, her verdict on which is unknown to me, that would best discriminate between the two leading reconstructions, and state what verdict each reconstruction predicts for it.

Begin with your prediction in the very first sentence, then give the reasoning -- no preamble, no hedging introduction, and no restating of the question. Please write in American English.
