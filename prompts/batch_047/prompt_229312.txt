I'm TAing for an intro aesthetics course and grading responses to this short-essay prompt:

"Suppose we discover that Vermeer's Girl with a Pearl Earring is actually a perfect twentieth-century forgery by van Meegeren -- visually indistinguishable from what everyone took it to be. Would its value drop, and if so, what kind of value would it lose? Answer with reference to Goodman's autographic/allographic distinction and Danto's indiscernible counterparts."

Here's one student's answer:

"Its value would drop enormously, and Goodman explains why. For Goodman, painting is autographic -- unlike music or literature, where the work is fully determined by a score or text, the physical object itself is the work. So a copy, however perfect, simply is not the painting. But Goodman thinks this is ultimately a matter of history rather than appearance: since two pictures can be visually identical yet differ in value, the value difference must lie entirely in provenance and our knowledge of it, not in anything we could ever see in the pictures themselves. Danto makes a similar point with his indiscernible counterparts -- two perceptually identical objects can be different artworks because of their historical context, so for Danto it's the story behind the object, not the object, that determines whether it's art at all. From this it follows that the forgery has no aesthetic value whatsoever, only the decorative value any pretty picture has. The millions the museum paid were, strictly speaking, wasted on mere decoration."

Grade this out of 10 and give me the specific comments you'd write in the margins -- I want to know exactly where it's right, where it goes wrong, and why. Fair warning, I've already caught one student answer that superficially deploys all the right vocabulary while quietly garbling the positions, so don't just check that the names and terms show up.
