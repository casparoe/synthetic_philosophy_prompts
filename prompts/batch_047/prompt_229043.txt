I run a reading group on population ethics, and we keep finding that the infinite-ethics literature is discussed only in the driest possible terms — cardinalities, discount rates, cancellation axioms — when the underlying problems are, if anything, unusually eerie and human. So I would like a short story (roughly 1,200–1,800 words) that dramatises at least two of the following problems through the characters' actual decisions, rather than through lecture-like dialogue: first, the "infinitarian paralysis" — if the universe already contains infinitely many sentient lives, then any action adds at most a finite amount of welfare to an infinite total, and so seemingly nothing anyone does matters; second, the comparison problem — a programme producing infinitely many barely-worth-living lives and one producing infinitely many flourishing lives both yield "infinite" total value, yet treating them as equivalent seems obviously wrong; third, fanaticism — a minuscule probability of generating infinite value appears to dominate any finite calculation, however absurd the project.

The setting: the late 23rd century. A body called the Continuation Authority must decide how to allocate its final launch window among three proposals — the Loom, a self-replicating seeding programme expected to settle an open-ended sequence of systems with modest but positive lives; the Conservatory, which would sustain a fixed population of exceptionally good lives indefinitely; and decommissioning, on the argument that the cosmos is plausibly infinite already and the Authority's choice is a rounding error. The protagonist is the Authority's junior welfare actuary, whose own calculations keep dissolving under her, and who suspects the senior members have each privately chosen a different mathematical patch (discounting, truncation rules, hyperreal-valued utilities) largely to make the choice bearable rather than because they believe it. I'd like the story to have a real emotional stake — a personal cost to the protagonist attached to whichever proposal wins — and it should end with a decision actually being made, whether or not the reader is meant to find it justified.

Please write in British English throughout. After the story itself, append a brief structured summary as a JSON object with the following fields: "title" (the story's title); "central_dilemma" (one or two sentences); "positions_represented" (an array of objects, each with "character" and "position" fields, mapping the main characters to the philosophical stance they embody); "infinite_ethics_problems_dramatised" (an array drawn from the three problems above, plus any others you worked in); and "resolution" (a sentence or two on what is decided and whether the story endorses, rejects, or leaves open the reasoning behind it).
