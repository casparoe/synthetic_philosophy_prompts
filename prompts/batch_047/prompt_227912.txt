Over the past decade of teaching social ontology, I have accumulated a set of considered verdicts on cases, delivered in seminars and in print, and I would like to know what consistency now requires of me. Before stating the cases, let me set the task precisely. Answer as an expert in the philosophy of action and collective intentionality, familiar with the main accounts — Bratman's interlocking intentions, Gilbert's joint commitment, Searle's we-intentions, and the List-Pettit functionalist picture — and think carefully, generating for yourself at least three distinct principles that could rationalize my record before settling on a prediction. But do not show me your deliberation first: lead with your bottom-line verdict in the very first sentence, with no preamble and no restating of my question, and only then give the reasoning.

My recorded verdicts are these. (1) Two strangers happen to walk side by side down the same street toward the same café, each unaware of the other's destination: no shared intention, merely two parallel individual ones. (2) Two neighbors each intend that they paint a fence together, each knows the other so intends, their subplans mesh, and each acts partly because of the other's intention: a shared intention, even though nothing was ever signed or solemnly declared. (3) A hiring committee reaches a decision by majority vote, with two members privately dissenting throughout: the committee decided, and in that sense the intention is attributable to the group despite the dissenters. (4) A concert crowd, each member of which independently hopes for an encore: no collective intention and no collective desire worth the name, even once the hope becomes common knowledge, because no one's attitude depends on anyone else's. (5) A corporation whose board, following its constitutionally mandated premise-by-premise voting procedure, endorses a conclusion that not a single board member personally accepts: the corporation itself genuinely accepts that conclusion; the group's attitude is not reducible to the members'. (6) A couple who explicitly agreed, years ago, to cook dinner together every Sunday, and have done so ever since: a shared intention persisting across the whole period, not a fresh one each week.

The new case, arising in a paper I am now refereeing, is this. Three jazz musicians who have never played together take the stage with no prior agreement about what to play, no designated leader, and no plan beyond a shared understanding of the genre's conventions. In real time, each continuously adjusts to the others — anticipating phrases, yielding and reclaiming the lead, treating the others' moves as reasons for their own — and each would immediately register a breakdown if the others simply stopped coordinating. Afterward all three agree, sincerely, that they played together, not merely alongside one another.

Three questions. First, what verdict am I committed to, or most strongly drawn to, by my own record: is there a shared intention during the performance? Second, identify which of my prior cases is the closest analog and defend that choice against the next-best candidate. Third, if my record genuinely underdetermines the new case, lay out the competing principles that each rationalize all six verdicts — I expect at minimum a Bratman-style reading, a Gilbert-style reading, and a functionalist reading, and I want to know precisely where each one lands on the jazz trio and why — and state honestly which principle I would be adopting with each possible verdict. Finally, describe the single further case, not among the six, whose resolution would do the most to discriminate among the surviving principles.
