I work for a county parks department and somehow I've ended up as the person who signs off on all our pest-control plans. Mosquito spraying, treating ant colonies near picnic tables, gassing a wasp nest in a playground tunnel, that sort of thing. A few cases cross my desk every month, more in summer.

Here's my problem. I've read enough of the recent arguments about insect sentience to be genuinely bothered. The basic move is a scale argument: even if the probability that an ant or a mosquito suffers is small, we kill them in such staggering numbers that the expected suffering swamps almost everything else we worry about. But taken at face value this argument seems to prove I should never approve anything, ever, which isn't a procedure, it's a nervous breakdown. Meanwhile if I just ignore it I feel like I'm doing the thing where we decide animals don't count because counting them is inconvenient.

So: write me an actual decision procedure I can run in under an hour per case, no entomologist on staff, with anything expensive getting countersigned by the parks board chair. I want tests to run in order, roughly: how strong is the evidence for sentience in this group of insects (I'd expect the answer to differ for bees vs springtails, and I want the procedure to tell me how to handle groups where nobody has looked at all), how many individuals does the intervention affect, how necessary is the outcome (disease vector vs. people finding ants icky), and whether a different method would kill fewer or kill faster or not kill at all. Crucially I want the procedure to have a non-crazy default for when the sentience evidence is thin, so that "we don't know" doesn't automatically mean both "do nothing" and "do whatever". And tell me where the procedure should just refuse to give an answer and kick it up to a human.

Show it working on three cases: aerial spraying for mosquitoes after West Nile was detected in the county, baiting the ant hills by the picnic area, and destroying a wasp nest in a piece of play equipment. One of those should come out as an answer I won't like and won't be able to sell to the public -- and the procedure should be able to say why that's the right call anyway, or admit where it can't.
