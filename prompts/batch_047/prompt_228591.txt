So I'm a novelist, mid-list, and a friend from my MFA program asked me to blurb her debut. I read it and honestly thought it was a mess -- the middle sags, the ending doesn't earn itself. But she's a good friend and blurbs are currency, so I wrote something like: a startlingly original voice, and a book I kept thinking about long after I finished. All of which is technically true! It IS original. And I did keep thinking about it -- mostly about how the third act collapses. She was thrilled. The publisher slapped it on the cover.

Here's what's eating me: last year my publicist wanted me to claim in an interview that I'd spent a decade researching my last book, when it was more like eight months plus a lot of Wikipedia, and I flat out refused because that would've been a straight-up lie. But apparently I have zero problem with the blurb thing, even though the practical upshot is identical -- a stranger reads the cover, believes something false about the book, and buys it. When I try to explain to myself why one is fine and the other isn't, I keep coming up with stuff like "well, every word in the blurb was true" and "readers know blurbs are puffery anyway," but that feels like lawyering, not ethics.

So: did I actually do anything better or worse in either case, or am I just drawing an arbitrary line to feel okay about myself? If misleading someone with true words is morally different from lying with false ones, what's the difference supposed to rest on -- what I said, what they ended up believing, what they could've figured out if they'd been more careful? And practically: should I say something to my friend, or would that just be offloading my guilt onto her to make myself feel clean?
