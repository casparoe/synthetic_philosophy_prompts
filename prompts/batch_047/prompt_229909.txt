I'm a philosophy PhD student working on population ethics, and I've been reading about the scale argument for insect welfare — roughly, even if there's only a tiny chance insects are sentient, there are something like 10^18 of them alive at any time, so the expected number of sentient insects massively outweighs every vertebrate we worry about. Here's what's bugging me though: expected value is just probability times magnitude, so doesn't this basically settle the matter? For any nonzero probability estimate, multiplying by that population swamps everything else we could possibly fund. So why do people in animal ethics still debate priorities at all instead of just doing the multiplication? Is the whole disagreement really just about what the right probability of insect sentience is — and if so, why hasn't anyone run the experiments to nail down that number so we can finally compute the answer and be done with it?
