I have no formal training in philosophy or cognitive science, but I've spent years as an amateur musician and sound designer, and I've been chewing on an idea I came across attributed to Douglas Hofstadter: that creativity is, at bottom, the making of variations on a theme -- that what creators do is identify the adjustable "knobs" of a concept and twiddle them, and that the deepest creativity lies in discovering that something you took to be fixed is actually a knob at all. I'd like to know whether my gut judgments about actual cases hang together under some principle along these lines, or whether they don't.

My verdicts, as honestly as I can report them:

Transposing a song to a new key and slowing it down feels barely creative at all -- the knobs were obvious and the settings were given by convention. Writing a set of variations on a theme, where the harmony is preserved but rhythm, texture, and voicing are re-imagined, feels very creative, even though in some sense it is "only" twiddling. A painter doing the same haystack thirty times in different light feels genuinely creative to me, though it's exploration of a single, obvious knob. An artist who decides that the number of sides of the canvas, or the expectation that a painting be rectangular, is itself a variable feels more creative still. Someone who sets parameters at random on a synthesizer and keeps only the good accidents: I want to say they deserve little credit, though the results can be wonderful. But a jazz improviser who reacts to accidents in real time and builds on them feels highly creative, and I can't cleanly say why that differs from the cherry-picker. Finally, a producer who heard a mistake -- a tape glitch, a clipping microphone -- and decided that distortion itself could be a musical parameter strikes me as creative in the strongest sense, stronger than any of the knob-twiddlers, even though what they did was arguably just finding one more knob.

So my questions are these. First: is there a principle in the spirit of the knob-twiddling model -- perhaps involving which knobs were visible, how deep in the structure they sit, or what the twiddler contributes beyond selecting among outputs -- that delivers all or most of these verdicts? Second: if the model forces me to give up one of my judgments, which should it be? I suspect the haystacks and the random twiddler are in tension, since both look like low-grade exploration of a known parameter, yet my gut rates them very differently. Third: I'd like to know what the resulting account predicts for a case I haven't settled: a chef who invents no new techniques or flavor pairings, but through years of patient adjustment of proportions produces a version of an existing dish that everyone agrees is revelatory. Is that creativity on the model, and should it be?

Please be honest about where my verdicts, rather than the model, are the problem.
