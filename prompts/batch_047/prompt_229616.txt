In six weeks I am giving the opening address at my borough's annual citizenship ceremony — the formal occasion at which roughly forty new British citizens, from about twenty countries, take their oath and affirmation before the mayor, a deputy lieutenant, their families, and (this year, awkwardly for me) a party of sixth-form politics students and my own former tutor, who has spent her career on democratic theory and will absolutely collar me afterwards. I chair the council's civic committee, so the speech is mine to write, but a recent controversy has made it harder than the usual boilerplate. Our borough has an old custom whereby each new citizen's name is entered into a leather-bound "Roll of the Borough" that has been kept since the 1920s. At a committee meeting last month, one councillor proposed, as a nice symbolic touch, that henceforth the newest citizens should themselves vote on any change to the ceremony's wording. My tutor, who was observing, objected publicly that this was incoherent: you cannot ask people to vote on the rules of the very process through which they became entitled to vote, and pretending otherwise papers over the fact that the boundary of the demos — who counts as one of "us" in the first place — was settled by people on one side of it, with those on the other side having no say. The councillor accused her of talking nonsense; the proposal was shelved; and now I must speak into that silence.

What I want is a speech in which the boundary problem does real structural work rather than appearing as a decorative name-drop. I think my tutor is broadly right, and I am not willing to say the comfortable false thing — that "the people" democratically welcomed these new citizens — because the people doing the welcoming are precisely those whose right to decide was never ratified by the people being welcomed. But I also refuse to say the cynical thing, that the ceremony commemorates an arbitrary act of exclusion dressed up as inclusion, because that would be a cruel thing to tell someone holding a certificate they worked years to earn. The speech needs a third framing: honest about the fact that every generation draws the line provisionally and unilaterally, while insisting that new citizens do not merely join the demos but alter it — the "we" that meets next year is not the "we" that met last year, and the Roll records that fact rather than concealing it. Please keep it to about five minutes (roughly 650–700 words), avoid the clichés of the genre (no "nation of immigrants" rhetoric, no invocation of British values as a list), include no direct quotations from philosophers, and make sure there are at least a couple of moments the sixth-formers and my tutor will recognise as philosophically serious without the families in the room feeling lectured at.
