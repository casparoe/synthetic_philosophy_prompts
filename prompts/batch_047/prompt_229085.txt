Two of my colleagues in a philosophy of probability reading group, Ana and Ben, fell into a long disagreement after our last meeting, and I would like to see it staged properly. The setup that divides them is the following. A wealthy host shows you two sealed envelopes and tells you, truthfully, that one contains exactly twice as much money as the other. You pick one at random, open it, and find €100. The host then offers you the chance to switch to the other envelope before taking anything home. Ana reasons as follows: the other envelope contains either €50 or €200; since you have no information favoring one possibility over the other, each seems equally likely; so the expected value of switching is one half of €50 plus one half of €200, which is €125 and hence better than the €100 in hand. Therefore you should switch — and indeed you should have wanted to switch no matter what amount you found, which suggests you should have preferred the other envelope even before opening yours. Ben replies that this cannot be right: the amounts were fixed before you chose, the situation is perfectly symmetric between the two envelopes, and any argument that recommends switching regardless of what you see must be flawed somewhere, since the very same argument would recommend switching back again.

Please write a dialogue of roughly 600–900 words between Ana and Ben in which they try to locate exactly where the other's reasoning goes wrong — or, failing that, where their own does. Both should be sharp and charitable; neither is a fool, and neither should be written as a mere foil for the other. I want genuine back-and-forth rather than two rehearsed monologues. Ana should press Ben on what, precisely, is supposed to be wrong with the arithmetic of expectations, and why he is entitled to dismiss a calculation rather than an intuition. Ben should press Ana on whether treating €50 and €200 as equally likely is really justified, and on what that assumption tacitly commits her to about the procedure the host used to fill the envelopes in the first place. Ideally, they would come to see that the dispute turns on how the amounts were generated: whether there exists any probability distribution over the smaller amount that makes switching profitable conditional on every possible value you might reveal, and what changes if the relevant distribution has an infinite expected value. They need not reach full agreement, but the dialogue should close with each of them stating, in a sentence or two, what they now take the real crux of the paradox to be and whether they still endorse their opening position.
