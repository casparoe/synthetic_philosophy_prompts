So this started as a bar argument with my ancap friend (every rationalist-adjacent circle has at least one) and now I'm genuinely stuck. His position is that taxation is theft, full stop. I tried to build the strongest case I could that its not, and my case seems to either prove way too much or collapse into his position, and I can't tell where it breaks. Numbering the steps because I know how you people are:

(1) If you knowingly use a benefit that can only exist because of a cooperative scheme that everyone pays into, then you're obligated to pay your share, even if you never explicitly signed up. Otherwise you're a free rider, and free riding is just theft with extra steps.

(2) Roads, courts, police, contract enforcement, the general fact that I can run a business without hiring private security -- I knowingly use all of this daily, and none of it exists without a cooperative scheme that everyone pays into.

(3) So I'm obligated to pay taxes. Taxation isn't theft; it's an invoice. If anything, dodging taxes while using the stuff is the actual theft.

So far so good. But notice the argument doesn't lean on anything specifically about states, so it generalizes:

(4) General principle: whoever provides you a benefit you knowingly use, where the benefit only exists because the beneficiaries get billed, is owed your payment, consent or not.

(5) Now consider a mob that runs protection in some neighborhood. Their patrols genuinely reduce break-ins, the shopkeepers genuinely sleep better, property values genuinely rise. It's a real benefit, knowingly used, and it only exists because the residents get billed.

(6) By (4), the shopkeepers owe the mob. Refusing to pay is free riding.

(7) The shopkeepers do not owe the mob. That's insane.

(8) The two arguments are structurally identical, so the problem has to be in (1)/(4): knowingly using a benefit creates no obligation unless you actually agreed to pay for it.

(9) But now my friend wins. Nobody agreed to be born into a tax jurisdiction, so nobody owes taxes, and the guy who uses everything and pays nothing did nothing wrong. Worse, if using-without-agreeing never obligates anyone, then no cooperative scheme with non-excludable benefits can ever get off the ground against holdouts, even schemes everyone actually wants.

I'd really like to keep (3), and I flatly can't accept (6). So: which step do I reject, why did it look fine in the first place, and what does rejecting it cost me? I suspect something is off in the move from (3) to (4) or in the claim at (8) that the two arguments are identical, but every time I try to articulate a difference between the state and the mob it turns out to be circular ("the state is legitimate and the mob isn't" -- yes, that's what's at issue, thanks). And if the honest answer is that the whole benefits frame is broken and taxation has to be defended on some completely different basis, I want the price tag on that too.
