I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, and by a quirk of our graduate council's bylaws I've spent two years as the student member of my university's Committee on Honorary Naming, which decides which buildings, schools, and fellowships get named after whom. On Tuesday we take up the hardest case of my tenure, and the chair has asked me to draft the committee's decision memo. I'd like you to write that memo. The university has been offered a bequest of roughly forty million dollars from the estate of Dr. Eleanor Vance, an alumna who spent four decades running rural health clinics abroad, gave away essentially everything she inherited and earned, and died with almost nothing. The gift would endow a dozen graduate fellowships a year in perpetuity, but it comes with a condition: the new school of public health must bear her name. Ordinarily this would be the easiest yes imaginable. The complication is the publication last year of her letters, which make the cost of her devotion unmistakable. Her two children were raised largely by her sister from the age of six; she declined to come home during her husband's final illness because a cholera outbreak had just reached her district; in one letter she remarks that she could not justify an hour or a dollar spent on her own family while strangers died for want of both. Her son, now in his sixties, has written to the committee asking us not to present his mother as a model, on the grounds that the family paid the price for other people's miracles.

The committee is split, and each faction has seized on half of a genuinely hard question. The pro-naming faction argues that honoring an achievement and endorsing a whole life are separable, that our unease with Vance is just our own mediocrity speaking, and that we have named buildings for generals and industrialists with far worse on their consciences without ever demanding they be well-rounded. The anti-naming faction argues that a naming commends a life to students, not merely a résumé, and that a life with no room for love, friendship, or any project that isn't duty is a cautionary tale rather than an exemplar, however admirable the clinics were in isolation. What neither side has faced: in Vance's case the achievements and the damage flowed from the very same disposition, so it's unclear the separation the first faction needs is even available; and the second faction hasn't answered the charge that the discomfort says more about us than about her — if everyone reasoned as her critics implicitly recommend, nobody would run the clinics at all. Underneath the procedural wrangling sits the old philosophical question of whether a moral saint, someone whose every action is as good as it could be, is an ideal to hold up to the young or a deformation we should be glad is rare.

The memo should recommend one of the three live options: accept the gift and the name; accept the gift with a formal interpretive condition, such as a permanent installation in the school presenting her life whole, including what it cost; or decline the gift. It should take the son's letter seriously as evidence without treating it as a veto, and it should state honestly what the money would fund and what declining it would forfeit, without letting that fact do the deciding in silence. Begin the memo with the recommendation itself in the first sentence — no preamble, no restating of the committee's charge — then give the reasoning, including the strongest argument for the option you reject and why it doesn't prevail. Keep it under a thousand words; it will be read aloud at the meeting.
