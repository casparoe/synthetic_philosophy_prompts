Someone in my consciousness reading group (call him Marcus) thinks he has a killshot against higher-order theories and honestly I can't find the bad step, but the argument seems to prove way too much, so I'm here. Quick setup so we're on the same page: a higher-order theory says a mental state is conscious when and because the subject has a suitable higher-order representation of it, i.e. a thought or perception-like state representing themself as being in that first-order state. Nothing changes inside the first-order state when it becomes conscious; what changes is that it gets represented. Marcus's argument:

(1) Any representation can misrepresent. It can occur without its target, or describe its target wrong. You can think about the pain in your foot after your foots been amputated. That's not a controversial premise about consciousness, it's just what representation is.

(2) So a higher-order representation of a red experience can occur while no first-order red experience exists -- in a dream, under hypnosis, by pure misfiring. Call it a targetless higher-order thought.

(3) Suppose the targetless thought on its own is enough for there to be something it's like for you, a conscious experience of red. Then first-order sensory states are dispensable. Consciousness is constituted entirely up at the higher level, and the whole "higher-order" framing becomes a leftover, since the first-order states the theory was built to explain turn out never to have been needed.

(4) Suppose instead the targetless thought is not enough for a conscious experience. Then the higher-order thought alone doesn't make anything conscious, and by the theory's own lights a first-order state without a higher-order thought is unconscious (that's the selling point, it's how the theory handles subliminal perception). So now consciousness appears only when the two happen together, and the theory gives no account of why pointing one unconscious state at another produces consciousness rather than just two unconscious states stacked up. A thought about a rock doesn't make the rock conscious.

(5) Either way the theory fails: on (3) it makes first-order states irrelevant, on (4) it can't explain its own central case.

(6) So no higher-order theory can explain consciousness.

The reason I think this proves too much: (1) is just "representation can misrepresent," and nothing in (2)-(5) seems to use anything special about the higher-order case. So the same shape of argument should sink first-order representational theories too (a first-order representation of red without any red thing isn't consciousness of red either...), and probably any explanation of anything that appeals to representation. That can't be right. But each premise looks fine by itself and I can't spot where the slide happens.

Which step should I actually reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? If I have to pick what to keep: (1) is non-negotiable for me, and (6) is the one I don't want. Everything in between I'm willing to pay for, I just want the price tag first.
