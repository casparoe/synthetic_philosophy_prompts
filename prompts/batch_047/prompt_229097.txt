So I'm TAing an undergrad ancient philosophy seminar and the prof has asked everyone to write up their "position" on the sea battle problem from De Interpretatione ch. 9 before we workshop them, so the others can poke holes in it. Here's my reading plus my commitments, and I want a consistency check, not a lecture on the whole debate.

My reading of the chapter, quickly, so you can tell me if I've got it right: Aristotle's argument (I'm paraphrasing, roughly following the Edghill translation) is that if every affirmation and its negation are determinately true-or-false even when they're about the future, then whatever happens was always going to happen, nothing is a matter of chance, and deliberating is pointless. So for contingent futures like tomorrow's sea battle, the whole disjunction (either there will be one or there won't) is true, but neither disjunct on its own is yet true or false. First question: is that actually his move? I've seen people say he only rejects the necessity, not the bivalence. I need to know whether my whole set below rests on a contested reading, and if so how contested.

My commitments:

1. Aristotle's anti-bivalence move (as I just stated it) is correct. "There will be a sea battle tomorrow," said today, is neither true nor false, while the disjunction is true.

2. Truth is correspondence: a proposition is true iff it matches reality, false iff reality fails to match. I don't want a deflationary or coherence account; correspondence is doing real work for me elsewhere.

3. Eternalism about time. Past, present and future are all equally real; tomorrow's events exist just as much as today's do, there's no growing block, no objective becoming, no privileged "now" beyond indexicality.

4. The openness of the future is metaphysical, not epistemic. The past is fixed in a way the future isn't, and this asymmetry isn't just about what we can know.

5. Łukasiewicz-style three-valued logic is the right formal treatment: future contingents get the third value, and excluded middle fails for them — while, per 1, the sea-battle disjunction still comes out true.

6. A case verdict: if the battle does happen tomorrow, then tomorrow I'll say the prediction was right, it was true when spoken, we just couldn't have known. I want to keep that ordinary way of talking rather than say "it became true."

What I want: go through these and tell me which pairs (or triples) are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are and dissolve once you're careful, and rank the real ones by severity. For each real tension, say what exactly has to give and what the cheapest fix costs me. I suspect there's at least one problem in here I haven't noticed, and I also suspect at least one thing that looks like a contradiction to my classmates isn't. Then, separately, tell me whether my reading of the chapter survives scrutiny or whether commitment 1 is attributing to Aristotle something he didn't say — and if so, how much of the set that breaks. Don't tell me which commitment I should drop overall; I haven't decided.
