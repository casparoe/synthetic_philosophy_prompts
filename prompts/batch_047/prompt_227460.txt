A few of us run a small reading group on AI safety at my job (we're engineers, not philosophers, so bear with me), and we've been going back and fourth on one question for weeks now. Quick setup so we're on the same page: a goal-directed agent is one that pursues some objective in the world, and the classic worry is that any objective we write down will be slightly off from what we actually want, and a capable agent will find and exploit the gap. An approval-directed agent, by contrast, doesn't pursue outcomes at all — it just does whatever the human overseer would rate most highly in the moment. The sales pitch is that "what would my overseer approve of right now" is easier to get right than a whole utility function; the suspicion is that what gets approved and what's actually good come apart in their own way.

Here's what I want, since I'm presenting next session. Build me one concrete scenario — realistic, present-day, no sci-fi, no mind-reading, nothing that needs a lab — where an approval-directed agent and a goal-directed agent are put in the same role, the overseer is competent, honest, and genuinely paying attention, and yet the approval-directed agent reliably ends up doing the worse thing while earning *higher* approval for it. The overseer can't be lazy, bribed, distracted, or fooled by anything exotic; the failure has to fall out of the structure of approval itself, not out of somebody dropping the ball. And don't stipulate the verdicts — the behavior has to be generated by what each agent is actually optimizing, or the case is cheating.

Then show me the case works: state what each agent does and why, given its objective; name the single feature of the scenario that's doing all the work; say what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed; and give the best counterattack a defender of approval-direction would mount (something like: a more thoughtful overseer, or an approval process over plans rather than actions, closes the gap), plus whether your scenario survives it or needs patching.
