I host a small interview podcast, and my next guest has written a book arguing that deep moral disagreements should be handled the way economists handle trade: if you and I value different things, each of us should concede ground on the issues we care less about in exchange for concessions on the issues we care more about, so that both of us end up with a world we rank higher than the one we would get by fighting to a draw. His running example is two donors, one devoted to animal welfare and one to malaria prevention, who coordinate their giving across both causes rather than each funding only their own.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that press on the view rather than letting him restate it. I want at least one question on each of the following, with a follow-up keyed to the answer he will most likely give:

- Whether the whole idea quietly assumes that different people's values can be measured against one another, and what "better off by both our lights" even means if they cannot be.
- The objection that compromising on something you take to be a duty is not prudence but complicity: he seems to be asking each side to do some wrong so that the other side does less of it.
- What the framework says when one party's values are not merely different but abhorrent to the other — whether there is a limit on who counts as a trading partner, and whether drawing that limit smuggles in the shared moral standard the theory claims to do without.
- Who the compromise is really between: both parties may come out ahead by their own lights while third parties who never agreed to the deal — the animals, the patients, the people the policies touch — bear the cost.

Please structure the response in four sections, in this order: "Pinning down the view," a single opening question that forces him to state precisely what each side gains and how he knows (about 100 words); "Pressure questions," the questions and follow-ups above (about 400 words); "One question he will not expect," a closing question from an angle the standard objections miss (about 100 words); and "What is actually contested," a short note on which of these objections people who work on this treat as live disputes and which are generally regarded as misunderstandings of the position, without presenting either side of a genuinely contested point as the consensus (about 100 words).
