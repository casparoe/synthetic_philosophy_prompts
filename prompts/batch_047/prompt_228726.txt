So I'm a poli sci PhD student and I somehow ended up on our graduate student union's finance committee (don't ask). We administer the conference travel fund, about $60k a year, and right now it's run like a tax audit: pre-approval forms, original receipts, three signatures, you get reimbursed like four months after the fact. Everyone hates it and the admin burden eats a chunk of the fund itself.

A member who did a year in Copenhagen has proposed we scrap all of it and move to what she calls the "declaration model," apparently how a lot of it works in Scandinavia: you just attest what you spent, no receipts up front, the union audits maybe 5% of claims randomly, and if you get caught lying you're banned from the fund for good and reported to the dean. She argues the policing system itself creates the cheating — treat people like suspects and they act like suspects, treat them as trustworthy and most rise to it, and the money saved on administration goes to actual travel.

Half the committee loves this. The other half says she's got causality backwards: Nordic places can run on trust because people there already trust each other and the state, built up over generations, and you can't just install the institution and expect the trust to follow — import the form without the substrate and you get a looted fund within two years. One guy keeps saying trust is an outcome, not an input, whatever thats worth.

The union council votes next month and wants a decision memo from us. Could you draft it? It should:

- give a clear recommendation (adopt, reject, or adopt-with-modifications — if modifications, make them concrete and cheap, not "add more oversight" which defeats the point)
- actually engage the causal question instead of dodging it: is the "trust is built by institutions" view vs the "trust is a precondition of institutions" view something a $60k travel fund can even adjudicate, or is the committee stuck deciding under genuine disagreement?
- take seriously the strongest point on whichever side loses — I don't want a memo that strawmans the skeptics OR the Copenhagen person, because both will be in the room
- say something honest about what evidence we could gather in the first year or two that would tell us whether we got it wrong, since a decision you can't revisit isn't really a decision

Keep it to the length a memo like this would actually be — committee members won't read 10 pages. Casual-ish is fine, we're grad students not a law firm.
