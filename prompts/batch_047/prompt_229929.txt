A colleague and I are arguing about a thought experiment he devised against the view that executing the right program suffices for thought. Picture a vast hall staffed by millions of small workers who pass marked tokens between them according to a rulebook; the token-passing is arranged to be isomorphic, step for step, to the symbol manipulations which, on the computational picture of mind, constitute a Chinese speaker understanding a story. No worker understands Chinese — each merely follows local rules. He calls the hall a careenium and the tokens simmballs, and concludes that since nothing in the hall understands, symbol-shuffling cannot amount to understanding.

I find myself stuck on two points. First, the usual reply is that the understanding belongs to the system as a whole rather than to any individual worker. My colleague says this merely re-locates the mystery: if every event in the hall is just a worker handing over a token, what could it possibly mean for the system, as opposed to its parts, to understand anything? Second — and this troubles me more — the symbols in a computer are supposed to cause the machine's output, yet in the careenium all the causal work seems to be done by the workers' hands and feet, with the meaning of the simmballs playing no causal role whatsoever. Do symbols ever genuinely push the substrate around, or is talk of the symbol level getting things done always just a convenient redescription of lower-level pushing and shoving?

Please give me a gut-level explanation of what is at stake in each of these worries, and take a definite position on both: whether the systems reply survives my colleague's objection, and whether symbol-level causation is something real or merely a façon de parler. Give me your reasons for the positions you take, rather than surveying the options and leaving me to choose.
