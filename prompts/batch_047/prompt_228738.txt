For a couple of centuries now, philosophers have been going back and forth over Hume's problem of induction, and I am struggling to see what the dispute is even about any more. As I understand the main camps: one side holds that no non-circular justification of inductive inference is possible and that we should simply accept this as a permanent feature of our epistemic situation; another argues, following Strawson, that the demand for justification is itself confused because being inductively supported is partly constitutive of what we mean by rational; a third, following Reichenbach, offers a merely pragmatic defence — if any method works, induction will, so we might as well use it; and the Bayesians claim the problem dissolves once you see that updating on evidence is justified by coherence constraints, with the catch that which conclusions you draw depends on your priors. My course treats these as rival answers to one question, but they seem to me to be answering different questions under a single name.

Here is what prompts my question. I also work in machine learning, where the no-free-lunch theorems tell us something that sounds remarkably like Hume's conclusion: averaged over all possible worlds, no learning algorithm outperforms any other, so induction can only succeed relative to assumptions about which worlds are on the table. My working hypothesis is that the philosophical debate has stalled precisely because one side is demanding the thing the no-free-lunch results show to be impossible in principle — a guarantee that is method-independent and assumption-free — while the other sides have each quietly redefined success so that something weaker can be achieved, and that the apparent disagreement is really a disagreement about what would count as a justification at all. But I am not confident of this diagnosis. Perhaps there is a real substantive dispute underneath, about, say, whether pragmatic or coherence-based defences genuinely transfer to particular inductive practices rather than to an idealised learner.

So: is this a stalled debate because it is fundamentally verbal, because it rests on starting intuitions neither side can argue for, or because it conceals a formal question that has in effect already been answered? Give me your diagnosis with reasons grounded in what the positions actually claim, not a survey of the literature, and tell me what progress would even look like given your diagnosis — a sharper distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or argue honestly that nothing could settle it and explain why.
