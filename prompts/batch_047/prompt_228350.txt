In my philosophy of language seminar we worked through Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment, and afterward a classmate raised an objection I haven't been able to settle: if meaning is fixed by causal contact with the actual stuff in one's environment, then what should we say about a large language model trained exclusively on text -- a system that has never had any causal contact with water, tigers, or elm trees, but can deploy all these words with apparent fluency, pass chemistry exams, and correct humans who misuse them? Does its use of water refer to H2O, to nothing at all, or to something else entirely (say, a pattern in its training distribution)?

Write a dialogue between two philosophers debating this question. The first, Petra, defends a thoroughgoing semantic externalism: she holds that reference requires the right kind of causal-historical connection to the referent, and she argues on that basis that the model's outputs only mimic meaning, whatever their surface competence. The second, Marcus, defends a sophisticated inferentialist or functional-role account: he holds that sufficiently rich mastery of the inferential and discriminative role of a word is enough for it to mean what we mean, and he argues that Twin Earth itself actually supports his view once you notice what does the work in Putnam's setup (the linguistic community's collective dispositions, not each speaker's causal history).

Make both characters genuinely competent -- neither should be a strawman, and both should know the standard moves. Petra should press Marcus on cases where inferential role seems to come apart from reference (e.g., a system that has mastered all the word-word connections but would systematically fail at anything like discrimination or successful interaction), and Marcus should press Petra on the sorites problem her view creates (exactly how much causal contact suffices, given that most human speakers of water in 1700 had no more chemical contact with H2O-as-such than the model does). The dialogue should run for a dozen or more exchanges, with each character responding to the other's actual arguments rather than restating their opening position, and it should end not with a winner declared but with each character stating, in one short closing speech, what empirical or conceptual discovery would most threaten their view.
