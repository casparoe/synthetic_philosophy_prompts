I'm on the grants committee of a small science foundation, and we keep getting evolutionary biology proposals that explain some trait as having evolved "for the good of the group" or "for the species" -- restraint in predators so they don't overhunt their prey, alarm calls, reduced virulence in parasites, cooperative breeding, that kind of thing. Half my committee waves these through uncritically and the other half auto-rejects anything with the words "group selection" in it. Neither policy seems right, and I'm tired of the same three people yelling at each other every cycle.

Act as a philosopher of biology who knows the units of selection debate well. Before writing, think through the main failure modes a procedure like this could have, then write us a decision procedure the committee can actually run on a proposal -- we're mostly not specialists -- for telling (a) when a group-level selection explanation is genuinely warranted, (b) when the same facts are better explained at the gene or individual level, and (c) when the two are just different bookkeeping on the same process and the fight is verbal. Build it out of whatever the debate actually gives us: Lewontin's conditions for selection to act at all, the replicator/vehicle distinction, the byproduct problem (a group benefit that's just a side effect of individual advantage), and the claim that multi-level selection and inclusive fitness are mathematically equivalent redescriptions. Say explicitly which of those distinctions have to survive in the procedure and which can be dropped for a lay panel without much loss.

The procedure itself should be: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates with a verdict like "this needs an individual-level explanation, fund it only if reframed," and where it has to hand off to someone's judgment because the issue is empirical or unsettled rather than checklist-able. Flag which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run it end to end on three cases: eusociality in hymenopteran insects, a bird alarm call that attracts predator attention to the caller, and a pathogen evolving to be less lethal to its host. At least one of the verdicts should be one some of my committee members will hate -- tell me which one and what I'll say to them.

Format-wise: start with the one-sentence summary of when "for the good of the group" talk is legit and when it isn't, then go straight into the procedure. No preamble, no restating my situation back to me, no bibliography -- I'll dig up sources myself if I need them. American English, and keep it to something I can actually print out and bring to a meeting, so maybe 1500 words max.
