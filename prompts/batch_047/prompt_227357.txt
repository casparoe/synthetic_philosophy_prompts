You are a decision theorist with expertise in normative ethics and the foundations of rational choice. I hold a position I want you to stress-test rather than defend, and I'd like you to think carefully and brainstorm broadly before settling into your structured answer.

The position is expected value fanaticism: in any choice situation where the options have well-defined expectations, you are rationally required to take the option with the highest expected value, full stop — no matter how small the probability of the enormous payoff that swamps the calculation, and no matter what is being given up for certain. So a one-in-a-trillion chance of some astronomically large good, if the arithmetic works out, beats a guaranteed excellent outcome, and mugging-style offers can in principle rationally clean you out.

What would count against this view? I want a map of its exposure, organized roughly as follows:

1. Intuitive cases. Which thought experiments — St. Petersburg-style setups, mugging variants, cases trading a certainty against a long-shot — put the most pressure on the view, and which of those would a committed fanatic simply bite the bullet on versus quietly try to re-describe? Is bullet-biting here a legitimate defense or an admission?

2. Formal results. Fanaticism presupposes that expectations exist and are well-defined. Walk me through what happens with games like the Pasadena game, where the expectation is undefined or the ordering of outcomes matters, and with infinitesimal or unbounded utility constructions. Do these count as defeaters for the view, or merely as boundaries on its domain — and who gets to decide that?

3. Hostage commitments. Fanaticism seems to inherit the problems of whatever value theory it is paired with (say, a total aggregative axiology in population ethics). Which of its apparent absurdities belong to fanaticism itself and which to its companions, and would severing the companions defuse or merely relocate the trouble?

4. The meta-question. Since this is a normative view, what could falsify it at all? Rank the candidate defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones a sophisticated defender would themselves concede as genuine costs, and give me an honest verdict on whether fanaticism has been qualified into something that risks nothing — and if so, whether that is a defect in a view of this kind.
