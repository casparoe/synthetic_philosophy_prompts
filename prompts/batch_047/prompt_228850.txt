I am a retired analytical chemist who has lately been reading philosophy with more enthusiasm than training, and I find myself needing a practical tool built out of David Lewis's account of convention. Here is my situation. I chair the bylaws committee of a two-hundred-member amateur astronomy society founded in the 1970s. Every few years we revise the bylaws, and this time the membership has asked us to go through the society's many unwritten practices and recommend, for each one, whether to write it into the bylaws, leave it alone, or actively discourage it. The committee has five members, none with any philosophy; we have two two-hour meetings before we must report; we cannot survey the membership before the vote; and bylaw changes require a two-thirds majority at the annual meeting, so a mistake, once codified, is hard to undo.

I have read Lewis's book on convention, but it is written for philosophers, and I need it turned into a working procedure. What I want from you is a numbered sequence of tests the committee can apply to any candidate practice, where each test says what observable evidence would count as passing or failing it given that we cannot poll two hundred people, what classification a failure at that point yields, and what the default recommendation is for each classification (codify, leave alone, discourage), with the exceptions to each default stated. Two things from the book must survive the translation intact, because I think my committee will be tempted to drop them: first, Lewis's insistence that a convention does not require any explicit agreement, which is precisely backwards from how my colleagues will use the word in a bylaws context; second, the condition that there be a genuine alternative regularity that could equally well have stabilized. Wherever a test cannot be made mechanical and a committee member simply has to exercise judgment, mark it plainly as such rather than letting the procedure pretend otherwise.

Please then run the procedure on these three real cases from our society, showing the work at each step:

1. At our dark-sky site, members set up telescopes in east-west rows on a first-come basis, with the earliest arrival anchoring the row nearest the gravel lot. Nobody designed this; it congealed in the 1990s. North-south rows would serve equally well. Everyone follows it, expects others to follow it, and seems genuinely content.

2. Only dim red lights are permitted once observing begins. A newcomer who switches on a white flashlight is corrected within seconds, and repeat offenders are talked about. Here, though, there is a real physiological reason behind the practice, which the first case lacks, and I am unsure what the procedure should do with that — or with the fact that some details of the practice (exactly how red, whether headlamps count) do seem arbitrary.

3. Senior members get first pick of the society's loaner telescopes, a practice about twenty years old. Newer members grumble about it privately but comply, and I suspect, though I cannot prove, that a majority would actually prefer a lottery while nobody wants to be the one to say so. I should disclose that I have been a member for thirty-five years and benefit from this arrangement, and I would rather the procedure tell me something I dislike than that it be bent.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot settle. I gather that Lewis's account is a description of what conventions are, not advice about whether a given one deserves the force of a written rule, and I would like the limits of the whole exercise stated honestly before I present it to the committee as more than it is.

Please write the answer in American English.
