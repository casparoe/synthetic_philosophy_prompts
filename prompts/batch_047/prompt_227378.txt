I am a novelist revising a speculative novel populated by animals, engineered creatures, artificial intelligences, and human societies, and I keep making intelligence serve the plot rather than arise from a creature’s actual cognitive equipment. I would like to operationalize a useful distinction among four kinds of minds: Darwinian creatures, whose effective responses are built in by selection; Skinnerian creatures, which can modify their behavior through reward and punishment during their lifetimes; Popperian creatures, which can test possible actions in an internal model before risking them; and Gregorian creatures, whose thinking is extended by language, symbols, institutions, and other culturally inherited tools. Treat these as capacities that may coexist in different degrees, not as four sealed species or as a ranking from less valuable to more valuable minds.

Please develop a reusable character-design and revision procedure that I can apply to any fictional agent in about twenty minutes. It should provide an ordered sequence of diagnostic questions, specify what kinds of behavioral evidence would count as sufficient answers, and show how to distinguish superficially similar achievements—for example, avoiding a trap because of an inherited reflex, because of past punishment, because of rehearsing the outcome internally, or because one has learned a warning convention. Include tests for mixed and borderline systems, such as collectives, artificial agents, children, and beings whose internal simulation depends on an external notation. I would also like the procedure to enforce what I call counterfactual debt: whenever a character displays a cognitive ability, the fiction must identify the evolutionary history, learning history, imaginative practice, or cultural infrastructure that could plausibly have paid for it; otherwise the ability should be flagged as an authorial convenience. Mark the places where the classification is genuinely underdetermined and where a novelist must make a principled imaginative choice rather than discover a fact.

Conclude by applying the procedure to three cases from my draft: a bioengineered guard animal that rapidly learns patrol routes but cannot conceive of a disguised threat; a swarm whose colonies appear brilliantly prescient because unsuccessful subcolonies die while successful ones proliferate, although no member represents the future; and my beloved alien oracle, whom I had imagined as a Gregorian supermind but whose every answer is generated from ancestral simulations fixed in its genome, with no capacity to learn after birth or borrow concepts from others. If the procedure assigns the oracle a less exalted kind of mind than I intended, do not rescue my preference; explain the result and then suggest the smallest change to the oracle’s history or mechanisms that would support the richer characterization. Finally, state what kinds of narrative mistakes the procedure can prevent, what it cannot settle about consciousness or moral status, and how it should be used as a revision tool without flattening fictional minds into a taxonomy.
