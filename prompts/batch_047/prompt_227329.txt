I'm a philosophy PhD student (epistemology of testimony mostly) who's been auditing a seminar on historical method, and a classmate walked me into an argument about the historical Jesus that seems to prove way too much, and I can't find the exit.

(1) The so-called criteria of authenticity -- multiple attestation, dissimilarity, embarrassment, coherence and friends -- were developed to sort material that plausibly goes back to Jesus from material the later church invented.
(2) But each criterion, when you push on it, either does no work or does bad work: dissimilarity just lets each scholar reconstruct a Jesus who is conveniently unlike both Judaism and the church (i.e. whatever the scholar wants), multiple attestation only shows a tradition is early or widespread, not that it's true, and people applying the same criteria to the same texts end up with wildly incompatible Jesuses.
(3) A method whose outputs depend this heavily on who's running it doesn't confer warrant at all.
(4) So the criteria give us no warrant for any positive claim about the historical Jesus.
(5) The criteria are basically the only tools the quest has, so we know essentially nothing about who Jesus of Nazareth was.

Here's why I think this proves too much: swap in Socrates and the arguement runs identically -- conflicting sources, every source has an agenda, no criterion settles anything. But surely we know *some* things about Socrates. So something in there has to give. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you? My classmate is most attached to (2) -- he insists the criteria really are that bad -- so if you reject (2) I'd like to hear why he's wrong specifically, and if you reject something later in the chain, explain why the step looked fine when it isn't.
