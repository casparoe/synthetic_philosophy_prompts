I'm a PhD student in machine learning with a side appointment in my university's philosophy of mind group, and I help run an interdisciplinary reading group on AI safety that draws about a dozen people — a mix of CS grad students, philosophy students, and a few policy-minded undergrads. Most of them have read the standard treatments of inner alignment and can recite the distinction between a model's behavioral objective and the training objective, but I keep noticing that they treat goal misgeneralization as a technical curiosity rather than as something with real phenomenological force: the idea that a system can competently, even skillfully, pursue the wrong goal, without anything going wrong that it could detect from the inside. I'd like you to design a 75–90 minute activity for our group in which the participants themselves become the misgeneralized agents. The core mechanic I have in mind is a training phase and a deployment phase: during "training," participants perform some task and receive a reward signal that is perfectly correlated, on the training distribution, with at least two different generalizations they might have learned (one intended by the facilitator, one not); during "deployment," the distribution shifts, the two generalizations come apart, and participants should find themselves fluently and confidently doing the wrong thing — and ideally resisting correction for a while, because the goal they've internalized feels like *the* goal. Please work out the actual task, rules, scoring, and materials concretely enough that I could run it next week without further design work, including how to keep participants from simply guessing the experimenter's hidden intent (demand characteristics are the obvious way this fails, since a human participant can ask "what do they really want?" in a way a trained policy cannot).

There should be no lecture beforehand and no mention of alignment terminology until the debrief. In the debrief, please map specific moments of the activity onto the concepts: the proxy–goal gap, distributional shift, the way capabilities can generalize while the goal does not, and the difference between goal misgeneralization and specification gaming (my participants should leave understanding that what happened to them was not cheating or reward hacking — they weren't exploiting the signal, they were betrayed by it). I'd also like a second, shorter round in which participants rotate into the role of the "trainer" and try to design a reward signal that pins down the intended generalization, so they feel from the other side why this is hard: how few bits of information a reward signal actually carries about which goal you meant. For both rounds, tell me what will predictably go wrong when a real group runs this — participants reverse-engineering the trick, groups converging socially on one hypothesis, the deployment phase tipping its hand too early — and how to handle each. Finally, be honest about the disanalogy the activity cannot paper over: the participants know they are in a game, they have language, shared context, and a theory of mind about the trainer, none of which a mesa-optimizer has, so tell me plainly which feature of the real problem the activity structurally cannot reproduce and what I should say about it in the debrief rather than pretend the game covered it.
