I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of mind, working on what makes pain bad -- the old question of whether the badness is built into the sensation itself or comes from some further attitude or reaction the subject has toward it. My commitee includes someone from cognitive science who keeps asking about machine suffering, so they've pushed me to make the last chapter about whether any of this generalizes beyond humans, which is how I fell down the pain asymbolia rabbit hole. The cases are wild: patients who reliably detect, localize, and even rate the intensity of painful stimuli, who wince and withdraw, but sincerely say it doesn't bother them -- Grahek's case studies describe some of them laughing during pinpricks. Philosophers clearly treat asymbolia as a load-bearing case (against representationalism, for hedonic-tone views, for splitting pain into sensory and affective components), but when I actually went and read the case reports, they seemed a lot messier than the philosophical use suggests.

So here's what I want. Given my actual constraints -- about a year and a half left, no lab access, and I need the metaphysics chapter and the generalization chapter to hang together -- which open questions about asymbolia and the dissociations around it are most worth me trying to settle, and in what order? Candidates I'm juggling: (1) whether the asymbolia cases really are pain-without-unpleasantness or whether something else (attention, memory, how the patients interpret the probes) explains the reports, i.e. whether the cases even show what everyone takes them to show; (2) whether there's a coherent "reverse asymbolia" -- genuine suffering or unpleasantness without any sensory-discriminative component, as people sometimes claim about grief or social rejection -- and whether the two directions of dissociation have to stand or fall together, which nobody seems to ask; (3) whether imperativism or evaluativism handles the asymbolic patient better, and whether that question is upstream or downstream of (1); (4) the machine angle my committee wants: whether a functionally organized system could in principle instantiate one component without the other, and whether that question is even well-posed before the metaphysics is settled. Feel free to tell me some of these are bad questions or that I'm missing the one that actually matters.

For the ranking, defend it on criteria rather than vibes: how much other work depends on the answer, whether it's tractable for an armchair philosopher in 18 months, and whether settling it would change what I say in the generalization chapter or just decorate it. If some question the literature treats as central isn't actually load-bearing for my project, say so -- I suspect at least one sacred cow here is doing no real work. And please no reading lists, I've got plenty of those already.
