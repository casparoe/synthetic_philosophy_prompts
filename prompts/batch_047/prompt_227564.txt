I'm a software engineer (backend systems, some dabbling in type theory and formal verification on the side) auditing a philosophy seminar this semester, and I need to submit a proposal for a term paper of about 6,000 words by the end of the month. I have no formal philosophy training, so the proposal has to be honest about that while still being a real plan of work.

Here's my half-formed idea, which I'd like you to sharpen into the proposal. In my day job, I routinely work with two very different things that both get called "infinite": on the one hand, lazy streams and unbounded loops, where infinity is just a promise that you can always ask for the next element; on the other hand, completed mathematical objects like the set of all natural numbers, treated as a single totality you can quantify over, take subsets of, and form power sets of. This maps loosely onto the Aristotelian distinction between potential and actual infinity. What strikes me, though, is that reasoning about the merely "potential" kind seems to smuggle the "actual" kind back in: to prove a property holds of a lazy stream, you typically use induction over *all* natural numbers, which appears to treat the natural numbers as a completed totality. So my working suspicion is that finitism — the position that only potential infinity is legitimate and completed infinities (Hilbert's hotel-style constructions, Cantor's hierarchy) are either meaningless or illusory — may be unstable: either it must give up ordinary induction, or it accepts induction and thereby concedes the very completed infinity it rejects.

Turn this into a proposal with the following components:

1. A precise statement of the question. Frame it so it has a determinate answer — something like: does any finitist position that retains full mathematical induction over the natural numbers covertly commit to the natural numbers as a completed totality, or is there a principled way to accept induction while denying that it ranges over a finished set? Flag explicitly where my framing might be confused (e.g., if quantifying over a domain doesn't actually require treating it as completed, I want to know what the alternative reading is, since that's where the interesting answer probably lives).

2. Why the existing positions don't settle it. Sketch, without citations, the main stances — classical Platonism about the natural numbers, the Aristotelian potential-infinity view, intuitionist or constructivist positions that accept induction but reject completed infinities, and strict finitism — and explain what each would say about my question and why none of them directly answers it as posed. Don't give me a bibliography; I'll fill in references myself.

3. The approach, and why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives. My inclination is to analyze the three standard "anti-finitist" arguments I keep encountering — that induction presupposes a completed domain, that Cantor's diagonal argument forces actual infinity on anyone who accepts the reals, and that Hilbert's hotel paradoxes show potential infinity is the only coherent kind — and to examine for each one whether it genuinely forces commitment to a completed totality or whether it trades on an ambiguity in what "completed" means. Tell me whether this argument-by-argument approach is the right one, and what I'm ruling out by not instead doing, say, a historical treatment of the finitism debate or a formal proof-theoretic investigation.

4. What a result would look like, and what would count as the project failing. Spell out the possible conclusions (finitism is unstable in the way I suspect; finitism is stable but only at the cost of restricting induction; the whole question rests on a false presupposition about what induction requires) and which considerations would decide between them. Say explicitly what failure modes look like — e.g., discovering the question dissolves on inspection, or that answering it requires formal machinery I can't acquire in one semester.

5. A realistic scope assessment. Given one semester and a writer whose background is software rather than philosophy, tell me honestly which parts of this are tractable, which parts risk ballooning (my guess is the diagonal argument section), and what you'd cut first if it had to shrink.

Keep the proposal to roughly a page and a half of prose, and write it in a register suitable for submission to the seminar instructor.
