My working view on Pascal's mugging is that the problem is entirely solved by insisting that rational agents have bounded utility functions -- if utilities are bounded, no finite probability, however small, can be leveraged into arbitrary expected value, so the mugger's threat just washes out. What would count against this? I want a map of where this solution is actually exposed: Are there versions of the mugging that survive boundedness (e.g. the mugger adjusting his offer once he knows your bound, or sequences of small muggings that compound)? Does boundedness itself generate mugging-like pathologies elsewhere, like making you fanatically insensitive to genuinely enormous but bounded stakes? And are there decision-theoretic results suggesting bounded agents get money-pumped or otherwise exploited in ways unbounded ones don't? Also curious whether defenders of the boundedness fix themselves concede any of these as real costs, or whether they've insulated the view so thoroughly that it no longer risks anything.
