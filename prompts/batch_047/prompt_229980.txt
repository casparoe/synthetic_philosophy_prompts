I have been trying to make J. R. Lucas's argument in 'Minds, Machines and Gödel' (Philosophy, 1961) watertight, and my reconstruction seems to destroy the very capacity it begins with. Lucas writes that Gödel's theorem shows that 'in any consistent system which is strong enough to produce simple arithmetic there are formulae which cannot be proved-in-the-system, but which we can see to be true'. Here is my version:

(1) For any consistent machine M capable of simple arithmetic, there is a formula G(M) which is true but which M cannot prove.
(2) Anyone who understands how G(M) is constructed from M's specification can see that G(M) is true; so a mind can know something that M cannot.
(3) If my mind is a machine, it has a specification; call it M-me.
(4) Nothing in principle prevents that specification from being written down and handed to a sufficiently able person — perhaps to me.
(5) Applying (2) to M-me: I could see G(M-me) to be true, even though M-me — that is, I — could not prove it.
(6) But seeing G(M-me) to be true amounts to recognising that a system whose specification I possess will never prove it; and by the second incompleteness theorem no consistent system can establish its own consistency, so I could occupy that position only by being inconsistent.
(7) Since nothing in the argument is special to me, it follows that no consistent reasoner whatsoever can see the truth of the Gödel sentence of the system that they themselves are — which is to say, the ability Lucas ascribes to minds in (2) vanishes exactly where the argument needs it.

The conclusion I cannot accept is (7): the reconstruction begins from the mind's distinctive capacity to 'see' Gödel sentences and ends by denying that capacity to every consistent reasoner. Which step should I reject, and why does it look so plausible? I would rather keep (1) and (6), which appear to be just the two incompleteness theorems, and I am reluctant to abandon (2), since it captures what made the paper compelling. I suspect the trouble lies in how Lucas's phrase 'we can see to be true' is to be read — unconditionally, or only against the background assumption that the system in question is consistent — but I cannot settle that reading myself, nor work out what each option would cost Lucas's wider claim that no machine can be an adequate model of the mind.
