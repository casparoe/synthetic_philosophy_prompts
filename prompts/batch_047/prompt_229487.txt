I serve on the five-member professional conduct committee of a state licensing board for civil engineers, and we have deadlocked on a case that I suspect is really a philosophy problem wearing a hard hat. The respondent, a structural engineer with twenty years of practice and a clean record, designed the roof of a municipal recreation center. He ran every load calculation the building code requires, used the standard snow-load tables, stamped his drawings, and documented compliance at every step. Two winters after the building opened, an unusual wind pattern drifted snow against a parapet wall in a configuration the code tables do not contemplate, the drifted load far exceeded anything in his calculations, and a section of the roof collapsed. Mercifully, the building was empty at the time. The board's investigation confirmed that he violated no rule and that no code provision, then or now, addresses this drift geometry — but it also found that an engineer who had simply stood on the site and thought for an afternoon about how wind moves around a parapet could have anticipated the problem, and two expert witnesses testified that a reasonably attentive practitioner would have done so.

The committee is split two-to-two, with me as the swing vote. One faction wants a formal reprimand. Their argument, roughly, is that professional judgment is precisely what distinguishes an engineer from the digger wasp in the old philosophical example — the wasp that drags its paralyzed prey to the burrow's threshold, goes inside to check things, and, if the prey has been moved a few inches while it was inside, drags it back to the threshold and checks the burrow again, repeating the whole routine over and over without ever varying it or grasping what has changed. A professional, they say, is someone we pay not to be sphexish, and "I followed the checklist" is no defense when the checklist's limits were there to be seen. The other faction replies that this amounts to punishing a man for lacking omniscience: codes exist so that engineers can rely on them, no one can think about everything, and a duty to always second-guess the rules is a duty no one can discharge — it would convert every routine act of compliance into a potential malpractice claim and would, ironically, push practitioners toward a fearful, context-insensitive rigidity of a different sort.

Acting as the committee's staff counsel — someone with training in both engineering ethics and the philosophy of action — please draft the memo I will circulate before next month's vote. The memo should (1) state a clear bottom-line recommendation on discipline, treating a non-disciplinary disposition (mandatory continuing education, or a board-issued practice advisory instead of a sanction against this individual) as a live option if you judge it best; (2) develop a principled account of when mechanical rule-following becomes culpable — that is, what distinguishes defensible reliance on a checklist from sphexish rigidity, and where, on that account, this engineer's conduct falls and why; (3) give the strongest honest statement of the losing side's argument and say what would have to be true for it to prevail; and (4) close with two or three general criteria our board could apply in future cases where a professional complied with every written rule and harm occurred anyway. Think carefully, before settling on a recommendation, about the difference between being caused to act and acting mechanically, and about what expectations of flexibility we can fairly place on people working inside rule-governed institutions. Write in American English, in a formal register, roughly 800 to 1,000 words.
