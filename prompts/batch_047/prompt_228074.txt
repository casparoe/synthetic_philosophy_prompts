Last night I spent two hours arguing with a friend about the familiar bank and airport cases, and we got nowhere. Her position was that whether a subject knows just plain depends on what is at stake for that subject; mine was that knowledge itself is fixed and what shifts with the situation is only the standard a speaker must meet to truly apply the word 'knows'. We kept agreeing about every case and disagreeing about what we were disagreeing about, which is exactly the failure mode I see in my students' essays. I TA an upper-level undergraduate epistemology seminar, and I want a final-exam essay question built around a fresh case that makes the difference between pragmatic encroachment and attributor contextualism impossible to paper over with the usual summary of both views.

Here is the case I have in mind, which I want the question to present in full. A hospital's night pharmacist reads a label and concludes that a vial contains the correct drug; on her evidence she is as well-positioned as pharmacists normally are when we count them as knowing. Her own situation is low-stakes for her -- she will go home on time either way -- but the patient who will receive the drug will die if she is wrong. In the first variant, a colleague watching her asks whether the pharmacist knows the vial is correct. In the second variant, the pharmacist herself does not know what is at stake: she has been told only that the vial is for 'a routine administration', and in fact it is for the critical patient. In the third variant, the next morning, having learned the patient survived, she says she never really knew, and the patient's furious family says she did know and is culpable. The question should ask candidates what each theory predicts in all three variants, whether any variant cleanly discriminates between the two theories, and which variant is hardest for each.

The question must be answerable in ninety minutes and worth 20 marks. Please write the question itself (presenting the case verbatim), a model answer of roughly 650 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit moves -- for instance, stating precisely whose practical situation each theory makes relevant and in what way (the attributor's context for the truth-conditions of ascriptions versus the subject's stakes for knowledge itself), and handling the second variant, where the subject is ignorant of her own stakes. For each move, give the marks it is worth and a one-line note on the specific mistake candidates typically make instead, and add a closing paragraph that says what separates an A answer from a B answer -- the A answer, I expect, will notice that the second variant pressures at least one of the two theories into a result its proponents rarely advertise, while a B answer will correctly state both views but treat them as making the same prediction everywhere.
