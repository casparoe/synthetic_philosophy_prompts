I retired from a career in experimental physics about five years ago and have been working my way through the big Russian novels ever since, which is how I ended up in the weeds of War and Peace. Most people warned me about the battle scenes, but nobody warned me that Tolstoy would stop the novel cold, right as Napoleon is crossing the Niemen, to lecture me about infinitesimal calculus. In Book Eleven, chapter 1 (I'm reading the Maude translation) he writes:

"Only by taking infinitesimally small units for observation (the differential of history, that is, the individual tendencies of men) and attaining to the art of integrating them (that is, finding the sum of these infinitesimals) can we hope to arrive at the laws of history."

He follows this with the claim that "the sum of human wills produced the Revolution and Napoleon, and only the sum of those wills first tolerated and then destroyed them." As someone who spent forty years doing integrals for a living, I find the analogy charming but I'm suspicious it might be doing less work than it appears to. Here's my problem: Tolstoy seems to think that the correct mathematical treatment of history rules out the great-man picture by its very method — since history must integrate over infinitesimal individual wills, no single person can be the cause of a mass movement. But that seems to me to build the conclusion into the setup. An integral over infinitely many small contributions can still be dominated by a few large ones; physics is full of point masses, singularities, delta functions sitting inside otherwise smooth distributions. Couldn't a Napoleon or a Hitler be, so to speak, a very sharp spike in the integrand — an individual whose coefficient is thousands of times larger than anyone else's? If so, the calculus picture is perfectly compatible with great men making a causal difference, and Tolstoy has just begged the question by fiat.

So my actual question is: what is the strongest version of Tolstoy's argument here, and does it survive this objection? I'd like you to unpack what his case against great-man history really rests on (I notice he also argues that taking one man's will as the cause of millions moving is taking "a weaker phenomenon as the cause of a stronger," and he has that nice bit about the clock striking ten when the church bells ring), and whether any of it is independent of the calculus metaphor. Then apply it to a case I argue about with my brother-in-law constantly: would there have been a Nazi Germany — the regime, the war, the Holocaust — without Hitler specifically? Presumably Tolstoy says yes, something like it was overdetermined by the sum of German wills after Versailles, hyperinflation, and so on. But is that actually what follows from his framework, or does even Tolstoy have to concede that the counterfactual question (remove the man, rerun history) is one his method can't answer, since you can't redo the integration with one term deleted? Please keep it conceptual rather than a historiography survey — I want to know whether the argument works, not who said what about it in 1953.
