As a retired chemist recently turned to moral and social philosophy, I have been reading about the distinction between honor cultures, dignity cultures, and what some writers call victimhood cultures: roughly, regimes in which slights must be answered personally and violently, regimes in which each person's worth is treated as inalienable and insults are to be shrugged off or handled by third parties, and regimes in which public appeals to authority on behalf of the aggrieved confer standing. During my reading I was struck to recall the playground proverb I was taught as a boy: sticks and stones may break my bones, but words will never hurt me. It now strikes me as a piece of dignity-culture catechism, but I suspect it is less simple than it looks. What precise claims could that proverb actually be making? Distinguish, for instance, an empirical claim about what words can do, a normative claim about how one ought to respond to insult, a pedagogical claim about what children should be trained to feel, and perhaps a constitutive claim about where one's worth resides. For each reading, say whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false, and whether someone in an honor culture or a victimhood culture would accept it. Finally, tell me which reading does the real work when a parent utters the proverb to a crying child.
