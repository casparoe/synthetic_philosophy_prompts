So I work for a mid-sized health insurer and I recently got moved onto the panel that decides which treatments we cover as "medically necessary" and which we exclude as elective or cosmetic-ish. We get maybe 40 of these a month, mostly contested stuff: growth hormone for kids who are short but not hormone-deficient, stimulants for people without a diagnosis who say they can't function at their jobs, laser work, a guy who wants his ACL repaired in a way that would leave the knee stronger than it was pre-injury, that sort of thing. Right now the panel basically argues each one from scratch and the decisions are all over the place -- I'm pretty sure the same request would get approved or denied depending who's on shift.

What I want is an actual procedure we could run in maybe 20-30 minutes per case, built on whatever the right way to think about the difference between treating a problem and just upgrading a person is. Like, the ordered list of questions, what counts as a good enough answer to move to the next one, where a "no" at some step just kills the application, and where the procedure should stop and admit it's a values call that has to go to a human vote rather than a checkbox. We're doctors and claims people, not philosophers, so it has to be runnable by someone who has never read anything on this topic. Also flag where the procedure will probably give wrong answers, because I assume it will sometimes.

A few things I'm already stuck on that it should handle: normal ranges are statistical, so "restore to normal function" gets weird when someone is naturally at the bottom of the range and wants to be moved up to average. Also species-typical functioning sounds clean until you hit someone who was born without a trait most humans have but isnt suffering in any measurable way. And there's the question of whether we should be comparing the patient to themselves-before, to the population average, or to some ideal.

Then actually run the procedure on three cases so I can see it working: (1) a 13 year old boy, no hormone deficiency, predicted adult height around 5'4", getting bullied, parents pushing hard; (2) a night-shift nurse with no diagnosis who wants a wakefulness drug she says she needs to not make errors; (3) a recreational runner whose knee surgery could be done the standard way or with a graft that testing suggests would make the joint more stable than it ever was. At least one of these should come out with an answer I'll hate, because if the procedure only ever agrees with my gut it's not doing anything. And be upfront about what this kind of checklist just cant capture no matter how we tinker with it.
