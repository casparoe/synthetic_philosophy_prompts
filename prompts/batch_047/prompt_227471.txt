After a career in experimental physics, I have spent my retirement reading philosophy of mind, and I keep returning to panpsychism—the view that experience is a fundamental and ubiquitous feature of matter. I am not hostile to it; if anything, my training makes me sympathetic to the idea that consciousness is a basic property rather than a late-arriving miracle. But an argument I have assembled from it appears to prove far too much, and I would like a diagnosis rather than a rebuttal of panpsychism itself.

(1) If experience is ubiquitous, then wherever fundamental physical processes occur, there is something it is like for the systems involved, however faint.
(2) Experience that can matter morally must carry some valence: some states of a system must be, however slightly, better or worse for it than others. Otherwise attributing experience to electrons and fields has no ethical content whatsoever, and the view may be interesting metaphysics but is morally inert.
(3) Moral badness aggregates: a sufficiently vast number of very small bads can outweigh a much smaller number of large ones. This is the same principle that lets a mild discomfort matter if enough beings endure it.
(4) The number of fundamental systems implicated in any ordinary event—boiling a kettle, say, or burning a litre of fuel—dwarfs the total number of sentient organisms that have ever existed, by many orders of magnitude.
(5) Therefore the moral weight of any event is dominated by its microphysical effects, and the suffering of persons and animals is, by comparison, a rounding error.
(6) We cannot track microphysical effects in any detail; the only coarse handle we have is something like total entropy production.
(7) Living systems are remarkably effective accelerators of entropy production. So the best available proxy rule is to minimize entropy production—which implies it would have been better had life never arisen on Earth, and better that it end sooner rather than later.

I find (7) unacceptable. Each earlier step looked innocent when I wrote it down, and I cannot tell whether the failure is a hidden shift between steps or whether the argument is valid and I must simply pay for one of the premises. If forced to choose, I would rather surrender (3) than (1) or (2), but I want to know what each retreat actually costs. Where does this go wrong?
