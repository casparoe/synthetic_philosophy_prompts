Modern states routinely restrict the franchise to citizens (resident non-citizens who pay taxes and are subject to the law for decades cannot vote), allocate social benefits preferentially to compatriots, and reserve the right to control immigration -- while simultaneously ratifying human rights instruments that proclaim the equal moral worth of all persons, and condemning as discrimination many distinctions drawn among their own citizens. In a short essay, analyze what account of special obligations among compatriots this cluster of practices presupposes. Does it commit the state to the view that shared citizenship grounds associative duties, or could it be reconstructed on universalist grounds (e.g., as a division of moral labor among states, or as compensation for the coercion states uniquely exercise over their members)? Identify which rival views -- thoroughgoing cosmopolitanism, nationality-based partialism, coercion-based accounts -- each element of the practice strictly rules out, and where the practice is underdetermined, licensing several incompatible theories. Finally, ask whether the practice's components presuppose consistent commitments, and what would have to change if the presupposed account were replaced. After the essay, provide a table with columns: Practice element; Apparent presupposition; Plausible universalist reconstruction; Views it rules out (if any); Strictly presupposed or merely compatible?
