In *The Question of German Guilt* (1946), written in the immediate aftermath of the Second World War, Karl Jaspers distinguished four concepts of guilt: criminal guilt for acts that violate positive law, to be established by a court; political guilt, which falls on every citizen of a state in whose name wrongful deeds were done, whether or not the individual supported them, and whose consequence is liability rather than punishment; moral guilt, which only the individual's own conscience can judge; and metaphysical guilt, arising from the failure to do whatever was humanly possible to prevent injustice, which exists between the individual and God alone. Jaspers insisted that guilt in the first, third, and fourth senses is irreducibly individual, and he treated the charge of collective guilt -- the idea that a people as such is morally or crinally guilty -- as an injustice in its own right, while still holding that political liability attaches to citizens simply in virtue of their membership in the political community.

Now take an episode Jaspers never addressed: the contemporary American debate over reparations for slavery. The salient facts, which you may take as given, are these. Chattel slavery was practiced under the authority of the United States and the states for over two centuries and ended in 1865; there are no living perpetrators and no living direct victims; the federal government and various state governments nevertheless claim institutional continuity with the governments that sanctioned the system; and current proposals for reparations are defended on varied grounds -- compensation for unpaid labor, redress for harms that persisted through Jim Crow and beyond, and rectification of wealth gaps inherited by present-day descendants of the enslaved -- while opponents object, among other things, that no living person is guilty of slavery and that holding present citizens liable punishes the innocent.

Analyze this debate through Jaspers's schema, in both directions. First, the mapping: which of the four concepts, if any, can attach to present-day Americans, and to which present-day Americans? Does political guilt survive the death of every perpetrator and victim, transmitting through the continuity of the state to citizens born a century later, or is Jaspers's notion of co-citizenship -- he wrote for people who had lived under the regime, however passively -- doing quiet work that a reparations argument cannot borrow? Where do arguments about inherited wealth and structural advantage fall: are they claims of moral guilt in disguise, a proposed extension of political liability, or something the schema has no slot for? Second, run the analysis the other way: what does the episode show about the theory? Jaspers designed the schema for contemporaries of the crime, within one generation of it. Say whether the reparations debate confirms his refusal of collective moral guilt, exposes it as an evasion that lets beneficiary populations off too easily, or reveals that the schema simply lacks a category -- call it historical or beneficiary guilt -- that the case demands. If you conclude the schema needs amending, specify the new category precisely enough that it does not collapse into the collective moral guilt Jaspers rejected; if you conclude it does not need amending, say what reparations claims, if any, survive the analysis.
