I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of mind drafting a dissertation chapter on personal identity and the unity of consciousness, and this week I've run into an inference I can't seem to block. Working through the classic commissurotomy findings — the divided visual field studies, the cases of intermanual conflict where one hand appears to undo what the other has done, the patients whose right hemisphere points to one answer while the left hemisphere verbally reports something entirely different — it seems to me these experiments establish that each cerebral hemisphere can independently sustain a complete stream of experience, with its own perceptual contents, preferences, and intentions. But if one hemisphere is individually sufficient for consciousness, and my own two hemispheres are each individually sufficient, doesn't it follow that I am already two conscious subjects right now, and that the corpus callosum is merely a fast communication channel keeping them synchronized rather than something that makes me one? Several people in my reading group pushed back when I floated this, but nobody could articulate exactly where the argument fails, which makes me suspect it might be sound.

The stakes, for my chapter, come from a medical case I'm using as a centerpiece: functional hemispherectomy in children with intractable epilepsy. On my reading of the split-brain data, if each hemisphere already houses its own subject prior to any surgery, then removing or disconnecting a hemisphere is not an operation performed on a single person's brain — it looks more like the destruction of one person for the benefit of another, or at minimum like one subject being absorbed into another's perspective. Am I right that the standard commissurotomy results license this conclusion, and if so, should hemispherectomy be regarded as raising the same kind of moral questions as, say, separating or sacrificing one of a pair of conjoined twins? If instead I'm misreading what the experiments actually show about the number of subjects present — either in split-brain patients or in the intact brain — I'd very much like to understand precisely which step of the inference goes wrong, and what the data do and don't establish about the unity of consciousness.
