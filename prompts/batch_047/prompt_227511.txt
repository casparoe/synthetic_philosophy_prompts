Last term, in a reading group on the philosophy of logic, a friend of mine unveiled an argument he is immensely proud of: he thinks it shows that the Liar paradox is not a puzzle to be solved but a proof that our concept of truth is defective, full stop. I have tried to reconstruct his reasoning as faithfully as I can, and I would be grateful for a diagnosis. His argument runs: (1) The Liar sentence ('this sentence is not true') is a meaningful declarative sentence, and the problem cannot be dismissed as a pathology of self-reference, since the same pattern arises without any — as in the card whose front says that the sentence on the back is true while the back says that the sentence on the front is false. (2) A theory of truth is adequate to our ordinary concept only if it settles, for every meaningful declarative sentence, whether that sentence is true. (3) Settling the Liar either way contradicts the platitude that a sentence is true just in case things are as it says they are. (4) So no consistent theory of truth is adequate to our ordinary concept. (5) The usual ways out — hierarchy, truth-value gaps, truth-value gluts, revision sequences — are each adequate to a fragment of the concept at best, and all are hostage to the same template: whatever verdict the theory officially records about the Liar, a sentence reporting that its own official verdict is the correct one regenerates the contradiction. (6) If no consistent theory can be adequate to a concept, that concept is incoherent. (7) So truth is incoherent; and since truth is the constitutive aim of belief and assertion, both rest on a notion that cannot be made good. (8) Finally, he cheerfully added, the same template runs for the concept of validity itself — one can construct an argument whose conclusion says of the argument that it is invalid — so the reasoning he has just used is incoherent too, which he took to confirm rather than embarrass the conclusion.

It is step (8) that first made me think the argument proves too much: a piece of reasoning that concludes that reasoning itself rests on an incoherent notion seems to saw off the branch it is sitting on, or at least to forfeit the right to be believed. But my attempts at a cheaper exit keep failing. When I offered the gap response to (3) — the Liar is settled, as neither true nor false — he replied that 'the Liar is not true' is then something the theory itself establishes, and the merry-go-round starts again. When I suggested (2) might be asking too much, he asked what could be more minimal than expecting a theory of truth to say, of a meaningful declarative sentence, whether it is true. I would very much like to keep (1), and I am reluctant to abandon the platitude invoked in (3), since it seems to be the whole point of having a truth predicate. So: which numbered step should I actually reject? Why does that step look so compelling when stated baldly — is there an equivocation or a shift in what is being demanded of a theory somewhere in the chain? And what does rejecting it genuinely cost, compared with the costs of the alternatives, including the cost of simply accepting (7) and becoming an inconsistency theorist about truth? Please give your answer in British English.
