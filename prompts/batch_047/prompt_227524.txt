So I'm a backend engineer, zero philosophy training, and last week my buddy (an econ PhD dropout) tried to explain the centipede game to me over beers. Two players, a pot of money that grows each turn, on your turn you either grab the pot and end the game or pass and let it grow, but if the other person grabs later you walk away with less than if you'd grabbed now. Fine. Then he walks me through backward induction: at the final node the second player would obviously grab rather than pass for slightly less, the first player knows this and so grabs one node earlier, and it unravels all the way back to the very first move. Game over before it starts, basically.

Here's where I'm stuck. He stated this like it was a settled theorem: backward induction "proves" that the only rational play is to grab immediately at node one, and anyone who ever passes is just being irrational, full stop. But that can't be the whole story, right? If I'm player two and I actually get a turn, that means player one just passed up free money, which on his own logic was the irrational move. So I've now observed behavior that contradicts the very assumption the argument runs on, and I have no idea what my opponent will do next. Doesn't the proof eat its own tail here? It seems to assume away exactly the situations it's supposed to give advice about. And yet I've also read that this unravelling result is airtight in some formal sense, so presumably I'm misunderstanding what backward induction actually establishes in the first place.

So my question: what exactly does the backward induction argument prove, and what does it not prove? Is "grab at node one" really a command of rationality, or is it conditional on some extra premise (I keep running into the phrase "common knowledge of rationality" — is that where the work is being done, and is that premise even coherent once the game leaves the equilibrium path)? And if you're sitting at a node that "shouldn't" have been reached, does rationality have anything left to recommend at all? Please answer in American English, and don't dumb it down too much — I'm comfortable with formalism, just not with philosophy jargon.
