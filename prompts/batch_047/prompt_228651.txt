In a reading group on the foundations of machine learning, someone dismissed the problem of induction with a wave of the hand: all statistical learning just assumes the future resembles the past, and that assumption has a perfect track record. I suspect this packs several different claims into one slogan, some of them empty and some of them load-bearing. In Section IV of his Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Hume argues (paraphrasing) that every inference from the observed to the unobserved presupposes that unexperienced cases will resemble experienced ones, and that this presupposition can be supported neither by demonstration — since a change in the course of nature is conceivable without contradiction — nor by experience, on pain of circularity. Please treat "the future resembles the past" as the slogan under analysis. Lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses it might express: for example, a metaphysical claim about the uniformity of nature; a definitional truth that falls out of how we happen to carve the world into predicates; a premise about which respects of resemblance are the right ones (here please bring in Goodman's grue, since "resembles" is predicate-relative in exactly the way the new riddle of induction exploits); a normative claim about which inductive habits are rational; and a merely autobiographical claim about what humans in fact expect. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, substantive but defensible, or false; whether Hume's circularity argument touches it; and whether it is the reading my colleague needed in order to deflect the problem of induction rather than merely restate it. Finally, state what would change your mind: which argument or distinction, if it held up, would lead you to a different verdict about which reading does the real work, and where your disambiguation is most likely to go wrong.
