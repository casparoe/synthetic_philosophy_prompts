I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a long piece about how institutions ration scarce resources: hospital triage protocols, vaccine allocation in a pandemic, and cost-benefit thresholds in public health. In my interviews, philosophers keep telling me that straightforward aggregation — saving the most lives, summing the most benefit — is morally crude, and that Scanlon's contractualism offers the serious alternative: an act is wrong if it would be ruled out by principles no one could reasonably reject, where each affected person's complaint is weighed individually rather than pooled. But when I ask how that framework handles the choice between saving one person and saving five strangers, I get a different answer from every interviewee. This, I gather, is the aggregation problem, and it's where the piece has to do its real work.

Before I report anyone's preferred solution, I want the specification. Take on the role of a moral philosopher who specializes in contractualist ethics and who has thought carefully about the numbers problem. Begin by brainstorming briefly and informally — list the pressures on any view in this space before organizing anything — and then write the desiderata: what an adequate contractualist treatment of aggregation must deliver. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. The list should at least cover: what the view must say about saving the greater number when individual complaints are equal in strength; how it must handle many trivial burdens spread across millions against one severe burden; whether it may take ex ante probabilities into account; what it must preserve of the separateness of persons; and why it must not simply collapse into sum-ranking consequentialism.

Finally, tell me which desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, which single requirement eliminates the most live contenders, and which items on your own list are genuine requirements versus the inherited prejudices of one camp in this dispute. A numbered list is fine; precision matters more than length.
