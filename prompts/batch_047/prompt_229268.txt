Is there a name for this pattern? A few times now I've watched a city try to make its justice system gentler, and the gentle thing ends up catching more people than the harsh thing did. Concrete cases: (1) a county sets up a diversion program so low-level drug offenders can avoid jail, but once diversion exists, prosecutors start charging cases they used to quietly drop, and people who take the deal end up with a year of check-ins, drug tests and court dates that a guilty plea never would've involved; (2) a city replaces cash bail with ankle monitors to shrink the jail population, and jail numbers do fall, but the monitors go on people who previously would've just been released with a court date, and now a dead battery or a missed charger counts as a violation that can land them in jail anyway; (3) body cameras get introduced to protect civilians from the police, and a few years later the footage is mostly being used as prosecution evidence against civilians.

In each case the reform did the thing it promised -- fewer people in cages, more oversight -- and yet the total number of people the system touches went up, not down. It doesn't feel like ordinary mission creep, because the stated mission didn't really change; the net just got wider at the same time as it got softer. I've seen people on the abolish side of the reform/defund/abolish argument gesture at this to argue that certain reforms actually grow the thing they're trying to shrink, but I've never seen the phenomenon itself cleanly named or picked apart from nearby failure modes.

So: is there an established term for exactly this? If there is, tell me how it's usually defined and where its edges are. Does it still count if the reform expands the population but genuinely reduces harm per person? Does it count if the expansion was the quiet plan all along, versus an accident? Does it require criminal justice specifically, or is the justice system just one instance of something broader? And if there's no single established term, coin one and define it carefully against mission creep, displacement (where enforcement just moves elsewhere), and plain policy failure. I'd also like a sentence or two on what the existence of this pattern does and doesn't imply for the reform-vs-abolish debate, since both sides seem to think it settles things in their favor.
