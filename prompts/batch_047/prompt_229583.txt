I serve on the hiring committee of a small organization, and I have noticed something about our decisions that I cannot find a word for. When we choose among three or more options -- candidates, budget plans, policy proposals -- we never vote on all of them at once. Instead the chair sets a sequence: first A against B, then the winner against C. Everyone, as far as I can tell, votes honestly at each step. And yet I have become convinced that whoever sets the sequence effectively chooses the outcome. With the same members holding the same honest opinions, candidate X wins under one ordering and candidate Y wins under another, and a chair who knows how people feel can arrange for her favorite to prevail. Nobody has lied about anything, and no rule has been broken.

I have a hazy memory of a famous theorem saying that every reasonable voting method can be gamed, but when I looked into it, the gaming there seemed to mean voters misrepresenting what they want -- which is not what is happening here, since everyone votes sincerely throughout. So: does the phenomenon I am describing have an established name and a literature? How exactly does it differ from the kind of manipulation that theorem concerns, and are there known results about it of comparable generality? I also want the edges marked out: sometimes the order makes no difference at all, and I would like to know precisely when and why. Please do not just hand me a label -- explain what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where it ends. Kindly write your answer in American English.
