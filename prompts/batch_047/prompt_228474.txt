So I'm in this informal reading group on population ethics and axiology, and last week a friend of mine who's been getting into Buddhist-flavored value theory pitched a view she calls tranquilism: roughly, the only thing that ultimately matters is the absence of craving and suffering. Pleasure doesn't add value on its own, it only counts insofar as it relieves or prevents craving, and a state of deep contentment with no unmet needs is, on her view, as good as experience gets -- but crucially not better than no experience at all. I was sympathetic at first, it has a certain appeal, but then she worked it into an argument that seems to prove something I can't swallow, and I genuinely can't figure out where it breaks. Here it is, as best I remember:

(1) The only thing good in itself is a subject's being free of craving and suffering; everything else (pleasure, knowledge, achievement) matters only insofar as it reduces or prevents craving.
(2) Therefore, how good a world is depends entirely on how much craving and suffering it contains -- the less, the better, and a world containing none at all is perfect.
(3) A world with no sentient beings in it contains no craving and no suffering.
(4) So an entirely empty world is perfect -- and, by (1)'s lights, no worse than a world full of perfectly tranquil, need-free meditators.
(5) Any world that actually contains sentient life will contain at least some craving or frustration somewhere, even if it's just mild hunger, boredom, or one unfulfilled preference.
(6) So every populated world is at least slightly worse than the empty world, and the best possible populated worlds can at best tie with it.
(7) From which it follows that, other things equal, it would be best to bring about the painless, instantaneous extinction of all sentient life -- and that creating a new being can never make things better, only worse or neutral, no matter how contented that being would be.

Now, (7) is monstrous, and even my friend squirmed when she said it out loud. But each individual step looked fine when we walked through them one by one. Where does this go wrong, if it does? If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (1) -- the core tranquilist idea still seems right to me -- than accept (7), but I want to know what keeping it costs, and whether there's some reformulation of her view that dodges the conclusion without smuggling back in the claim that pleasure is intrinsically good. I'm also curious how much of the weight is being done by (5), since she basically just asserted that one and I'm not sure it's even true in principle.
