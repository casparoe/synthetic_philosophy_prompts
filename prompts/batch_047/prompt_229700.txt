okay so I need some help and its kind of a weird request. I'm a novelist, mid-draf on my third book, and I've painted myself into a corner. My ending depends on a character who has lost everything -- money, family, reputation, the works -- standing up in the last chapter and saying, basically, "what happened to me was right, and I'd choose it again." My writing group says nobody talks like that, nobody FEELS like that, and that people who get crushed either break or get bitter. I keep insisting there's precedent, and the precedent I'm thinking of is this strange little thing Dostoevsky does in the Brothers Karamazov, in the chapter everyone knows as the Grand Inquisitor, but NOT in the part everyone quotes.

Here's the setup: before Ivan even gets to the Inquisitor speech, he's rambling about this old convent poem where the Mother of God tours hell and negotiates a yearly break in the tortures. And the poem ends, in the translation I found (this ancient Blavatsky one that was floating around online), with "a chorus of the 'damned' singing loud praises to God from their 'bottomless pit,' thanking and telling Him:

  Thou art right, O Lord, very right,
  Thou hast condemned us justly."

So the damned, in the moment their suffering is briefly paused, use the breath to say the sentence was just. And then a page later the Inquisitor starts his whole speech, the core of which (I'm paraphrasing) is that human beings will hand over their freedom to anyone who feeds them and takes the terrible burden of choosing off their shoulders, and they'll be grateful for it, happy for the first time.

Here's my question. Argue that this little prologue detail is not decoration but is Dostoevsky planting the refutation of the Inquisitor before the Inquisitor opens his mouth: that the chorus of the damned shows a free judgment that cannot be bought off even by the end of suffering, let alone by bread and comfort -- that people can look at the worst possible outcome for themselves and still affirm something against their own interest, which is exactly what the Inquisitor's whole theory of human nature says we can't do. I want the strongest version of that reading: why the placement matters (Ivan tells this story FIRST), what work the "very right" repetition is doing, how it connects to what Ivan himself does later in the novel with his whole rebellion-and-returning-the-ticket thing, and why the Inquisitor's bargain collapses if even one chorus in hell sings like that.

Also, because I have to actually USE this: if the argument goes through, tell me what it implies for how I should write my last chapter -- specifically whether my character's affirmation has to be serene like the chorus, or whether it can be furious and broken and still count as the same move. And be honest with me at the end: if you think the writing group is right and this reading is a stretch, say so and tell me which premise fails. I'd rather rewrite three chapters now than publish something built on a misreading.
