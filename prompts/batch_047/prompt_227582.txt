I help run a small AI safety reading group (mostly grad students plus a couple of engineers who drop in), and we keep circling back to the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence in a way that's started to feel genuinely pathological. At this point both camps have their lines fully memorized: one side says orthogonality is almost trivially true — there's no contradiction in a very capable system pursuing paperclips, so intelligence and terminal goals are independent axes, full stop — and that instrumental convergence (self-preservation, resource acquisition, goal-content integrity, etc.) follows for a huge range of goal contents. The other side says this whole framing smuggles in a picture of intelligence as a disembodied expected-utility maximizer, that real cognitive systems (human or trained) have their goals shaped and constrained by the process that produced them, and that the convergence claims are so vague about what counts as a "goal" or "capability" that nothing could falsify them. Same arguments every session, nobody's moved in months, and I'm honestly not sure anyone's even disagreeing anymore.

Here's the twist that's bugging me. Someone dug up Hume and pointed out that the "trivial" side of this debate is basically already in the Treatise (Book II, Part III, Section III, "Of the influencing motives of the will"), where he argues that reason's only job is to work out means, and writes "the impulse arises not from reason, but is only directed by it," and even more starkly, "It can never in the least concern us to know, that such objects are causes, and such others effects, if both the causes and effects be indifferent to us." So one reading is: Hume gave the orthogonality thesis away for free in 1739 and the modern debate is just footnotes to him. But my friend in the group insists the passage cuts the other way — that little clause "if both the causes and effects be indifferent to us" is doing everything, and the critics' whole point is that for any actual mind built by an actual process, the objects are never indifferent; the goals and the cognition grow up together. I find both readings kind of plausible, which is probably a bad sign.

So what I want from you is not a verdict on orthogonality itself but a diagnosis of why this debate never resolves. Is it a substantive disagreement about the space of possible minds, a verbal dispute about what "intelligence" and "goals" mean when applied to trained systems, or two questions — a modal one (could a highly capable system have arbitrary goals?) and an empirical one (will the systems we actually build be like that?) — being argued under one heading, with each side winning its own? My working hypothesis is the last one, but hold it loosely. Please also tell me whether the Hume passage really does prefigure the modern thesis or whether my friend's reading is right and it's closer to the critics. And give me, concretely, what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a formal result, an empirical observation about how real training runs behave, or an admission that the question needs to be split and the old framing retired. Finally, be upfront about how confident you are in the diagnosis overall, and single out the one point in your answer you're least sure of — I'd rather know where the wobble is than get a tidy story.
