I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on population ethics, and I am setting this year's final examination. Draft one essay question on Benatar's asymmetry argument for antinatalism that candidates could answer in 90 minutes, along with a model answer of roughly 600 words and a marking scheme out of 20 points.

The question must be unanswerable by anyone who merely reproduces the four-part asymmetry from a textbook. Specifically, it should force candidates to do the following. First, state the asymmetry with precision, including why the fourth claim — that the absence of pleasure is not bad — requires a clause restricting its evaluation to cases in which there is no one who exists to be deprived of the pleasure, and what goes wrong if that clause is dropped. Second, evaluate the strongest version of the objection that the asymmetry is ad hoc: press candidates on whether Benatar's appeal to our other intuitions (for instance, that we regard it as strange to lament the pleasures that never-conceived possible people are missing, but not strange to regard the pain they are spared as a reason in favour of never conceiving them) genuinely supports the asymmetry or merely re-describes it, and whether an explanatory-unification defence of this kind can succeed without circularity. Third, test whether candidates can keep distinct two claims that are constantly conflated: that coming into existence is always a harm relative to never existing, and that a life once begun is not worth continuing — the asymmetry speaks only to the first, and candidates should have to explain why the argument does not license conclusions about the second. Fourth, ask candidates to assess a constructed rival principle — "the absence of pleasure is bad whenever the pleasure would have been good for someone had that person existed" — and to determine whether Benatar can reject it without begging the question.

The marking scheme should be built around five must-hit moves, stating how many marks each is worth (totalling 20), and for each move give a one-line note on the characteristic way candidates fumble it — for example, omitting the person-restricting clause, treating the four intuitions as independent premises rather than as data the asymmetry purports to explain, sliding from "coming into existence is a harm" to "life is not worth living," or dismissing the rival principle by simply restating the asymmetry. The model answer should itself make all five moves cleanly rather than merely gesturing at them.
