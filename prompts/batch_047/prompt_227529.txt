Last night a close friend and I argued for nearly three hours about Molinism and middle knowledge, and we parted without either of us conceding an inch. He holds the Thomist line: God's knowledge of what creatures will do follows from the divine decree, and there is simply nothing there for God to know prior to willing a world. I defended the Molinist view, that before any decree God possesses scientia media -- knowledge of what each possible creature would freely do in any complete set of circumstances in which it might be placed -- and that providence works through this middle knowledge rather than around it. The sticking point, as always, was the grounding objection: if those circumstances never obtain and the creature never exists in them, what makes the counterfactual true at all? What is it true in virtue of?

Rather than relitigate the evening by email, I would like to send my friend a poem, and I would like your help writing it. It should be a villanelle: nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, with the two refrains doing real philosophical work rather than decorating the verse. I would like one refrain to voice the grounding objection and the other to voice the Molinist reply -- that the counterfactuals are true, and known to God pre-volitionally, in a way that sits between the necessary truths He knows by His nature and the contingent truths He knows through His will. Because a villanelle's refrains return and interlock whether or not the speaker wants them to, the form should enact something about the dispute itself: the same two claims keep reasserting themselves, each unchanged by contact with the other, and the poem should let that stand. Please do not resolve the argument in the final quatrain; let the two refrains meet there without either subsuming the other. The poem should be recognizable as *about* middle knowledge to someone who has thought carefully about it -- not merely a poem with the words "middle knowledge" dropped in -- but it should also work as a poem for my friend, who will read it over coffee on Saturday morning. A measured, somewhat formal register would suit both the subject and the friendship.
