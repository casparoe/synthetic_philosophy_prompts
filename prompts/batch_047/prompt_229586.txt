At a philosophy of mind reading group, a friend summarized the anti-mechanist argument associated with J. R. Lucas’s 1961 article Minds, Machines and Gödel, and later developed by Roger Penrose, as follows:

Gödel proved that in any formal system powerful enough to express arithmetic, there are true statements that the system cannot prove. For any computer proposed as a model of human mathematical reasoning, we can therefore construct its Gödel sentence. The machine cannot establish that sentence, but a human mathematician can step outside the machine’s formal rules and see that it is true. Human mathematical understanding consequently outruns every machine. If the machine changes its rules, learns, or behaves probabilistically, the same trick can simply be applied to its later state or to its overall pattern of behavior. Nor is it relevant that human mathematicians sometimes contradict themselves: the argument concerns ideal mathematical competence, not actual human performance. Lucas therefore refuted mechanism, while Penrose’s version merely adds that the non-computational feature of thought must ultimately be explained by physics. It follows that no computational system could, even in principle, reproduce human mathematical insight.

I am sympathetic to the idea that there is something philosophically important here, but I suspect this summary moves too quickly from Gödel’s theorem to a conclusion about minds and machines. Please audit it carefully. Which parts are accurate, which depend on unstated technical assumptions, and which conflate Gödel’s mathematical result with a further theory of human understanding? Where appropriate, distinguish what a careful Lucas-style defender might still claim from the stronger conclusions my friend draws.

To make the issue concrete, apply your analysis to two systems. System A is a deterministic theorem-prover whose complete current program and arithmetical axioms are publicly known. We construct a Gödel sentence for the theory generated by its current state. A does not derive that sentence unless the theory is inconsistent, but a human who accepts the theory’s consistency says the sentence is true. A can subsequently revise itself and adopt the sentence as a new axiom. System B is a stochastic, continually learning system whose future arithmetical commitments are not known to constitute a fixed effectively enumerable theory. Does the anti-mechanist argument treat these systems differently, and does A’s ability to revise itself answer the argument or merely generate a new version of it?
