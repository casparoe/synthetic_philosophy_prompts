I've spent years reading decision-theory threads on LessWrong and adjacent forums, and the centipede game is where backward induction either vindicates itself or embarrasses itself, depending on whom you ask. Most discussions I see rehearse the same three moves and stop. I'd like a dialogue that goes further.

Setup: a one-shot, anonymous centipede between strangers. Twenty nodes, alternating moves. At each node the pot grows (roughly doubling), and the player who stops takes somewhat more than half while the other gets somewhat less. Rules and payoffs are common knowledge. There is no reputation, no iteration, no outside context.

Write a dialogue (roughly 1200–1500 words) between two equally competent interlocutors:

- One holds that backward induction is simply correct here: the unique rational play is to stop at the first node, and any argument for passing is confused about what rational advice even means once the opponent can hear it.
- The other holds that rational players should pass deep into the game, and that backward induction rests on a premise — stable common knowledge of rationality at every node, including nodes its own reasoning declares unreachable — that the game's structure undermines.

Requirements:

1. Real back-and-forth. Each character must engage the other's actual arguments, not a caricature. The backward-induction defender should know the strongest objections (the off-path problem; the claim that common knowledge of rationality is self-undermining in this game) and have serious replies, not shrugs. The passing defender should know the strongest replies in turn (notably: any public argument for passing must survive the opponent knowing you'd follow it, and "the opponent might be irrational" can't ground *rational* advice without special pleading) and must answer them on the merits.

2. The off-path problem must be confronted explicitly at some point: what does backward-inductive reasoning even recommend at a node that that same reasoning says will never be reached, and can the defender answer without quietly importing trembles, mistakes, or bounded rationality into a model that was supposed to exclude them?

3. Here's the part I most care about, and the twist intended to break both sides out of cached arguments. Midway through the dialogue, one character proposes an operational test: a "centipede league" in which you'll play many one-shot anonymous centipedes against an unknown population — some mix of strict backward-inductors, habitual deep-passers, and noisy agents — with no information about any individual opponent beyond what your own games against the pool teach you. Both characters must state a concrete policy for the league, precise enough to be implemented as a short program (stopping rule, updating rule if any, stopping condition). Then each must attack the *other's policy*, not the other's philosophy. The back half of the dialogue should turn on what happens when abstract positions are forced into code: do the two policies converge in expected payout even though the philosophies remain opposed, or diverge sharply even though the philosophies sounded close? Does either character's policy require smuggling in assumptions their stated position forbids? Is the original disagreement substantive, or does it partly dissolve once cashed out?

4. No moderator, no declared winner, no forced convergence. It's fine — encouraged, even — if they end by locating the disagreement at a specific, crisply stated crux (for instance, whether rationality can be coherently common knowledge at unreached nodes, or what "advice" amounts to when the advice itself is public information). But the crux should emerge from the argument, not be announced up front.

Keep the tone like a good rationalist-thread exchange: precise, willing to follow implications, no posturing. No citations or references to specific papers — argue from the structure of the game itself.
