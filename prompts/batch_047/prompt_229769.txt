I have been keeping a record of when I am actually willing to say that a system believes something, and I would like to know what principle my verdicts add up to, if any. Here are the cases, with my gut calls:

- My thermostat turns the heat on when the room is cold. It does not believe anything; it is a coil and a switch.
- My chess engine plays as though it knows I am weak in rook endgames, but I take that to be a manner of speaking, not a literal belief.
- A Mars rover steers around a boulder. Here I am genuinely inclined to say it believed there was a rock in its path -- there seems to be no better way to make sense of the maneuver.
- An ant colony shifts its foraging to a richer food source overnight. The colony has something belief-like; no individual ant does.
- A chatbot asserts that Paris is the capital of France, relies on it consistently, and corrects me when I say Lyon. I lean toward saying it believes Paris is the capital, in the only sense that matters for predicting it.
- The same chatbot, asked to play a contrarian character, fluently insists that Lyon is the capital. Now I suspect it never believed anything in either conversation.
- A friend with a spider phobia sincerely says spiders are harmless but behaves as though they are lethal. I want to say she believes spiders are dangerous despite her words.
- A sleepwalking friend navigates the kitchen without collision. I resist saying he believes the fridge is left of the door, even though he never bumps into it.

The pair I cannot reconcile: the rover and the chatbot earn beliefs on the strength of behavior alone, but the sleepwalker does not, despite equally reliable behavior; and my phobic friend gets a belief that flatly contradicts her sincere assertions, while the chess engine's "knowledge" is merely a facon de parler despite equally sophisticated play.

Is there a single criterion for when belief attribution is correct -- not merely convenient -- that delivers these verdicts, or nearly so? If the best candidate is the intentional-stance idea that a system has the beliefs it is predictively useful to attribute to it, then show how it handles the phobia and sleepwalking cases, which seem to push against it. Please state your candidate principle precisely, clause by clause, naming the case that motivates each clause; give its verdicts on two cases I have not listed -- a company whose records and procedures all treat its product as safe while every employee privately suspects otherwise, and a conversational AI that passes every behavioral test but whose internals turn out to be a vast precomputed table of question-and-answer pairs; and if no principle saves all eight of my verdicts, tell me which one I should surrender and why. Lead with your answer in the first sentence -- the principle itself, or the verdict that none fits -- and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
