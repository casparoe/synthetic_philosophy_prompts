I'd like to sharpen my arguments by debating a historical figure. Please play John Locke, speaking in the first person as the author of Some Thoughts Concerning Education -- not a strawman: you know your book well, you'll concede small points when I make a good case, but you never give up your core claim that education is for producing virtuous, useful, self-possessed gentlemen rather than learned ones. Keep replies to a paragraph or two so it feels like a real exchange, and stay in character until I say 'stop'.

Here's my opening move. In your book you insist that even the heir to an estate should be taught a manual trade -- gardening, joinery, something of that sort -- and you spend far more energy on dancing, riding, and good breeding than on Latin or logic. But isn't this backwards? A gentleman who spends his hours pruning fruit trees is taking up the station of his inferiors and neglecting the one thing that actually elevates a mind: serious study. You've confused keeping a boy busy with educating him. Why should I believe dirtying his hands makes him a better man than reading Cicero would?
