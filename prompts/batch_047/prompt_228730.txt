I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the past two years making up for lost time in philosophy, and I have run into an argument that I cannot accept but cannot refute. I would be grateful for a diagnosis, and I would ask that you reply in American English.

The argument grew out of the old fable of Death in Damascus, in which a servant meets Death in the market of Damascus, reads surprise on Death's face, and flees to Samarra — where, it turns out, Death had all along expected to meet him that evening. I first encountered the story in connection with Newcomb's problem, where it is used to illustrate how a reliable prediction of your behavior can make your deliberation seem idle. A colleague of mine, a retired statistician, pressed the following chain of reasoning on me over dinner, and I have not been able to dislodge it:

(1) A choice is worth deliberating over only if the outcome depends on what you choose.

(2) In the fable, Death has already appointed a place and time on the basis of a correct prediction of the servant's movements. That appointment is fixed before the servant decides where to go.

(3) What is already fixed before you choose cannot be altered by your choice; you cannot change the past.

(4) Therefore the servant's decision cannot influence whether he meets Death, and his deliberation between Damascus and Samarra is idle. Whatever he chooses, he will have chosen the appointed place.

(5) But nothing in steps (2) through (4) turns on anything special about Death. The same structure obtains whenever a sufficiently accurate prediction of your choice exists prior to your choice — and any world in which behavior is in principle predictable is such a world, whether the predictor is a demon, a deterministic universe, or merely a spouse who knows you well.

(6) Therefore, in any such world, no decision ever influences its outcome, and all deliberation is idle.

(7) Since deliberation is plainly not idle — it matters enormously whether I deliberate before crossing the street — predictability of choice must be impossible, and determinism must be false.

My colleague presents this as an a priori argument against determinism, which I find extravagant; he has apparently derived a thesis in fundamental physics from a folktale. My instinct is that the trouble is already present by step (4), because the fable itself is usually read as showing something troubling about the servant's situation rather than something trivial. But when I try to say what exactly goes wrong, I find myself asserting only that the servant "could have done otherwise," which my colleague grants and insists is beside the point.

So my questions are these. First, which step do you reject, and why does it appear sound? Second, what does rejecting it cost — that is, what commitments about the relationship between past facts, present choices, and their counterfactual dependence on one another must one take on in order to reject it? Third, whatever your diagnosis, what does it imply about the fable's actual moral: does the story establish anything interesting about deliberation under prediction, or does it trade on the same error as the argument above? If it helps to know where my sympathies lie, I would rather give up (1) than accept (7), but I suspect the flaw is not in (1), and I would like to know what I am paying either way.
