I had this dumb idea after a friend showed me a fight in her town council over whether to keep a statue of a forgotten general, preserve every rotting object connected to the town founder, or formally apologize for the town’s entire industrial past. Write a deadpan parody of a consulting report submitted to the council by an invented heritage firm called ClioMetrics Strategic Memory Partners. The firm proposes three billable service packages based on Nietzsche’s monumental, antiquarian, and critical relations to history: one that supplies inspiring ancestors at scale, one that preserves the past because it is ours, and one that puts deceased people on trial so the living can feel morally renovated. The immediate problem is that the council has bought all three packages for the same six-month civic renewal initiative.

The report should be about 900–1,100 words and formatted like a real consulting document, with an executive summary, project scope, deliverables, performance metrics, risk register, implementation timeline, and a final invoice. Make the satire come from a serious understanding of the uses and disadvantages of each historical mode: monumental history should genuinely energize public action while quietly sanding away inconvenient differences between past and present; antiquarian history should express real gratitude and rootedness while treating decay, contradiction, and change as preservation failures; critical history should expose inherited injustice while becoming addicted to condemnation and implying that the present has been born innocent. Include an invented Civic Plasticity Index measuring how well the town can digest its past without either choking on it or vomiting it up, plus at least one recommendation about the constructive use of forgetting. The jokes should land for someone who has never read Nietzsche, but a reader who knows the essay should recognize that the firm’s absurdities exaggerate actual tendencies in the three modes rather than random clichés about history.
