I recently learned about the Gibbard--Satterthwaite theorem, and I'm puzzled about a body of research that seems to ignore its implications. As I understand it, the theorem establishes that any non-dictatorial voting rule over three or more alternatives, provided every alternative can win, is manipulable: at every election, some voter can always secure a better outcome for themselves by misrepresenting their true preferences rather than voting sincerely. If every reasonable voting rule is manipulable in this way, then it seems to me that no voting rule can be better than any other with respect to strategic voting -- manipulability is a universal constant, fixed by theorem, so there is nothing left to compare.

And yet political scientists and social choice theorists continue to publish work comparing voting rules on precisely this dimension: asking whether instant-runoff voting is more or less manipulable than plurality rule, whether approval voting invites more strategic behavior than ranked-choice systems, and so on. Practitioners, too, treat the choice between these systems as if their vulnerability to strategic voting were a real design consideration.

So my question is: is this entire literature and practice resting on a mistake? Given what the Gibbard--Satterthwaite theorem proves, shouldn't everyone involved simply accept that all non-dictatorial rules are equally manipulable and turn their attention elsewhere? Or is there some way of making sense of comparisons of manipulability that survives the theorem -- and if so, what exactly would such comparisons have to mean to be coherent?
