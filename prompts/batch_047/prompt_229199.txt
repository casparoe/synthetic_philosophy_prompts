I teach a mixed seminar of philosophy and econ majors, about 18 students, one 80-minute session, and I want to run the dollar auction live in class. But here's the catch: I don't want the standard party trick where everyone giggles at the sucker who paid $3.50 for a dollar and we all nod about how irrational people are. The thing I actually want them to wrestle with is a real disagreement about what the game shows. On one reading, escalation is a cognitive bug -- people can't ignore sunk costs, hate losing, get carried away. On the other reading, the trap is in the structure, not in the skull: at every single step, bidding again can be the locally rational move, and the disaster is produced by perfectly sane responses to perverse incentives. These diagnoses matter because the cures differ. If it's a bias, you teach people to cut their losses. If it's a trap, the lesson is to never sit down at the table in the first place, or to change the rules of the game.

So design me the session with all of that built in. Specifically:

1. The trap should spring on them before anyone names it. No lecture up front, don't say "dollar auction" or "sunk cost." Figure out how to set it up (maybe as one item in a series of normal auctions?) so they discover what they've walked into.

2. Run the game at least twice with a rule change in between -- you could vary the stakes, switch from open outcry to sealed written bids, or toggle the rule that the runner-up also pays. The point of the variants is that afterwards students should be able to argue, from how their own behavior changed, which diagnosis it supports. Tell me which variations you think are most diagnostic and why.

3. There should be real but small stakes so it stings a little. I can spend a few bucks of my own money, or use candy or participation points -- advise me on what works and what backfires.

4. Game out the predictable failure modes: the one student who's read about this game and spoils it, a room that refuses to bid at all, two friends colluding to split the prize, the auction that ends in ten seconds. For each, tell me what to do in the moment and what it does to the debrief if it happens anyway.

5. A debrief that maps concrete moments -- the first bid over half the prize, the point where losing the auction became worse than making the next bid -- onto the two diagnoses, and that pushes students to say which one fits what they actually felt. Also: where the literature genuinely disagrees about whether this game demonstrates irrationality or rational entrapment, flag that as contested. Don't let me present "it's the sunk cost fallacy" as settled science if it isn't.

6. Finally, an honest note on what the activity can't reproduce about real escalation -- wars, strikes, lawsuit spirals, contracts you now can't afford to lose. No fixed prize, no announced end, face and careers on the line, and often no option to just not play. What does the facilitator have to supply by hand?

Give me a timeline with rough minutes per phase, the exact rules as you'd read them aloud, and the debrief questions. And recieve my thanks in advance for not writing me a worksheet.
