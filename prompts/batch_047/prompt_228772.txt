I run a small podcast with a friend from my old philosophy program where we do these goofy "time machine interviews" -- a scholar comes on and answers questions in character as a dead philosopher, staying as close as possible to what that person actually wrote. Episodes have done pretty well so far (the Hume one got us briefly featured on the front page of our uni's student paper lol). Next month we're doing John Stuart Mill, focused on Utilitarianism, and our guest is a professor who's defended a basically Millian view in her own work, so she'll be good at staying in character. I'm writing the question list now and I want to avoid the usual greatest-hits stuff (higher vs lower pleasures, "better to be Socrates dissatisfied," etc.).

Here's the angle I want instead. In Chapter 2 Mill grumbles that critics hit utilitarianism from both sides at once -- he writes that the same sort of persons, and often the very same persons, denounce the theory "as impracticably dry when the word utility precedes the word pleasure, and as too practicably voluptuous when the word pleasure precedes the word utility." That stuck with me because the modern version of the "impracticably dry" complaint is the demandingness objection: if right actions are the ones that promote the most happiness, aren't I morally required to donate most of my paycheck, skip vacations, never buy a coffee I could've given away? Meanwhile the "too practicably voluptuous" complaint survives as the idea that utilitarianism is a crude anything-goes calculus. My co-host thinks Mill's double-barreled complaint is basically a rhetorical dodge -- you can't answer both horns at once without the answer to one feeding the other -- and I think he might be write.

So: draft 8-10 interview questions I can put to "Mill" that build off this passage rather than asking him to recite the Greatest Happiness Principle. I want at least one question on whether the too-demanding and too-permissive criticisms can both be true of the same theory at the same time (and what it would show if they were); at least one pressing him on whether anything at all is merely nice-but-optional on his view, or whether every spared expense is a small moral failure; and at least one on the fact that he himself kept a day job at the East India Company and wrote books in his spare time rather than devoting everything to relieving suffering -- does his life contradict his doctrine or illustrate something about it. For each question, give me a follow-up keyed to the reply he's most likely to give in character, since our guest never breaks character and I need to be ready to press. Also flag any question where you think the honest Mill answer is "the critics have a point here" -- those are my favorite moments on the show and I want to know where they're hiding.
