I chair the clinical-algorithms oversight committee of a mid-sized regional health system. Three years ago we deployed a machine-learning tool that continuously scores ward patients for deterioration risk and recommends ICU transfer above a threshold. Our regulator approved it on one condition: every recommendation must receive "meaningful human review," meaning the attending nurse may accept or override the tool, with override rates reported to us quarterly.

The problem is what that review looks like in practice. Overrides occur in 0.6% of cases. When they do occur, they are usually correct — we estimate that about two in five overrides prevented an inappropriate transfer or caught a deterioration the tool missed — but they are vanishingly rare, because overriding requires a written justification, a phone call to the ICU attending, and a note in a log that nursing staff privately call the audit list. Last month a patient whose score sat just below the threshold deteriorated sharply; her nurse had documented unease about her breathing two hours earlier but did not trigger an override. The patient survived, barely. The nurse's statement reads: the tool said the score was fine, and I wasn't going to put myself on the audit list over a hunch.

This is where I want the committee's reasoning to be philosophically honest rather than merely procedural. Cognitive scientists use the term "sphexishness" for the behavior of the digger wasp, which repeats a fixed routine — dragging its paralyzed prey to the burrow entrance, leaving it, checking inside, retrieving it — even when an experimenter moves the prey a few centimeters during the check, causing the wasp to start the whole sequence over from the beginning. The wasp looks flexible; it is not. My worry is that our "meaningful human review" is sphexish in the same way: formally discretionary, behaviorally rigid. One committee member goes further and argues the review is worse than nothing, because it manufactures a liability shield — the algorithm said so, the human agreed — without adding judgment. The nursing representative counters that the rare overrides prove the loop works when it matters and that the fix is to stop punishing them.

The committee must choose among three options: (a) retain the system as is; (b) drop the human-review requirement and let the tool page the ICU team directly, on the grounds that theatrical review is worse than honest automation; (c) redesign the review so that overriding is cheap — for example, a no-questions-asked override channel, with audit applying to non-overrides in flagged cases instead.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before our next meeting. It should recommend one of the three options with implementation specifics and a timeframe; state what would have to be true of human reviewers for "meaningful review" to be more than sphexish compliance, and whether that condition is achievable in our institution or only on paper; and give the strongest argument for the runner-up option fairly enough that its supporters would recognize their position in it.
