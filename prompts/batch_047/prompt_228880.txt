I’m a software engineer with no formal philosophy background preparing a workshop handout on whether qualia are coherent theoretical posits. I understand the familiar Chase-and-Sanborn coffee-taster case only in outline: after years of professional tasting, one person says the coffee tastes as it always did but that their evaluative reaction to it has changed, while another says their preferences and evaluative standards have remained fixed and that the taste itself has changed. The dispute interests me because it resembles deciding whether a change in a system’s observable behavior reflects a changed internal representation, a changed downstream consumer of that representation, or a changed interpretation of the logs. Please do not assume, however, that the software analogy settles the philosophical issue.

Construct a taxonomy of positions as an indented decision tree. Begin with the minimal datum that adult humans can ordinarily report changes in what their experiences are like, then build the tree from diagnostic forks rather than from a list of theory names. Use seven to nine branch nodes and produce eight to twelve leaves. Each branch should be a concrete question about a case, answerable by someone who does not already know the labels. At minimum, include variants in which: sensory discrimination remains fixed while emotional reaction changes; discriminative and behavioral capacities change while preference remains fixed; two subjects have the same discrimination, memory, attention, and reports, yet one insists that an intrinsic taste quality has changed; an archived memory conflicts with a present introspective report and there is independent evidence that one record is corrupted; and a behaviorally and functionally indistinguishable artificial system confidently describes the taste of coffee.

The leaves must include robust realism about intrinsic, irreducible qualia; functionalist realism; representationalism; illusionism; eliminativism; and, if the logical space permits, a quietist or genuinely undecidable position. Introduce panpsychism only if one of the forks actually requires it. For every branch, state what accepting each answer commits one to. For every leaf, give the position’s name, a one-sentence formulation, and its most awkward consequence. Also identify any unoccupied combinations of answers and diagnose whether they are unoccupied because they are incoherent, unattractive, or merely underexplored. Finally, flag rather than conceal any positions your tree cannot genuinely separate—particularly functionalism versus representationalism and illusionism versus eliminativism if your proposed forks do not distinguish them. Do not provide citations or a reading list.
