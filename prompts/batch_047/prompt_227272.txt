In about ten days I have to present the first chapter of my dissertation to our department's value theory workshop, and I'm starting to dread the Q&A more than the talk itself. The chapter defends tranquilism: roughly, the view that nothing is good in itself except the absence of craving and suffering, so that a state of untroubled contentment is not improvable by adding pleasures on top of it. The punchline I actually want to defend is the population-ethical one: bringing a new happy person into existence does not make the world better, because there's no one beforehand whose craving goes unrelieved, while bringing a miserable person into existence does make it worse. I got into this after a long argument with a friend about antinatalism where I kept insisting you can get the asymmetry without any gloom about existing life, and the chapter is my attempt to cash that out.

The room: mostly people raised on the standard population ethics canon (total utilitarians, one average utilitarian), a very sharp preference utilitarian who runs the workshop, one committed suffering-focused / negative-leaning utilitarian who I think is closer to an ally than a threat but will want to push me on why relief of craving isn't itself a positive good, and a philosopher of mind who works on affective phenomenology and has said in passing that she doesn't buy any clean line between craving and pleasure. Draft me a prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from *this* audience (not from a generic ethics crowd), phrased the way each person would phrase them in their own vocabulary, ordered roughly by how much damage they'd do to the chapter as stated. For each one give me what I should concede up front, what I should hold, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and a honest note on where the reply is soft. I'm especially worried about two shapes of objection: the "pinprick vs. utopia" comparison (does my view really rank a world with a single stubbed toe close to a flourishing world of billions, since both are one craving away from perfection?) and the phenomenologist's challenge that listening to music while content, or eating when not hungry, seems good without relieving any craving I can identify. And please include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, which I suspect has something to do with whether tranquilism quietly endorses putting everyone on a perfect sedative, because I'm not sure my answer to that isn't just biting a very large bullet.
