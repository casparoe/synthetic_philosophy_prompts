Last week I got into a fight on a decision theory Discord that I keep turning over in my head. Somebody proposed what they called an "acausal trade": I should donate a chunk of money to thier favorite cause now, on the grounds that agents like them in other possible worlds (or future simulations, or wherever) would then have reason to benefit people like me, and we all supposedly do better by reasoning this way even though neither side can verify anything. I said I'm not paying real money based on an uncheckable counterfactual about agents who may not exist, and three people at once hit me with the same line: "why ain'tcha rich?"

I've seen that slogan used as a mic-drop in a bunch of places -- one-boxing on Newcomb, paying up in counterfactual muggings, cooperating with a twin in a prisoner's dilemma you'll never meet -- and I genuinely can't tell what claim it's supposed to be making. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing? Off the top of my head:

- Maybe it's just the empirical observation that people who follow causal decision theory foreseeably walk away poorer in certain setups, which nobody disputes but which by itself settles nothing?
- Maybe it's a normative claim that a decision theory is refuted if following it predictably costs you money -- and does that survive the obvious reply that ANY theory can be punished by a suitably rigged environment?
- Maybe it's some deeper "rationality just means winning" pragmatism, in which case does it collapse into "do whatever produces good outcomes," which isn't a theory at all?
- And in the acausal trade context specifically, does the slogan smuggle in an assumption about what "rich" even means? Money is a surrogate for my actual goals, not the goal itself -- if taking the trade means acting against something I value for its own sake (like not being the kind of person who pays off unverifiable counterparties), then "you'd end up richer" might be scoring me on the wrong scoreboard entirely.

For each reading, tell me whether it's trivial, substantive but defensible, or just false, and crucially which reading -- if any -- actually supports telling me to wire money on the basis of agents I can't confirm exist. I want a menu of readings I can paste back into the thread, not a defense of one interpretation. And if the slogan's whole rhetorical power comes from sliding between the readings depending on who's pushing back, say that plainly.
