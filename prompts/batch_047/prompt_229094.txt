I've been reading LessWrong and its orbit for over a decade, and at some point I noticed I've never actually stress-tested the cluster of views I've absorbed about Pascal's mugging. Here is my position set, roughly as I'd defend it in a comment thread, and I'd like an audit before I keep building on it:

1. Paying the mugger is irrational, full stop. Any decision theory that advises handing over the wallet in the original scenario is thereby disqualified -- that's a reductio, not an interesting edge case.

2. It is illegitimate to dismiss outcomes merely because their probability is tiny. The entire case for prioritizing existential risk depends on taking seriously small probabilities of astronomically large stakes, and I won't give that up to solve a thought experiment.

3. I'm a fairly orthodox expected value maximizer: I broadly accept the standard axiomatic route to probabilistic coherence and utility, and I don't think utility functions are bounded in any way that does real work in ordinary deliberation.

4. The standard resolution -- that the rational credence in the mugger's ability or willingness to pay shrinks as the promised payoff grows, so the expected value never actually blows up -- is correct and fully dissolves the paradox. Nothing further needs fixing.

5. There is no principled line between the mugger's offer and any other claim about vast payoffs: the same decision rule should handle a stranger on the street, a demonstrated superintelligence making a verified offer, and longtermist arguments about the far future. If my rule refuses one and accepts another, it had better cite a real difference rather than an intuition about which conclusion I wanted.

My questions: which of these commitments genuinely pull against each other, and which only appear to? Please order the tensions by severity rather than flagging everything. For each real tension, identify the cheapest revision and what it costs -- in particular, I'd like to know whether any single change cleans up most of the damage, and whether some pair I think is in conflict (say, 1 and 2) actually dissolves once the discounting move in 4 is stated carefully. Note also where the discounting move itself has teeth I may not have priced in -- cases where it forces a verdict I'd flinch at.

Finally, be explicit about your own exposure: what argument or consideration would change your verdict about where the real tension lies, and where is your analysis most likely to be wrong?
