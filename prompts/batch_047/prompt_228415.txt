ok so i'm a third-year philosophy PhD (dissertation on natural kind semantics, broadly) and our metaphysics of language reading group makes you circulate a one-pager of your commitments on rigid designation before you present, so people can attack it. i'd like an audit before i send mine out friday. here's where i've landed:

1. Kripke got names right: they're rigid designators, they pick out the same object in every world where that object exists, and this (plus the necessity of identity) gives us the necessary a posteriori. Hesperus is Phosphorus, water is H2O -- necessarily true, but only discovered empirically.
2. I'm also a direct reference person: the semantic content of a name is just its bearer. Names don't abbreviate descriptions and there's no extra mode of presentation hiding in the proposition expressed.
3. The a posteriori part of "water is H2O" is fully explained by the history: we found out empirically which necessity is actual. Once you say that, there's nothing epistemically special left over to explain.
4. I do accept the contingent a priori: whoever fixes the reference of "one meter" off the standard stick knows a priori that the stick is one meter long (at that time), even though it's contingent that it is.
5. Necessity of origins, full strength: this table couldn't have been made from a wholly different hunk of wood, and I couldn't have come from a different sperm and egg.
6. That said, obviously I could have been born a couple weeks earlier than I actually was -- when you're born is just a contingent fact about you.
7. And I think the two-dimensionalist response to all this is a mistake. There's no interesting sense in which "water is H2O" is contingent; the twin earth cases just show a different substance that the word would have latched onto.
