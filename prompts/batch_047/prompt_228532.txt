I have no formal training in philosophy or political science, but I have lived on both sides of the Atlantic, and I have been turning over a question in my head for some time: whether the American or the European attitude toward economic inequality is the more defensible one. Recently I came across Alexis de Tocqueville's Democracy in America, in the old Henry Reeve translation, and one of his observations has stuck with me. In the second volume he argues that democratic peoples come to love equality more ardently and more enduringly than they love freedom, and that the desire for equality only becomes more intense and more insatiable as conditions grow more equal — so that a people can be simultaneously more equal than any before it and more tormented by the remaining differences. This puzzles me, because if he is right about the democratic temperament, one would expect the United States, the most self-consciously democratic society he describes, to be the place most hostile to inequality; yet by every measure I know, Americans tolerate far wider income and wealth gaps than Europeans do, and seem less inclined to redistribute. So either Tocqueville's diagnosis is wrong, or something about the American case needs explaining, or I am misunderstanding what kind of equality he meant.

In the course of thinking about this I have arrived at a rough set of views, and before I commit to them further I would like to know whether they actually hang together. Here they are: (a) Inequality of outcome is not unjust in itself, provided that opportunities are genuinely equal — what matters is the fairness of the race, not the spacing of the finishers. (b) Talent, work ethic, and even the capacity to make an effort are substantially matters of luck — of birth, upbringing, and temperament — and people cannot claim much moral credit for them. (c) Envy is not a legitimate basis for political demands; the fact that I resent my neighbor's success gives me no claim against him, and politics organized around such resentment is corrosive. (d) Extreme concentration of wealth is nonetheless dangerous, because it buys disproportionate political influence and erodes the sense among ordinary citizens that the system is theirs. (e) The American tolerance for inequality rests partly on a belief in upward mobility — the expectation that anyone can rise — and I think this belief is, as a matter of fact, mostly mistaken in the contemporary United States. (f) On balance I find the European attitude — more redistribution, more suspicion of great fortunes — the more defensible one, and I think Americans are the ones who have been fooled.

My questions: Which of these commitments are in real tension with each other, and which only appear to be? I would like the tensions ranked by severity, not treated as all equally serious, and for each one I would like to know exactly which commitment would have to be given up or modified to restore consistency, and what that revision would cost. I am particularly unsure whether (c) leaves me any honest way of holding (d), and whether (b) quietly undermines (a), but I may be wrong about where the real problems lie, so please do not take my suspicions as a guide. Also, if any of my commitments depend on a reading of Tocqueville that he cannot actually support — for instance, if the equality he says democrats love is not the kind whose absence I am worried about — I would like that pointed out too, since I have built a fair amount on his authority.
