For a seminar paper on self-amending legal systems, I am working through Peter Suber's The Paradox of Self-Amendment: A Study of Law, Logic, Omnipotence, and Change (1990). In Section 3, "The Dilemma," Suber writes:

"So it seems that we must choose among immutable rules, infinite regress, and paradox. To this dilemma, or trilemma, we might add revolution. But we are now considering only the lawful change of law."

I want to know whether my own judgments about concrete cases can be organized by a single principle, and where they sit relative to Suber's trilemma. Here are my verdicts, stated as I actually find them:

Case 1. A neighborhood association's bylaws provide that they may be amended by a two-thirds vote of members present. By a two-thirds vote, the members raise the threshold to three-quarters. Verdict: plainly valid; nothing is lost.

Case 2. The same association, years later, votes unanimously to add: "The amendment clause itself shall not be subject to amendment." Verdict: not valid. I cannot shake the feeling that a power of change that can extinguish itself is a contradiction in motion, even though the unanimity ought to cure almost anything.

Case 3. A city charter provides that no ordinance shall restrict access to the public library, and that this clause may be amended only by a unanimous council vote. The council first votes unanimously to replace the unanimity requirement with a simple majority, then, in the same session, restricts library hours by a 5–4 vote. Verdict: the restriction is invalid; the two steps are one transaction in disguise, even though each step, taken alone, complied with the requirement then in force.

Case 4. A supply contract states that it may be modified only by a written instrument signed by both parties. The parties later agree orally to a new delivery schedule and both perform under it for a year. Verdict: the oral modification governs. The parties who made the rule may unmake it, and their conduct shows they did.

Case 5. A state constitution prescribes a laborious amendment procedure. A specially elected convention, acting outside that procedure, drafts a new constitution that is then ratified by popular referendum. Verdict: valid; the people are not creatures of their own constitution's amendment clause.

Case 6. In a game of Nomic, where players take turns proposing amendments to the rules, a player proposes that the rule governing the order of play shall henceforth be immutable. The proposal is adopted by the vote the existing rules require. Verdict: valid. Fencing off one substantive rule restricts the amendment power but does not destroy it, so this differs from Case 2.

My tentative principle: a rule of change may authorize any amendment that leaves a functioning rule of change in place, but none that would extinguish or permanently insulate either itself or the system it serves.

Please do the following. First, test this principle against each case, identifying precisely where it fails (I suspect it mishandles Case 3, since the two-step maneuver leaves a functioning amendment clause behind). Second, offer a repaired principle, or two competing candidates, stated precisely, naming the case that motivates each clause. Third, say what your preferred principle predicts for two cases I have not listed: a statute whose sunset clause repeals both the statute and itself, and a parliament that uses its ordinary legislative power to restrict the legislative power of its successors. Fourth, tell me honestly which of my six verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether Cases 3 and 4 can really be kept together without an ad hoc contract-versus-constitution exception. Finally, map the cases onto Suber's trilemma, and say whether my verdict in Case 5 shows that he was wrong to bracket revolution from the inquiry.

End your response with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing the numbers of the verdicts your recommended principle must sacrifice, or \answer{none} if it can save them all.
