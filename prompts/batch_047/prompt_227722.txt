I'm a novelist -- midlist, three decades in -- and my agent Martin is retiring next month after 34 years in the business, and I've been asked to give the toast at his dinner. About forty people will be there: half publishing folks (editors, agents, a couple of his other authors) who will catch every reference and privately decode which real people each anecdote is about, and half his family, including grandkids who think publishing is just, like, books existing.

Here's what I want the toast to actually be about. Martin is mildly famous in the industry for talking his clients OUT of things. In 2007 he refused to let one of his authors turn her quiet second novel into a vampire book, even though the money on the table was stupid. He never once pitched a manuscript as "X meets Y." Meanwhile everyone in publishing complains constantly that the industry is a treadmill -- every editor chasing the same five books, every author nudged toward whatever sold last year -- and everyone hates it, and nobody can step off alone, because the quiet book that fits no trend dies in a week. Which is all to say: when I read Scott Alexander's essay Meditations on Moloch a few years ago, the line that knocked me over was "Every single citizen hates the system, but for lack of a good coordination mechanism it endures." Alexander goes on to say the only real fix would be if "everyone agrees to stop doing this at once," and that nobody inside the system can pull that off without great risk to themselves.

So the toast has a genuine puzzle baked in, and I want the speech to take an actual position on it rather than gesture at one: was Martin the coordination mechanism Alexander says can't exist from inside the system -- one guy carving out little pockets where his authors were allowed to stop -- or did he just get away with it because two bestselling clients gave him cover, i.e. Moloch tolerates the defectors it can afford? Or is the darker answer right: that Moloch won anyway, given what the industry looks like now, and Martin was a stay of execution rather than an escape? I need about five minutes. The idea behind the passage has to be explained in one breath for the family half of the room -- no jargon, definately no phrase like "multipolar trap" -- but it can't be decoration either: the passage should structure the whole speech, including a real answer to whether he beat it. And it has to land as affection, not a lecture and not a doom speech about publishing, because his grandkids are there and Martin himself can't stand industry eulogies. One or two concrete anecdotes the publishing half will recognize as true-to-type would be great -- you can invent them, but make them plausible, he'll know.
