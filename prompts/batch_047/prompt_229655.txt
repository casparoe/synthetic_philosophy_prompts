I've come to accept the Dennett-style view that dreams are not experiences undergone during sleep: there is no "show" playing in the theater of the sleeping mind, and what makes something a dream is only the narrative content deposited in memory at waking, with the impression of duration and phenomenology being retrospective confabulation. Malcolm's skeptical arguments about the impossibility of verifying dream reports pushed me here, and the alternative strikes me as committed to an unverifiable inner movie.

Tell me what would count against this view. In particular, I'm unsure how to weigh the following, and I'd like your assessment of each as a defeater, ranked from fatal to merely awkward: (1) lucid dreamers who signal with pre-arranged eye-movement codes and correctly answer questions put to them while physiologically asleep; (2) the apparent incorporation of external stimuli -- a ringing phone becoming a fire alarm in the dream -- which seems to require real-time narrative construction rather than instant insertion at waking, unless one accepts an implausibly fast "instant cassette"; (3) the sheer intuition that something it is like to dream persists even for unremembered dreams. Also consider a case I find genuinely troubling: suppose a trained lucid dreamer is given, before sleep, a random number to memorize, and signals it via eye movements twenty minutes into a verified REM episode -- does that force real-time experience, or can the memory-insertion view absorb it, and at what cost to its coherence? Finally: has this position been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that a defect for a view whose original motivation was precisely verificationist scruples about uncheckable inner episodes? I'd like you to distinguish what the view's defenders would themselves concede as damaging from what they'd dismiss as question-begging.
