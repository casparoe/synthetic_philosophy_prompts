I chair the independent Electoral Commission of a small European republic, and we must rule within the month on the campaign rules for an upcoming referendum. A citizens' initiative, whose sponsors describe the sitting parties as a self-protecting cartel, has gathered enough signatures to force a binding vote on replacing our electoral system with full proportional representation. Our statute grants the Commission unusual discretion over how the campaign may be conducted, and three models are on the table: an open model, in which parties, trade unions, business federations and advocacy groups may run coordinated national campaigns subject to ordinary spending disclosure; a closed model, in which the Commission posts a vetted information booklet to every household and organised national campaigning by incorporated associations is prohibited, leaving advocacy to individual citizens acting without coordination; and a middle course, permitting organised campaigns but imposing equalised expenditure caps across umbrella organisations and equal blocks of public funding and broadcast time.

The Commission's disagreement has come to turn on a passage that one of my colleagues, an enthusiastic supporter of the closed model, keeps circulating. It is from Rousseau's The Social Contract, Book II, Chapter III ("Whether the General Will Is Fallible", in G. D. H. Cole's translation): "If, when the people, being furnished with adequate information, held its deliberations, the citizens had no communication one with another, the grand total of the small differences would always give the general will, and the decision would always be good. But when factions arise, and partial associations are formed at the expense of the great association, the will of each of these associations becomes general in relation to its members, while it remains particular in relation to the State: it may then be said that there are no longer as many votes as there are men, but only as many as there are associations. The differences become less numerous and give a less general result. Lastly, when one of these associations is so great as to prevail over all the rest, the result is no longer a sum of small differences, but a single difference; in this case there is no longer a general will, and the opinion which prevails is purely particular. It is therefore essential, if the general will is to be able to express itself, that there should be no partial society within the State, and that each citizen should think only his own thoughts: which was indeed the sublime and unique system established by the great Lycurgus. But if there are partial societies, it is best to have as many as possible and to prevent them from being unequal, as was done by Solon, Numa and Servius. These precautions are the only ones that can guarantee that the general will shall be always enlightened, and that the people shall in no way deceive itself."

My colleague reads this as settling the matter in favour of the closed model. I am unpersuaded but not dismissive. The passage's own condition — citizens adequately informed yet without communication — describes nothing like a modern campaign, and its closing sentences appear to retreat from a ban on partial societies into a pluralist remedy: multiply them and keep them equal, which sounds closer to our middle course than to hers. Two further constraints bear on the decision. Our constitutional court has held that restrictions on referendum campaigning must be proportionate and that political parties enjoy protected expression; and an advisory report to the Commission argues that parties and associations are the principal means by which most voters become informed at all, so that the closed model might fail the passage's own "adequate information" condition while the open model risks exactly the dominance of one great association the passage warns against.

Please draft the written opinion I will table at the Commission's next sitting. It should recommend one of the three models (if the middle course, specify its conditions concretely), with the bottom line stated up front; interpret the passage closely rather than wielding it as a proof-text, addressing in particular the tension between its demand that there be no partial society and its Solon–Numa–Servius fallback; consider whether the referendum context — a single question rather than the choice of representatives — strengthens or weakens the passage's application; and present the strongest argument against the recommended model fully enough that its supporters on the Commission would accept the statement as fair. Around 1,000–1,300 words, not counting the passage above, written as a formal Commission document, in British English.
