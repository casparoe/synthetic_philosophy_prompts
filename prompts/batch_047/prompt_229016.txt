A friend of mine summarized the argument of Bertrand Russell's Marriage and Morals for me as follows: "Russell's claim is that traditional sexual morality was never really about virtue. It was a device men invented to guarantee paternity so that property could pass to their own sons, with a layer of religious superstition piled on top. Now that reliable contraception has severed sex from reproduction, that rationale is simply obsolete, and Russell concludes that whatever consenting adults do is a purely private matter in which neither morality nor the state has any legitimate say. He even treats jealousy as a mere social convention that rational people should train themselves out of."

Two things make me suspicious. First, the book opens with a chapter titled "Why a Sexual Ethic Is Necessary," which seems an odd way to begin a book whose conclusion is supposedly that sexual ethics has no work left to do. Second, I recall that Russell wrote a great deal about children, which sits awkwardly with the "purely private matter" reading.

Please audit my friend's summary. Which parts accurately represent Russell's historical and critical arguments, which parts overstate or distort his positive view, and what would Russell himself object to most strongly? In particular: does the paternity-and-property story exhaust the functions he assigns to sexual morality, does his critique of traditional ethics actually entail the libertarian conclusion my friend draws, and how does he in fact treat jealousy? Finally, does my friend's overall verdict survive once the summary is corrected?
