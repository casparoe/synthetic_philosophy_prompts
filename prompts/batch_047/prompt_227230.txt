I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the years since leaving the laboratory working through moral philosophy, and I have lately been stuck on Nozick's utility monster: the creature who derives so much more pleasure from each unit of resource than the rest of us that total utilitarianism appears to recommend handing it everything. The standard replies I have encountered — that diminishing marginal utility makes such a creature empirically impossible, that we should simply override the intuition and accept the conclusion, or that the theory needs deontological side-constraints — strike me as answering the case as stipulated without identifying which feature of the stipulation does the damage. When a thought experiment yields a scandalous result, my instinct from the laboratory is to vary the parameters and see which variations preserve the scandal. I have therefore devised five variants of the monster, and I would like you to rank them from most to least damaging as objections to total utilitarianism, with a short paragraph of justification for each placement. Note that I am asking which monster is most damaging to the theory, not which is most repugnant; my reading group has blurred this distinction repeatedly, so please keep it sharp. For each variant, address explicitly whether the three standard replies mentioned above still work against it, and whether the variant exposes a weakness the original monster leaves hidden.

The variants:

(1) The Threshold Monster. Below a vast level of consumption, this creature converts resources into welfare less efficiently than any ordinary person; beyond that threshold, its marginal utility rises without limit. The theory seems to recommend starving everyone until the monster is wealthy — and then starving them harder.

(2) The Insatiable Monster. Everyone else's marginal utility eventually diminishes toward zero; this creature's never diminishes at all. At every finite stage the recommended allocation looks defensible, but in the limit everything flows to the monster.

(3) The Sadistic Monster. It derives enormous utility specifically from witnessing the suffering of others — say, ten units of pleasure per unit of pain observed. Here the bullet-biting reply appears to require defending the deliberate feeding of sadistic preferences.

(4) The Relay Monster. Its utility is a fixed multiple of the aggregate welfare of everyone else: it is happy exactly in proportion to how happy others are. Its existence seems, if anything, to amplify the value of helping ordinary people — but I suspect a subtler problem about whether the total remains well-defined once one member's welfare is a function of the aggregate to which it contributes.

(5) The Mimic Monster. Its inner life may be monstrous or may be exactly like ours, but no behavioral or physiological observation can distinguish the two cases. As a scientist, I note that interpersonal utility comparisons are not operationally measurable in any case, and I wonder whether the monster problem is one the theory could never be in a position to answer, even in principle.

Please conclude with your ranking stated in fixed, machine-readable form on the final line, using the labels and ordered from greatest to least threat, for example: \answer{Threshold > Insatiable > Sadistic > Relay > Mimic}
