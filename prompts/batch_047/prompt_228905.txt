I'm a novelist, and here's what kicked this off: when I write a first-person scene where a character is panicking, my own chest gets tight. I seem to panic a little, on demand. A friend who works on consciousness says higher-order thought theory explains this perfectly: a mental state is conscious just when you have a suitable higher-order thought representing yourself as being in it. Then she walked me through this argument and now I'm stuck, because it seems to prove the theory makes experience way too cheap.

(1) A state is conscious iff the subject has a suitable (non-inferential, assertoric) higher-order thought representing herself as being in that state.
(2) The theory's defenders insist the target state doesn't have to actually exist or be as represented -- that's the whole point of how they handle misrepresentation and so-called targetless higher-order thoughts.
(3) So the conscious experience is purely a matter of having the thought; the first-order state is optional.
(4) I can get myself to think "I am seeing crimson" -- I do this kind of thing for a living, dropping into characters' heads by an act of will.
(5) So I can give myself a conscious experiance of crimson in a pitch-dark room, or panic with nothing to panic about, or the taste of coffee without coffee.
(6) But nobody can conjure experiences like that just by deciding to. If I could, my job would be much stranger than it is.
(7) So higher-order theory is false.

I'd really like to keep (1), since it's the nicest explanation I've heard of the novelist thing, and (6) feels obviously true. So which step actually breaks, why did it look fine, and what does it cost me to break it there rather than somewhere else?
