I run a little philosophy-of-mind podcast and next month I'm interviewing a philosopher who defends what she calls phenomenalism about pain's badness: roughly, that what makes pain bad is how it feels, full stop, not the fact that we dislike it or want it gone. So on her view the aversive reaction is downstream evidence of the badness, not the thing the badness consists in.

The obvious battleground is pain asymbolia. Patients with this (usually after certain brain lesions or cingulotomy) still say they feel pain, can locate it and describe its intensity, but report that it no longer bothers them and they don't move to avoid it. She takes the standard line that their pain is still bad for them even though they don't mind it. I want to really press her on that, not let her do the usual two-minute version.

Draft me eight to ten interview questions. I'd like them structured in three blocks, in this order:

1. "Setting up the cases" (about 3 questions, maybe 150 words total): establish the asymbolia profile plus the morphine case, where people say the pain feels the same but they no longer care about it. Get her to commit on record to exactly what's bad in each case.
2. "The squeeze" (4-5 questions, around 250-300 words): this is the heart of it. Include at least one question on how she can tell the badness isn't in the aversion given that the two never come apart in normal experience -- what non-question-begging evidence does she have? One on whether asymbolia patients are making a prudential error by not avoiding pain, and if so, what they're failing to respond to. And one where I raise the worry that her view implies a subject could be stuck in agony, sincerely not minding at all, and it would still be just as bad for them -- does she bite that bullet or does she have a limiting move?
3. "What would change your mind" (2 questions, ~100 words): one asking what a case would have to look like to count against phenomenalism, and one on whether the debate is even empirical or whether both sides are just digging in on intuitions.

For each question, add a one-to-two sentence follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give -- e.g. if she says asymbolia patients are just failing to appreciate their own badness, the follow-up should ask what could possibly distinguish that from the aversion theory saying the patients are fine. Total around 600-800 words. No softballs and nothing about how she got into philosophy, I can handle the chit-chat myself.
