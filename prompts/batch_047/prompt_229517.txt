My wife and I are trying to decide whether to have a child, and we got into an argument that I keep losing on paper while remaining unconvinced. Her reasoning: when we tally up the prospective child's life, we should count all the suffering it would likely endure — illness, heartbreak, grief, eventual death — as a real cost. But we shouldn't count its prospective happiness as a comparable benefit, because if we never conceive, there is no one who is deprived of that happiness; nobody loses out. Nonexistence, she says, isn't a tragedy the way misery is. So the ledger can only ever come out neutral or negative, and creating a person is at best a wash.

I tried countering that the same logic would make it obligatory to *prevent* any birth, no matter how wonderful the projected life, which seems like a reductio. She replied that the asymmetry is just a brute fact about how harm and benefit work for merely possible people, and that my discomfort is sentimentality, not argument. I've read enough decision theory and population ethics to suspect this isn't a new move, but I can't locate the flaw — if there is one. Is her ledger sound? If not, where exactly does it break? And if it is sound, what follows for someone who still wants kids for partly selfish reasons?
