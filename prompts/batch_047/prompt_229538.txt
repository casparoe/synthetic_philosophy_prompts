So here's where I've landed after teaching Burke, Oakeshott, and the rationalism debate in my political theory seminar for years: I now hold a pretty strong version of the Chesterton's fence principle. Not the weak methodological one (figure out what a rule does before scrapping it — who disagrees with that?). The strong one: if nobody can currently give an adequate functional account of a long-standing institution, that fact alone shifts the burden of proof decisively against the reformers, and purely consequentialist cost-benefit cases for removal are never sufficient on their own, because the institution likely embodies tacit, distributed knowledge that won't show up in any analysis the reformers could run.

Two wrinkles I've built in that I think make the position interesting. First, I take it to survive what I call the exaptation problem: even if an institution arose for terrible reasons, it may have acquired functions since, so "we know its origin was bad" doesn't count as understanding it. Second, I deny there's any neutral way to measure an institution's costs while it's still in place, since it shapes the preferences and baselines you'd use to do the measuring in the first place.

What would count against this? I'm not interested in the lazy rejoinders (slavery was long-standing! — every defender has a canned answer about how the principle is defeasible and only applies to fence-shaped things, whatever exactly those are). I want to know: what observations, cases, or arguments would actually move a committed fence-theorist, as opposed to ones they'd just absorb? Are there abolitions where the reformers openly admitted they didn't understand the practice, scrapped it anyway, and it went fine — and would my view be committed to calling those mistakes even in hindsight? What would have to be true about tacit knowledge for my second wrinkle to collapse — and is the "you can't measure it from outside" claim falsifiable at all, or have I insulated the whole thing? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about whether I've built a position that risks anything.
