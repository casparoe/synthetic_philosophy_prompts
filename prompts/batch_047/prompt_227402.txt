I recently sat in on a graduate philosophy of mind seminar where we spent three weeks on the frame problem, and I came away with the unsettling feeling that the discussion was not really a discussion. To summarize the history as I understand it: the problem surfaced in the late 1960s when McCarthy and Hayes noticed that a classical planning system has no cheap way to represent what does not change when an action occurs, and it ballooned into the question of how any agent, biological or artificial, keeps track of the few relevant consequences of an action among a combinatorial explosion of irrelevant ones. Dennett sharpened this with his parable of a robot that pulls its spare battery out of a room without realizing the bomb sitting on the same wagon comes along for the ride, and philosophers spent the following decades debating whether the problem is one of inference cost, one of relevance, one of what counts as a proposition at all. Then, in my reading, the debate simply petered out -- the engineers stopped writing frame axioms because probabilistic and learning-based systems sidestepped the explicit-representation setup entirely, and the philosophers mostly moved on without declaring the question answered, dissolved, or shown to be ill-posed.

In the seminar this produced a strange standoff. One camp, mostly the AI-adjacent students, held that the frame problem was never a philosophical problem at all -- it was an engineering bottleneck that dissolved once we abandoned logic-based architectures, and modern language models, which seem to track relevance and unstated background conditions effortlessly without any frame axioms, are the empirical refutation of the worry. The other camp insisted that nothing has been resolved: LLMs exhibit relevance-sensitivity without our having any account of how, and the original problem -- how a finite agent limits what it must consider when acting -- is as open as ever, merely relocated from explicit representation to whatever mechanism lets a network generalize appropriately. Neither side moved in three weeks, and both seemed to regard the other as having missed the point entirely rather than as being wrong in some checkable way.

Here is my hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the two camps are disagreeing about what the problem was a problem of -- one side reads it as a claim about a particular class of architectures, the other as a claim about any physically realized agent whatsoever, and the debate stalled because nobody stated which reading they were defending. But I would like a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is this disagreement verbal (the same phrase, frame problem, naming a prediction problem for logicians and a relevance problem for philosophers), substantive but hidden (an empirical bet about whether relevance-sensitivity can emerge without explicit machinery, which LLMs are now settling without anyone having said so), or the kind of bedrock difference -- about whether exhibiting a competence counts as explaining it -- that neither side can argue its way out of? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a sharper statement of what the original problem actually asked, a specific experiment on whether LLMs genuinely solve the relevance problem or merely ape its surface, or an argument that the question was ill-posed from the start and the forty years of literature should be written off?
