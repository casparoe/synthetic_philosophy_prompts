I teach philosophy of physics at a small liberal arts college, and each spring in my philosophy of time seminar we spend two weeks on Gödel's rotating cosmological models and the argument he built from them against the objective reality of temporal becoming. One of my graduate students, preparing her qualifying paper, has reconstructed the argument in a form she claims is airtight, and I have to admit I cannot find a clean place to stop it -- which bothers me, because the conclusion strikes me as one the argument has no right to reach. Here is her version. (1) Time is objectively real in a universe only if there is an objective lapse of time in that universe: a succession of global "nows" that partitions all events, everywhere, into an objective past, present, and future. (2) In any universe whose geometry contains closed timelike curves -- Gödel's rotating solutions being the standard case -- no such global ordering can be defined: any candidate time coordinate either fails to increase along every future-directed timelike curve or returns to an event it has already passed, so no objective lapse exists there. (3) Those rotating models are not mere mathematical curiosities: they satisfy the very same field equations our own universe does, and hence are physically possible worlds governed by our laws, differing from ours only in the contingent global distribution and rotation of matter. (4) Whether time itself is real or ideal cannot turn on contingent facts of that sort; it is fixed by the laws of nature, so any two worlds sharing the same laws must agree on whether time objectively lapses. (5) Our universe shares its laws with the Gödel models. (6) Therefore time is ideal in our universe as well, and the passage of time is a feature of our perspective, not of the world. She notes, correctly I think, that nothing in the argument depends on whether our actual universe rotates, so the usual observational objections are beside the point.

My worry is that the argument proves too much, or at least proves its conclusion too cheaply. Each premise has a plausible motivation -- (1) reflects a venerable conception of real becoming, (2) looks like a geometrical fact, (3) and (5) look like textbook physics, and (4) has the feel of a principled supervenience claim -- yet I resist the idea that the reality of time in the actual world can be settled by the existence of merely possible worlds, however law-abiding. My own inclination, which you should feel free to overrule, is that (2) and (3) are the hardest premises to surrender and that the trouble, if there is trouble, lies among (1), (4), and the transition to (6). But I want a committed verdict rather than a menu. Identify the single step or premise you would reject, explain why it looked innocent -- including what genuine insight it encodes about the rotating models -- and state plainly what it costs to reject it, measured against the costs of rejecting each of the alternatives you considered. If you conclude that the argument is in fact sound, say so and tell me what I must give up to accommodate that.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- the exact premise or step you reject, stated in one sentence -- and then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restating of the argument or of my question, and no survey of options without a verdict: I am asking for a diagnosis I could defend in front of the student, not an annotated bibliography of positions.
