Here is the homework question as I set it:

A gambler faces an unlimited sequence of independent even-money bets, winning each with probability p = 0.55. (a) State the Kelly criterion and derive the fraction of current wealth it dictates wagering on each round. (b) Explain why maximizing the expected logarithm of wealth, rather than expected wealth itself, is the appropriate objective for long-run repeated play, addressing the fact that betting one's entire stake every round maximizes expected wealth. (c) A fellow gambler proposes wagering half the Kelly fraction in order to reduce volatility. Assess this proposal. (Total: 10 points, weighted 3/4/3.)

Here is the student's answer, verbatim:

(a) Let f be the fraction of wealth wagered. After one round, wealth is multiplied by (1 + f) with probability p and by (1 − f) with probability q = 1 − p. The expected logarithm of the wealth multiplier is g(f) = p ln(1 + f) + q ln(1 − f). Setting the derivative to zero gives p/(1 + f) = q/(1 − f), so f* = p − q = 2p − 1 = 0.10. The gambler should wager ten percent of current wealth each round.

(b) Wealth compounds multiplicatively: W_n = W_0 · X_1 · X_2 · ... · X_n, where each X_i is the round's wealth multiplier. Taking logarithms converts the product into a sum, so by the strong law of large numbers the average growth rate (1/n) ln(W_n / W_0) converges almost surely to E[ln X]. The strategy maximizing E[ln X] therefore almost surely achieves the highest exponential growth rate over the long run. Betting everything maximizes expected wealth per round but makes ruin practically certain, since the probability of surviving every round tends to zero. This shows that the Kelly criterion is simply expected utility maximization with a logarithmic utility function: because the logarithm is concave, Kelly bettors are risk-averse, and it is this risk aversion that justifies refusing to bet everything despite the higher expected value of doing so.

(c) Half-Kelly is a sensible compromise. Halving the stake halves the size of the swings in wealth from round to round, and correspondingly sacrifices half of the long-run growth rate. The gambler is therefore buying a proportional reduction in risk with a proportional reduction in growth, which may be a fair trade for someone with low tolerance for drawdowns.
