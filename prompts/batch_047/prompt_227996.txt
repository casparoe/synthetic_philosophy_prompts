I cover science and ideas for a mid-size magazine, and every year around the holidays we run a reader essay contest where people take a crack at some famously weird argument in science or philosophy. This year's topic was the doomsday argument, and the prompt we gave readers was: "Explain the doomsday argument as clearly as you can. What does it conclude, what assumption does the heavy lifting, and what's the strongest objection to it -- and does that objection succeed?" We've narrowed it down to a finalist, and part of the deal is that I send the winner detailed feedback on their entry, which we may excerpt in the column. Problem is, I'm a journalist, not a philosopher, and while the essay *feels* right to me I have a nagging suspicion some of it is subtly off and I can't put my finger on where. Could you grade it for me? I'd like a grade (out of 10 is fine) plus point-by-point comments I could adapt for the feedback letter -- specific things it gets right, specific things it gets wrong or muddles, not just a general vibe.

Here's the finalist's entry:

"The doomsday argument, associated with the philosopher John Leslie and the physicist Brandon Carter, tries to figure out how long humanity will last using nothing but our own birth position. Roughly 100 billion humans have lived so far. Now suppose humanity will eventually number a trillion people. Then you and I, born around position 100 billion, would fall in the first 10% of all humans ever, which would make us oddly early. But if humanity only ever totals, say, 200 billion, we're right near the middle, which is exactly what you'd expect. It's like drawing a ball labeled 60 from a lottery urn of unknown size and concluding the urn probably didn't hold a million balls. So the argument should shift us toward thinking humanity's total run will be small -- doom sooner rather than later.

The assumption doing the work is the self-indication assumption: we should reason as if we were randomly sampled from the set of all possible humans, which is why finding ourselves unusually early would be a strange coincidence that needs explaining away.

Taken literally, the argument's prediction is that there is a 95% chance humanity will end within roughly the next 9,000 years, which is the forecast it makes about our future.

The strongest objection, in my view, is the reference class problem. The argument only works if 'human' is the right class to be sampling from, but why not count earlier hominins, or posthuman descendants, or every intelligent observer in the universe? Change the class and you change the conclusion. I think this objection succeeds completely: since there's no principled way to choose the reference class, the argument tells us nothing whatsoever about when we'll go extinct."

For what it's worth, the two runner-up entries were definately worse than this one, so I'm inclined to score it highly -- but I'd rather you grade it on the merits than take my word for it.
