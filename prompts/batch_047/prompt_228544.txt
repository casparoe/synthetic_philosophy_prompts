I work as a software engineer at a company that, like most of the industry, runs blameless postmortems. After any serious outage, the team produces a write-up tracing the causal chain -- the deploy, the missing test, the alert nobody saw -- and the standing norm is that the engineer whose change triggered the failure is never faulted personally. The standard justification is that anyone in the same position, facing the same system, could have made the same mistake. Yet the same company runs code reviews where people bluntly criticize each other's work, fires engineers for repeated negligence, and during the postmortem itself participants freely say things like "I should have caught that." I'm trying to understand what this cluster of practices actually presupposes about blame and responsibility. Does blamelessness rest on the idea that blame requires a kind of standing that none of us possesses, since every one of us has shipped the change that took down production? Does it instead presuppose that failures are caused by systems rather than individuals -- and is that even coherent, given that every write-up ultimately cites specific human actions and decisions? Or is the norm purely instrumental (blame makes people hide mistakes, so we ban it) and therefore compatible with any theory of blame whatsoever? Please reconstruct the implicit theory or theories, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits well with, and describe what would have to change if we instead adopted the view that blame can be appropriate even when it comes from the equally guilty. Please write your answer in American English.
