Next month I will present a paper at a moral philosophy colloquium defending a version of strong longtermism, and I want a rigorous prep sheet for the question period. My thesis: in a wide range of the most consequential decisions currently available to us, the dominant determinant of an option's value is its expected effect on the long-run future. My paper departs from the standard presentation in two ways. First, I do not rest the case on maximizing expected value across vast populations; I argue instead that longtermism follows from a much weaker claim — that we can predictably alter the probability and timing of certain irreversible events (extinction, permanent lock-in of bad institutions) even when we cannot predict the content of the far future at all. Call this the trajectory-versus-content strategy. Second, I concede that we cannot steer the far future toward any specific vision, and build my case entirely on avoiding irreversible losses rather than pursuing distant goods.

The audience will include: a population ethicist who is a committed totalist and will probe whether my weakened argument still delivers the strong conclusion; a contractualist who rejects aggregation across persons and suspects the whole framing smuggles in consequentialism; a philosopher of science who works on deep uncertainty and will press complex cluelessness — the worry that my interventions are as likely to backfire as to help; and a political philosopher who suspects longtermism functions to rationalize deprioritizing present-day suffering. There will also be graduate students asking broad questions.

Give me the objections in roughly the order and frequency I should expect them, phrased as each of these people would actually phrase them. For each: how damaging it is to my specific argument (not to longtermism generally), what I should concede outright to maintain credibility, what I should hold, and a reply calibrated to about ninety seconds. Identify the weakest joint in the trajectory-versus-content strategy that I should prepare to defend actively rather than hope goes unnoticed, and tell me which question I am secretly hoping no one asks.
