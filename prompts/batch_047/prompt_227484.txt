I've been keeping a running list of my own verdicts on markets that make people uneasy, and I want to know what principle, if any, is doing the work behind them. Here are the cases and my verdicts, stated as honestly as I can.

1. A regulated market in kidneys from living donors, with the state as the sole purchaser, mandatory screening, and a fixed price well above a year's median income: permissible, and probably better than the status quo of people dying on the waiting list.
2. A hardware store tripling the price of generators the day before a hurricane makes landfall: permissible, and arguably good, since the alternative is the first five customers buying all forty units.
3. A binding, enforceable market in votes for public elections, where votes are bought and sold like shares: impermissible, and it should be criminal even between fully informed, consenting adults.
4. Paying citizens a substantial cash sum to receive a vaccine during a pandemic: permissible.
5. A charity offering cash to women with drug addictions on condition that they accept long-term contraception or sterilization: impermissible, and I feel this strongly, even though the structure -- a cash incentive aimed at poor people to do something beneficial for third parties -- looks identical to case 4.
6. A university openly auctioning ten percent of each entering class to the highest bidders, with full transparency: impermissible. But legacy admissions, which function as an opaque auction with a much lower reserve price, strike me as merely distasteful. I notice I can't defend this asymmetry.
7. An investor visa program in which a country effectively sells permanent residency to anyone who invests a large fixed sum: permissible, if unseemly. An open auction of the same residency slots to the highest bidder, which allocates to almost exactly the same people at almost exactly the same prices: somehow much worse. Again, I can't articulate why.
8. Prediction markets on election outcomes: permissible and epistemically valuable. Prediction markets on terrorist attacks or assassinations, which would aggregate dispersed information exactly the same way and might even help prevent the events: impermissible.
9. During the American Civil War, conscripted men could pay a commutation fee or hire a substitute, and this was widely considered a scandal. Today a volunteer army recruits the poor with signing bonuses and hazard pay, and I consider that arrangement broadly acceptable, even though the final allocation -- the wealthy stay home, the poor fight -- is nearly the same.
10. A medieval-style arrangement in which you can pay a fee to a moral authority and be licensed to continue doing the thing you agree is wrong: obviously corrupt when it's the Church selling indulgences. And yet I think carbon offsets are permissible, and I buy them myself.

What I want: a principle -- or a small number of jointly necessary conditions -- that sorts these cases the way I do. I know the standard menu of objections (coercion under desperation, corruption of a good's meaning, blocked exchanges between spheres, objectionable inequality between transacting parties), so don't just recite the menu; I want to know which combination, stated precisely, actually reproduces my verdicts, and what it must say about the pairs I've flagged as indefensible. If the price of saving my verdicts in cases 5 through 10 is a principle so gerrymandered that it has no independent plausibility, say so, and tell me which verdicts I should surrender and in what order.

Finally, apply whatever principle you land on to two cases I haven't listed: first, a donor-priority scheme in which registering as an organ donor moves you up the transplant queue should you ever need an organ, creating a kind of futures market in one's own organs; and second, a shareholder democracy in which corporate votes, unlike political votes, are freely bought and sold along with the shares. I want to know whether the principle treats these as analogous to any of my ten cases, and whether its verdict on them makes me more or less confident in the principle itself.
