You are a moral philosopher with research expertise in the ethics of blame -- particularly the recent literature on standing to blame, hypocrisy, complicity, and meddling -- seconded as a consultant to a professional body. Please think carefully and begin by brainstorming the distinctions from that literature that a usable procedure would need to preserve, before drafting anything.

Here is the situation. I chair the conduct committee of a national professional association (roughly 4,000 licensed members). Any member may file a complaint against any other member for breaches of our code -- overbilling, misrepresenting qualifications, poaching clients through deception, and so on. Historically the committee has only ever assessed the accused's conduct. But in the past two years we have had complaints from members who were themselves guilty of the same practice and never made amends, complaints from participants in the very scheme being reported (usually after a falling-out among the participants), and complaints about matters in distant chapters that the complainant has no discernible connection to. Handling these has become ad hoc and politically fraught. The board wants a standing screen: a procedure our intake officer (not a philosopher, roughly one hour per complaint before it goes to a hearing panel) can apply to decide whether a complainant's own conduct or relation to the matter disqualifies them from bringing this complaint, before anyone touches the merits.

Please write that procedure. Requirements:

1. First, your brainstorm: the distinctions the screen must keep (e.g., forfeited standing versus standing never possessed; hypocrisy versus complicity versus mere third-party meddling; whether the fault must be unacknowledged and unremedied to undermine standing; whether lack of standing affects the truth of the complaint or only the propriety of this person pressing it) and which subtleties can be safely dropped for a non-specialist operator.
2. The procedure itself: an ordered sequence of questions with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, explicit stopping rules where the screen returns "standing lacks" or "standing intact," and the points -- mark them clearly -- where judgment cannot be eliminated and the intake officer must escalate to a person rather than a rule.
3. A rule for what the office does when standing lacks: can a true and serious complaint be blocked, and if so, what is done with the information?
4. Three worked applications: (a) a member who reports a rival's inflated billing while having billed the same way for years, unrepented; (b) a member who took part in the complained-of bid-rigging and now reports the ringleader after being cut out; (c) a member who reports questionable conduct in a chapter three states away that does not affect their practice. Arrange it so that at least one worked case yields a conclusion the committee will find genuinely uncomfortable, and say why the procedure requires it anyway.
5. A closing section on the procedure's known failure modes: the kinds of cases where it will misfire, and a note on how to defend its decisions to complainants who are told they lack standing.
