I’m a software engineer with no philosophy training, and I’ve talked myself into a fairly strong reading of Bernard Williams’s integrity objection: I think his cases show that utilitarianism fails as a complete account of morality, not merely that it can be inconvenient or psychologically demanding. The problem, as I understand it, is that utilitarianism treats an agent’s deepest projects and attachments as if they were just interchangeable sources of utility. It can therefore tell someone to think of their own commitments as accidental causal levers, and the one thought too many point is that even deliberating in the impartial way it recommends can already estrange someone from the people and projects that make their life theirs.

What would count against that reading? Please don’t just give me the usual utilitarian reply that doing the right thing can feel horrible. I want a proper map of the objection’s exposure, roughly sorted from fatal to merely awkward. In particular:

- Which interpretations of integrity would make my conclusion too strong? For example, does the argument need integrity to be morally valuable in itself, or only to be something a good moral theory must leave room for?
- Which possible cases should worry me? I’m interested in ones where refusing to alienate someone from a project looks plainly monstrous, and ones where partiality seems inseparable from love, friendship or responsibility.
- How much turns on distinguishing a moral criterion from a decision procedure? If utilitarianism says we should cultivate loyal dispositions because they produce the best outcomes, does that answer Williams or just relocate the problem to the level of motivation?
- What would show that Williams is relying on an overly romantic or self-indulgent idea of the self? Could a person’s projects matter without being immune from impartial assessment?
- Does the objection generalise too far? If Kantian, contractualist, virtue-ethical or commonsense views also sometimes require people to act against their deepest commitments, does that weaken it?
- Is there a principled line between compromising a project and being forced to regard it as merely contingent or replaceable? That distinction feels central, but I’m not sure it survives scrutiny.
- What would defenders of Williams themselves have to concede as damaging, as opposed to what utilitarian critics would say? And are there empirical facts about moral psychology, institutions or prediction that would genuinely bear on the dispute rather than just decorate it?

I’d especially like to know whether the strongest version proves something narrower than I think: perhaps that utilitarianism cannot be both true and consciously used as an all-purpose deliberative algorithm, without proving that its criterion of rightness is false. If that is the most it can establish, explain exactly why, and whether that would still be a serious result. My current instinct is that the alienation from one’s own reasons is the crux, but I’m defintiely open to being moved.
