A friend and I spent last evening arguing about a stretch of Pascal's wager that tends to get skipped over: the end of the famous fragment in the Pensées, where the interlocutor objects that he simply cannot believe, and Pascal replies not with more proofs but with a regimen -- live as the believers live, take up their practices, and belief will follow. My friend reads this as Pascal quietly abandoning the expected-utility argument and substituting a piece of self-manipulation; I read it as the argument's completion, since a rational agent who sees the wager's force but cannot believe on command should rationally use the indirect means available to him. We could not agree on which open question this passage actually turns on. So here is what I would like from you. Suppose a small philosophy reading group plans to spend a year working through the wager as a live problem rather than a historical artifact. Which open questions should they take up, and in what order? Candidates might include whether the passage marks one argument or two, whether indirectly engineering one's own beliefs is epistemically permissible, the many-gods objection, and the problem of infinite utility in finite decision theory -- but do not feel bound by my list. For each question you select, explain why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is for non-specialists, and how settling it would bear on the disagreement between my friend and me. Defend the ordering explicitly: I want your criteria and your ranking, not a survey.
