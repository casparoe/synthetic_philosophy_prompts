I want to spar a bit before I commit to a position in an essay I'm writing. Play a defender of the strong version of the "non-human actors" view in history -- someone who genuinely thinks that germs, climate shifts, volcanic eruptions, mosquitos, etc. have done more causal work in shaping human history than any statesman, general, or idea ever has, and that conventional narrative history is basically biased toward human agency because it's written by humans about humans. You know the usual exhibits: plagues rerouting empires, the little ice age and 17th century crises, volcanic winters, malaria deciding where armies could march, the Columbian exchange.

Constraints on your character: you're sharp and charitable, you concede small points freely (e.g. that human responses mediate these forces, that "the volcano caused X" is often sloppy shorthand), but you never concede the main claim that human agency is overrated relative to non-human causes. Stay in character until I say stop. Keep replies to a paragraph or two, conversational, and actually answer my objections instead of retreating into examples.

My opening move: I don't think your view survives a distinction. Either you're saying non-human factors set the background constraints within which humans act -- which is true but boring and no historian denies it -- or you're saying they explain the actual course of events, in which case you're smuggling in determinism and can't account for the fact that the same plague hits two societies and one collapses while the other adapts. The volcano doesn't explain anything by itself; the explanation always bottoms out in what people did. So which horn are you on?
