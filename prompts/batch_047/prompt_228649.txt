EXAMINATION QUESTION

State Aumann's agreement theorem precisely, identifying the assumptions it requires. Then explain why the theorem requires common knowledge of the agents' posterior beliefs, rather than the weaker condition that each agent knows the other's posterior. Finally, comment on what the theorem does and does not establish about persistent disagreement among rational people.

STUDENT'S ANSWER

Aumann's agreement theorem says that if two agents share a common prior probability distribution over the states of the world, and if each agent's posterior probability for some event E is common knowledge between them, then the two posteriors must be equal. This is often summarized by saying that rational agents with a common prior cannot agree to disagree.

The theorem requires two assumptions. First, the agents must have the same prior; if they start from different priors, nothing in the theorem forces their beliefs to coincide. Second, the posteriors must be common knowledge: each agent must know the other's posterior, and each must know that the other knows theirs.

The common knowledge condition is needed because it is what allows each agent to treat the other's stated posterior as fully informative about that agent's private evidence. Once Alice knows Bob's posterior and Bob knows Alice's, each has all the information needed to compute what the other has learned, so any remaining difference in their estimates would have to be arbitrary. Finite levels of mutual knowledge are enough in practice, since after a few rounds of "I know that you know" the agents have effectively absorbed each other's evidence.

The mechanism behind the result is that the agents repeatedly announce their current estimates to one another and update on each announcement; because the announcements are truthful and the prior is shared, this process must converge, and at convergence the estimates coincide. Aumann's insight was to show that this convergence is guaranteed whenever the agents are Bayesians with a common prior.

As for real disagreement, the theorem shows that genuinely persistent disagreement between rational people is impossible when both are honest about their beliefs, so long as they share a prior and communicate their estimates. Where disagreement persists, at least one of these conditions must fail.
