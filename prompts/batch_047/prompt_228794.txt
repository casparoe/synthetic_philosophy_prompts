I recently sat in on a reading-group session on early modern philosophy where two graduate students, whom I'll call Dana and Marcus, got into an extended disagreement about Berkeley's master argument -- the passage where Berkeley challenges his reader to conceive of something, say a tree in a forest, existing unconceived, and then claims the challenge cannot be met, since in attempting it you conceive of the tree after all. Neither of them would concede, and I found myself unable to settle it, so I'd like you to adjudicate their exchange move by move. Their positions, as best as I can reconstruct them, ran as follows.

Dana opened by calling the argument a transparent fallacy of equivocation. Yes, it is trivially impossible to conceive of a tree without thereby conceiving of it -- but that shows only something about the act of conceiving, not about the tree. The tree that figures in my thought is conceived, obviously, but the tree whose mind-independence was in question was never my thought in the first place. She offered an analogy: you cannot photograph an unphotographed landscape either, since photographing it makes it photographed, but nobody concludes that landscapes depend on cameras. Marcus replied that this objection only works if ideas are treated as mere representations pointing at something beyond themselves -- and that representational gap between the idea of a tree and the real tree is precisely what Berkeley denies exists. On his reading, when Dana says the tree whose independence is in question was never my thought, she is simply reasserting that there is mind-independent matter behind the idea, which is the very thesis under dispute. So her charge of equivocation begs the question rather than exposing a fallacy.

Dana then shifted ground: if Marcus's reading is right, she said, then the master argument is not an argument for idealism at all -- it does real work only for someone who has already accepted that everything we encounter consists of ideas, which means it either commits the fallacy or presupposes the conclusion it is supposed to establish. At best it is a memorable illustration of a premise Berkeley needed to establish elsewhere, and calling it a master argument is false advertising. Marcus's final move was to concede that the argument is not a freestanding proof, but to insist it is nonetheless a genuine dialectical achievement: it confronts the materialist with the fact that any content they try to give to the notion of a mind-independent material substance is drawn entirely from experience, so that the notion collapses back into ideas whenever they attempt to make it determinate. The argument, he said, is a consistency challenge internal to the materialist's own position, and consistency challenges can be decisive without being proofs. Dana said this downgrades the argument so severely that Marcus had essentially agreed with her while refusing to say so.

Please adjudicate the exchange point by point: for each of the four moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or somewhere in between, and justify your assessment. In particular, I want to know whether Dana's photograph analogy succeeds, whether Marcus's charge that she begs the question is fair, whether Dana's dilemma -- fallacy or circularity -- holds up against his final construal of the argument as an internal consistency challenge, and what, if anything, both of them are overlooking. I'd also like a verdict on who is more right overall.
