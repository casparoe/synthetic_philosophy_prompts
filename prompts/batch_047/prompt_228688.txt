I run a reading group for final-year PPE students and next term we're doing Keynes's essay Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren (1930). I want to give them a "which position on growth and the good life do you actually hold?" quiz before the session, so they arrive having committed to something rather than nodding along. Here's the passage I want the whole quiz to hang off:

"My purpose in this essay, however, is not to examine the present or the near future, but to disembarrass myself of short views and take wings into the future. What can we reasonably expect the level of our economic life to be a hundred years hence? What are the economic possibilities for our grandchildren?"

(That passage doesn't count towards the length of anything you write.)

Note what Keynes is up to in it: he's telling his readers, slap in the middle of the Depression, to bracket the short run and think about a horizon on which the economic problem might be solved altogether. The rest of the essay, which my students will have read, argues that within a century or so compound accumulation could free us to work a few hours a week and get on with living well, and that in the meantime motives like avarice are to be tolerated as useful rather than admired. The quiz should sort students by how they react to that whole package, not by whether they can summarise it.

Specs:

- Eight questions, each a concrete case or hypothetical the student has to take a side on: a rich country deciding whether to keep targeting GDP growth once everyone's comfortable, a talented graduate choosing between the lucrative grind and a modest life with time in it, a society that's automated away half its jobs, a government weighing redistribution now against investment for people not yet born, that sort of thing. Two or three answer options per question, written in plain first-person language, no position labels and no economist jargon.
- A scoring key mapping answer patterns onto seven leaves: Keynes's own position (growth as a means with an expiry date, then leisure and the arts of life); the growth-optimist revaluation (striving and accumulation aren't a grim means, they're where the good life lives); the degrowth or steady-state view (there's no daylight at the end of the tunnel, stop now); the egalitarian objection (you can't disembarrass yourself of short views while some lack necessities — redistribute first); the longtermist extension (the horizon is far longer than a century, keep accumulating for the sake of the far future); the fulfilling-work position (work is where meaning lives, so distribute it rather than abolish it); and the character objection (a society that practises avarice instrumentally for a hundred years will find it has stuck — the strategy defeats itself).
- Every question must genuinely discriminate: at least two leaves should come apart on each one, and no question should be answerable by spotting which option "sounds like Keynes".
- If you think the logical space contains a coherent position I've missed, add it as an extra leaf and justify it; if you think two of my seven collapse into each other, say that instead of forcing the key to seperate them artificially.
- Flag any pair of leaves the quiz can't reliably tell apart, with one line on why.
- End with one sentence per leaf naming its most awkward commitment.

Make sure the questions probe these fault lines in particular: whether accumulation is a means with an expiry date or an end in itself; whether the "we" who'd enjoy the leisure is the same "we" doing the accumulating; whether instrumental greed can be switched off on schedule; and whether the hundred-year horizon is too short, too long, or about right. Roughly 1,200–1,500 words for the whole deliverable, and keep the scoring key tight — the cleverness should live in the questions, not the key. British English throughout please.
