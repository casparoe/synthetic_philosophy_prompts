So a friend of mine who does famine history walked me into this over beers last month and I've definately been chewing on it since.

Background that I don't think is really in dispute: Sen's entitlement work showed famines often happen with food around. Bengal 1943 had rice; rural wages collapsed against the price while wartime policy did little. Ireland exported food through the famine. The Great Leap had state grain seizures while people starved near full granaries. Fine.

His argument:
(1) A famine death is a political crime when the death runs through human decisions that foreseeably channeled food away from the starving, rather than through a shortfall nobody could have fixed.
(2) In Bengal the shortfall was fixable: there was food in India, the panic and inflation were substantially policy-driven, and London chose other priorities. Similar for the Great Leap and Ireland.
(3) So Bengal was a political crime, not a natural disaster.
(4) But the test in (1) has no built-in threshold. Any preventable death that runs through someone's foreseeable decision qualifies: the person who dies because the government funded a highway instead of a hospital ward, or set the speed limit at 70 rather than 50, also dies through decisions that foreseeably cost lives.
(5) So by (1), essentially every preventable death under any government is a political crime, and "natural disaster" is an empty category — even a flood victim drowns through someone's decision not to build a higher levee.
(6) (5) is absurd. So (1) is wrong, and the whole famine-as-crime framing collapses.

I feel like the slip is somewhere in (4)–(5), like "foreseeably channeled food away from the starving" is doing more work in (1) than it can bear once you generalize it, but I can't say exactly where it breaks. If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (3) than accept (5): I want Bengal and the Great Leap to come out as crimes without every traffic death being one. So: which step do I reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular — does whatever fix saves the famine/traffic distinction still convict Churchill's government, or does saving the distinction end up acquitting him too, which my friend would hate?

One request: flag where you're stating something genuinely settled among people who know this stuff (the entitlement analysis is basically mainstream by now, as I understand it) versus where you're picking a side in a live fight (whether London could realistically have shipped enough grain in '43, whether "crime" requires intent or just foreseeability, how much of the Great Leap toll was policy vs. weather). Don't dress a contested reading up as the consensus.
