I'm the producer and occasional host of a small podcast run out of our university's history and politics departments, and we've booked a guest I'm both excited and nervous about: a historian of nationalism who defends an uncompromising modernist position. His view, roughly, is that nations as we understand them are creations of the last two or three centuries — mass-produced by print capitalism, standardized state schooling, conscription, censuses, and deliberately engineered symbols and ceremonies — and that claims to ancient national continuity are retrospective mythology. He works in the tradition associated with Hobsbawm's account of invented traditions and Anderson's account of nations as imagined communities, and in a previous interview he brushed off critics by saying that antiquity is always asserted, never demonstrated, which I found rhetorically effective but evasive. Our listeners include graduate students who will have read the standard ethnosymbolist and perennialist criticisms, so a soft conversation would be wasted.

Draft a sequence of eight to ten interview questions, ordered so the pressure builds, and for each question include a follow-up keyed to the reply he is most likely to give. I want the set to cover at least the following ground: the medieval and early-modern evidence that people seem to have understood themselves in recognizably national terms long before industrial modernity (with a follow-up for when he replies that such cases involved elites, not masses); the ethnosymbolist objection that modern nations are built out of pre-existing ethnic myths, memories, and symbols rather than conjured from nothing (with a follow-up for when he concedes the raw material but denies it was a nation); the puzzle of why invented traditions take root at all — if national identities are fabricated by states and intellectuals, why do ordinary people die for them rather than experience them as fabrications; Anderson's own question of why nations are imagined as limited and sovereign, rather than as universal communities on the model of Christendom or the umma; and whether his framework actually distinguishes between nations and nationalism, or quietly defines the former by the latter and so wins by stipulation.

Two further requests. First, somewhere in the middle of the sequence, include a question built around a hard case of his own choosing — ask him to name the nation whose antiquity he finds hardest to explain away, and then press on whether his treatment of that case uses standards of evidence he'd reject from a primordialist. Second, end the sequence with a question about what evidence or historical finding could count against the modernist thesis at all: if no possible discovery about, say, twelfth-century political communities would move him, I want listeners to hear him say so explicitly. Please no generic warm-up questions about how he came to the field — we handle that in the introduction.
