I have no training in philosophy, but I've gotten interested in the fact that sometimes saying something doesn't just describe the world, it changes it -- when I say "I promise," I haven't reported on a promise, I've made one. I've been collecting my own gut verdicts on when the saying actually does the thing, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, lies behind them. Here are the cases:

1. I tell my neighbor "I promise I'll water your plants while you're away," while privately intending not to bother. My verdict: I really did promise. The insincerity makes the promise a worse thing to have done, but it doesn't erase it -- I owe her the watering, and she can hold it against me.

2. Alone in my kitchen, I say out loud "I promise to exercise more." My verdict: nothing happened. There's no promise at all here, not even a bad one.

3. During a heated argument with my boss, I shout "Fine, I quit!" and storm out. The next morning I insist I didn't mean it. My verdict: too bad -- I quit. Regretting it doesn't un-say it.

4. At a baseball game, a fan in the stands screams "He's out!" at a close play. My verdict: obviously nothing happened; the runner is whatever the umpire says he is.

5. I say "I apologize" in a flat, sarcastic voice because I've been ordered to apologize. My verdict: no apology happened. The words came out, but the thing itself wasn't done, and it would be strange for the other person to say "well, at least he apologized."

6. I tell my dog "I promise I'll be back soon" as I leave for work. My verdict: this is just something I say for my own comfort; no promise was made. But note that here my heart is entirely in it -- I fully intend to come back.

7. At a wedding, both people say "I do" and the officiant pronounces them married, but unknown to everyone, the officiant's authorization lapsed a month earlier. My verdict: I think they're married anyway, though this is the one I'm least sure of.

What I can't figure out is whether one principle gets all of these. Cases 1 and 5 seem to pull against each other: in the promise, sincerity doesn't matter; in the apology, it's the whole game. Cases 2 and 6 suggest the listener matters, but then why does intending matter in 6 and not in 1? Case 4 suggests some utterances need the right job title, but 7 suggests maybe the job title matters less than I thought.

So: is there a principle -- or a small set of them -- that captures these verdicts, stated precisely enough that I can apply it to new cases? If some of my verdicts have to go, tell me which ones and why, rather than contorting the theory to save them all. And two follow-ups: first, what does the best principle say about the case I keep going back and forth on -- telling someone who wronged me "I forgive you" while still feeling plenty of resentment? Did I forgive them? Second, a friend claims the rule is simply "the saying counts if the other person reasonably takes it to count." Does that rule handle my cases, and if not, where does it break?
