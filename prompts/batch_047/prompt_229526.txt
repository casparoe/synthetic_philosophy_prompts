In two weeks I'm leading a one-hour session of a reading group of software engineers — no philosophy background, but comfortable with formal logic — on epistemicism versus supervaluationism as rival theories of vagueness. Plan the explanation rather than delivering it. Specify: (1) the order in which the components must be introduced — the sorites paradox, the tolerance intuition that one grain or one hair cannot make the difference, the epistemicist's sharp but unknowable cutoff, and the supervaluationist's apparatus of precisifications and supertruth — and why each step depends on the one before it, so that both theories emerge as motivated responses to a real contradiction rather than as arbitrary technical stipulations; (2) the single carrying example to run the whole session on, and what properties it must have and lack so that neither theory looks obviously absurd when applied to it; (3) the misconceptions this particular audience will predictably bring — that vagueness is just measurement imprecision, that fuzzy logic with degrees of truth dissolves the problem, that the epistemicist is claiming we could locate the cutoff with better instruments, and that supervaluationism is merely three-valued logic — and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than allowed to harden; (4) the check for understanding that would show the distinction between the two theories has landed, not just the vocabulary; and (5) what I should openly decline to cover at this level (for instance, higher-order vagueness) and how to defer it without appearing to hand-wave. At most 600 words.
