I'm preparing a two-page proposal for a one-year graduate fellowship in political philosophy, and I want the project built around a distinction I think the precommitment literature underuses. The standard picture — from Ulysses binding himself to constitutional entrenchment — assumes the later self is compromised: tempted, weak-willed, short-sighted, or captured by the passions of the moment. But there is a harder class of cases where the later self is *epistemically better off* than the earlier one: it has information the earlier self lacked, and its divergence from the earlier plan is rational updating rather than failure. My half-formed idea is that justifying precommitment against a better-informed future self requires a different argument than justifying it against a weaker one — possibly something about the earlier self's privileged position on values as opposed to facts, or about which kinds of learning the earlier self can legitimately discount. Turn this into a proposal: a precise statement of the question, an explanation of why existing justifications of precommitment (individual and constitutional) leave this case unaddressed, a month-by-month method, an account of what possible conclusions the project could reach and what would decide between them, and what would count as the project failing. Scope it realistically for one year. Don't include citations; I'll add those myself.
