Take the run on Silicon Valley Bank in March 2023 and analyze it as an information cascade. The basic facts as I understand them: SVB announced it had sold a big chunk of its bond portfolio at a loss of about $1.8 billion and planned to raise new capital to plug the hole; within roughly a day, depositors -- lots of them startups whose VCs were telling them over group chats and Twitter to get their money out -- tried to pull tens of billions of dollars, and regulators shut the bank down the next morning. So first do the careful mapping: what private signals did different depositors plausibly have, what was publicly observable to whom, and at what point does it become rational for someone with a decent private signal (say, a CFO who thinks the bank is probably fine) to ignore it and run anyway becuase of what everyone else is visibly doing? Then tell me where the cascade model breaks. Bank runs are usually modeled as coordination games with multiple equilibria rather than cascades, and SVB had genuine balance-sheet problems, not just a confidence spiral -- so is the cascade framing actually earning its keep here, or is it just a redescription of a coordination failure in fancier vocabulary? Also, cascades are supposed to be fragile, reversible by a small amount of credible public information. Was there anything anyone could realistically have said on the Thursday that would have stopped the run, and should the government's weekend move to backstop all deposits count as a cascade being reversed, or as the game being changed entirely? Wrap up by telling me how confident you are in each part of the analysis, and single out the one claim in your answer you're least sure about.
