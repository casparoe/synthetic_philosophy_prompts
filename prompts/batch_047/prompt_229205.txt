I'm a philosophy PhD student applying for a one-semester departmental grant (about 5,000 euros, no fieldwork budget), and I need a research proposal of roughly two pages. The project sits at the intersection of philosophy of mind and machine ethics, and the motivating worry is this: our debates about which systems might be sentient -- organoids, insects, language models, simple robots -- almost always stall on the same hidden question, namely which physical processes actually count as *implementing* a morally relevant computation, as opposed to merely being describable as one under some clever mapping. A stone, on some views in the literature, implements any finite-state machine you like if you choose the mapping freely enough; yet everyone writing about organoid ethics or AI welfare quietly assumes we know how to draw the line. My half-formed idea is that the debate is stuck because people treat "implementation" as a binary verdict about a system at an instant, whereas the morally relevant notion might be about which counterfactuals the system's causal organization supports -- a system whose internal states would respond appropriately to counterfactual inputs implements its computation robustly, and a giant lookup table or a video of a brain does not. I suspect this distinction does real work: it may separate systems that are behaviorally indistinguishable but differ in whether anything it is like is going on inside, and it may explain intuitions about whether "replaying" a recorded experience is a new experience.

Please turn this into a proper proposal. It should contain: (1) a precisely stated research question, sharpened so that it is actually answerable within one semester rather than "solve the hard problem"; (2) a statement of the gap -- why the existing families of answers (purely behavioral criteria, causal-role functionalism without further constraints, and the "it's all interpretation anyway" deflationary response) each leave the question open or push it somewhere unhelpful, stated without citations since I will add those myself; (3) the concrete method and a realistic month-by-month plan, including what I would actually do: which test cases I would construct (e.g., lookup-table agents, systems whose computations are spatially scattered or intermittently active, systems that implement the same function at different grain sizes), what argumentative work each case is meant to do, and what the written output of each month looks like; (4) why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives, such as doing an empirical survey of what ordinary people count as sentient, or just stipulating a criterion and defending it by intuition; and (5) a frank section on what would count as the project failing -- for instance, if the counterfactual criterion turns out to be circular, to collapse into behaviorism, or to let in stones after all -- and what salvageable result a failure would still leave me with. Keep it at two pages, write it in first person as a grant proposal, and don't pad it with praise of the field's importance; the committee has read that sentence a thousand times.
