I'm on the board of a small family foundation (about a $40m endowment). Our founder, who died two years ago, made our then-chair -- her son -- promise on her deathbed that the foundation would never fund political advocacy, only direct service work. Importantly, she could have written this into the trust deed and didn't; she said she trusted him and wanted the foundation to stay flexible. He's since retired. Now our program staff have found what looks like an unusally effective opportunity in exactly the banned area (some campaign finance reform work where our money could plausibly do more good than anything else we fund), and the board is split. Half says a promise is a promise, and the dead can be wronged. The other half says she's dead -- she has no expectations left to disappoint and no plans to frustrate, the trust deed is what governs, and the fact that she deliberately declined to amend the deed shows she wanted future boards to be able to revisit this.

I have to draft the decision memo for next month's meeting. Give me a clear recommendation -- fund it or don't -- but the memo needs to actually take a stand on why promises bind at all, because the board keeps talking past each other on precisely this point. Is it that promises create expectations and reliance, that breaking one disrespects the promisee's will, or that the whole practice of promising only works if promises stick? Each answer seems to give a different verdict in this case, so work through which account should govern here and what follows from it for us. The strongest argument against your recommendation should get a genuine hearing too, since I'll be circulating this to people who will vote the other way. Around 800 words.
