A modern-history survey text I encountered describes the Fischer controversy this way:

“Fritz Fischer demonstrated that Germany’s leaders consciously decided in 1914 to begin a European war in order to make Germany a world power. The crucial evidence was a December 1912 council at which the Kaiser and his advisers settled on war but chose to wait until the army was fully prepared. During the July Crisis, Berlin therefore ordered Austria-Hungary to crush Serbia, issued the ‘blank cheque,’ and treated Russian mobilization merely as a pretext. Austria-Hungary was effectively Germany’s instrument, while Russia, France, and Britain did little more than react. Fischer thereby demolished the older view that Europe’s governments had all stumbled unintentionally into war, and his work explains why the Versailles Treaty formally assigned sole legal and moral guilt for the war to Germany.”

The same text later says Fischer’s work “permanently settled the question of war guilt” and shows that “the German nation, rather than merely its governing elite, bore collective culpability.”

Please audit this characterization sentence by sentence. Identify what Fischer plausibly established, what is a reasonable simplification of his stronger claims, and what is misleading or false. In particular, distinguish among deliberately choosing a local war, accepting a substantial risk of a continental war, and pursuing a settled plan for one; explain the disputed significance of the December 1912 meeting; assess whether Fischer denied meaningful responsibility to Austria-Hungary and the other powers; and clarify what the Versailles Treaty actually did or did not establish about legal, historical, and moral responsibility. Finally, say whether the survey’s later conclusions—that Fischer permanently ended the debate and that responsibility attaches collectively to the German nation—follow from a corrected account of the controversy.
