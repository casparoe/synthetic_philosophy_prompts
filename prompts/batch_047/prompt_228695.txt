A colleague of mine has been pressing the following argument on me, and although I find its conclusion hard to accept, I cannot identify a step I am prepared to reject. I would like a diagnosis: which premise or inference should I give up, why did it look acceptable in the first place, and what does abandoning it commit me to?

First, some background. The standard case for a rapid intelligence explosion, or "fast takeoff," runs roughly as follows: (A) the rate at which a system improves its own cognitive capabilities is a function of its current cognitive capabilities; (B) an AI's capabilities are implemented in software, which can be copied, modified, and rerun at will, so an AI can iterate directly on the thing doing the thinking; (C) therefore, once an AI system becomes competent enough to contribute meaningfully to AI research, each improvement will immediately accelerate the next, and the improvement process will compound into a runaway.

My colleague's counterargument is that this reasoning proves too much. His steps, as faithfully as I can reproduce them:

1. If the standard argument is sound, it must rest on some feature that AI possesses and human researchers lack; otherwise the identical reasoning would apply to human research, with whatever conclusion follows there.
2. Recursion is not such a feature. Human civilization already improves its own instruments of inquiry, and that improvement is carried out by means of those very instruments: better mathematics enables better physics, which yields better scientific instruments and computers, which in turn accelerate mathematics and empirical research. The cognitive work of upgrading our intellectual toolkit is performed by that toolkit. This is exactly the self-referential structure invoked in step (C).
3. Copyability and parallelism are not such features either. Human science already operates in parallel across millions of researchers, and an insight, once produced, is reproduced at near-zero cost through publication and education. Whatever advantage software has here is one of degree, not kind, and a difference of degree cannot carry a qualitative conclusion like runaway takeoff.
4. Hence, if (A)–(C) were sound, human collective intelligence should already have undergone a runaway takeoff, at the latest since the scientific revolution.
5. But nothing of the sort occurred. Individual human cognitive ability has not measurably increased over this period, and by many measures research productivity has grown only linearly, or has even declined per researcher, over the past century.
6. Therefore at least one of (A)–(C) is false, and the standard case for a fast AI takeoff collapses.

The steps I am least willing to surrender are (2) and (5): the recursive structure of human science seems to me undeniable, and the historical record seems plainly to lack any runaway event. I also note that (1) has the form of a perfectly ordinary demand that an argument's conclusion be traceable to its premises. Yet the overall conclusion, that the entire fast-takeoff case rests on an error, still feels like a swindle. Where does the argument go wrong — or, if it is valid, which premise is cheapest to abandon, and what do I lose by abandoning it?
