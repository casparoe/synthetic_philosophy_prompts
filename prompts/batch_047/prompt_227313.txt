I'm a novelist, about two-thirds through a draft of a book about a logic professor who slowly stops being able to infer things -- she accepts premises, nods along, but the step from premises to conclusion just wont happen for her anymore, and everyone around her keeps handing her more premises as if that could fix it. The seed of it was that old Lewis Carroll dialogue with the tortoise and Achilles, which I read years ago and which honestly ruined deductive reasoning for me a little bit. Anyway, working through her character has pushed me into an actual philosophical position, which I now hold with some confidence: a rule of inference like modus ponens can't be justified by any argument, because any argument for it would itself have to use it (or use something equally in need of justification), so the only honest stance is that inferring is just something we *do* -- a primitive practice, bedrock, not the kind of thing that could ever get or need a proof. Adding the rule as an extra premise is a category error, regress is unavoidable, end of story.

What I want from you is: what would count against this position? Not the Carroll regress itself restated -- I obviously already accept that, it's my premise -- but what would have to be true for my quietist, it's-just-what-we-do conclusion to be wrong. Some candidate defeaters I've been chewing on while writing, which I'd like you to assess rather than just echo back: (a) the possibility that some formal treatment -- showing the rule is in harmony with the meaning of the connectives, or something in that neighborhood -- actually does justify it in a non-circular way, so that my character's illness is a real loss of something she once *had* and not just a quirk; (b) the fact that there are apparently competent reasoners who reject certain classically valid inferences (relevance logicians, intuitionists), which maybe suggests inference rules are more like contestable claims than like bedrock practices, since bedrock presumably wouldn't be up for dispute; (c) ordinary cases where explicitly teaching someone the rule as a premise genuinely seems to change their behavior, which the tortoise story says shouldn't help but, empirically, sometimes seems to. Are these real threats? Which are fatal, which merely awkward, and which would a committed primitivist just shrug at -- and is the shrugging legitimate or a tell?

Also, be honest with me about the meta-question, because it matters for the book: has my position been insulated against refutation by its very shape -- anything that looks like a counterargument can be absorbed as just another premise the tortoise refuses to act on? If a view like this can't lose, should that bother me, or is unfalsifiability less of a sin for a view about the foundations of reasoning than it is for, say, an empirical theory? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, plus what a defender of my view would themselves concede counts against it, since that's different from what critics throw at it. My own bias, for what it's worth: I'm most persuadable by cases, not formalism -- if you can describe a situation where the primitivist picture clearly misdescribes what's going on, that will move me more than any technical result.
