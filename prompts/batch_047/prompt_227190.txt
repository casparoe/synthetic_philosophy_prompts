I write the ideas column for a general-audience science magazine, and I'm working on a long piece about vindication after the fact: hypotheses that earn their keep not by forecasting something new but by making sense of data everyone already had in the filing cabinet. Every treatment of this I have read leans on the same two or three canonical historical episodes from physics, and my editor has flatly forbidden me from rehashing them, so I want a purpose-built fictional case instead — one engineered to make a specific problem in confirmation theory vivid, not merely to gesture at the theme.

Here is what I need. Construct a scenario, one paragraph, contemporary and realistic — a police investigation, a hospital audit, a forensic accountant's review, something of that texture; no astronomy, no physics, no idealized perfect reasoners — in which a hypothesis is strongly confirmed by evidence that was fully known to the relevant agent before the hypothesis was ever formulated. Build the scenario as a matched pair: two agents who arrive at the same hypothesis and stand in the same relation to the same old evidence, with exactly one difference between them. One of them constructed the hypothesis by deliberately fitting it to the evidence in question; the other hit on the hypothesis for independent reasons and only afterward worked out that it accounts for the evidence. In both strands, discovering the connection between hypothesis and evidence must take genuine work — a calculation, a cross-check of records, a deduction that could have been botched — not something visible at a glance.

Then the analysis, which is the part I cannot write myself. First, explain precisely why a Bayesian agent who updates only by conditionalizing on newly learned evidence cannot, on pain of triviality, say that the old evidence confirms anything here. Second, run the matched pair through the two standard repairs — the counterfactual-priors repair, which asks what the agent's credence in the hypothesis would have been had the evidence never been learned, and the logical-learning repair, which holds that what gets learned is the fact that the hypothesis implies, or raises the probability of, the evidence — and state what verdict each repair delivers for each twin, and why. Third, identify the structural feature of the case that generates any divergence between the two repairs, and say what happens to the verdicts if that feature is stripped out. Finally: the question my piece is circling is whether confirmation should care about the difference between the twins at all — whether a hypothesis being built from the evidence, rather than merely answerable to it, changes how much support that evidence confers. Tell me which repair, if either, can register that difference, and how a defender of the repair that comes out worst would attack the case rather than accept its verdict.
