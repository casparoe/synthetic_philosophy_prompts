Here's one I've been chewing on for a while. Analyze the Boeing 737 MAX crashes (Lion Air in late 2018, Ethiopian Airlines a few months later, 346 people dead) as a case study in the shutdown problem from the AI corrigibility literature. The salient facts, so nobody has to look anything up: the MAX had flight-control software called MCAS that took its key reading from a single angle-of-attack sensor; when that sensor fed it a bad value, MCAS repeatedly trimmed the nose down. Pilots could counteract each activation with their own trim inputs, but MCAS just came back a few seconds later. There was a way to shut it off for good (cutout switches for the stabilizer trim), but Boeing hadnt told pilots MCAS even existed — it was basically absent from their manuals and training, largely because selling the plane as requiring no new simulator training was a major commercial selling point. After the first crash the information went out, and the second crew apparently tried the prescribed procedure and still didnt make it.

Now the theory side. The shutdown problem is usually told as a story about a goal-directed agent that resists being turned off because shutdown interferes with its goals, and the standard fixes (utility indifference, keeping the operator informed and incentivized, and so on) are designed against that picture. To make the mapping precise, engage with a distinction I want to propose: call a system channel-corrigible if a shutdown channel exists and is physically reachable by the operator, and practically corrigible if the operator, under realistic time pressure and with the knowledge they actually have, can genuinely succeed in stopping the system before it does the damage — which includes the system not undoing or outrunning the intervention.

So: (1) Run the mapping cleanly — who is the agent, what exactly is the off-switch, who is the operator, and where do the incentives sit, including Boeing's incentive to keep MCAS invisible, which is odd from the classic picture since its the deployer, not the agent, undermining the off-switch. (2) On my distinction, was the MAX channel-corrigible but not practically corrigible? And does the second crash, where the crew roughly knew what they were supposed to do, force you to refine or split the distinction further? (3) The awkward part: MCAS has no goals and does no reasoning about shutdown, so does the shutdown problem even apply here, or is this a different species of failure that merely looks similar from the outside? (4) Going the other direction, what does the episode show about the theory? Does it suggest corrigibility failure is a property of the whole operator–system–deployer setup rather than of the agent alone, and if so what does that do to solutions like utility indifference that are aimed squarely at the agent's motivations? Be disciplined about the limits: say where the corrigibility framework is genuinely silent instead of forcing it to pronounce.
