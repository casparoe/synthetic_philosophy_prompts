I serve on the clinical ethics committee of a large hospital in a jurisdiction where voluntary euthanasia has just been legalized for competent adults and, under strict conditions, via advance directive. Until now the committee could only ever advise on withholding and withdrawing treatment and on palliative care, so our track record was built entirely within that constraint, and I want to understand what it actually commits us to before our first case under the new law arrives. Our published verdicts over the past decade: we approved discontinuing dialysis at the request of a competent, terminally ill patient who understood she would die within weeks; we approved escalating morphine doses for a dying patient in refractory pain, explicitly acknowledging the dose might hasten death; we declined to support a competent terminally ill patient's request for a lethal prescription to end his life immediately, referring him to palliative care instead; we approved withdrawing artificial nutrition from a patient with advanced dementia who was no longer competent, on the strength of an advance directive written years earlier, even though her family reported that she seemed content in her current state; and we declined to support a request for help in dying from a patient with severe, treatment-resistant depression who had been assessed as competent, citing among other things the possibility that her evaluation of her future was colored by her illness.

The first case under the new law: a woman of 68, diagnosed with early Alzheimer's three years ago, who while still fully competent wrote a detailed, witnessed advance directive stating that if she progressed to the point of no longer recognizing her children, she wished to receive euthanasia rather than live on in that condition. She has now reached exactly that stage. She no longer recognizes anyone, is no longer competent by any assessment we would apply, and by every report is mildly content: she enjoys music, takes walks, eats with appetite, and shows no signs of distress. Her directive says to proceed. Her two children disagree with each other about whether it should be honored.

Predict the committee's verdict, and show your work. I want the candidate rules our five precedents are jointly consistent with laid out explicitly -- an act/omission rule on which we permit letting die and palliation but never killing, a precedent-autonomy rule on which the competent earlier self's directive governs the incompetent later self, a current-interests rule on which the contented patient before us is the one whose welfare counts, and anything else that fits the record equally well -- along with what each rule predicts for this case and which precedent is the closest analog, since our dementia directive case matches this one on decision-source but not on means, and our refused lethal-prescription case matches on means but not on decision-source. If you conclude the precedents genuinely underdetermine the verdict, say so, and tell me what principle we would be adopting with each choice. Finally, tell me what would change your prediction: which additional verdict from our past, or which hypothetical case put to us, would most efficiently discriminate between the rules you have identified, and where your reconstruction of our implicit reasoning is most likely to be wrong.
