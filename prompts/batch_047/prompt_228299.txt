I'm a PhD student in computational neuroscience and somehow ended up as the grad student rep on our institute's model systems oversight committee (the body that usually just rubber-stamps organoid and brain-slice work). We've got a case that's out of our depth and I've been volun-told to draft the decision memo before Thursday.

The situation: one of the labs has built a closed-loop simulation of the fruit fly larva's nervous system — it's the one animal brain that's been fully mapped, just a few thousand neurons — wired up to a small robot body. The sim runs at about 1000x real time. Now they want to do a parameter sweep: roughly 10,000 parallel instances with slightly different neuromodulator settings, to find the configuration that best solves a foraging task. The part that made our organoid people nervous: each instance gets a synthetic damage signal whenever its robot collides with something, because that signal is exactly what drives the avoidance learning the lab cares about. When a committee member asked whether the instances "feel" anything, the PI said, roughly, that it's just linear algebra and you might as well ask whether a weather simulation gets wet.

Draft the memo. It has to recommend one of three things: approve the sweep as proposed, approve with conditions (say which, precisely), or reject pending further criteria. There was alot of back-and-forth in the meeting and the memo really has to take a stand on two things rather than just minuting the disagreement:

1. The copy question. If one running instance has any moral weight at all — even a small probability that there's something it's like to be that system — do 10,000 near-identical copies carry 10,000 times the weight? One of the junior faculty says obviously yes, that's just expected value. The PI says the copies are one computation the way two printings of the same novel are one novel, and you don't build a library by photocopying. Both can't be right and the recommendation changes depending on which way you go.

2. The damage signal. Is there a principled line between a damage channel that shapes avoidance behavior in a connectome-grounded sim and whatever does that job in the actual larva? And does the answer move if the sim were a generic recurrent net trained on the same task instead of being built from the mapped circuit — i.e., does the circuit's provenance do moral work, or only its functional organization?

Also: steelman the PI's "it's just linear algebra" line before answering it — he'll be in the room and he'll (fairly) shred the memo if his position shows up as a strawman. And any conditions you impose have to be things the lab could actually implement or concretely argue against, not vibes like "monitor for distress."

Format-wise: open with the recommendation itself in the first sentence, then the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate the case back to me, I lived it.
