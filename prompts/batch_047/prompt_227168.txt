I'm taking a course on the philosophy of mathematics this semester, and last week our unit on intuitionism left me with a worry I can't shake. My understanding is that Brouwer and his followers rejected the law of excluded middle on the grounds that it had been shown to be false — that is, that for at least some mathematical propositions P, they proved that neither P nor its negation holds. This seems to me to have devastating consequences. Classical mathematicians use the law of excluded middle constantly: proof by contradiction, case analysis, the whole apparatus. If the law has been refuted, then every classical proof that relies on it is not merely unfounded but rests on a principle known to fail, and classical mathematics is, strictly speaking, unsound — full of theorems that aren't true. Yet classical mathematicians apparently carry on as before, and even constructivists seem to treat classical results as a kind of respectable mathematics rather than as a tower of falsehoods. A friend in the course suggested that intuitionists "just have a different logic," but that can't be the whole story: if one side proves a principle and the other side disproves it, at least one of them is wrong, not merely different.

So my question is: given that intuitionists disproved the law of excluded middle, why doesn't anyone treat classical mathematics as refuted, and why do constructivists still bother engaging with classical results like the intermediate value theorem rather than simply denouncing them?
