I'm a novelist, usually realistic stuff, but I've gotten stuck on a weird metaphysical chapter and I want to try it as a standalone short story first. Write me that story.

The hook is from Leibniz's Monadology, the bit around sections 53-55 where he says the possible worlds all exist as ideas in God's understanding and in some sense push toward existence, each in proportion to how much perfection it contains, and the one that actually gets to exist is the best -- where "best" isn't "nicest," it's more like the maximum of richness and variety governed by the simplest possible laws. Don't quote him, just run with the idea.

So: the story is the audition. Possible worlds are, I dunno, petitioners, or drafts of manuscripts, pleading their case. What I'm after: a world that loses. It should be a genuinely good world -- maybe gentler than ours, fewer catastrophes -- and it loses anyway, and the story should make the reader feel both that the verdict is somehow defensible on Leibniz's criteria and that something has gone badly wrong. Bonus if you dramatize compossibility: the losing world can't exist not because it's bad but because some feature of it doesn't fit together with the maximal whole, like two perfect characters whose arcs ruin each other.

The thing I can't figure out as a writer: what does a non-existent world want? Does it know what it's missing? Please don't resolve that with a tidy moral -- I'd rather the ending stay uncomfortable. Around 1500 words, no Candide-style satire, play it straight.
