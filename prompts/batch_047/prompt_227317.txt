In the ethics seminar I’m TAing, we’re comparing the usual broad-brush picture of European countries as prohibitionist about surrogacy with the more commercial American market. One student keeps arguing that the moral issue is simple: surrogacy is “renting a womb,” and egg donation is “selling a body part,” so paid arrangements turn women’s bodies into property and children into products. The analogy is doing real work for them — they think it explains why bans are justified and why American-style contracts are objectionable even when everyone consenting is an adult.

Audit the analogy rather than just accepting or rejecting it. Give the correspondence table: what is supposed to be the property, the owner, the tenant or buyer, the rental payment or purchase price, and the thing ultimately transferred? Then separate gestational surrogacy from egg donation and point out where the same metaphor fits the two cases differently. I’m especially interested in disanalogies involving bodily labor versus detached tissue, payment for time/risk versus payment for the child or gamete itself, the possibility of ongoing relationships, and whether regulation could protect people without pretending the arrangement is morally identical to leasing an apartment. Which objections survive once the sloppy property language is cleaned up, and could a better analogy — organ donation, adoption, sex work, military service, professional caregiving, or something else — do the argumentative work more honestly?
