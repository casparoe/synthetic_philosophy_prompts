I'm a high school junior, and I keep getting into the same argument with my friends: is doing something bad worse than merely letting something bad happen? I thought I'd settle it by writing down my gut verdicts on cases, but they don't line up under any rule I can state. Here they are, roughly from most obviously fine to most obviously awful:

1. I could donate the $40 I earned tutoring to a charity that would use it to prevent a child's death from malaria, and instead I spend it on concert tickets. My verdict: not great, but not wrong. I don't owe a stranger my savings.
2. A classmate is drowning in the shallow end at a pool party and I'm standing right there with a towel and working arms. I keep eating my chips. My verdict: monstrous, basically as bad as if I'd pushed her in.
3. My grandmother is terminally ill and in pain, and the doctors ask the family whether to stop the treatment that is keeping her alive. We say stop. My verdict: sad but permissible.
4. Same grandmother, but instead a doctor gives her an injection intended to end her life at her request. My verdict: this feels worse than case 3, meaningfully so, even though the outcome and her wishes are identical.
5. I notice a teammate cheating on a group project and don't tell the teacher. My verdict: a bit cowardly, but not the same as cheating myself.
6. I notice someone has put peanuts in a sandwich and my allergic lab partner is about to eat it. I say nothing because I don't like her. My verdict: that's not "doing nothing," that's murder by silence.
7. I decline to give my friend the answers to a test he's failing, knowing he'll probably be held back. My verdict: completely fine, maybe even virtuous.
8. I don't warn my friend that the person she's dating cheated on my cousin, though I know it for a fact. My verdict: genuinely torn — it feels like an omission but also like a betrayal of her.

My tentative principle: it is permissible to let harm occur when preventing it would cost you something significant or when the harmed person has no special claim on you, and impermissible otherwise. But 2 already strains it (a towel is not a significant cost, so it comes out impermissible, good), while 1 seems to satisfy it and yet people I respect call case 1 a serious moral failure, and 6 has the same structure as 2 but feels closer to active killing than 3 or 4 do, and I can't say why.

So: is there a principle that captures most of these verdicts — especially the killing-vs-letting-die difference between 3 and 4, and why 6 feels like killing while 5 doesn't? Please state it precisely, flag which of my verdicts it has to overturn, and tell me what it implies about the cases I keep going back and forth on: 1 and 8. If no single principle works without becoming a list of exceptions, I'd rather hear that honestly than get a tidy rule that secretly changes the subject.
