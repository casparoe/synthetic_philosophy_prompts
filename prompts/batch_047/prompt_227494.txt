I am a retired molecular biologist who, in my post-career reading, has lately taken an interest in philosophy. I also sit on my former university's research integrity panel, and we are currently seized of a matter that I find philosophically vexing. I would like you to draft a formal memorandum to the panel recommending how we should decide it.

The facts are these. Two researchers, in unrelated laboratories and unknown to one another, committed what our investigation has found to be materially identical violations of our sample-storage monitoring protocol: each disabled the temperature alarm on an ultra-low-temperature freezer over a holiday period in order to silence nuisance alerts, contrary to explicit written policy. In the first case, the lapse was discovered by a routine audit before any failure occurred, and no samples were lost. In the second, the freezer in fact failed during the lapse, and an irreplaceable collection of clinical samples — the substrate of a doctoral student's nearly completed dissertation — was destroyed. Nothing in the record suggests any difference in culpability, intent, or carelessness between the two researchers; the difference in outcome is, so far as anyone can tell, entirely a matter of which freezer happened to fail.

Our standing regulations direct the panel to calibrate sanctions to the severity of the outcome, which would mean a substantially heavier penalty in the second case. The panel's chair has proposed that we instead sanction the two identically, on the grounds that identical misconduct merits identical response. The university's compliance office opposes this, noting that funders, insurers, and the affected student all look to outcomes, not merely to conduct. I have been asked to circulate a memo before our next meeting.

Please write that memo. It should contain: (i) a clear, actionable recommendation on whether outcome should enter into the sanction, and if so how; (ii) the reasoning supporting it, written so that a panel of scientists and administrators — not philosophers — can follow and apply it to future cases; and (iii) a serious, ungrudging statement of the strongest argument for the position you reject. In your reasoning, please engage directly with the philosophical problem the case raises: whether it can be just for two agents who behaved identically to be treated differently solely because of how things happened to turn out. In particular, I would like the memo to address whether the distinction between what an agent is blameworthy for and what an institution must hold an agent answerable for does any real work here, or whether it merely papers over the difficulty. If you conclude the two should be sanctioned differently, please specify what principle governs the size of the difference, since the panel will have to defend the rule, not just the result, in future cases.
