A friend of mine reckons he's dissolved supererogation entirely, and I hate his argument but can't find the break in it. It goes: (1) If one of the acts open to you is morally better than every other available act, you ought to do it — that's just what "ought" picks out, the top option on the menu. (2) A supererogatory act is meant to be one that's morally better than anything duty demands while still not being demanded: the passerby who runs into the burning building does something better than staying back, yet staying back isn't wrong. (3) But by (1), the passerby ought to run in. (4) So "supererogatory" acts are really just obligatory acts we're too squeamish to demand of each other, and supererogation doesn't exist. (5) And notice (1) doesn't care that the example was heroic: apply it to any choice whatsoever and only the best available act is ever permissible. (6) So there's no moral latitude at all — me watching telly tonight instead of volunteering isn't a permissible-but-suboptimal choice, it's just wrong, full stop. I refuse to swallow (6), and I'd really like to keep the idea that saints and heroes go beyond duty rather than merely doing their jobs, but each premise looks perfectly tame on its own. Which step should I reject, why did it look so harmless, and what do I pay for rejecting it compared with the alternatives?
