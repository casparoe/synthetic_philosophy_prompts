I'm a longtime reader of the rationalist blogs and I organize a monthly forecasting-and-epistemics meetup for a mixed crowd -- a few actuaries and engineers, some history hobbyists, one economist, and several people whose entire exposure to Tetlock consists of secondhand summaries. For next month's session I have fifty minutes and a whiteboard to teach the outside view properly: not the slogan "use base rates," which everyone in the room will nod at, but the actual discipline of reference-class forecasting, with the lifespan of empires as the carrying example. Plan the explanation rather than giving it. What has to come first in the sequence and what depends on what -- my instinct is that the inside view must be defined before the outside view so the contrast does the work, that base rates as priors must precede any talk of reference-class selection, and that the class-construction step has to come last because it's where everything breaks -- but tell me if that ordering is wrong and why. Explain why the empire case carries the idea better than the shopworn planning-fallacy examples (student essays, kitchen renovations), which this audience is already inoculated against, and what the example must have -- a roughly enumerable class, a distribution rather than a single number, outcomes distant enough that no one in the room has emotional stakes -- and what it must avoid.

Then give me the misconception map, since this is where I expect to earn my keep. I anticipate at least the following: base-rate neglect in the classic form ("this empire is different, look at its institutions"); reference-class tennis, where each member of the audience proposes a narrower class until the base rate says whatever they wanted; survivorship bias in how the class itself gets built, since we only remember the empires worth writing books about; treating the outside view as a verdict rather than a prior to be updated by inside-view evidence; and a confusion between the base rate of past empires and something like a Lindy expectation for a currently-existing one. For each, tell me where in the sequence to head it off and what single sentence or whiteboard move defuses it -- and flag any misconception I'm missing that this particular audience is likely to generate. Note that the definitional question of what counts as an empire will eat the whole session if I let it; tell me how to fence it off with a working definition up front without pretending the problem is solved. Finally, give me one end-of-session question -- ideally a new forecasting case, not an empire one -- whose answer would show the idea landed, and what a wrong-but-confident answer would look like so I can tell the difference in real time. Please write in American English.
