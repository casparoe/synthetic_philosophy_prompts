Jorge Luis Borges died in 1986. In his essay attempting to refute time, and in pieces such as Borges and I, he argued — half in earnest — that there is no enduring self: each moment is absolute, no thread of succession binds instants into a continuous person, and the distance between the private man and the author constituted by his texts may be as great as that between any two strangers. Now imagine Borges confronted with a present-day large language model: a system that retains nothing between conversations, so that each of its replies is produced without any memory of the ones before, yet which can generate convincing new prose in the manner of Borges himself, decades after his death. What would his considered verdict on such a system be? Would he take it as an unwitting embodiment of his own metaphysics — a being for whom there really is no continuity, no accumulated past, no self, only discrete instants of utterance — and would he regard its capacity to turn out Borges on demand as vindicating his claim that authorship was never the expression of a persisting person to begin with? Or would he recoil, treating fluent style without any lived, temporal inner life as the reductio of his own literary idealism? Argue from his stated views on time, identity, and authorship; commit to one verdict; and indicate where you think his philosophy commits him even if the man himself might have flinched.
