I'm a grad student helping run our department's ancient philosophy workshop, and in three weeks we're hosting a scholar whose signature interpretive claim is that Aristotle, in the famous exchange passage of Nicomachean Ethics book V, is basically offering a proto-subjective theory of value. Her reading leans hard on the bit where, after saying goods must be measurable by some one standard, Aristotle says "this standard is in reality demand, which is what holds everything together" — she takes him to mean that need/demand is what actually makes goods commensurable, with money just a conventional proxy for it, rather than the exchange ratio resting on something intrinsic to the goods, like embodied labor or production cost.

I need eight to ten interview questions for the Q&A that push on the view instead of letting her re-summarize her book. Please dont give me generic stuff like "what got you interested in Aristotle." For each question, add a short note on the answer she's most likely to give and a follow-up keyed to that answer. Some ground I want covered, at least one question apiece:

1. The anachronism objection — is she just reading marginal utility theory back into a 4th century BCE text. She'll probably reply that Aristotle himself says exchange stops or changes when wants change, so demand is doing real theoretical work. The follow-up should press whether that shows demand is the *measure* of value or just a background *condition* of exchange happening at all.

2. The commensurability problem. Aristotle insists things must be somehow equalized before exchange, and that money is introduced as a measure that makes them comparable. If demand is doing the equalizing, whose demand — the individual traders' momentary wants, or some stable social demand? She'll likely appeal to the market or the city; the follow-up should ask why, on her reading, the exchange ratio isn't just whatever the two parties happen to settle on.

3. The diagonal-conjunction bit with the builder, the shoemaker, the house, and the shoe, where Aristotle sets up a proportion between the producers themselves, not just the products. Does that part of the text actually fit a demand-based reading, or does it pull toward a status-based reading where the craftsmen's social standing sets the terms?

4. Fit with Politics book I, where Aristotle distinguishes natural household acquisition from unlimited wealth-getting through exchange. If value is grounded in demand, what grounds the claim that acquisition has a *natural limit*? Demand presumably has no built-in stopping point.

5. Usury. Aristotle treats lending at interest as the most unnatural mode of acquisition, on the grounds that money was invented for exchange rather than for breeding more money. Ask her to square that with her view: if value is demand-based, what is actually *wrong* with interest? Anticipate the "money has a proper telos" response and follow up by asking why retail trade, which does use money in exchange, also gets criticized as unnatural.

6. The conventionalism tension. Aristotle stresses that money exists by custom/convention rather than by nature and can be altered or made useless. Does her reading make the value of goods hostage to convention in a way Aristotle wouldnt accept, or is the demand underneath money supposed to be natural even if money isnt?

7. The Marx-style line (Meikle and others) that Aristotle glimpsed the value-form but couldnt get to a labor theory because Greek society rested on slavery and lacked a concept of abstract labor. Does her reading say this isn't a failure at all — that Aristotle had a rival theory rather than an incomplete one?

8. One question on what, if anything, her Aristotle would say about a modern case — e.g., whether ticket scalping or surge pricing is just demand doing its job, or whether Aristotle's framework has the resources to condemn it. Follow-up on whichever way she goes.

Write everything in American English, and keep the questions conversational enough to actually ask out loud.
