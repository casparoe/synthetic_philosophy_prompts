You are a professional epistemologist with particular expertise in higher-order evidence and epistemic rationality, and you have been asked to referee the following dispute between two postdocs, Mara and Jonas. Think carefully, reconstruct the strongest version of each move, and then adjudicate move by move.

Mara reviewed six studies on a drug interaction outside her usual subfield, working under time pressure, and came to believe with confidence 0.85 that the combination is safe. She then learns that her hospital's audit system has flagged her: on past occasions when she reached high confidence in an unfamiliar subfield under time pressure, her conclusions were mistaken 60% of the time.

Jonas argues that rationality requires Mara to lower her confidence substantially right now, before re-examining any study: maintaining a belief at full strength while believing that beliefs of that kind, so formed, are usually mistaken is a paradigm of epistemic akrasia, and if the audit flag cannot move her, higher-order evidence does no work at all.

Mara replies that the flag touches none of her reasons: the six studies say what they say regardless of her track record. The flag gives her a reason to re-check her work, but unless re-checking turns up an identifiable error, revising her confidence merely in response to the statistic would amount to discounting the studies twice — once in weighing them, once again in weighing herself. Jonas counters with an analogy: a pilot whose altimeter reads safe altitude but who is told the instrument is unreliable in these conditions may not keep trusting the readout; Mara's judgment is her instrument, and the audit shows it is unreliable. Mara answers that the cases differ: the pilot has no independent access to altitude except through the instrument, whereas the studies remain directly before her and her reasoning about them is assessable on its merits — and a thinker who had to abandon every belief upon news like the audit could never rationally trust her own revisions either, since those too are products of the same fallible mind.

Please adjudicate: (a) whether higher-order evidence can rationally require revision absent any identified first-order error; (b) whether Mara's double-counting worry succeeds here; (c) whether the pilot analogy holds or fails, and why; and (d) whether Mara's regress concern about her own revisions is cogent or instead proves too much. Then say who is more right overall, and identify anything both are missing — in particular, whether the 60% figure is even the right reference class for Mara's case, and what considerations would settle that.
