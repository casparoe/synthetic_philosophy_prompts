I host a tiny philosophy podcast (we're talking a few hundred listeners, mostly grad students who put us on while doing dishes), and in two weeks I'm interviewing a logician who has spent her whole career defending dialetheism — the view that some contradictions are genuinely true, with the Liar sentence as her star witness. I've interviewed people on paradoxes before and the conversations always die the same death: I ask "but surely a sentence can't be both true and false??" and she says "why not?" and we stare at each other for forty minutes. So I want to skip that loop entirely this time.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that assume she already buys the basic picture, and instead press on the stuff where dialetheists actually sweat? Concretely, I'd like at least one question on the revenge problem — the moment she says the Liar is both true and false, someone wheels out a sentence like "this sentence is false only" or phrases things in terms of her own truth predicate, and I want to know if saying "that one's both too" is a stable resting point or an infinite game of whack-a-mole. At least one on Curry's paradox, since "if this sentence is true, then the moon is made of cheese" seems to force her to mutilate the logic of the conditional, and I want to know what exactly she gives up and why that isn't ad hoc. At least one on the practical costs: if disjunctive syllogism has to go, how is she allowed to reason in ordinary life, and does her answer mean dialetheists need a special dispensation to do sudoku. And at least one on the weird expressive worry that her own theory can't cleanly say that some sentences are just plain true and not also false — that the dialetheist seems to need a notion of "true and not false" that her own framework keeps absorbing.

For each question, please also give me the follow-up, keyed to the answer she's most likely going to give — she's polished, she's heard every objection, and she will 100% try the move of saying "that objection assumes the very consistency I'm denying," so the follow-ups should corner her on something more specific than that. Oh and no softballs like "what got you interested in logic" — the audience wants blood.
