So in two weeks I'm presenting at an internal workshop at my company (we build a general-purpose chat assistant) on when the assistant should flat-out refuse or override a user versus just warn them and then help. My talk's core claim is that Mill already solved the skeleton of this problem in chapter 5 of On Liberty, "Applications," and that assistant policy should basically inherit it. The passage I'm anchoring everything on is this one:

"The maxims are, first, that the individual is not accountable to society for his actions, in so far as these concern the interests of no person but himself. Advice, instruction, persuasion, and avoidance by other people if thought necessary by them for their own good, are the only measures by which society can justifiably express its dislike or disapprobation of his conduct. Secondly, that for such actions as are prejudicial to the interests of others, the individual is accountable, and may be subjected either to social or to legal punishment, if society is of opinion that the one or the other is requisite for its protection."

My gloss: an assistant should relate to a competent adult user roughly the way Mill thinks society should relate to an individual -- advice, warnings, persuasion, even refusals to be *involved*, but no forcible overrides of self-regarding choices. So: helping someone plan an extremely unhealthy diet, fine after a clear warning; helping them stalk an ex, not fine, since that's other-regarding. I also lean on the bridge case from the same chapter, where Mill says you can physically grab someone about to walk onto a bridge you know is unsafe if there's no time to warn them, because they presumably don't want to drown, but once they've been warned and still want to cross, you have to let them. I argue this maps onto hard interrupts (the assistant breaking flow to stop an imminent irreversible mistake the user obviously doesn't intend, like pasting their own private key into a public repo) versus mere warnings.

The audience is the problem. It's about fifteen people: four or five alignment/safety researchers who think consumer assistant policy is basically rehearsal for much higher-stakes refusal decisions and tend to think deferring to users is how you get walked into catastrophe; three policy/legal people with civil-liberties instincts who think a private company deciding which self-regarding user goals are "worthy" is creepy and unaccountable; a couple of product managers who mostly care about not getting us sued or ratio'd; and one actual political theorist who knows the Mill secondary lit cold and is the one I'm most scared of.

Flag the spots where my Mill reading is doing more work than the text supports (I know "self-regarding vs other-regarding" gets mushy fast with stuff like a user asking for help writing something that only harms them but normalizes harm to others).
