Read Isaac Asimov's Foundation trilogy through the lens of base rates, reference classes, and the outside view. Hari Seldon's psychohistory is basically reference-class forecasting raised to a science: it treats the Galactic Empire as one more instance of the class "large decaying empires" (Asimov famously modeled the thing on Gibbon's Rome) and predicts only statistical aggregates of trillions of people, refusing to say anything about any individual. So what does the story show about when that style of forecasting works and when it breaks? The Mule seems like the key case: one mutant who wrecks the Seldon Plan because the historical reference class contains nobody like him. Is that a fair dramatization of how outside-view forecasting actually fails -- fat tails, unprecedented actors, black swans -- or does Asimov cheat by making psychohistory infallible about everything except a single freak, which no real forecaster ever gets? And what do we make of the Second Foundation quietly steering history back onto the Plan: does that vindicate the method, or turn it's predictions into self-fulfilling prophecies maintained by a hidden elite? I want a reading that takes both the forecasting theory and the books seriously, and says where the allegory illuminates the real debate about base rates and where the novels push back against it.
