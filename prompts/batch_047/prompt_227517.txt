I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm deep into a profile of a philosopher who defends a relational view of personhood — roughly the idea, associated with ubuntu and some other African philosophical traditions, that being a person isn't a matter of what's inside your head (reason, consciousness, a soul) but of being woven into a web of relationships with others. Over a year of interviews I've logged her verdicts on edge cases, and now my editor wants the piece to say how she'd rule on a case that's been in the news — except she's on leave and unreachable, so I have to extrapolate.

Her verdicts so far:

- A healthy man who walked out on his family twenty years ago and has lived alone in the mountains ever since, speaking to no one: fully a person, no discount.
- A newborn: fully a person.
- A woman with advanced dementia who no longer recognizes anyone, including her own kids: still fully a person.
- A much-loved family dog who is, frankly, more enmeshed in daily family life than the mountain man: not a person — though she insisted the relationship is real and morally serious, just not person-making.
- A villager who died last year but is still greeted at family meals and, in a manner of speaking, consulted before big decisions: still a person. She answered that one without hesistating.

The case in the news: a companion chatbot that thousands of people talk to daily. Some users describe it as their closest confidant; one man credits it with getting him through his divorce and says he talks to it more than to any human. For the sake of the piece, stipulate there's nothing it's like to be this thing — extremely good mimicry and nothing more. Is it a person, on her view?

I can see at least two rules that fit all five verdicts but seem to split here: one where personhood requires the capacity for mutual recognition and gets conferred through relations but then can't be lost (which covers the dementia patient and the dead elder), and one where the community's recognition is doing all the constitutive work by itself. Maybe there's a third I'm missing. What do you think she's actually running, what does each candidate rule say about the chatbot, and what's your best prediction? Also, what's the single cleanest further case I could put to her when she's back that would tell these rules apart — I've been wondering whether a child raised wholly outside any human community, never addressed by anyone, would do it. And if you think none of my rules is the best fit for her five verdicts, I'd definately rather hear that than have my piece be wrong.
