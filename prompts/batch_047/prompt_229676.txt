I cover physics and the philosophy of science for a magazine, and at a conference reception last week I listened to two of my regular sources — a theoretical physicist and a philosopher of physics — argue about the Everett (many-worlds) interpretation. Neither would concede, and before I write anything I need a referee's report. The physicist opened by claiming Everett is the only interpretation that takes the formalism at face value: unitary evolution alone, no collapse postulate, and therefore strictly simpler than Copenhagen. The philosopher countered that the simplicity is borrowed, since Everett never actually recovers the Born rule — probability is arguably meaningless in a world where every outcome occurs — so the postulate it saves is repaid with an unsolved foundational problem. The physicist answered that decision-theoretic and symmetry-based derivations of the Born rule exist and do real work. The philosopher granted the derivations for the sake of argument but replied that at best they establish what a rational agent in a branching universe ought to expect, not what the physics itself predicts, and that the rationality axioms they require are doing unacknowledged interpretive labor. The physicist's final move was that, whatever Everett's costs, its vagueness is milder than Copenhagen's, which never defines measurement and so can apply its collapse rule only by convention. Please adjudicate in exactly six numbered items, each a sentence or two, written in American English, with no introduction or conclusion: items one through five should assess the five moves in order, stating who is right on that specific point and why, and item six should identify what, if anything, both parties are missing.
