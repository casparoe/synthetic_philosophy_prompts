At a philosophy department happy hour I ended up sitting between two graduate students, Maya and Daniel, who spent nearly an hour arguing about whether "better than" can fail to be transitive. I couldn't tell who won, and I'd like a referee. Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it.

(1) Maya opened with a spectrum argument. Take a superb life -- a century of rich, varied, deeply satisfying experience. Now consider a sequence of lives, each imperceptibly or near-imperceptibly worse in quality than the last but otherwise comparable, until you reach a life of gray tedium that is barely worth living. Each life in the sequence, she said, is at least arguably no worse than its neighbor -- the quality drop between adjacent lives is too small to ground a judgment of worseness -- so chaining the comparisons forces the conclusion that the drab final life is no worse than the superb first one. Since that conclusion is absurd, and the absurdity can't be pinned on any single step, she concluded that the transitivity of "better than" itself is the weak link: our evaluative comparisons simply do not chain.

(2) Daniel replied that the argument proves too much, and in doing so reveals its real engine. The relation actually being chained in her spectrum, he said, is not "better than" but "not noticeably worse than," and everyone already knows that relation is non-transitive -- it is the same structure as a series of color patches each indistinguishable from the next, where the first is clearly red and the last clearly orange. Just as the color case shows nothing about color, only about the limits of discrimination, the spectrum shows nothing about betterness. Genuine betterness, unlike perceptual indistinguishability, is a ranking relation, and rankings are transitive by their nature.

(3) Maya answered that the discriminability diagnosis fails on its own terms, because the spectrum can be rebuilt with differences that are small but plainly noticeable: at each step, replace one afternoon of genuine delight with one of mild boredom, a difference anyone would register when it is pointed out. The intuitive pull of the argument survives this repair, she claimed -- you still cannot find the step where the superb life starts losing to the drab one -- so the puzzle is not an artifact of fuzzy perception. She added a further challenge: if Daniel insists that betterness is transitive, he owes an account of which of her premises he rejects, and each rejection looks as costly as hers, since denying that adjacent lives are comparable in the way she described seems to deny something obvious.

(4) Daniel shifted ground. He granted that the repaired spectrum is harder but argued that the real problem is that the comparisons change their basis as you move along the sequence. Early steps are evaluated by fine-grained standards appropriate to nearly identical lives; later steps smuggle in the coarser standard appropriate to very different ones. "Better than," like "flat" or "heap," tolerates context-dependent standards, and the argument equivocates across contexts. Transitivity holds within any fixed standard, he said, and that is all the rationality of evaluation requires. He also pressed a dialectical point: giving up transitivity to save intuitions is a strange trade, because transitivity is what makes evaluation action-guiding at all -- an intransitive betterness ordering would leave a rational agent unable to say, of three options, which to choose, and would seem to expose anyone who acts on their comparisons to being cycled through trades indefinitely.

(5) Maya's last move was to call the context-dependence story a redescription rather than a solution. If the standard shifts somewhere along the sequence, she said, then there must be a place where it shifts -- an adjacent pair where the comparison that looked innocent under one standard fails under another -- and Daniel still has not located it. Until he can point to the pair and say why its comparison is defective, his position amounts to "transitivity must hold, so something in the spectrum must be wrong, somewhere." She conceded that the cycling worry was the strongest thing he had said, but replied that it shows at most that rational choice needs a transitive rule, not that the underlying evaluative facts are transitive: perhaps agents must impose a consistent ranking on comparisons that do not themselves form one.

Please adjudicate the dispute move by move. For each of the five moves, say whether it succeeds, fails, or partially succeeds against the position it targets, with a brief justification -- a move can be a good reply to what was actually said while still leaving the replier's own position exposed. Then give your overall verdict: on which specific points, if any, is each party right, and is there a consideration that both are missing -- for instance, about what "better than" comparisons must be like if they are to guide choice, or about whether the dispute turns on a shared assumption about how evaluative comparisons relate to the standards used to make them.
