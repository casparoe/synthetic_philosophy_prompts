I'm interviewing a philosopher on our podcast whose new book defends the precautionary principle in its strong form: where an activity threatens serious or irreversible harm, regulators may restrict it even in the absence of established causal evidence, with the burden of proof shifted onto the activity's proponents. He is polished at the standard defenses, so I want a sequence of six or seven questions that press where the literature presses hardest, each question followed by the reply he will most likely give and the follow-up question to that reply. The arc should move through: the objection that the principle paralyzes itself because action and inaction alike carry potentially serious risks, so it can be invoked on both sides of any regulatory question (anticipate his appeal to irreversibility, and press on why irreversibility doesn't cut equally against inaction, say when forgoing a technology predictably costs lives or locks in an existing harmful infrastructure); the claim that precaution is just rational maximin under genuine uncertainty (press on what justifies classifying regulatory contexts as uncertainty rather than risk, and on who gets to make that call); and the risk-risk tradeoff problem, where regulating one hazard foreseeably worsens another, as when phasing out one energy source raises mortality from its replacement. The sequence should close with a question about whether the principle has an exit condition at all: what would have to be established, empirically or epistemically, for him to concede that a precautionary ban should be lifted. Write the whole sequence as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, since I want it in a form I can read straight from during the interview.
