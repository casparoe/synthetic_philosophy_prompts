I recently started volunteering as a community mediator with a neighborhood dispute-resolution center, and it did not take long to notice that a large share of the conflicts that come through our door are, at least in part, fights over what a word means -- whether a landlord's repeated texts count as harassment, whether a finished basement is part of the leased premises, whether a noise was unreasonable. I now have to train a new cohort of volunteer mediators in a single two-hour evening workshop on how to recognize and handle merely verbal disputes as opposed to substantive ones. The trainees are intelligent adults with no philosophy background, and I want a plan for the session, not a lecture I could memorize.

Please lay out the explanatory arc for the workshop. First, the order in which the component ideas have to be introduced and why -- specifically, what must already be in place before the distinction between disagreeing about the world and disagreeing about language can even be stated without sounding like an accusation that the parties are arguing over nothing. Second, the one carrying example the whole session should be built around: I want it drawn from an actual mediation-style conflict rather than the standard philosophical stock cases (no tree falling in the forest, no hot dog, no free will), so tell me what features the example must have and what it must avoid -- for instance, it needs real stakes so that calling the dispute verbal does not read as dismissing it, but the semantic hinge must be easy to isolate. Third, give me a live diagnostic the trainees can actually run in the room with disputants -- something like stipulating both candidate meanings and checking whether the fight survives -- and tell me when in the sequence to introduce it and what its failure modes are. Fourth, the misconceptions: I anticipate at least three -- that verbal means trivial, that verbal means easily settled by a dictionary, and that labeling a dispute verbal insults the parties -- so specify which others to expect, the order in which they will surface, and exactly where in the plan each should be headed off rather than merely answered afterward. Fifth, a closing exercise or question that would let me tell whether the trainees grasped the distinction rather than just the vocabulary. Finally, note what I should explicitly refuse to cover in two hours -- I suspect whether heavyweight philosophical disputes, such as whether numbers or composite objects really exist, are themselves substantive is beyond scope -- and give me one sentence for saying so without making it sound like the interesting part was withheld.

Please write the answer in American English.
