I'm in a second-year set theory course and after a few weeks of choice-related theorems I've realized my instincts are all over the place. A group of us were studying for the midterm and I kept defending positions my friends said contradicted each other, and honestly I couldn't tell if they were right. Here's roughly what I believe:

(1) The axiom of choice itself seems obviously true to me. If you've got a family of nonempty sets, of course there's a function picking one element from each -- what would it even mean for that to fail? People who doubt AC strike me as pedantic.

(2) That said, I don't really believe the reals can be well-ordered. If there were a well-ordering of ℝ, surely someone would have written one down by now. The well-ordering theorem feels like a thing we prove on paper, not something actually true of the real number line.

(3) Every vector space has a basis. Our professor proved this using Zorn's lemma (Hamel bases, e.g. ℝ as a vector space over ℚ) and I have zero problem with it -- linear algebra without bases would be crippled, and the proof felt like totally standard math.

(4) I distrust choice-based pathologies like non-measurable sets and the Banach-Tarski decomposition. These seem like artifacts of pushing an axiom past its intended use rather than facts about genuine mathematical objects, and if it were up to me we'd stick to definable sets and just drop whatever breaks.

(5) Countable choice and dependent choice are completely unproblematic -- basic analysis needs them and nobody serious doubts them.

So does this package hang together? Please don't just tell me everything conflicts with everything -- I want to know which pairs are in genuine tension, which only look like they are, and I want the real tensions ranked by severity rather than listed as equally bad. Two things I specifically can't sort out: is (2) actually compatible with (1), or am I conflating "nobody can define one" with "none exists"? And is (3) really safer than (4), given that a Hamel basis of ℝ over ℚ seems like exactly the kind of non-constructive object I said I distrust -- does a Hamel basis have the same pathology-generating features as the stuff in (4), or is there a principled line between them? Finally, if something has to go, what's the least costly revision and what would I actually lose by making it? Feel free to mention what the mathematical landscape looks like on each option (like what survives if you keep definable sets and drop full choice).
