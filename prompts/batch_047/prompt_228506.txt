I chair the conscientious objection review panel in a country with mandatory national service — two years, military by default, with a civilian alternative for recognized objectors. We hear about fifteen cases a month, each decided on a written statement, two supporting letters, and a forty-five-minute hearing; all three panel members must sign every decision, and a denied claimant who still refuses induction faces prosecution. Our charter is one sentence long: exemption is owed to anyone whose conscience opposes participation in war in any form. Last month we split two-to-one over an applicant, I cast the deciding vote with real misgivings, and the dissenter — our military liaison, a decent man — asked me afterward what principle of mine had actually done the deciding. I had no good answer. We currently run these hearings on a collage of gut feelings and each member's favorite stock questions, and I want an actual decision procedure built out of the philosophy of pacifism: the distinctions it draws, such as absolute versus contingent pacifism and opposition to war as such versus opposition to all violence, should survive translation into something my two colleagues, neither of whom has read a page of philosophy, can apply at the table.

The procedure must therefore tell us, in order, what to establish and what counts as establishing it. It needs a test for generality — is this objection to war as such? — that does not collapse into a purity exam no living pacifist tradition could pass: the applicant who concedes he would use lethal force against someone attacking his sister, the applicant who pays the taxes that fund the army, the applicant untroubled by armed police. It needs a rule for the selective objector who condemns only the war our country is currently fighting. It needs a way of probing sincerity that does not reward articulate, well-coached applicants over clumsy sincere ones, and that does not quietly turn into a theology exam for the religious and a skepticism exam for everyone else. And it needs a verdict rule for the total objector who refuses even civilian service on the grounds that performing it releases another conscript to fight. Wherever judgment cannot be eliminated, say so plainly rather than dressing the judgment call up as a test.

Then run the procedure on three recent applicants, anonymized: a twenty-year-old with no religious affiliation, fluent and well prepared, who calls all war organized murder but concedes the sister case under questioning; a devout applicant with a decade of documented activism against our current war, which she calls unjust, who allows in the hearing that she can imagine a genuinely defensive war she might support; and a quiet, halting applicant who refuses the civilian alternative because it frees another young man for combat, and who could barely explain himself beyond that. Give me the whole answer as exactly ten numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: items 1–6 the steps of the procedure in the order we should run them, each stating the question, what counts as passing it, and what happens on failure; item 7 the points where judgment cannot be eliminated; items 8–10 the three applications, each ending in grant, deny, or refer, with the reason — and flag which of the three outcomes I will like least.
