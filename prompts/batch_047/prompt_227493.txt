I work as a software engineer and have no background in philosophy, but I've ended up in a running disagreement with a colleague about whether very small harms, spread across enough people, can ever add up to something worse than a serious harm to one person. It began as a mundane design dispute — he wanted to ship a change that would add a fraction of a second of latency to every request for every one of our users, hundreds of millions of times a day, in order to fix a rare bug that locks a handful of people out of their accounts for a full day each month — and I found myself insisting that the aggregate of trivial inconveniences simply doesn't compete with one person having a genuinely bad day, without being able to say why. Since then I've been stress-testing that instinct against cases, and I've noticed my own verdicts don't sit comfortably together. I'd like help working out what principle, if any, is generating them.

Here is what I actually think, case by case. Given the choice between relieving one person's hour-long migraine and sparing ten million people a single barely noticeable itch lasting one second each, I say relieve the migraine, and I don't think the number of itchy people changes that. Between preventing one death and eliminating a mildly irritating buzzing noise that a billion people will each hear exactly once, I say prevent the death. Between an hour of torture for one person and a single dust speck landing in the eye of every person who will ever live, I say spare the tortured person, and I genuinely believe no size of population flips this one. But here is where I trip over myself: within my own life, I would unhesitatingly accept ten thousand one-second itches spread over the coming years rather than one hour of severe pain next week — so across time within a single person, the tiny harms plainly do add up for me, and it's only across people that I claim they don't. And a fifth case makes it worse: between one person enduring moderate chronic back pain for a year and a hundred million people each losing one night's sleep to discomfort, I find myself leaning towards relieving the many, even though a bad night is surely worse than a dust speck and chronic back pain is surely milder than torture — which suggests I'm operating with some middle tier where aggregation kicks back in, and I can't locate its edges.

So what I'd like is this: state precisely the principle (or two or three candidate principles) that best captures these verdicts, and indicate which cases motivate each clause of it. In particular, I want to know whether the right account is that harms below some threshold never aggregate against harms above it, and if so where the threshold might plausibly sit and whether it can be sharp without being arbitrary; or whether the real work is being done by the within-a-life versus across-lives distinction, and if so why person-boundaries should matter to arithmetic that I'm happy to run over my own future selves. Test your principle against cases I haven't considered — for instance, a harm just barely above whatever threshold you propose, or distributing the same total suffering differently across people — and give its verdicts on them. Finally, be honest about the cost: if no principle that isn't gerrymandered beyond recognition can save all five of my verdicts, tell me which one has to go and why that one is the weakest.
