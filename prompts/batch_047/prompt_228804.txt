Last night a friend and I argued for several hours about Nietzsche's genealogy of morality, and I would like an outside audit of the analogy on which his entire case rested. His claim was that Nietzsche's account of the slave revolt in morality — the powerless, unable to take revenge in deed, taking it in imagination by inverting their masters' table of values — is nothing more than Aesop's fable of the fox and the grapes writ large: what the weak cannot attain (strength, pride, wealth, worldly success) they declare evil, just as the fox declares the unreachable grapes sour. On this basis he argued that the genealogy debunks slave morality outright: once one sees that egalitarian values originated in self-serving denial, one can no longer take them seriously as values.

Please treat the analogy as an argument and audit how far it holds. Specifically:

1. Give the correspondence table: what plays the role of the fox, the grapes, the height of the branch, the declaration of sourness, and the fox's walking away.
2. Distinguish two things the fox might be doing — call them doxastic souring (coming to believe the grapes are sour while still prizing sweetness) and valuational souring (ceasing to prize sweetness itself) — and say which of the two Nietzsche's ressentiment is supposed to be, given that he portrays the slave's No as itself a creative act that brings forth new values rather than merely denying old ones. Does the fable survive the reading on which ressentiment is genuinely value-creating rather than merely self-deceiving?
3. Identify what the analogy quietly imports. Above all: grapes are sweet or sour independently of any fox, so the fable presupposes an objective standard against which the fox is simply mistaken. Say whether values have any counterpart to sweetness, and how much of my friend's debunking conclusion depends on that presupposition.
4. Consider the repair I attempted in the argument: amend the fable so that the fox founds a community, teaches that sweetness is corrupting, raises descendants who genuinely cannot enjoy it, and within a few generations the sour-preferring foxes run the vineyard. At what point in this amendment does the debunking force drain away — or does it not?
5. State whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, separating those that matter for his intended use from those that do not; and if the analogy is beyond repair, propose a better one for whatever truth it was gesturing at.

Please answer in British English.
