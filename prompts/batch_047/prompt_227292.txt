I co-run a monthly "strange problems" night at a local board-game café — about eight to twelve adults, no academic backgrounds, mixed comfort with math, but patient and argumentative in the best way. A while ago I stumbled on a probability puzzle in a popular book, apparently called the Judy Benjamin problem, and it split our group for an entire evening. My retelling: a soldier is parachuted at night into a square region divided into three zones. The Blue zone covers half the map; the other half is Red territory, split evenly into a northern Red sector and a southern Red sector, each a quarter of the whole map. She has no information about where she landed, so her credences are one-half, one-quarter, one-quarter. Then headquarters radios a single message: we cannot pinpoint you, but if you are in Red territory, you are three times as likely to be in the north sector as the south. Nothing in the message mentions Blue at all. The strange part — the part we argued about until closing time — is that on the most natural way of taking that message at face value and shifting her numbers accordingly, her confidence that she is in the Blue zone goes up, past one-half, even though the message concerned only the Red zones. Some of us think that result is obviously correct, some think it is a reductio of that way of updating, and one friend keeps insisting the right answer depends on how headquarters decided what to say, which the puzzle never tells you.

I want to stage this at the meetup as a game rather than a lecture. Design a roughly 75-minute activity, using only café-grade materials (cards, chips, paper, tape), in which participants are effectively "dropped" themselves: for instance, a game master secretly places each player's token according to the prior, an assigned dispatcher relays an analogous conditional hint, and players must make repeated small-stakes decisions — betting chips on zones, buying information, trading positions — so that the Blue-side drift happens to them, in their own chips, before anyone names the puzzle. I'd like complete rules, roles, a payoff scheme that doesn't use real money, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments of play onto the philosophical questions: how one ought to update on a conditional, whether the probability of the untouched region is even allowed to move, and the difference between treating the message as a constraint on one's credences versus treating it as evidence about what the dispatcher would say in various situations. Please also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — a previous attempt of mine with playing cards collapsed into half the table doing silent algebra while the other half ignored the hint entirely — and be honest about which feature of the puzzle the game structurally cannot reproduce and must be supplied by discussion instead.

Please take on the role of an experienced designer of philosophy classroom games — someone who thinks in terms of incentives, information flow, and group dynamics rather than mere correctness — and think carefully about how to make the paradox emerge from the play itself rather than from the facilitator's explanation. Before committing to a design, brainstorm two or three candidate core mechanics in a sentence or two each, indicate which one you would choose and why, and then develop the winner in full detail. No citations or reading lists are needed; the debrief must work in plain language for smart laypeople, and participants should not hear the puzzle's name until after the debrief.
