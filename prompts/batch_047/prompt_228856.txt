A colleague of mine in media studies sorts online communities into three bins — echo chamber, epistemic bubble, or neither — and her scheme is loosely indebted to C. Thi Nguyen's distinction, though she's never stated her rule explicitly. Here are her verdicts on five communities I've described to her:

1. An online community devoted to a fringe health practice. Members almost never encounter mainstream medical views, simply because their feeds and friendships formed elsewhere. When outsiders do show up, members argue with them politely and occasionally change their minds. Her verdict: epistemic bubble.
2. A political movement whose members share a standing story that journalists and scientists are bought. Any external criticism is answered by pointing to that story, and exposure to outside evidence leaves members' confidence untouched or increased. Her verdict: echo chamber.
3. A platform where a recommendation algorithm quietly removes dissenting posts. Users don't know this is happening, hold no particular distrust of outsiders, and engage reasonably with dissent when they meet it offline. Her verdict: epistemic bubble.
4. A group whose members each came to distrust outside experts through separate, uncoordinated bad personal experiences. There is no shared discrediting narrative, no leadership, no doctrine — but the cumulative effect is that no outside claim ever gets a hearing. Her verdict: echo chamber.
5. A reading group that distrusts mainstream commentary on its subject, argues constantly among itself, and actively seeks out hostile criticism to sharpen its views against. Her verdict: neither.

Now the new case: a religious community with an explicit, taught doctrine that outsiders' criticisms of the faith are temptations deliberately designed to deceive — a fully articulated discrediting story, reinforced in sermons and study groups. However, sociologists studying the community find that members who maintain close friendships with outsiders fairly often do quietly revise or abandon the targeted beliefs after those personal conversations; the doctrine exists on paper but routinely fails in practice.

Please lead with your predicted verdict in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the setup. In your reasoning: identify at least two general rules that each fit all five of her earlier verdicts, state what each predicts about the new case, explain which verdict or verdicts in her track record most constrain the prediction and why, and describe one further hypothetical community whose classification would decisively discriminate between the rules you've identified.
