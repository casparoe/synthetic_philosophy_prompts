Write a parody FAQ page, roughly 600 to 750 words, formatted as a sequence of questions and answers, for a fictional ethics-consulting firm — call it something like Standing Aside Partners — whose entire business is helping clients convert doings into allowings. The firm's founding insight is that the moral character of a bad outcome depends on whether you brought it about or merely failed to prevent it, and its pitch is that, with the right advice, almost any awkward doing can be restructured as a blameless omission: do not push, decline to catch; do not administer, neglect to prescribe; do not unplug, simply let the contract lapse. The voice should be serene, upbeat corporate compliance-speak, completely deadpan throughout, and the firm must never once concede that anything it offers is controversial.

The humor has to arise from the actual structure of the doing/allowing distinction as it appears in the philosophical literature, not from absurdities invented for the occasion. The FAQ should therefore work with the cases and fault lines a specialist would recognize: the famous near-symmetry pairs in which agent and bystander intend the same death by means that differ only in muscle movement; the contested difference between withdrawing aid and withholding it; the work the distinction does in debates over euthanasia, triage, and medical futility; and above all the redescription problem — the notorious fact that whether something counts as a doing or an allowing often depends on which description of the episode you adopt, and that "I didn't do anything" and "what I did was refrain from intervening" can be said of the same afternoon. At least one question should press directly on this — the customer essentially asking whether the firm is selling moral advice or a way of talking — and the firm's answer should be a masterpiece of evasion that is nonetheless technically responsive.

Constraints: the target is the doctrine itself and the way people actually lean on it as moral bookkeeping, not any individual philosopher; do not name any philosopher, living or dead, anywhere in the piece. It should be funny to someone who has read the canonical literature on killing and letting die and perfectly readable to someone who has not. The irony must remain legible without winking — no asides confessing the satire, no narrator breaking character. And the final exchange should be constructed so that a reader comes away genuinely uncertain whether the distinction tracks a real moral boundary or whether, as the firm's flourishing business model itself suggests, it is an accounting convention — that is, the piece should end at the real philosophical question rather than merely at the joke.
