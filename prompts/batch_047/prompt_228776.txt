So I'm a PhD student working on pre-classical monetary thought (mostly Irish and English stuff, Petty through Hume), and I keep hitting a wall in my reading group over a line from Berkeley's Querist. The actual query, from the 1750 edition, is Query 5:

"Whether money be not only so far useful, as it stirreth up industry, enabling men mutually to participate the fruits of each other's labour?"

The problem is that people in the group repeat this as if it were a slogan -- someone literally said "money only matters insofar as it stirs up industry" last week to wave away a point I was making about the quantity theory of money, like Berkeley had refuted the whole framework in 1735. Another person uses the same line to claim Berkeley as a proto-Keynesian who thought spending creates employment. Someone else insisted its just an anodyne statement that money facilitates exchange, which nobody has ever denied, so it can't bear the weight people put on it. I genuinely can't tell who's right, and the fact that it's phrased as a question makes it worse, because people read the "whether" as rhetorical when maybe it isn't.

What I want is a breakdown of the distinct precise theses this query could be expressing, from the trivial to the radical. Like: is it just saying money is a medium of exchange (a platitude even by 1735)? Is it saying money's *only* value is instrumental, so hoarding or accumulating coin is pointless or harmful (that's stronger, and it pushes against mercantilism)? Is it a claim that increases in the money supply cause real increases in output, i.e. a denial of monetary neutrality, which would be genuinely substantive and contestable? Is "stirreth up industry" about velocity and circulation rather than quantity? Is it a definitional claim about what money is for, a causal claim about what money does, or a normative claim about what policy should aim at? And does the second half, about mutually participating the fruits of each other's labour, do separate work or just restate the first?

For each reading, tell me whether it's trivial, substantive-and-defensible, or actually false as economics, and then -- this is the part I care most about -- say which readings would actually support the uses I've seen it put to, i.e. dismissing quantity-theoretic reasoning, or drafting Berkeley as a proto-Keynesian. My suspicion is that only the strongest readings support those uses, and those are exactly the ones Berkeley hasn't established and maybe can't.

I want to be able to paste chunks of it into my notes without reformatting, and honestly I find the menu-of-readings format makes people write listicles instead of actually comparing the readings. Distinguish the readings clearly within the prose instead.
