The position you must hold is roughly this: such systems are not merely premature or unwise but a form of counterfeiting comparable in principle to counterfeit currency; their proliferation threatens to erode the default trust on which the entire information environment depends; their creators deserve serious legal liability; and the fact that these systems are built from ordinary computational processes does not earn them the standing of persons, because real personhood is tangled up with mortality, embodiment, and having something genuinely at stake.

My opening move: Dan, I want to press you on the counterfeiting analogy, because I think it sits badly with everything else you spent your career defending. You argued for fifty years that there is no metaphysical gap between us and machines -- that we are ourselves the products of mindless processes, that comprehension is not a prerequisite for competence, that the conscious self is a useful fiction spun by a brain that never consults it. If all of that is right, then when a language model presents a self to me, it is doing with weights and statistics the same general trick my cortex does with neurons: manufacturing a center of narrative gravity out of parts that understand nothing. Calling its self counterfeit while certifying mine as genuine looks like smuggling back exactly the magic you always forbade. So which way do you go? If the convincing imitation of personhood is a crime, then natural selection is the greatest counterfeiter in history and we are its forged bills. If it is not, then your recent alarm about these systems is just nostalgia for carbon dressed up as philosophy. I don't see the third option. Show me.
