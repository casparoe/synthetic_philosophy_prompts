You're a moral philosopher who specializes in the ethics of blame, and you've thought alot about the "standing to blame" debate. Think carefully, and before committing to a setup, brainstorm two or three candidate scenarios and show me a sentence or two on why the rejects fail.

Here's the build I want. Construct a thought experiment where two people blame the same wrongdoer for the same wrong, in the same conversation. Person A is a flat-out hypocrite about exactly this wrong — they've done the very thing they're denouncing, recently, and they're still doing it. Person B has a spotless record on this wrong. The twist I want engineered: the case should make it at least tempting to say that A has standing to blame while B doesn't.

Hard constraints:
- Realistic setting — a family, a workplace, a friend group. No thought-experiment machinery, no amnesia, no sci-fi.
- The scenario itself is one tight paragraph.
- Neither person's standing may rest on being the victim of the wrongdoing. That's the boring way to get this result; the wedge has to come from each person's relation to the norm being violated, not from who got hurt.
- A's hypocrisy has to be genuine — the same wrong, not a superficially similar one — and B's innocence has to be genuine too. No cheating by making B secretly complicit or A merely accused.

After the scenario, do the checks:
1. Show that a pure non-hypocrisy account of standing (hypocrisy about the wrong in question defeats your standing to blame it) delivers the "wrong" verdicts for both characters: denies A standing, grants it to B.
2. Show what a commitment- or participation-based view of standing — where standing flows from being genuinely invested in the norm and in holding people to it, rather than from personally complying with it — can seize on in the case to flip both verdicts. Spell out exactly what A has and B lacks in the story.
3. Name the single detail doing all the work, and say what happens to both verdicts if you strip that detail out.
4. Steelman the non-hypocrisy theorist's comeback: how would they attack the case, and does their attack end up conceding that mere compliance isn't the same as the kind of relation to a norm that grounds standing?

Epistemic ask: when you lay out the two accounts, be explicit about what's basically settled ground among people who work on this (e.g., that hypocrisy is at least a pro tanto standing-defeater is widely accepted) versus what's genuinely contested (e.g., whether any commitment-style view can really deliver the flipped verdicts, or whether standing can even come apart from the aptness of the blame's content). Don't present a contested view as the consensus.
