In Section I of An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, David Hume writes: “While they attempt this arduous task, they are deterred by no difficulties; but proceeding from particular instances to general principles, they still push on their enquiries to principles more general, and rest not satisfied till they arrive at those original principles, by which, in every science, all human curiosity must be bounded.”

A colleague of mine uses this passage to defend the following argument, which he calls the “projection collapse” argument:

1. If a body of observations justifies projecting a predicate onto unobserved cases, then it equally justifies projecting every predicate that applies to exactly the same observed cases.

2. Call an object grue if and only if either it is examined before January 1, 2100, and is green, or it is not examined before January 1, 2100, and is blue.

3. Suppose that, before 2100, we examine a large and varied sample of emeralds from different mines, geological periods, lighting conditions, and methods of cutting. Every examined emerald is green. By definition, every examined emerald is also grue.

4. Therefore, by (1), the sample equally supports both “All emeralds are green” and “All emeralds are grue.”

5. These hypotheses diverge for emeralds first examined after 2100: the former predicts that they will be green, while the latter predicts that they will be blue. Equal support for both would therefore leave us with no reason to prefer either prediction.

6. Since the same construction can be repeated with any predicate, any date, and any pair of afterward-diverging properties, observations alone can never privilege one inductive projection over its rivals. What we call confirmation must instead reflect a prior linguistic or social decision to treat some predicates as projectible.

7. Consequently, Hume’s movement “from particular instances to general principles” cannot be a movement compelled by the evidence itself. The evidence merely supplies instances; our inherited vocabulary selects which general principle counts as the natural continuation of those instances.

8. It follows that the familiar distinction between lawlike and accidental generalizations is not discovered in nature but manufactured through the conventions governing which predicates we happen to take seriously.

The conclusion seems to prove too much. I can accept that the grue construction exposes a real defect in the thought that positive instances automatically confirm every formally compatible generalization. But my colleague appears to move from that defect to the much stronger claim that evidence has no independent role in distinguishing green from grue, or laws from accidents. I would prefer not to rescue induction merely by saying that green is familiar and grue is artificial, since a community whose basic color vocabulary included grue could presumably regard green as artificially time-indexed.

Which step in the argument should be rejected, and why does it initially appear plausible? In answering, distinguish among at least three possibilities: that the problem lies in the evidential-parity principle in (1), that the inference from underdetermination to conventionalism in (6) is too quick, and that (8) conflates a predicate’s being definable with its being fit to express a law. Please also explain what each diagnosis costs: for example, whether it requires admitting background knowledge into confirmation, denying that all hypotheses supported by the same observations are supported equally, or giving an account of projectibility that does not simply privilege our actual vocabulary. Finally, say whether Hume’s description of inquiry survives the diagnosis: can one still move rationally from instances to general principles if the instances alone do not dictate which principle to project?
