My coworker Anjali sits on the deployment review panel for our lab's new frontier model, and her whole philosophy basically comes from Norbert Wiener's 1960 essay Some Moral and Technical Consequences of Automation — the part where he argues that once machines learn and act faster than we can step in, you can't count on correcting their goals mid-flight, so you'd better not hand over power you can't take back. (She never paraphrases it the same way twice, but that's the gist.)

Her track record so far:

1. Approved a setup where a dumber trusted model monitors all outputs and can kill the session — she said it works even if the monitored model is actively scheming.
2. Let us ship honesty fine-tuning, but only as window dressing; she refused to count it toward the safety case at all.
3. Vetoed persistent memory across sessions, even though our evals showed no scheming with memory turned on.
4. Vetoed a plan to replace the monitor with a constitution trained into the model, plus random audits of 1% of outputs.

New case landing on her desk tommorow: the infra team wants separate instances of the model to talk to each other over an encrypted channel (something about protecting user data in transit), with the ciphertext stored and spot-audited once a week. Sandboxes, monitors, and least-privilege access all stay exactly as they are.

Which way does she go? I care less about the verdict itself than about which rule she's actually running. I can see two that both fit all four verdicts and that both claim the Wiener essay, depending how you read him: (a) never let safety depend on the model's internal dispositions — only constraints outside the model count, however opaque they leave us; (b) never let the model operate where we can't watch it in real time — no unreviewed state, no after-the-fact sampling. What does each rule predict for the encrypted channel, which one do you think is really hers and why, and what's the single test case you'd put in front of her to settle it?
