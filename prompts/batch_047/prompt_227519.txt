I'm in a philosophy of history reading group (grad students plus one very patient professor) and last week we spent a full hour on this and got nowhere, so I want an outside referee. Take the fall of the Berlin Wall on November 9th, 1989. The salient facts as I understand them: the GDR had been bleeding people for months after Hungary opened its border with Austria in May, there were massive weekly protests in Leipzig and elsewhere, Gorbachev had made clear the Soviet tanks weren't coming this time, the East German economy was in dire shape, and then on the 9th a politburo member, Günter Schabowski, apparently not properly briefed, told a press conference that new travel rules were in effect right away, crowds massed at the Bornholmer Straße checkpoint, and the officer in charge there, with no orders coming, opened the gate rather than shoot.

Now the theory part. The textbook paradigm of overdetermination is the firing squad: two bullets, each independently sufficient, arriving at the same time. Historians talk as if episodes like this one were overdetermined all the time -- the regime was doomed anyway, the Wall was coming down one way or another, Schabowski just sped it up. But when I actually try to map the Wall case onto the firing squad, the fit seems terrible: the candidate causes aren't simultaneous and independent, they're a chain (Gorbachev's stance enabled Hungary's opening which enabled the exodus which fed the protests), and they interact alot. So: (a) work out whether anything in this episode is genuinely overdetermined in the firing-squad sense -- are there two or more independently sufficient causal routes to the Wall opening, that night or eventually? Maybe the exodus and the Leipzig protests count as separate sufficient pressures on the regime, maybe they don't. (b) If it's not overdetermination, say what the causal structure actually is -- a chain, a conjunctive cause where each factor was necessary, a bundle of INUS-style conditions, or a deep cause (the regime's rot) plus a mere trigger (Schabowski's slip) -- and which of these the ordinary but-for counterfactual test supports. (c) Say where the overdetermination machinery just doesn't apply rather than forcing it to speak: does counterfactual-sufficiency talk even make sense for a one-off event where "the same outcome, later, by another route" is pure speculation about how history would have gone? And (d) run it in the other direction too: if this paradigm case of historical "overdetermination" turns out to be chain or conjunctive causation instead, what does that show about the concept -- is genuine overdetermination just rare in history, or is the historian's use of the word doing a different job entirely, like downgrading contingency or arguing against great-man accounts?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- overdetermined, conjunctively caused, chain-caused, or whatever verdict you land on -- then give the reasoning. No preamble, don't restate the setup.
