You are a philosopher specializing in practical rationality and the theory of intention. Think carefully through the following cases before answering.

Here are my verdicts on what one can and cannot rationally intend, and I want to know what principle unifies them. In Kavka's toxin case, where I am offered a fortune merely for sincerely intending at midnight to drink a toxin tomorrow afternoon that will make me mildly ill for a day, I think I cannot form the intention: knowing I'll have no reason to drink come morning, I cannot now settle on drinking. But I think a state can rationally intend to retaliate against an attack even though retaliating will accomplish nothing once the attack has occurred, provided the intention is formed in advance and visible. I also think Ulysses can rationally intend to sail past the Sirens while bound, and more generally that I can rationally intend now to do something later that I predict will then seem unappealing, so long as I've arranged to be unable to back out. And I think a person can rationally intend to keep a promise even when keeping it will be against their interests at the time. What troubles me is that the toxin case and the deterrence case look structurally identical -- both involve intending to perform an act that will be pointless or costly when performed, where the value lies entirely in the prior intending -- yet my verdicts differ.

Is there a single principle about the rationality of intention that delivers all four verdicts, or must one of them go? Take a definite position: state your principle precisely, say which case motivates each of its clauses, and identify which of my verdicts you would overturn and why, rather than surveying the options and leaving the choice to me. Then apply your principle to a case I haven't listed: the procrastinator's toxin, in which I am paid to intend at midnight to drink the toxin tomorrow only if I haven't finished my manuscript by then, drinking being optional if I have.
