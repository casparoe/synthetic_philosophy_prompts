You are a scholar of international security and the history of technology whose specialty is how policymakers reason by historical analogy. Think carefully before answering, and begin by laying out the analogy's mapping explicitly before you evaluate anything.

Analyze the US export-control regime on advanced semiconductors — beginning with the October 2022 controls on sales of high-end AI chips and chipmaking equipment to China, later joined by Dutch and Japanese restrictions on lithography tools — through the lens of the nuclear analogy for transformative AI: the claim that advanced AI resembles nuclear weapons in conferring a decisive strategic advantage on whoever attains it first, and should therefore be governed like fissile material, through chokepoint control, stockpile accounting, and eventually negotiated limits between rivals.

Do three things. First, the mapping, done carefully rather than gestured at: what plays the role of fissile material, of enrichment capacity, of the weapons test, of delivery systems, of on-site inspection — and note the structural oddity that the key chokepoints are private firms (TSMC, ASML) rather than states, and that the 'weapon' is also a commercial product sold worldwide. Second, where the analogy breaks: on verifiability, on offense–defense balance, on the civilian–military line, on the speed and mode of diffusion, and on whether compute is really the analog of uranium rather than of electricity or machine tools. Third, run the analysis in the other direction: what has the episode so far — reported evasion and stockpiling, accelerated indigenous chip programs, the extraterritorial reach of the controls, allies' reluctance — shown about the analogy itself? Does the observed trajectory vindicate the nuclear frame's implicit predictions about racing and controllability, or does it suggest the analogy smuggled in assumptions (state monopoly, slow diffusion, a sharp test) that AI does not satisfy? If another historical technology fits the observed dynamics better, name it and say why.

Throughout, separate what is settled among economists and security scholars who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested premise — for instance, that capability scales with compute in the relevant way, or that first-mover advantage in AI would be decisive — as though it were the consensus.
