I'm in a cog-sci seminar this semester and we just did the poverty of the stimulus unit, and I got into this argument with a friend who works in ML. His position is basically that LLMs settled the debate: a system with no built-in linguistic knowledge, trained on raw text, ends up with near-human grammatical competence, so the whole idea that children's input is too poor to support language acquisition without rich innate structure is just empirically dead. I have a feeling he's running several different claims together, but when I tried to push back I realized I couldn't cleanly separate them myself. There's the old logical point that any finite body of data underdetermines the grammar, there's the more empirical claim about what kids actually hear (noisy input, supposedly no reliable negative evidence, etc.), and there's the specific claim about structure dependence being unlearnable from the data alone. I suspect the LLM case lands differently on each of these, partly because LLMs get way more data than any child and partly because "domain-general" is doing a lot of work when your learning mechanism is a transformer.

So: please explain the conceptual difference between the poverty of the stimulus as (a) an underdetermination argument in principle, (b) an empirical claim about the actual input available to children, and (c) an argument for specifically linguistic innate constraints as opposed to any priors at all, and then say which of these an LLM's success actually bears on, if any. Also I want to understand why "the data underdetermines the grammar" is really a claim indexed to a particular kind of learner -- i.e. why adding more data by itself doesn't address the argument if the learner's hypothesis space is the real issue. Maybe I'm wrong about that though.
