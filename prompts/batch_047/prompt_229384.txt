I teach an upper-level undergraduate ethics seminar (14 students, 75 minutes). We are about to take up standing to blame: the idea that someone who faults others for a wrong they themselves commit is thereby disqualified from blaming, even when what they say is true. In past years students have flattened this into a bit of etiquette — don't be a hypocrite — without feeling that anything real is lost when the hypocrite speaks. Design a structured activity (roles, hidden information, rules, and a rough timeline) in which students have occasion to blame one another for staged wrongs and then experience their own blame becoming impotent, rather than merely being told that it ought to be discounted. If possible, build in a way for a participant to "come clean" mid-activity so the group can discover for itself whether, and why, confession restores anything. Include a debrief that maps specific moments of the activity onto the distinction between the aptness of blame and the standing to issue it, and onto the question of what victims lose when blame comes from a compromised source. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and state frankly which feature of the phenomenon the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by discussion instead.
