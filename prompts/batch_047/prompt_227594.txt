I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background, but I read a lot of AI safety stuff on weekends and one piece of it has been bugging me for weeks. There's this idea, I think originally Yudkowsky's, usually called "coherent extrapolated volition" — instead of hand-coding values into an AI, you build it to want what you would want if you were smarter, knew all the relevant facts, had unlimited time to think, and were more like the person you wish you were. The whole selling point is that the AI's values supposedly come from you, not from the opinions of whoever built it.

Last week I was arguing with a coworker about this over lunch and I talked myself into what looks like a proof that the idea can't work, and I can't find the hole, which in my experience usually means the hole is somewhere embarassing. Here it is:

1. An AI's goals only count as coming from me — as opposed to from its designers' views about the good — if they're fixed by facts about me.
2. My actual preferences can't fix them, because my actual preferences are a mess: inconsistent, short-sighted, half formed by ignorance and advertising. That's the entire motivation for extrapolation rather than just asking me what I want.
3. So you extrapolate: model a version of me that's smarter, better informed, fully deliberated, and read off what that version endorses.
4. But extrapolation is a procedure, and procedures have settings. How much smarter does simulated-me get? Which of my biases count as errors to correct versus values to keep? What information does it get shown, in what order? How long does it get to think before you freeze it and read off the answer? Anyone who's trained models knows you never get one output from one dataset, you get a distribution over outputs indexed by your hyperparameters.
5. Nothing about me fixes those settings. My actual messy self has no settled view about how it should be idealized — and if it did have such a view, that view would just be another one of the messy preferences that itself needs idealizing, so you're back at step 2.
6. So the output is fixed by whoever picks the settings. The designers.
7. So CEV fails by its own standard from step 1: the extrapolated volition is really the designers' volition run through a me-shaped filter. Paternalism with extra steps.

What bothers me is that every step feels like something I already believe for unrelated reasons, so I don't know what to give up. The closest I got was my coworker's response: he said step 4 confuses a spec with an implementation, the way "sort this list" picks out one function even though quicksort and mergesort get there differently, and you'd never say sortedness is "fixed by the programmer's choices" just because somebody had to pick an algorithm. But that felt too quick to me — sorting has a success criterion that's independent of the algorithm, and I'm not sure extrapolation does. So which step should I actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost? For what it's worth I'd really like to keep 1 and 2, since 2 seems like just a fact about human beings.
