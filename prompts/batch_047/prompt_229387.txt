Write a dialogue between two philosophers arguing about the metaphysics of causation, staged around a concrete modern case rather than the classic example of fire burning cotton.

Amira defends an occasionalism in the tradition of al-Ghazālī's seventeenth discussion of the Incoherence of the Philosophers: there is no necessary connection between what we call a cause and what we call its effect. Fire does not burn cotton; God creates the burning on the occasion of contact. What we call causes are occasions on which God habitually acts, and nothing more. Daniel defends a broadly Aristotelian causal-powers view: things possess irreducible dispositions to produce their characteristic effects, causal claims are not reducible to regularities (divine or otherwise), and the modal connection between cause and effect is real even if we never directly observe it.

The occasion for their dispute is a well-run randomized controlled trial showing that a new antibiotic lowers post-surgical infection rates. Daniel takes the trial's design — manipulation, randomization, holding background conditions fixed — to demonstrate something beyond correlation: it reveals a power in the drug, since intervening on the drug while randomizing everything else changes the outcome. Amira replies that an intervention is simply one more event occurring on the occasion of others, and the debate opens out from there.

The dialogue must, at some point, genuinely engage each of the following pressure points rather than merely gesturing at them:

1. Whether interventionist evidence (learning what happens when you manipulate one variable while controlling others) actually decides between the two views, or whether both can account for it equally well — and, if both can, whether the disagreement is empirically idle or still matters for some other reason.
2. Whether "God's habit" differs from a Humean regularity in any way that does real theoretical work, or whether occasionalism collapses into a regularity theory with extra theological overhead.
3. The inductive worry both directions: if every connection is contingent on God's will, what justifies trusting that the sun will rise tomorrow, and does the Ash'arite tradition have resources here that are not simply question-begging? Conversely, is the powers theorist's "necessary connection" even intelligible, given that all we ever observe is succession?
4. Human action: whether occasionalism must extend to our own choices — God creating our acts as well — and what that does to agency and moral responsibility, and whether the powers theorist actually fares any better on this front.
5. Whether the dispute has any stakes for scientific practice: would a working scientist who converted to occasionalism overnight design a single experiment differently, predict anything differently, or only say different things at conferences?

Requirements for the dialogue itself: both characters should be equally competent; neither is a foil. Each must respond to the other's actual arguments, not rehearsed versions of them, and each should concede at least one genuine point. The dialogue should not end with either side declaring victory; it should end with the two of them locating, as precisely as they can, the exact proposition on which their disagreement still turns and what would settle it if anything could.

After the dialogue, append a brief closing note (a paragraph or a few bullets) that separates what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material — for example, what the seventeenth discussion actually argues versus how scholars still disagree about whether al-Ghazālī himself endorsed unqualified occasionalism or left room for created secondary causes, and whether occasionalism is in real tension with scientific realism or merely with a particular metaphysical gloss on it. Do not present a contested reading of al-Ghazālī as the scholarly consensus.

Aim for roughly 900 to 1,400 words for the dialogue, followed by the note.
