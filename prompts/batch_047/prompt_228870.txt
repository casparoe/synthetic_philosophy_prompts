I TA an intro philosophy course, my recitation sections are ~16 students, 75 minutes, and I want an activity on Moore's paradox -- the weirdness of saying something like "it's raining but I dont believe it is." The catch: I don't want to mention Moore, assertion, or belief-norms up front. I want them to *produce* Moorean sentences themselves and feel the squirm before anyone names anything.

Design me the activity. My rough sketch of what I'm imagining, which you should feel free to overhaul:

Something with sentence-building. I'm thinking students work in pairs and get dealt a pile of fact-cards -- mundane stuff like "there are an even number of chairs in this room," "the cafateria is serving pasta today," "your partner's backpack is blue" -- some checkable on the spot, some not. Their job is to construct announcements by combining a fact-card with a belief-disclaimer ("X, but so-and-so doesn't believe X") and read them out, with the class voting on whether each announcement is "a fine thing to say" or "something's off here." The trick being that the exact same content lands totally fine when it's about someone else ("the chairs are even, but Maya doesn't believe it") and becomes deeply strange in the first person ("the chairs are even, but I don't believe it"), even though both could easily be true. Ideally the vote tally on the board makes the asymmetry visible without me saying a word.

Whatever the final mechanic, I need:

1. Full runnable rules -- setup, materials, round structure, timings, and what the scoring or voting incentives are, if any. There should be some reason for students to actually try the first-person versions rather than playing it safe, otherwise the contrast never shows up.
2. A debrief plan: how to map specific moments from the game onto the pieces of the paradox (the omissive version vs. "p but I believe not-p," the fact that the sentence can be true but not sayable, the first-person/third-person asymmetry, the thought that answering "do you believe p?" seems to just be answering "p?"). Also how to connect it to the cases where Moorean talk sounds fine, like past tense or "I probably don't believe it."
3. Failure modes: what will predictably go wrong. E.g. I'm worried some students will insist the first-person sentence is fine because "I could just be wrong about what I believe," or the whole thing collapses into "it's just a grammar joke." Tell me how to handle the three or four most likely derailments.
4. Be honest about what the activity can't deliver -- which feature of the paradox the game mechanics fundamentally can't reproduce and I have to just supply by talking.

Also keep prep under 20 minutes and don't require any tech.
