I serve on the collections committee of a mid-sized university museum in Europe. Our single Benin Bronze plaque has become a problem we can no longer defer. A provenance review commissioned two years ago has now established, to the committee's satisfaction, that the plaque left Benin City with a British officer who took part in the 1897 punitive expedition and was purchased by our founder at a London auction the following year. Nobody on the committee disputes that the plaque was looted, and our director has publicly said we intend to make things right. The difficulty is that making things right has turned out to mean several incompatible things at once.

We have received two formal claims. The first comes from Nigeria's federal museums authority, which regards all objects taken in 1897 as state property under Nigerian law and proposes to display the plaque in a new national gallery, with our museum credited as the transferring institution. The second comes from the traditional court of the Oba of Benin, whose representatives argue, with considerable historical plausibility, that the bronzes were commissioned by and belonged to the royal palace across generations -- that the wronged party in 1897 was not a modern nation-state that did not yet exist but a royal household that still does. Complicating matters further, our museum's founding charter obliges us to hold objects for public education and forbids deaccessioning into private hands, and it is not clear whether transfer to the Oba's court would count as private transfer. A coalition of students and faculty, meanwhile, insists that any deliberation beyond immediate unconditional return is just delay dressed up as ethics, and a senior donor has privately suggested that we instead offer a permanent loan and fund a digitization project, on the grounds that restitution should not leave our own public with nothing.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before next month's meeting. It should recommend one concrete course of action -- return to the federal authority, return to the Oba's court, loan and digitization, or some structured combination -- and give reasons the committee can actually vote on. In particular, I want the opinion to engage seriously with the question of who the rightful recipient of corrective justice is when the wronged community has multiple competing heirs, rather than treating the choice of recipient as a merely diplomatic detail, and it should give the strongest version of the student coalition's and the donor's positions before setting them aside, if they are to be set aside.
