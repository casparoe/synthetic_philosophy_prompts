So I'm putting together a position sheet for my grad seminar on philosophy of language / modality and I want someone to kick the tires on it before I embarrass myself in front of the group. Here's where I've landed, roughly:

1. Proper names are rigid designators. So "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is necessary a posteriori, and the necessity is genuinely metaphysical -- it's not just that we happened to be ignorant of which planet was which. The ancients were wrong about a fact, not about how words were being used.

2. The standard meter stick case works fine. If I stipulate that "one meter" is to pick out the length of stick S at noon today, then "stick S is one meter long at noon" is something I know a priori even though it's contingent -- the stick could have been a different length. So reference-fixing by description is a legit route to a priori knowledge of contingent truths.

3. Natural kind terms behave basically like names. "Water is H2O" is necessary a posteriori for the same reason the Hesperus case is: both terms lock onto the same underlying stuff in the actual world, and once they do, the identity holds in every world where the stuff exists at all.

4. At the same time, I'm pretty convinced that competent speakers don't need any chemistry to use "water" correctly. What makes something water is that it fits the surface description -- the clear, drinkable liquid that fills the lakes and comes down as rain around here. Whatever satisfies that profile is water, full stop. People in 1700 were talking about water, and they were right about what they were talking about.

5. Conceivability is a defeasible but genuine guide to possibility. If I can really, clearly conceive of a scenario, that's evidence it's possible, and the burden is on whoever says it's not.

6. Pain is identical to some brain state (say C-fiber firing, whatever it turns out to be), and if that identity is true it's necessarily true, since both sides rigidly designate. I can perfectly well imagine pain without any neurons at all -- disembodied minds, octopus pain -- but that's just the a posteriori part doing its thing.

My worry is that 3 and 4 came from different places in my head and I've never checked them against each other. And I'm honestly not sure whether 5 and 6 are a problem or just look like one. Go through the list pair by pair where it matters, tell me which tensions are real, rank them by how bad they are, and for each one say what the cheapest fix would be. Then tell me which single revision would do the most cleanup work, because I'd rather drop one belief than rewire three.
