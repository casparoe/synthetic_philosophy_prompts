I am a PhD candidate writing a dissertation chapter on whether choice architecture of the Thaler–Sunstein sort can be genuinely autonomy-respecting rather than merely autonomy-tolerating. I keep going in circles on the chapter's central claim, so I would like you to commit to a position on my behalf and show me your working.

Here is the precise proposition I want a credence for. Suppose a nudge satisfies what I'll call the Transparency Ladder, a four-rung condition I have defined for my own purposes: (1) the intervention targets a documented reasoning failure rather than the content of the chooser's ends; (2) its existence, mechanism, and sponsor are disclosed in a place where an ordinarily attentive person would encounter it; (3) opting out costs no more than the original choice itself; and (4) the chooser's end-relevant information is left intact rather than strategically filtered. My proposition: a substantial share of real-world nudges that satisfy all four rungs are autonomy-respecting in the strong Millian sense — that is, they treat the chooser as the sovereign author of her own ends — rather than merely less objectionable than outright coercion.

I am not asking for a verdict about nudging in general; I am asking about this idealized subset. Please give me:

1. Your credence in the proposition, as a number.
2. A decomposition of that credence into the sub-questions you take it to depend on. At minimum, I would expect these to include: the probability that nudges actually operate by bypassing deliberation rather than by assisting it (an empirical-psychological question); the probability that autonomy in the relevant sense requires some form of endorsement or deliberative uptake, rather than merely non-interference or the availability of an exit; the probability that rungs 2 and 3 are achievable at scale in practice, given that disclosure may either be ignored or itself become a debiasing nudge; and any further terms you think the credence should factor through — for instance, whether it matters whose failure-detection standard identifies the reasoning failure in rung 1. Assign each a rough probability with one or two lines of justification, and show how you combine them (and note where the combination rule itself is doing work).
3. Identification of the term to which your final number is most sensitive, and the term in which you have least confidence — these need not be the same.
4. Finally, the falsification condition: state the single argument or finding — conceptual or empirical — that would move your credence by the largest amount and in which direction, and say where your decomposition is most likely to be structurally wrong (e.g., a dependence between terms you have treated as independent, or a sub-question you have failed to factor out).

For context, my own credence is roughly 0.45, and I suspect my hesitation lives in the second term above. If your number is far from mine, tell me which sub-question you think I am getting wrong.
