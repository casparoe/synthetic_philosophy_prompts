I am a PhD student in philosophy writing a dissertation on decision-making under moral uncertainty, and I sit as the graduate-student representative on my university's animal research ethics committee. The committee must rule on a proposal from a neuroscience lab to study learning in octopuses using brief electric shocks as an aversive stimulus, administered without analgesia. The lab's position is that standard analgesics have no validated effect in cephalopods, and that in any case the animals are probably not sentient in a morally relevant way.

The committee is split. Roughly half think the evidence for octopus sentience is thin enough that the proposal should be approved as submitted. The other half, including the member I tend to agree with, concede that the sentience hypothesis may well be false but argue that this misses the point: if there is even a modest chance the octopuses suffer, the moral cost of being wrong is so large, across so many animals, that we should require the protocol to be redesigned as though sentience were established, even at real cost to the science. A third member objects that this reasoning is incoherent. On her view, you cannot multiply a probability by a moral cost that one of the candidate moral positions refuses to recognize as a cost at all, and under genuine moral disagreement each member should simply vote her own conscience rather than follow a hedging rule whose output nobody on the committee actually endorses. She adds a slippery-slope worry: if the committee hedges for low-probability moral patients here, there is no principled stopping point, and we will soon be hedging for fish, insects, and cell cultures.

I have volunteered to draft the decision memo. Please write it: recommend approval, rejection, or approval with conditions, state the bottom line in the opening paragraph, and engage seriously with the hedging argument, the conscience objection, and the stopping-point worry, including an honest statement of the strongest consideration against whatever you recommend. Where the memo takes a position on how an institution, as opposed to an individual, should act under moral uncertainty, it should explain why that position is appropriate for a committee with public-facing obligations, not merely defensible in the abstract. Write in continuous prose only: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; the memo should read like a formal letter.
