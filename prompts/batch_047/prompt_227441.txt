So I've been chewing on that whole idea of figuring out what people "really" want by extrapolating what they'd want if they were smarter, better informed, more coherent -- the coherent extrapolated volition thing, or idealized preferences more generally. And I keep testing it against cases and getting verdicts I can't make fit together. Here they are:

My grandma hates cilantro, and no amount of information or deliberation would change that, so "idealized grandma" also hates cilantro -- obviously fine to just serve what actual grandma wants, the idealization does nothing here.

My friend wants to text his ex at 2am while drunk; sober, rested, fully-informed him would beg us to take his phone away. Deferring to idealized him over actual him: completely fine, I'd do it and feel good about it.

My cousin has been depressed for a year and says she wants nothing, doesn't care about anything anymore. Idealized her -- the version with her old capacity to care restored -- would want to see her friends, finish her degree. Nudging her based on idealized-her instead of actual-her: I think this is right, maybe even obligatory, but it already feels paternalistic in a way the drunk texting case doesn't, and I'm not sure why.

A lifelong smoker, fully informed about every risk, coherent, no compulsion, still wants to smoke. Here I think you have to respect it -- the idealization comes out the same as the actual preference, so there's nothing to appeal to over his head.

Now the ones that break me. A woman raised in a community that taught her education isn't for people like her, and she genuinely doesn't want it -- and suppose even her idealized self wouldn't want it, because "not wanting it" is load-bearing for her whole identity. My gut says the idealized preference being stable here doesn't settle anything, it's an adaptive preference and the stability is the symptom. But a devout friend of mine, fully informed, coherent, idealized to the nines, would -- on his own best guess -- end up deconverting, because he thinks the arguments against his faith are strong and he only stays devout by not looking at them. Do I say his "real" preference is atheism? My gut says absolutely not, that's the procedure smuggling in a verdict and calling it discovery. But I can't state the difference between his case and hers in a way that isn't just "the one where I like the outcome."

My tentative principle: defer to the idealized preference only when the gap between actual and idealized is caused by missing information or confusion, not when closing the gap requires changing what the person cares about. But the depression case breaks it -- her gap is exactly a changed capacity to care, and I still want to defer there. And the adaptive preference case breaks it the other way, since her gap IS just missing information and I don't want to defer.

So: what principle, or small set of principles, actually captures these verdicts? State it precisely, say which clause each case motivates, and be honest about which of my verdicts you'd overturn. Also tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: someone's idealized self turns out to want the experience machine / wirehead button, even though actual-them recoils from it -- does my principle commit me to saying that's what they really want?
