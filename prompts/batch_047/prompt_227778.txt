Last weekend I had a long, unresolved argument with a friend about how multi-member bodies should reach decisions, and I want my position stress-tested rather than defended. My position is this: for any collegial body whose verdicts depend on logically connected premises — an appellate court deciding liability on the basis of duty and breach, a tenure committee weighing research, teaching, and service against a threshold standard, a grant panel judging significance and feasibility — the premise-based procedure (vote by majority on each premise separately, then derive the verdict by logic) is the correct default, and the discursive dilemma is a price worth paying rather than a refutation. My friend holds that voting directly on the conclusion is obviously superior, since the conclusion is what the institution exists to decide, and she thinks cases where a defendant wins or loses against the majority's judgment on the outcome itself are simply absurd results that settle the matter.

Tell me what would count against my position. I want a map of its exposure, not a balanced survey. In particular:

First, which kinds of cases are the genuinely damaging ones? My instinct is that a defender of premise-based voting should be more troubled by some configurations than others — for instance, cases where every premise passes by a different majority so that no single member endorses the full rationale, versus cases where a stable majority bloc agrees on everything but loses the verdict anyway. Rank the threatening case-types from fatal to merely awkward, and say which of them a committed premise-based theorist would concede as counting against the view, as opposed to dismissing as framing artifacts.

Second, my fallback in the argument was that the dilemma is really a problem about how the decision problem gets carved into propositions, not about aggregation itself — change the agenda or the individuation of premises and the paradox dissolves. My friend replied that this move makes my position unfalsifiable, since any embarrassing outcome can be redescribed as a bad agenda rather than a bad procedure. Assess whether she's right, and whether being insulated in this way is actually a defect for a view about institutional design, where perhaps no procedure is falsifiable by outcomes alone.

Third, I proposed a hybrid that I thought settled things: use premise-based voting except when the conclusion carries independent normative weight — when the verdict itself, not just its grounds, is something the members have standing to care about (as a convict-or-acquit decision arguably does, and a pure intermediate finding arguably does not). Does this scope restriction save the position, or does it quietly concede my friend's point by letting conclusion-voting in wherever it matters most?

Finally, be honest about your own verdict's stability: state which argument, case, or consideration would lead you to a different overall assessment, and identify the point in your reasoning where you think it is most likely to fail.
