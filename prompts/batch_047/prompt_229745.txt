I recently ended up on the ethics advisory group at my local hospital (long story) and I keep bumping into something that feels philosophically inconsistent, so I want you to act as a moral philosopher who knows Scanlon's contractualism inside out but also understands how actual institutions work. Think it through carefully -- brainstorm the candidate implicit theories first before committing to one.

Here's the practice. In ICU triage, the committee's standing rule is basically: compare patients one at a time and prioritize whoever stands to lose the most. If it comes down to one person who'll die versus a hundred people who'd each be spared a week of misery on a ventilator, the one person wins -- the smaller burdens never get added up across people. But the same committee signs off on vaccine and screening programs whose entire justification is summing up thousands of tiny benefits and burdens across the population, where no single individual's stake comes close to the costs being imposed.

So: what does each half of this practice presuppose about whether burdens and benefits can be aggregated across persons? Is the triage rule secretly committed to something like Scanlon's individualist picture, where only the strength of each person's separate complaint counts, or can a boring utilitarian justify it too on some other grounds? And is the committee just being inconsistent, or is there a principled line -- maybe about identified vs statistical lives, or ex ante vs ex post -- that makes both rules hang together? Finally, if the committee went full contractualist and banned aggregation everywhere, what concretely would have to change, and is there any version of that policy a hospital could actually live with?
