I've come to accept the standard reading of Kavka's toxin puzzle: the prize is unwinnable, because at midnight I will know I have every reason not to drink the toxin and no reason to drink it, so I cannot now rationally intend to drink it -- and this reveals a genuine asymmetry between the rationality of intending an act and the rationality of performing it. Tell me what would count against this position. In particular: which conceivable cases (arrangements of incentives, mechanisms of intention-formation, modifications of the puzzle) would show that the winning intention is after all formable, and would that actually refute the asymmetry claim or merely relocate it? Which results in the theory of dynamic and resolute choice would embarrass the position -- for instance, if it turns out to collapse into a disguised version of a decision theory I'd otherwise reject? And what would it take to show the puzzle trades on an illicit premise about what reasons can survive their own fulfillment? Distinguish defeaters a defender of my view would concede as decisive from ones they would absorb by qualification, rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and say whether the position has been insulated by those qualifications to the point of risking nothing.
