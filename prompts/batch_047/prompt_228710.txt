So I recently rewatched Dr. Strangelove for the first time in years and I want a reading of it through a decision-theory lens I've been chewing on. The idea, roughly: if you have a large group of people who each privately form their own judgement about whether some drastic action is worth taking, and any one of them can just go ahead and do it without needing anyone else's sign-off, then the action becomes way more likely than the group's average judgement would ever support -- because you only need the single most optimistic (or most paranoid, or most reckless) member to pull the trigger, and that person by definition has the most extreme reading of the evidence. The group's collective caution is worthless; the distribution's worst tail decides everything.

Strangelove seems almost engineered to illustrate this: Plan R gives lower commanders authority to launch on their own judgement under certain conditions, the CRM-114 device makes the attack unrecallable once started, and Ripper is exactly the tail of the distribution, the one guy whose private "evidence" (fluoridation, precious bodily fluids) nobody else shares. But the film also seems to complicate the idea in ways I find interesting. The curse as I've described it assumes everyone is a basically rational estimator with noisy signals -- Ripper isn't miscalibrated, he's just mad, so is it really the same phenomenon? And the doomsday machine is the opposite design move: it removes unilateral human discretion entirely by automating retaliation, which the film treats as also catastrophic. So the film seems to say you're doomed whether discretion is distributed (someone acts alone) or eliminated (no one can stop the machine).

Write me an essay-style reading of the film along these lines: where does it nail the logic of the unilateralist's curse, where does it get it subtly wrong or trade on a different failure mode, and what does the ending (Strangelove's mineshaft survival plan, the actual detonation) suggest about whether the problem is fixable at all? I'd especially like you to take seriously the possibility that Kubrick is making a point about institutional design that the abstract version of the argument misses -- something about how the "rational" people in the war room (Turgidson, de Sadesky, the President) each make locally reasonable moves that compound into disaster, which is arguably a different curse than Ripper's. Keep it grounded in actual scenes, since I can't rewatch it before I have to talk about this at our film-and-philosophy night next week.
