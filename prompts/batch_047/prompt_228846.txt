Next month I'm giving a work-in-progress talk to my department's philosophy of science seminar, presenting the central chapter of my dissertation, and I want a serious prep sheet for the question period. My chapter defends a particular line on Goodman's new riddle of induction. The standard natural-kinds reply says 'green' is projectible and 'grue' (green if examined before some future time t, blue otherwise) is not, because green picks out a natural kind and grue doesn't. My chapter argues this reply fails for a reason I haven't seen pressed in the literature: grue-style predicates can be constructed out of theoretical vocabulary too, not just observation vocabulary -- one can define 'grue charge' or 'grue mass' with a time-indexed switch, and whatever makes charge and mass natural does not by itself tell us why the grue variants are illegitimate, since the naturalness was supposed to be doing the excluding. My positive proposal is that projectibility should be relativized to test procedures: a predicate is projectible relative to a way of testing generalizations involving it, and grue-type predicates have no uniform test procedure across the boundary time, which is why they can't support induction. So my claim is that the new riddle is really about the epistemology of testing, not about metaphysics of kinds or about entrenchment.

The audience is about fifteen people. Three matter most. There's a Goodman scholar in the department who works on the historical reception of Fact, Fiction, and Forecast and tends to object on exegetical grounds -- she'll likely say I've misunderstood what the new riddle was supposed to show, namely that confirmation is irreducibly language-relative, and that treating it as a problem with a 'solution' already misses the point. There are two formal epistemologists, Bayesian confirmation theorists, who think the grue problem is handled by priors -- grue hypotheses just get lower prior probability -- and who are generally skeptical that anything non-formal is needed. And there's a metaphysician who defends a naturalness-based account and will want to defend the kinds-reply against my theoretical-vocabulary argument. The rest are graduate students and a couple of philosophers of biology who mostly follow along. I have about forty minutes of Q&A.

Prepare me a prep sheet. Give me the objections I'll actually get from this audience, in roughly the order and form they'll come, each phrased the way that specific person would phrase it -- the Goodman scholar sounds different from the Bayesians. For each objection: how much damage it does if it lands, what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and a reply I can deliver in two minutes or less, with a note on where the reply is soft. I especially need help with the obvious circularity worry (test procedures will presumably have to be individuated using predicates that are themselves assumed projectible), and with whether my relativization move just reproduces the language-relativity the Goodman scholar says was the point all along. Tell me honestly which of my two arguments -- the grue-ification of theoretical vocabulary against the kinds-reply, or the positive test-procedure account -- is the weaker link and will draw more fire.
