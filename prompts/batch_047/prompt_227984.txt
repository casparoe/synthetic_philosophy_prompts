I am a novelist revising a manuscript whose plot turns on a sequence of rescue decisions. To keep the story’s moral logic consistent, I have been testing the scenes on a philosopher friend whose instincts are broadly influenced by Singer’s drowning-child argument but who is not a simple expected-utility maximizer. Her verdicts so far are:

1. If she is the only person who can save a child drowning in a shallow ornamental pond, and doing so will ruin an £800 suit, she is morally required to wade in.
2. If the same child is caught in a dangerous current and any attempted rescue would carry a one-in-ten chance of killing her, she is not required to attempt it, though doing so would be admirable.
3. If she can donate £800 to a thoroughly vetted charity that will certainly use that marginal gift to save an unseen child abroad, she is required to donate. Distance, nationality, and lack of personal acquaintance make no difference.
4. If the charity can save one child for every four £800 gifts but cannot connect any particular gift to any particular child, she is still required to donate. Statistical rather than identifiable rescue does not by itself excuse her.
5. If she could steal £800 from a billionaire who would never notice the loss and use it to save a child with certainty, she would not be permitted to steal, even though refusing to donate £800 of her own would be wrong.
6. If saving the child in the pond requires breaking a trivial dinner engagement, she is required to break it. Not every claim or commitment is an absolute constraint.
7. If she can spend the next ten minutes either rescuing the child before her or arranging certain rescue for two equally endangered children abroad, she should rescue the child before her. She says the immediate emergency has priority, even though acting otherwise would save more lives.

The climax of my novel introduces a harder case. During a flood, the protagonist encounters an unconscious child whose survival probability is 10 percent unless she receives the single dose carried by a privately operated medical drone. The drone is already en route to another child, similarly endangered and otherwise alike, whose survival probability will be 70 percent if the dose arrives as scheduled. By rerouting the drone without the owner’s permission, the protagonist can raise the first child’s survival probability to 90 percent while reducing the second child’s to 20 percent. No third option is available, she bears no responsibility for either child’s danger, and she would face neither physical danger nor punishment. The owner has expressly refused permission for the diversion.

Predict my friend’s verdict. First reconstruct at least three internally coherent principles that fit all seven earlier verdicts—for example, principles concerning personal sacrifice, probabilistic rescue, immediate emergencies, property, and the redirection of resources already assigned to someone else. Then state what each principle predicts about the drone case and identify the closest precedent. Give your best guess about her actual verdict despite the underdetermination, explain why that guess best preserves her pattern, and describe one further rescue case whose verdict would most sharply distinguish the two leading principles. Finally, say what each possible verdict would reveal about the difference between sacrificing one’s own resources to create a rescue and commandeering a rescue already owed to another person.
