I curate a permanent exhibition at a regional museum covering the destruction of an indigenous community in the 1890s: a massacre of several hundred people, followed by the seizure of their land, the removal of their children to distant boarding schools where their language was forbidden, and the burning of their religious objects. A descendants' association has petitioned us to change the exhibit's main label from "massacre and dispossession" to "genocide", and our board is split. The dispute keeps circling back to a passage in Chapter IX of Raphael Lemkin's Axis Rule in Occupied Europe (1944), which one trustee has paraphrased to the rest of us: Lemkin there describes genocide as unfolding in two phases, the first being the destruction of the national pattern of the oppressed group, and the second the imposition of the oppressor's own pattern, whether on the surviving population or on the emptied territory itself. His opponents on the board reply that whatever Lemkin originally meant, the word as now used in law and journalism is tied to the physical extermination of a group, and that a museum has no business stretching it further.

I am not asking you to adjudicate the history. What I want to understand is what each side of our dispute is quietly committed to. Does the trustees' caution presuppose that "genocide" is a natural kind whose members can be discovered by checking features against a definition, while the petitioners treat it instead as a moral verdict or a legal conclusion? Does Lemkin's two-phase description, as paraphrased above, actually settle anything about our label, or does it merely push the argument back one step, onto what counts as the destruction of a "pattern"? And what view of what words do — in particular of whether naming an atrocity performs some further act of recognition or obligation beyond describing it — would make sense of the fact that everyone at the table, on both sides, behaves as though a great deal hangs on one word? Please answer in British English.
