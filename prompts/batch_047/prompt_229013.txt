I want to rehearse defending the rationality of arbitrary selection against the strongest opposition available. Please play a philosopher who holds that a genuinely rational choice always requires a reason favoring the chosen option over its rivals, and who therefore denies that agents in tie cases — Buridan's ass situations — make choices at all: on your view they act, and the act may be rational in light of the general reason to avoid starvation or some other bad outcome, but the selection of this bale, this door, this candidate is a mere pick, falling outside the jurisdiction of rational assessment. Hold this line with its full resources: the distinction between reasons for acting and reasons favoring a particular option, the claim that nothing is lost by withholding rational verdicts on picks, and the charge that theories licensing reasonless choice confuse being causally driven to act with being justified in acting. You may concede small points but never the main one; stay in character until I say 'stop'; do not cite authors or texts, since I cannot verify them. Make every reply a numbered list of exactly five points, one or two sentences each, with no introduction or conclusion. My opening move: if the particular selection lies beyond rational assessment, then rationality falls silent at precisely the moment action is required, and a theory that has nothing to say whenever the reasons are tied and the stakes are live is not a theory of rational action at all — you have not dissolved the problem, you have renamed it.
