I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've been stuck on something since an incident review at work last quarter. We operate a large distributed system, and we suffered a cascading outage in which, as far as we could establish, every service behaved exactly as designed: clients retried with backoff, load balancers shed traffic, autoscalers spun up capacity. No component malfunctioned, yet the system as a whole collapsed under a retry storm that no individual node in any sense "decided" to produce. In the postmortem, a colleague wrote that the failure was a property of the system rather than of any of its parts, and that sentence has been rattling around in my head ever since. It brought to mind something I half-remembered, and when I tracked it down it turned out to be from Émile Durkheim's The Rules of Sociological Method (1895), where his second rule for the explanation of social facts states that "[t]he determining cause of a social fact should be sought among the social facts preceding it and not among the states of the individual consciousness."

Durkheim appears to be claiming something strong here: not merely that it is sometimes convenient to explain social phenomena by reference to other social phenomena, but that the real causal action operates at the social level, so that appeals to individual psychology are simply the wrong place to look. My outage makes this feel intuitive — the retry storm wasn't located in any node; it lived in the interactions, and a purely node-by-node audit would never have surfaced it. But another colleague pushed back hard: the storm was entirely determined by the code, the configuration, and the network conditions, so a sufficiently exhaustive component-level analysis would in principle have predicted it. Calling it a "system-level cause," on his view, is just an honest confession of our ignorance about how the parts compose — and, by analogy, Durkheim's rule is at best a practical heuristic and at worst a reification of abstractions. There is no retry storm over and above the packets, and no society over and above the individuals.

Please write an essay that uses this analogy as a test case for adjudicating the individualism–holism dispute in the social sciences. Weigh Durkheim's position against my colleague's rejoinder, while keeping distinct three questions that the postmortem debate conflated: whether social phenomena are ontologically reducible to facts about individuals; whether social-level explanations are dispensable in principle; and whether they are dispensable in practice for finite inquirers like us. Consider where the analogy between distributed systems and societies holds (interactions producing outcomes no part intended or contained), where it breaks down (my services don't internalize norms, form beliefs about the system, or owe their very identity as "services" to the system they constitute), and what the right verdict implies for how Durkheim's rule should be read — as a claim about causation, about explanation, or only about method. Reach and defend a conclusion rather than surveying the options; a conditional verdict (say, holism about explanation but individualism about ontology) is acceptable, provided you argue for it instead of simply splitting the difference. Finally, tell me how confident you are in your verdict, and single out explicitly the step in your argument that you are least sure of.
