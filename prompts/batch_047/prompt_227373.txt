So I recently got put in charge of a small research institute's internal funding committee -- we review maybe a dozen ongoing projects twice a year and decide what keeps getting funded. The problem is the decisions are basically vibes right now. Senior people defend their pet projects, nobody wants to kill anything, and "give it one more year" is the default answer to everything.

I read some Lakatos a while back and I keep thinking his progressive vs. degenerating research programme stuff is exactly the framework we need, but as he writes it it's way too loose to actually run a meeting with. Like, novel predictions, the protective belt absorbing anomalies, when is a problemshift content-increasing vs. just patching things ad hoc -- I want that turned into an actual procedure we can run on a project in about an hour of committee time.

Concretely, I'd like:

- A step-by-step review protocol: what questions to ask about a project, in what order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, and how to tell a genuine novel prediction from something retrofitted after the fact (this is the part I expect people to game).
- Some way to handle the obvious objection that a programme is allowed to go through rough patches -- Lakatos himself says you don't abandon ship at the first anomaly -- so the procedure needs a principled answer to "how long do we wait" rather than just a vibe about patience.
- Clear stopping rules: where the procedure outputs "defund," where it outputs "fund," and where it has to punt to human judgment because the question isn't one a checklist can settle. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedure costume.
- Then run the thing on two made-up projects so I can see it in action: (1) a psychology project that keeps "predicting" effects it's already found, with the hypotheses getting more elaborate each cycle to explain away the failed replications; (2) a materials science project that's had two quiet years but whose one surprising result last year opened up a genuinely new line of experiments.

One more constraint: one of the projects we review is my own former PhD topic, so the procedure will ideally produce at least one verdict that's uncomfortable for someone in the room -- if yours can't plausibly do that, it's probably too soft and I'd like you to say where you think it would get watered down in practice. Also say what the procedure just can't do, so I don't oversell it to the committee. Don't bother with citations or a reading list, I can track down the sources myself.
