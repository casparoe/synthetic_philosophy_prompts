I am preparing a graduate seminar on Molinism. Before comparing it with Thomism, open theism, and simple foreknowledge, I want a specification for what any theory of divine providence based on middle knowledge would have to accomplish.

Begin with one sentence giving your bottom-line judgment about the hardest requirement—or the strongest cluster of mutually incompatible requirements—facing such a theory. Do not preface the answer or restate the assignment. Then give the specification itself, preferably as a table followed by analysis.

For each desideratum, state:

1. what the theory must be able to do;
2. why that is required for an account of providence rather than merely desired by one theological camp;
3. a concrete case that would test it;
4. whether it is non-negotiable, negotiable, or a partisan preference.

Address at least the following issues:

- the grounding problem: what makes a counterfactual of creaturely freedom true when the creature has not yet chosen, or perhaps does not yet exist;
- explanatory priority: how God’s creative decision can be guided by middle knowledge without the relevant counterfactuals becoming true because God decides to create;
- libertarian freedom: whether the theory preserves the agent’s ability to do otherwise, and whether ability, causal indeterminacy, and sourcehood should be distinguished;
- would-counterfactuals versus might-counterfactuals: whether providence requires a determinate truth about what every possible free creature would do in every fully specified circumstance, or only probabilities;
- chance: what middle knowledge could amount to if a genuinely free action has no fact of the matter about which alternative would be chosen;
- feasible worlds: how the theory explains why God cannot simply actualize a world in which every free creature always chooses rightly, without assuming the conclusion in favor of Molinism;
- prophecy and petitionary prayer: how God can infallibly reveal or arrange future contingents without causing the free actions themselves;
- moral responsibility: how the theory avoids making God the author of sin while retaining meticulous providence;
- temporal versus logical priority: how much of the account changes if God is timeless;
- evil and damnation: what kinds of divine permission the theory can justify, and what explanations become unavailable if there is no best feasible world.

Also identify any subset of these desiderata that appears jointly unsatisfiable. Be explicit about whether the conflict is logical, metaphysical, or merely theological, and say which requirement a Molinist would most plausibly weaken.

Finally, briefly test classical Molinism, a probabilistic or open-future variant, Thomist divine causation, and simple foreknowledge against the specification. Keep this scorecard subordinate to the requirements themselves. Do not supply citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; reason from the concepts and cases directly.
