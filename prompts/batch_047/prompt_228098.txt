I serve on the five-member governing board of a learned society for amateur astronomers, about 3,800 members, and I need a memo I can circulate before our quarterly meeting in three weeks. The board must vote on whether to adopt, amend, or reject a staff proposal for distributing a donor's bequest, and the proposal raises a design question I want the memo to confront head-on.

The background: a deceased member left us $1,000,000 with the stipulation that it be distributed to the membership by a mechanism that, in her executor's paraphrase, rewards collective restraint. Staff have proposed what they call the Perseid Prize. Any member may enter, free of charge, during a one-week window, and may submit as many entries as they like. After the window closes, the fund is divided by the total number of entries received to determine the value of a single entry; one entry is then drawn at random, and the member who submitted it receives that amount. The remainder of the fund stays in the endowment. Staff's pitch is that the mechanism is self-regulating: every member can see that a flood of entries dilutes the prize to nothing, so every member will submit few entries or none, keeping the per-entry value high; and since each member faces exactly the same situation and the same reasoning, all will converge on that restrained strategy, whatever it is, so no one is exploited for holding back.

Before proposing this, staff ran a pilot with 240 volunteer members and a $24,000 fund. Two hundred eleven volunteers submitted exactly one entry. Twenty-six submitted more than ten thousand each, three of them using scripts; the largest single submission was a number written in mathematical notation that our software could not parse and had to be treated as an overflow. The final per-entry value came to 1.7 cents, and the drawn winner received a check for that amount. Staff call the pilot a success because, they argue, it shows the membership is overwhelmingly cooperative, and the defectors can be handled by capping entries per member at, say, one hundred. Two board members are unpersuaded and want the whole thing scrapped in favor of dividing the bequest into ordinary equal shares.

A junior staffer has also floated an alternative she considers more elegant: a prize awarded only if exactly one member claims it during the window, in which case that sole claimant receives $100,000 and the rest of the fund returns to the endowment; if zero or more than one member claims, nothing is paid out at all. She argues that rational members, knowing every other member is reasoning identically, will each adopt the same probabilistic strategy rather than the same categorical one.

Please draft the memo, around nine hundred words, addressed to the board. It should end in a concrete, votable recommendation, and it must do three things: first, give the strongest honest version of the case for the Perseid Prize as staff designed it, including whether the pilot actually supports or undermines it; second, take a position on whether an institution may legitimately build a mechanism whose success depends on every participant, being identically situated, arriving at the same conclusion, and if so what distinguishes a mechanism that harnesses that convergence from one that merely hopes for it; third, assess the junior staffer's variant on its merits rather than dismissing it as a curiosity, including whether the entry cap proposed for the Perseid Prize dissolves the original problem or merely renames it.
