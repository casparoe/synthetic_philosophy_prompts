Last week a close friend and I got into an argument about Kurt Vonnegut's story Harrison Bergeron that ran well past midnight and ended with neither of us conceding an inch. Her position: the story is a decisive reductio of egalitarianism as such. Any serious political commitment to equality, pushed far enough, becomes the Handicapper General, and Vonnegut simply had the honesty to follow the idea to its end. My position: the story skewers only one crude answer to the question of what, exactly, egalitarians should be trying to equalize — something like equality of demonstrated performance across whatever traits happen to be visible — and that the serious contenders in the philosophical literature, namely equality of welfare, equality of resources, and the capability approach, each diagnose the story's dystopia differently, and most of them condemn it at least as firmly as my friend does. She says I'm protecting egalitarianism by redefining it; I say she's refuting a position nobody actually holds.

Please write the reading I wish I'd had at the table: an essay interpreting Harrison Bergeron through the equality-of-what debate, which I can send to her afterward. Specifically:

- Take each of the three main currencies of equality in turn — welfare, resources, capabilities — and ask what the regime in the story actually equalizes, and whether it succeeds even on its own terms. Does weighting down the strong equalize resources or merely destroy them? Does masking the beautiful and scrambling the thoughts of the intelligent equalize capabilities, or does it systematically prevent capability from ever being exercised, which is arguably the opposite of what the capability approach wants?
- Address my friend's strongest point directly: the regime's officials might reply that outcome-equality across people is the only kind that doesn't smuggle in a theory of the good, and that welfarist, resourcist, and capability metrics each impose contested judgments about what a life should contain. Give that reply its due before answering it.
- Consider whether the story's regime is better described as pursuing equality at all, or as leveling down — and whether the leveling-down objection, which philosophers already press against egalitarianism in the abstract, is doing all the real work in the story's horror.
- Be honest about where the story resists my reading. Vonnegut's satire is broader and sloppier than any taxonomy: note where it seems to mock not a metric but the very project of politically enforcing any metric, and where it seems to be aiming at something else altogether — television, distraction, the public's amnesia in the final scene.
- End by saying what, if anything, the story adds to this debate that the journal literature doesn't — for instance, whether fiction can show something about the lived texture of enforced equality that the leveling-down objection can only state abstractly.

The reading should be one my friend would recognize as fair to her side: neither a strawman of Vonnegut as a primitive libertarian pamphleteer, nor a strawman of egalitarianism as obviously innocent of the charge.
