I'm a doctoral student working on the normativity of meaning, and a friend in my cohort — who has been reading the rule-following literature with more enthusiasm than charity, in my view — has put together an argument she thinks shows that no speaker ever computes any function at all, not even a finite one. We argued about it over dinner and I couldn't cleanly locate the error, though I'm sure the conclusion is false. Here is the argument, as faithfully as I can reconstruct it:

(1) For any speaker S and any finite stretch of S's actual behaviour with a symbol — every answer S has ever given, every keystroke, every utterance — there are infinitely many distinct functions consistent with that behaviour, differing only on inputs S has never encountered. (2) S's dispositions, however idealised, are finite in reach and subject to interference, so they underdetermine which of these functions S is tracking. (3) The facts surveyed in (1) and (2) — past use and dispositions — exhaust the candidate facts about S that could fix which function S computes; anything further (an inner intention, a mental state) is itself just another item that stands in need of interpretation and so inherits the same indeterminacy. (4) Appealing to the community's use or dispositions merely aggregates more finite, error-prone behaviour, and aggregation cannot manufacture determinacy out of what is severally indeterminate. (5) Therefore no fact about S, actual or possible, determines which function S computes. (6) If no fact determines which function S computes, then S does not determinately compute any function. (7) Therefore S does not compute any function — not addition, not even the successor function on the first hundred natural numbers. Since S was arbitrary, nobody has ever computed anything.

Now, the conclusion strikes me as something no one can actually believe — my friend presumably computed a tip at dinner. But each premise seemed reasonable when she stated it, and I found myself conceding steps I now regret. What I'd like from you: identify which premise or step should be rejected, and explain why it looked compelling in the first place. Please also price the alternatives — that is, for each of the standard responses in this neighbourhood (the reductive dispositionalist who digs in at (2), the meaning primitivist who rejects (3), the communitarian who resists (4), and the sceptical-solution theorist who accepts (5) but blocks the move from (5) to (6) by reconstruing what meaning-attributions are for), say what each has to give up and which cost is smallest. Two constraints on your answer: I would strongly prefer to keep the claim that meaning is genuinely normative, and I would strongly prefer to keep the claim that there are facts about what people mean. If one of these has to go, tell me which and why; if both can be kept, show me how the argument's appearance of soundness is to be explained away.
