Next month I am giving the after-dinner speech at the retirement celebration of my doctoral supervisor, a radio astronomer who spent four decades mapping distant galaxies. Half the room will be her colleagues and former students; the other half will be her family, her neighbors, and her book club, none of whom know a quasar from a pulsar. Every retirement speech she has ever sat through, she tells me, has reached for the same move: the universe is unimaginably vast, our lives are vanishingly brief and small, and isn't it humbling and strangely beautiful that she gave hers to the stars. She finds that move cheap, and I have promised not to make it.

Instead I want to build the speech around an idea from Frank Ramsey, the Cambridge philosopher and mathematician who died in 1930 at only twenty-six. In a talk he gave as a very young man, Ramsey refused the conclusion that the scale of the cosmos should make us feel insignificant. His picture of the world, he said, was drawn in perspective rather than to scale: the foreground is occupied by human beings, and the heavens, for all their physical enormity, are in the background, because the stars cannot think or love or take an interest in anything, and we can. Size, on his view, is not the same thing as importance, and there is nothing servile owed to mere bigness. He paired this with a genuine excitement about humanity's future, despite knowing his own would be short.

Please write the speech, roughly six hundred and fifty words. It should do three things at once: honor a woman who devoted her working life to objects billions of light-years away; refuse, on Ramsey's grounds, the standard rhetoric in which that devotion makes her small; and end by looking forward, as Ramsey did, to what human beings may yet do and understand. The astronomers in the room should feel that the argument is real and not a greeting-card inversion of the usual cliché, and the family should be able to follow every sentence without a single technical term being explained to them. Write it as continuous prose suitable for reading aloud, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
