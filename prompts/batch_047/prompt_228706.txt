I'm running a single ninety-minute seminar for a first-year philosophy reading group on the cogito, and I want you to plan how I should teach it rather than teach it yourself. The students will arrive knowing "I think, therefore I am" as a cultural slogan and little else. I expect most of them to assume it is a three-line syllogism that proves the existence of an immaterial soul, and a few of the cleverer ones to have picked up somewhere that it's circular and therefore worthless. Both groups are wrong, and I want the session to leave them understanding what the argument does and does not establish.

Plan the explanatory arc with attention to what must be in place first: the students need to grasp methodological doubt as a tool rather than a personality disorder before the cogito can even be posed as a question, and they need some grip on the difference between being psychologically unable to doubt something and that thing being epistemically secure. Tell me which formulation of the argument to build the session around and what it must have and must lack to do the work I need — in particular, whether to present it as an inference with a hidden premise or as something performed rather than derived, and what each choice costs me pedagogically.

Map the misconceptions in the order you expect them to surface during discussion — that "therefore" signals a conventional argument, that the "I" is smuggled in where only a thinking is warranted, that surviving doubt proves a persisting self rather than a momentary thinker, that certainty here licenses certainty elsewhere — and mark the point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than corrected after it takes root. Specify the check for understanding that would show a student has grasped the scope of the conclusion, not just its charm. Finally, tell me what to leave out at this level (the wax passage, the trademark argument, mind-body dualism, the later reception) and why omitting it is principled rather than lazy.

Since the plan will inevitably rest on a reading of an argument whose correct interpretation is contested, flag where your pedagogical choices lean on one interpretive side rather than the other, state how confident you are in each of those choices, and single out the part of the plan you are least sure of.
