I'm a high school senior in an extracurricular philosophy club, and this month we're working through the metaphysics of dispositions. I've hit a distinction I can't get a grip on, and I'd like it explained carefully. I understand the simple conditional analysis of dispositions: a glass is fragile, roughly, because it would break if struck. I also understand the classic problem cases where a "fink" intervenes — for instance, a wire that is live, but connected to a device that would cut the power the instant anyone touched it, so it would not deliver a shock even though we still want to call it live. What I don't understand is how a "mask" is supposed to be different. People say that a glass packed in styrofoam is still fragile but merely masked: it would not break if struck, yet the fragility remains in place. To me this looks structurally identical to the fink case — in both, the conditional fails while the disposition allegedly persists. So my real question is: what, precisely, distinguishes removing a disposition from merely blocking its manifestation, and is that a difference in the object, in the interfering factor, or just in how we choose to describe the situation? Relatedly, I invented a case and can't classify it: a glass coated in a chemical that hardens the glass the moment a strike is imminent, then softens again afterward, so it never actually breaks. Is the coating a fink (because it temporarily removes the fragility), a mask (because the glass is "really" still fragile underneath), or does the case show the distinction breaking down? Finally, does any of this force us to abandon the conditional analysis entirely, or can it be patched? Please give your answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two long, with no introduction or conclusion.
