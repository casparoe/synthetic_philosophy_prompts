A friend and I spent last night arguing about spectrum arguments against the transitivity of "better than," and we reached an impasse: she insists that if A is better than B, and B than C, and so on down a long sequence of pairwise comparisons, then A must be better than the final item — yet the final item is clearly worse, and neither of us could point to the single step where the comparison fails. Rather than continue the argument in prose, I'd like a poem that enacts the problem instead of describing it. Write a poem of at least twelve short stanzas in which each stanza differs from its predecessor by only one minimal alteration (a single word changed, added, or removed), beginning with a stanza that describes something plainly good and ending with one that describes something plainly bad. The constraint should do the philosophical work: a reader should be able to compare the first and last stanzas and immediately agree which is better, while being unable to identify the step at which "better" broke down — mirroring how each link in the spectrum seems locally undeniable while the whole chain yields an absurdity. Close with a turn that poses, without resolving, the question of what has to give: transitivity itself, or our confidence in the pairwise comparisons. Someone familiar with the literature on intransitivity and spectrum arguments should recognize the poem as an accurate rendering of the problem, not merely a meditation on gradual change.
