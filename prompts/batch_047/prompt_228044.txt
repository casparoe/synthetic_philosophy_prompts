I'm a software engineer, and I keep running into a particular move in conversations about machine intelligence and, lately, whole-brain emulation. A senior colleague of mine argues that the brain is a computer, full stop, and he treats John von Neumann as the authority who made this respectable -- after all, von Neumann designed the architecture our machines still run on and spent his last years writing a book comparing the computer and the brain (published posthumously in 1958 as The Computer and the Brain, based on lectures he never got to deliver). My colleague's conclusion is that substrate doesn't matter: neurons are just slow, wet hardware, and once we can simulate the relevant computation faithfully enough -- or once our neural nets get big enough -- we have the mind, running on new hardware. Details like ion channels, glial cells, neuromodulators, and the body are, on his telling, implementation details, the way vacuum tubes versus transistors are implementation details.

Here's what makes this interesting to me: I skimmed the von Neumann book he's invoking, and it seems to me to cut against him. Von Neumann draws the analogy and then spends much of the book on disanalogies -- he argues the brain is a mixed analog-and-digital system rather than a purely digital one, that individual neurons are imprecise and that the brain compensates with massive parallelism rather than clock speed, and that the brain's internal scheme for representing and transmitting information is fundamentally unlike the formal languages of mathematics and logic we design computers around.

So please audit this analogy properly. Give me the correspondence table first: in the strongest version of brain-as-computer, what plays the role of the CPU, the clock, the memory, the stored program, the hardware/software distinction, and the input/output channels? Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and -- this is the part I care about -- which of those breakdowns actually matter for my colleague's substrate-independence conclusion and which are superficial. In particular: does von Neumann's mixed analog/digital point threaten the idea that a digital simulation reproduces the mind, or only that it reproduces it efficiently? Does the imprecision-plus-parallelism point show the brain computes something different, or just that it computes differently? Does his point about the brain's representational scheme bear on whether a simulated brain would be a mind, or only on whether we could understand it? Finally: does my colleague's conclusion survive once the disanalogies are priced in, or was the analogy doing the argumentative work the whole time? And if brain-as-computer is the wrong analogy for this purpose, is there a better one -- or is the honest answer that we don't have a good source domain for the brain at all?
