Got into an argument with a friend who's basically a Hobbes/Xunzi type -- thinks people are selfish by default and only law plus incentives keep us civilized. I pushed back with "Mencius says human nature is good" and he just asked me what that even *means*, and honestly I got stuck. Then I went and actually read some Mencius (Legge translation) and found this weird exchange with the philosopher Kâo in Book 6, Part 1, ch. 3. Kâo says nature just means life, i.e. whatever you're born with, and Mencius needles him: "Is the whiteness of a white feather like that of white snow, and the whiteness of white snow like that of white jade?" and then "Is the nature of a dog like the nature of an ox, and the nature of an ox like that of a man?"

So now I'm not even sure the slogan is an empirical claim at all. Can you lay out, as a menu, the different precise theses "human nature is good" could be expressing -- e.g. a statistical claim about tendencies, a claim about capacities, a claim about how people develop absent interference, a normative claim dressed up as descriptive, or (as the passage above makes me suspect) a stipulative move about what counts as a thing's "nature" in the first place, which would make the slogan almost true by definition? For each reading: is it trivial, substantive-but-defensible, or just false? And for each, say (a) whether the feather/snow/jade argument actually supports it or secretly just assumes it, and (b) whether it's a reading my Xunzi-fan friend would even bother denying, or whether he'd shrug and say it does no work against him. End with which reading you think best fits that Kâo exchange, because honestly the whiteness bit reads to me like Mencius is playing language games.
