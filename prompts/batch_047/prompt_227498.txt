I have a set of verdicts about when an agent displays weakness of will, and I would like to know what account of weakness, if any, they add up to.

Case 1. At dinner, Theo weighs everything and sincerely concludes that, all things considered, he ought to decline the third drink; he says so to a friend, the judgment is vivid and undisturbed, and ten minutes later he orders the drink anyway. My verdict: paradigmatic weakness of will.

Case 2. Priya decides at noon that she will be in bed by ten, having weighed the very considerations she expects to be salient all evening. At 9:50, midway through a film, she reflectively and sincerely judges that finishing it is worth the tired morning; she stays up and regrets it at seven. My verdict: no weakness here at all, because her judgment at the moment of action endorsed the act.

Case 3. Marcus knows that his 9:50 self will flip the way Priya's does, so at noon he sets a device that locks his screen at ten and forfeits a deposit if overridden. At 9:50 he curses the device but leaves it alone. My verdict: Marcus displays a genuine achievement of will — successful self-government — even though no temptation was resisted in the moment.

Case 4. Amara plans to begin her dissertation on Monday. On Monday she finds she simply cannot start, not because her judgment has shifted — she still judges she should — but because, as she puts it, the judgment feels inert, something she watches from a distance rather than acts from. My verdict: weakness, indeed the clearest case of the five.

Case 5. Yusuf plans to skip a party to finish a grant application, then learns at the last minute that a collaborator he badly needs will attend; he reconsiders and goes. My verdict: plainly not weakness.

The difficulty I cannot resolve is this. Cases 2 and 3 seem to pull against each other: if Priya's evening judgment-flip exempts her from weakness, then Marcus's device constrains an agent who was never weak in the first place, and it becomes unclear what his achievement consists in — yet my verdicts insist there is one. Cases 1 and 4 together put pressure on Case 2: if weakness requires acting against a judgment held at the moment of action, then the line between Priya's flip and a mere rationalization of the same temptation becomes load-bearing, and I am not sure I can distinguish Case 2 from a version of Case 1 in which Theo's judgment conveniently clouds over as the waiter approaches. And I suspect, without being confident, that Case 5 is the control case: revising judgment in response to new information is fine, while revising it in response to a prospect's mere proximity is not — but I cannot state the difference precisely.

Is there a single, precisely statable account of weakness of will that delivers all five verdicts — separating judgment-sensitive weakness from preference reversal, crediting precommitment as an achievement, and marking off rational reconsideration from motivated drift? If not, which verdict is cheapest to surrender, and why? Whatever account you settle on, say what it predicts for two cases I have not listed: an agent with stable, consciously endorsed present-biased preferences, who at every time judges that he prefers the nearer reward and so never acts against his judgment; and an agent whose discounting is time-consistent but steep, who at every time prefers the earlier reward and never reverses. If the account treats those two differently, say whether that asymmetry is a feature or a defect.
