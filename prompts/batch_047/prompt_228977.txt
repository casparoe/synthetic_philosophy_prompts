In 2030, for the four-hundredth anniversary of Johannes Kepler's death, the Swabian town of Weil der Stadt, where he was born, is unveiling a new monument to him, and I — a historian of astronomy — have been asked to give the main address, about ten minutes. The audience will be awkwardly mixed: the mayor and town council, a group of schoolchildren, the local amateur astronomical society (whose members visibly wince at the word astrology), and, because the invitations went out broadly, a delegation from a German astrological association that has been loudly claiming Kepler as one of its own. The mayor has urged me to keep the remarks dignified and uncontroversial, which I suspect would amount to saying nothing at all.

The problem is that the honest Kepler satisfies none of these audiences. He earned a substantial part of his living as a working astrologer: from his years as a provincial mathematician in Graz through his tenure as imperial mathematician in Prague, horoscopes and annual prognostic calendars were simply part of the job, and his first calendar, for 1595, was remembered for decades because it apparently foretold both a bitterly cold winter and a peasant uprising. Yet he held the astrology of his day in open contempt — he characterized it as the foolish daughter of respectable astronomy, a poor relation tolerated only because she earned her mother's keep — and in a short book whose very title casts him as a third party intervening between two combatants, he attacked both camps at once: the astrologers for peddling elaborate rules with no account whatsoever of how the heavens could act upon the earth, and the skeptics for dismissing out of hand what he took to be genuine celestial effects on the weather and on human temperament. His own attempt to rebuild astrology on physical foundations, deriving the traditional aspects from the same harmonic ratios that would later yield his third law of planetary motion, persuaded neither side.

Please write the speech. It cannot launder Kepler into a modern skeptic — that is false, and the astrologers present will know it — nor can it hand him over to them, since he would have savaged their practice, and the astronomers know that. The address must therefore answer, in terms a schoolchild and a skeptical mayor can both follow, the two questions I cannot dodge. First: can we honor a man's achievement while believing that the framework he himself thought unified it was false — given that his mistaken cosmology of nested Platonic solids is precisely what drove him to seek out Tycho Brahe's data, and that the harmonic convictions underlying his astrology are what delivered the third law? Second: what actually distinguishes being wrong in the way Kepler was wrong from being wrong in the way the astrologers he despised were wrong, given that both believed the heavens touch earthly life? I do not want either answer to rest on his sincerity, or on the thought that he should be judged by the standards of his time; both strike me as refined ways of declining the question. Ten minutes, no equations, one moment of genuine warmth for the town, and it should end at the monument itself.
