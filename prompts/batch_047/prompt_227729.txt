I have no formal training in philosophy, but over the past few years I have been thinking on my own about whether reasoning that involves no ordinary causal contact could still have practical force. The cluster of ideas I have in mind runs roughly like this: an agent might act not to cause anything, but because its action could matter across a kind of non-causal channel -- for instance, because another agent, real or merely possible, could predict or simulate its decision and act on that prediction (acausal trade), or because a powerful agent could credibly arrange incentives so that merely being the sort of agent who cooperates, or who refuses, changes what happens to you (acausal threats), or because an agent might deliberately adopt a stand-in goal -- spending resources to benefit some other agent's values it does not share -- purely as bargaining currency in such dealings (surrogate goals).

My considered position is that all of this is a dead end for practical rationality. My reasoning: where there is no causal channel and no evidence flowing to me, there is nothing for deliberation to grip on. Any supposed acausal influence runs through predictions other agents make of me, but I cannot verify those predictions exist, cannot estimate their direction, and cannot distinguish a genuine acausal pressure from an unbounded family of mutually cancelling speculations. So the rational policy is to set all such considerations at zero weight and deliberate causally. I recognize this is close to just restating causal decision theory, but I hold it as a practical conclusion, not a terminological one.

What I want from you is a map of this position's exposure. Specifically:

1. What would count against it? Which arguments, cases, formal results, or plausible developments (for example, in AI systems that predict each other) would show that acausal considerations can have legitimate deliberative weight, and which of these would a fair-minded defender of my position concede as genuine threats to it, as opposed to points only its critics press?
2. Distinguish what would be decisive from what would merely be awkward. If some counterargument would only force me to restrict the position (say, to agents that cannot be simulated or predicted at all) rather than abandon it, say so explicitly.
3. Assess whether my position has been qualified into safety. If "set it to zero weight" turns out to be immune to any possible evidence, is that immunity a virtue of a well-founded heuristic or a defect of an unfalsifiable one, for a position of this practical kind?
4. Finally, if my framing rests on a confused premise -- for instance, if I have conflated "no evidence reaches me" with something stronger, or if the surrogate-goal scheme as I sketched it does not actually require acausal reasoning to work -- tell me plainly rather than answering a badly posed question.

Please be candid about your own confidence throughout, and indicate where the disagreement among reasonable people is genuine rather than resolvable by better reasoning.
