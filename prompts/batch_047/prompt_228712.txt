In a reading group I convene on philosophy of mind and machine intelligence, one participant keeps reaching for Stevan Harnad's 1990 paper The Symbol Grounding Problem to settle, as he puts it, "the LLM question". Harnad frames his problem with the question: "How can the semantic interpretation of a formal symbol system be made intrinsic to the system, rather than just parasitic on the meanings in our heads?" His famous illustration is that trying to extract meaning from a purely symbolic system is like trying to learn Chinese with nothing but a Chinese–Chinese dictionary: every entry you consult merely directs you to further entries in the same uninterpreted vocabulary, so the process of looking things up can go round indefinitely without ever touching down in anything whose meaning you already possess. My colleague's argument is that a language model trained exclusively on text is in precisely the dictionary-learner's predicament, only deeper, since it lacks even the grounded first language a human second-language learner brings to the task; hence, he concludes, the model's outputs are meaningful only in the derivative way dictionary ink is meaningful, and any impression of understanding is projected on to the system by us.

I suspect this analogy is doing considerably more argumentative work than it is entitled to, and I would like a proper audit. Please provide the following, in British English, in roughly 1,200 words:

1. An explicit correspondence table. What, in the case of a text-trained language model, plays the role of the dictionary, the entries, the act of consultation, the learner, the learner's pre-existing grounded vocabulary, and the moment of "landing" on a meaning? Be honest where the mapping is forced: a dictionary is a static artefact consulted after the fact, whereas training is a dynamic, error-driven process that leaves its traces in the system's dispositions, and the model never consults anything.

2. A separation of the disanalogies that matter for my colleague's conclusion from those that do not. Candidates I want assessed include: the dictionary is sealed, whereas the training corpus is a causal sediment of grounded human activity, so there may be a chain of deference back to the world that a closed Chinese–Chinese dictionary lacks; Harnad's premise that the symbols' shapes are arbitrary, whereas the statistical shape of text arguably encodes non-arbitrary structure about the world that produced it; and the fact that the human learner in the illustration already has grounded capacities, whereas the model has none — does that asymmetry strengthen or subtly weaken the analogy, given that the model also lacks the learner's capacity to *consult*?

3. An account of what the analogy quietly imports. In particular: the assumption that meaning must be intrinsic rather than parasitic (but parasitic meaning serves us perfectly well in dictionaries and books — why must it be intrinsic *in the system*?), and the assumption that grounding must ultimately be sensorimotor, which appears to prejudge the case against inferential-role or use-theoretic accounts of meaning on which text-internal relations might constitute more of meaning than Harnad allows.

4. A verdict. Does the conclusion that text-only models cannot understand survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the dictionary picture carrying it the whole time? If the analogy fails, propose a better one and show that it does better; if it survives in weakened form, state precisely what weaker conclusion it supports and whether that conclusion is anything my colleague should still be pleased with.
