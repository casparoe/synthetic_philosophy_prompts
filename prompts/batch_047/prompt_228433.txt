I'm enrolled in a college seminar on grand narratives of human history, and for our unit on Guns, Germs, and Steel each student has to write an essay engaging a piece of published criticism of the book. I chose a confident, well-written magazine review, since I'm inclined to trust it — the reviewer writes for a serious outlet and clearly knows the material. My seminar partner, who admires Diamond enormously, insists the review is a caricature, and we've been arguing about it for a week. Here is the review's summary of the book, which my essay would be built on: "Diamond's thesis is that the fate of peoples was sealed by continental geography. Eurasia's east–west axis let crops, livestock, and inventions diffuse easily across similar climates, while the north–south axes of the Americas and Africa blocked diffusion; Eurasia's stock of domesticable species yielded food surpluses, dense populations, states, and crowd diseases, and the resulting guns, germs, and steel did the rest. On Diamond's account nothing else mattered: culture, institutions, religion, and individual choice are treated as noise, and the European conquest of the Americas appears inevitable not merely in outline but in its particulars. Admirers praise the book for refuting racial explanations of history, but what it actually does is swap biological determinism for an equally rigid geographic determinism — one that cannot even account for divergences within Eurasia itself, such as why a China that led Europe technologically for centuries had fallen so far behind by the nineteenth century. The result is less an explanation of history than an alibi for it: if geography did it, then no one did."

Before I commit my essay to this review's verdict, I'd like a granular audit. Which parts of that summary faithfully describe the book's argument, which parts overstate or distort what Diamond actually claims, and which of the reviewer's charges does Diamond explicitly anticipate or attempt to answer in the text itself? I'm especially unsure about four things: the claim that he treats culture and institutions as noise; the claim that he presents the conquest as inevitable in its particulars; the claim that the book has nothing to say about why Europe rather than China industrialized; and the closing characterization of the book as an alibi. For each, I'd like a verdict that distinguishes three cases — where the reviewer is reporting a criticism that serious readers have genuinely pressed, where he is describing the book's actual content accurately, and where he is misdescribing the text — since I need to defend each verdict against my partner, who has read the book closely and I have not.

Finally, if some of the reviewer's charges fail as stated, I'd like to know whether the determinism worry survives in a weaker and fairer form, and what that form would be. Our seminar goes on to read Sapiens and The Dawn of Everything as rival grand narratives, so it would also help to know, briefly, whether whatever critique of Diamond survives is a problem specific to his geographic method or a standing risk of the grand-narrative genre as such.
