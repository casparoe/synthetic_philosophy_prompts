I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm drafting a column on when it's rational to plead complete ignorance. Rather than gesture at the principle of indifference in the abstract, I want to pressure-test my own instincts. Here are my verdicts on a run of cases, and I'd like to know whether any coherent principle underwrites them.

Coin flips. I know nothing about a particular coin except that it will be flipped once. Assigning heads and tails a probability of one-half each strikes me as unobjectionable.

Newborns. I know nothing about an expectant family beyond the fact of the pregnancy. Fifty-fifty on the sex of the child feels fine, maybe even mandatory.

Alien life. Asked whether there is microbial life elsewhere in the galaxy, I find it absurd to answer fifty-fifty on the grounds that there are two possibilities and I know nothing favoring either. So: same binary structure as the coin, wildly different verdict.

The biased coin. I'm handed a coin of unknown bias, with no information about how it was manufactured. Spreading my credence uniformly over every possible value of its propensity to land heads, from 0 to 1, strikes me as a reasonable expression of ignorance -- and I'm comfortable that this yields a one-half predictive probability for the first flip, matching the fair-coin case.

The cube factory. A factory produces cubes with edge lengths somewhere between 1 and 2 centimeters, and I know nothing else. A uniform distribution over edge length implies one chance in four that the edge exceeds 1.5 cm; a uniform distribution over volume implies roughly two chances in five. Both feel equally arbitrary, so my verdict is that the question has no rationally privileged answer -- the proper response is to refuse to pick, not to pick one.

The circle and the chord. A chord is drawn at random in a circle; what is the probability it exceeds the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle? Depending on how one parameterizes "at random" -- by the chord's midpoint, by the angle at the circumference, by the position along a fixed radius -- one gets different answers, and I see no grounds for preferring one. Same verdict as the cube: ill-posed.

The mixture. A bottle contains water and wine in some ratio between one-to-one and two-to-one, nothing else known. Uniform over the ratio is inconsistent with uniform over its reciprocal, so again: no legitimate assignment.

My puzzle is the biased coin. Structurally it seems no better off than the cube: I'm choosing a parameter (propensity rather than, say, the log-odds of that propensity) and spreading myself evenly over it, and different choices deliver different predictions. Yet my gut treats the two cases completely differently. Likewise the coin versus the aliens: both are symmetric binary ignorance, and I split them. Either there is a principle that separates the legitimate appeals to indifference from the illegitimate ones, or some of my verdicts have to go.

So: what is the best candidate principle -- or small set of candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself -- that explains as many of these verdicts as possible? For each clause of your principle, tell me which case motivates it. Tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot save, and whether you'd overturn the verdict or complicate the principle. And finally, apply your answer to a case I haven't considered: a workshop produces metal rods whose lengths lie somewhere between 10 and 100 centimeters, and I know nothing whatsoever about the machine that cuts them. What, if anything, may I believe about the next rod?
