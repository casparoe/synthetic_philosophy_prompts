I'm preparing a paper for a history of economic thought seminar on Adam Smith and empire, and before I commit to an outline I want a consistency audit of the interpretive claims I currently hold. The paper pivots on a passage in Book IV, Chapter VII of The Wealth of Nations (the chapter Of Colonies, in its third part), where Smith, after arguing at length that the colony trade monopoly is economically ruinous, steps back and judges the European discovery of America and the sea route to the East Indies around the Cape of Good Hope to be among the most consequential events in recorded history, while insisting that their effects so far have been mostly calamitous for the peoples of both the East and West Indies. Crucially, he traces that calamity not to anything intrinsic in the new intercourse itself but to a contingent fact: that fortune had put such overwhelming superiority of force on the European side that Europeans could act unjustly in those distant places without fear of consequences. He then holds out the prospect that, as force equalizes, mutual fear will teach respect for rights and the intercourse could eventually benefit everyone, including the colonized. My paper argues that this passage is the key to Smith's whole view of empire, so I need the claims around it to cohere.

Here is what I currently believe and intend to defend. First, Smith's case against the colonial system is fundamentally economic: the monopoly of the colony trade artificially raises merchants' profits, draws capital away from more productive domestic employments, and leaves the mother country worse off overall, which is why empire does not pay. Second, Smith's condemnation of how the colonies were actually conducted is a moral verdict grounded in the impartial spectator standard of The Theory of Moral Sentiments: the injustice done to native peoples is wrong simpliciter, and would be wrong even if the ledgers had balanced. Third, empire as Europe actually practiced it was an unambiguous mistake, something Smith thinks should never have happened and should simply be repudiated. Fourth, Smith believed that free commerce between independent nations, including an emancipated America, would be mutually beneficial, so peaceful trade can fully substitute for domination. Fifth, Smith regarded the discoveries themselves, considered apart from the injustice that accompanied them, as tending in the long run toward the benefit of humankind as a whole. Sixth, Smith was acutely alive to the way organized merchant interests capture commercial policy and dress up private gain as the national interest, and this explains who actually profited from the colonial system.

Please audit this set rather than summarize Smith for me. Which pairs of these commitments genuinely pull against each other, given the passage above and the arguments around it, and which pairs only appear to be in tension while actually dissolving on inspection? For each genuine tension, tell me what exactly would have to give, how severe the damage is, and what the cheapest repair costs my overall reading; then rank the tensions and tell me which single revision would do the most to make the set coherent. In particular, I want to know whether my first and second claims can both be the ground of Smith's anti-imperialism, whether my third and fifth claims can survive the passage's distinction between the discoveries and their accidental accompaniment, and whether anything in my first claim is in trouble given that Smith concedes the monopoly enriches the very merchants who pushed for it. I genuinely don't know where the weak joint is, so don't assume I've flagged it correctly.
