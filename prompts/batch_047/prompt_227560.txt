I'm a third-year taking a PPE seminar on welfare economics and moral philosophy, and I have to submit a one-page proposal next week for the term paper (about 5000 words, due in eight weeks). We spent two weeks on the Pareto principle and the standard complaints about it — that it's toothless because real policies almost always create losers, and that it's not toothless at all because it quietly treats the status quo as beyond criticism. What stuck with me is a tension in how people defend Pareto improvements. The defense is always that they're uncontroversial because nobody loses. But there's a difference between a change where nobody is worse off relative to how things currently happen to be, and a change where nobody is worse off relative to what they could reasonably demand. I keep thinking about cases where the status quo is itself exploitative: say a deal that raises wages in some awful working arrangement a bit, so the workers are better off than before and the employer is too, so it's a Pareto improvement, but the whole thing only goes through because the workers' outside options are terrible. Everyone "consents," nobody loses, and yet calling that arrangement an improvement in any normatively loaded sense feels like it trades on the badness of the baseline. My half-formed idea is to argue that only the second kind of change deserves to be called a *safe* Pareto improvement — safe in the sense that endorsing it commits you to nothing about the justice of the baseline — and that most policy appeals to Pareto only ever deliver the first kind, so the principle's reputation as the one uncontroversial bit of welfare economics is undeserved.
