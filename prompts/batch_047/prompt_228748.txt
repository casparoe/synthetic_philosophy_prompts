So I'm taking a phil of probability course this term and we just got through the unit on the frequency interpretation and the reference class problem, and I got into a long argument with my roommate (stats major, very annoying about it) that ended with me committing to a position I now want properly stress-tested. My view, roughly: the reference class problem isn't a special problem about probability at all, it's just model selection wearing a trenchcoat. When we ask for the probability that this particular 40-year-old smoker who exercises will get lung cancer, the "problem" of which reference class to use (all 40-year-olds? smokers? exercisers? both?) is the same problem a statistician faces when choosing how complex a model to fit, and it has the same kind of solution: pick the reference class that gives you the best compression of the data you actually have, in the minimum-description-length sense. Finer-grained classes let you fit the past better but cost you in encoding the class itself, so there's a principled tradeoff and no mystery left over. I even think this vindicates the old frequentist advice to use the narrowest class you have reliable statistics for -- "reliable" just is the compression tradeoff talking. My roommate says this is obviously circular but couldn't say why in a way that convinced me.

So: what would count against this view? I want the actual defeaters, not just vibes about it being reductive. Does the view secretly inherit all the old problems through the choice of encoding language, and is that a fatal objection or just the usual underdetermination that every framework lives with? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one say whether someone who actually defends the MDL-style picture would concede it counts or whether they'd bat it away, and on what grounds.
