I'm a philosophy professor and this term I'm running a little reading group with some final-year students on the incompleteness theorems and their afterlife outside mathematical logic. Next week we're doing the biological offshoot: the idea, popularised by Douglas Hofstadter in Gödel, Escher, Bach, that the living cell is eerily like a formal system — DNA as the strings, the genetic code as a kind of Gödel numbering, ribosomes and enzymes as the inference machinery chugging away on them. To make the analogy concrete Hofstadter cooked up a toy formalism he calls typogenetics: you have strands over the alphabet A, C, G, T, and little "enzymes" that bind to a strand and do things to it (snip it, copy bits of it, slide along it, switch letters around), where which operations an enzyme performs is itself fixed by the letters of the strand it was transcribed from, via a simple pairing code. So a strand can code for the very machinery that operates on strands, and you can pose toy versions of self-replication — can a strand encode enzymes that, acting on that strand, reproduce it?

Here's the thing. Gödel died in 1978, the year before Hofstadter's book appeared, so he never saw any of this. But he plainly thought hard about mechanism, self-reference and the mind — there's his famous disjunction to the effect that either the human mind surpasses every finite machine, or there are mathematical questions that are absolutely undecidable — and he was at the Institute in Princeton while von Neumann was working out self-reproducing automata down the corridor, so the raw materials were all around him. What I want is a worked-out answer I can hold up against my students' attempts: if Gödel had lived a few more years and been walked through molecular biology and typogenetics, what would he have made of the DNA–Gödel isomorphism? In particular: (1) would he accept that a genome together with its expression machinery is a genuine instance of a system encoding claims about itself, or would he dig in and say the analogy is loose — that a Gödel numbering is something we deliberately devise so that syntax can talk about itself, whereas the genetic code is just chemistry that natural selection stumbled into, and only a projection of ours makes it "say" anything? (2) Would the existence of self-replicating systems of this sort have nudged him towards or away from the anti-mechanist horn of his disjunction? And (3) where would he expect the analogy to break down first? Don't give me a menu of options — commit to a definite prediction, justify it from what we actually know of his views, and flag the places where the evidence runs thin and you're extrapolating. Also, please write in British English; my students keep submitting things in American spelling and its slowly driving me round the bend.
