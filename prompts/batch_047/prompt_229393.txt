At a workshop last month a philosopher dismissed evolutionary psychology as "just Kipling just-so stories": you observe that some trait exists, invent a Pleistocene scenario that would produce it, call it an explanation. A biologist friend of mine then turned the same comparison on its head -- he said Kipling's stories are actually a decent model of science, since they start from a hard fact (the leopard really does have spots) and reason backwards to what could explain it, which is what hypothesis generation looks like everywhere. So the same analogy is doing opposite work in two different mouths. Audit it for me. Give me the correspondence table -- what plays the role of the leopard's spots, the storyteller, the imagined history, the audience being entertained -- then show where the mapping breaks under each use, what each version quietly smuggles in (e.g. that the ancestral past is unknowable, or that any story fitting the facts is as good as any other), and whether either conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table: the philosopher's "not science," or my friend's "perfectly respectable method."
