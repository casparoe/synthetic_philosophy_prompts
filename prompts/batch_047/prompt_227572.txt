I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy with a background in machine learning, and I've been invited to present at an interdisciplinary AI safety seminar whose attendees split roughly into two camps: technical alignment researchers who have internalized the iterated amplification and distillation literature and tend to treat the scheme as a promising (if stalled) research direction, and philosophers of mind and epistemology who are skeptical of the whole framing but haven't engaged with the technical details. My talk defends the following thesis: iterated amplification preserves neither competence nor alignment, because the two failure modes are actually the same failure mode. The core of my argument is that when a task is decomposed into subtasks small enough for a human overseer to verify, the overseer can only check subanswers against their local context, so reasoning that depends on global structure — long chains of inference, rare edge cases, judgments whose correctness only becomes apparent several levels up — gets silently degraded at each distillation step, and the errors compound over iterations rather than washing out. I then argue that value-relevant judgments are exactly the kind of judgment whose correctness is globally structured in this way, so whatever pruning kills exotic competence also kills the overseer's ability to transmit anything richer than shallow, locally-verifiable normative heuristics. My conclusion is not that amplification is useless but that it can only amplify what the overseer already possesses in decomposable form, which means the scheme is conservative where it needed to be generative.
