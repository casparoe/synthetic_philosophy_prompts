I am a professor of philosophy giving a colloquium paper defending moderate pragmatic encroachment: the thesis that, holding a subject's evidence fixed, whether she knows that p can depend on how much is at stake for her in whether p. My version is deliberately moderate in two respects. First, stakes affect knowledge only by affecting how much evidence is needed for knowledge-level justification, not by altering the evidence the subject possesses. Second, I accept the cost this brings: in some paired low- and high-stakes cases with identical evidence, the low-stakes subject knows while the high-stakes subject does not.

The paper's central argument rests on a case I call the Delegation Problem. A building inspector, for whom the stakes are modest, reviews the engineering report and thereby comes to know that the scaffold is secure. Her sign-off is then relied upon by the site manager, for whom a collapse would be catastrophic and whose evidence is exactly that same report plus the sign-off. If stakes destroy knowledge, the manager seems not to know the very proposition the inspector knew, on essentially the same evidence, merely because the decision has been delegated upward — and yet institutions are built on precisely such transfers of epistemic authority. I argue that the moderate view handles this better than its rivals, since the manager can inherit the inspector's justification while needing independent grounds before her own reliance rises to knowledge.

The audience is mixed, and I need to be ready for all of it: (i) orthodox invariantists who hold that knowledge is a purely epistemic matter and will defend intellectualism come what may; (ii) contextualists about 'knows' who will argue that I have located in the subject's practical situation what properly belongs in the attributor's conversational context, and that my view is contextualism with gratuitous metaphysics attached; and (iii) one decision theorist who will insist that everything my cases show is better handled by credences plus the norms of rational action, leaving knowledge entirely untouched.

Prepare my Q&A prep sheet. Give me the eight to ten objections I should actually expect from this particular audience — not a generic survey of the literature — ordered by the damage each would do if I answered it badly, and flag which questioner each is likely to come from. Phrase each objection the way that questioner would in fact put it. For each: state what I should concede, what I should hold, give me a two-minute reply, and note where that reply is soft. The sheet must cover at least the following three exchanges: the invariantist who grants my intuitions about the paired cases but relocates them to the norms of assertion or to justified belief rather than to knowledge; the contextualist who argues that the Delegation Problem is a feature of her view rather than mine, since the inspector's 'she knew' and the manager's colleague's 'she didn't' can both come out true in their respective mouths; and the decision theorist who proposes that the manager simply faces a higher evidential threshold for action, not for knowledge, and asks what explanatory work my extra layer of stakes-sensitive knowledge is doing. End with the one objection I am hoping nobody asks, why it is dangerous, and my best available answer to it anyway, followed by a candid assessment of whether the moderate version of the view survives the colloquium intact or whether I should retreat to a weaker claim. Do not pad the sheet with citations or reading lists; I want arguments, not bibliography.
