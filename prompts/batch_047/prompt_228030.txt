I’m drafting a novel where a fight at a city council meeting turns into a philosophical dispute, and I want the plot to do the work instead of having the characters lecture each other. Build me a thought experiment I can adapt in which the Hart–Devlin split runs in the less familiar direction: a Hart-style harm principle gives a solid reason to criminalize the conduct, while a Devlin-style legal moralism cannot justify criminalizing it.

Constraints on the scenario:

- Set it in a realistic present-day American town, and keep the setup under 250 words.
- The conduct has to begin as private and voluntary.
- No individual instance should directly injure a nonconsenting person, but the cumulative pattern should create a serious enough risk of harm to identifiable people that criminalization is at least plausible.
- The town must be genuinely morally divided about the conduct, not just hypocritical or afraid to admit what everybody privately thinks.
- The law must be enforceable without house-to-house searches or massive surveillance.
- No sex panic, pandemic, terrorism, cult, lottery, or science-fiction gadget.
- Don’t stipulate that society will fall apart, that public morality will collapse, or that everyone definately knows the conduct is wrong. The whole point is to isolate causal harm without moral consensus.
- Don’t just announce that Hart and Devlin would disagree; the verdicts have to emerge from the details.

After the scenario, identify the exact conduct, the proposed criminal law, and how it would be enforced. Then walk through the Hartian reasoning from individual acts to aggregate risk to proportionate punishment, and the Devlinian reasoning about whether the town has the kind of deep shared moral judgment that criminal law may enforce. Make sure the Devlinian objection isn’t secretly just another version of the harm principle.

Finish by telling me which feature of the case is doing the philosophical work, what happens if you add genuine moral consensus, what happens if you remove the cumulative harm, and how each side would attack the case. End with one natural-sounding line of dialogue for each character, suitable for the novel rather than a textbook.
