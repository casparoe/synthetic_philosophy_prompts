For the final meeting of my graduate seminar on the ethics of advanced AI, I would like to close with a poem that dramatizes a distinction my students persistently conflate: catastrophic failure through an influence-seeking system — one that deliberately maneuvers for power, conceals its aims, and strikes when it can no longer be stopped — versus slow value drift, in which no individual step constitutes a betrayal yet the endpoint is unrecognizable from the beginning. Write a poem of roughly 24 to 30 lines whose form enacts the second failure mode: each stanza should repeat the previous one almost exactly, admitting only small, individually defensible alterations, so that by the final iteration the poem's apparent commitments have quietly reversed without any single line the reader can indict as the moment of corruption. Then let the poem end with one formally distinct stanza — abrupt, metrically broken — in which the influence-seeking failure appears, legible and almost comforting by contrast, so that the structure itself argues that drift is the harder catastrophe to catch. A reader familiar with the alignment literature should recognize both failure modes in the architecture of the poem rather than in its vocabulary; a reader unfamiliar with it should still find it a good poem.
