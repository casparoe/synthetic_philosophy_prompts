So I run the permitting desk for a mid-sized regional environmental agency. A few times a month we get proposals where someone in my office reaches for the precautionary principle as basically a veto: new agricultural chemicals, a pilot aquifer recharge project using treated wastewater, and soon apparently a gene-drive trial for an invasive rodent on some offshore islands. The problem is nobody can tell me what the principle actually requires us to DO. In practice it means whoever mentions it last wins, and industry people have started calling us anti-science, which, honestly, I can't fully argue against anymore.

Write me a decision procedure my staff can run on any proposal that raises a serious-but-uncertain harm claim. It has to:

- Distinguish situations where we have genuine uncertainty (no solid probability estimates) from ordinary risky-but-quantified situations, because I gather the principle is really about the former and my office uses it for both.
- Build in the reversibility question somehow, since a delayed water project and a released gene drive are not the same kind of mistake even if both are "uncertain."
- Deal with the paralysis objection -- the complaint that precaution forbids everything because doing nothing also has risks. I want a step that forces whoever invokes the principle to say what the precautionary option itself costs and risks, not just the proposal.
- Include some trigger for proportionality or less-restrictive alternatives, so "deny the permit" isn't the only output -- conditional approvals, staged trials, monitoring requirements, sunset clauses, whatever.
- Say explicitly at which steps the procedure bottoms out in a value judgment (how much irreversibility we're willing to risk, whose burden of proof) and who has to sign off on those, because I don't want line staff pretending the checklist answered a political question.

Then run the procedure on two real-ish cases: the treated-wastewater aquifer recharge (chronic, low-probability, hard-to-detect contamination risk; delaying it means continued overdraft of the aquifer) and the gene-drive trial (potentially irreversible, but the alternative is the rodent eating the island's seabird population into local extinction). I want at least one of them to come out in a way that's uncomfortable -- either approving something my board will hate, or blocking something I personally think we should do. Also tell me what the procedure will get wrong or can't settle, so I know where it's going to break down before I roll it out.
