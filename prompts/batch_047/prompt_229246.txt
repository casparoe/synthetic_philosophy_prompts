Next month I am giving a forty-five-minute talk at an interdisciplinary colloquium on AI and human agency. My thesis is that the usual debate between the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence is malformed because it moves among three different claims: architectural orthogonality, according to which no formal limit prevents an intelligent system from having almost any final goal; motivational orthogonality, according to which greater intelligence need not alter the system’s operative goals; and selection-level orthogonality, according to which the processes that produce and preserve capable systems are indifferent to those goals. I will defend the first, qualify the second, and reject the third. I will also defend a restricted account of instrumental convergence: certain subgoals are likely only relative to a family of environments, incentives, capabilities, and goal representations, not as near-universal implications of intelligence. To make the position less familiar, I introduce a distinction between a system’s nominal objective, its learned behavioral policy, and its terminal commitments, understood as whatever goal-like states would be preserved under reflection, self-modification, and opportunities for greater power. My claim is that instrumental convergence is strongest for terminal commitments and much weaker for nominal objectives or observed policies.

The audience will contain three camps. Analytic philosophers of mind and ethics will be wary of treating goals as though they were naturally identifiable parts of a system. Cognitive scientists will suspect that I am anthropomorphizing optimization while ignoring embodiment, development, and social scaffolding. Machine-learning researchers will be comfortable discussing training incentives and proxy objectives but skeptical of the notion of terminal commitments, especially if it cannot be operationalized. For every objection, provide a reply of roughly ninety seconds, state what I should concede immediately, identify the part of the reply that remains vulnerable, and indicate whether I should answer with a conceptual distinction, a hypothetical case, or an empirically testable formulation. Please include at least one objection aimed at each of my three orthogonality claims and at least two aimed at the nominal-objective/policy/terminal-commitment distinction.

I am especially worried that the discussion will either reduce architectural orthogonality to the trivial claim that intelligence and value are logically distinct, or treat instrumental convergence as a mystical tendency for all intelligent systems to seek power. The prep sheet should help me prevent both reductions without pretending that my selection-level claim is a theorem.
