So I'm working on a long feature about whether farming was a disaster for our species -- the whole debate around hunter-gatherer affluence, the origins of inequality, skeletal health declining after domestication, all of that. Every book I pick up (Diamond's worst-mistake take, Harari's luxury-trap chapter, Scott on the early states, Graeber & Wengrow pushing back on everyone) seems to be running a totally different implicit scoreboard, and I want to pin down the scoreboard itself before I weigh any of them.

So: write me the desiderata. What does any adequate answer to "was the agricultural revolution a mistake?" actually have to do to count as adequate -- not which answer is right, but what an answer must be able to handle. I mean things like: the population problem (agriculture supported maybe a hundred times more people, so average vs total welfare gives you opposite verdicts), the non-identity problem (the people who suffered through early farming aren't the same people who'd exist in the hunter-gatherer counterfactual, so who exactly was wronged?), the trap dynamic (each individual step might have been locally rational even if the endpoint is worse, so does "mistake" even require a chooser?), reversibility and lock-in, distribution vs averages (average welfare could dip while inequality explodes, or vice versa), whose goods count and whether autonomy, leisure, and calories are even commensurable, and the empirical requirements (the skeletal and stature evidence, Sahlins-style work-hours claims and the pushback on them, the fact that farming was adopted independently multiple times which cuts both ways). Whatever else belongs on the list too -- I'm sure I'm missing some, like how to treat the question of whether the comparison class should be 10,000 BC foragers or some plausible modern alternative.

For each requirement give me the case or consideration that motivates it, flag which ones are genuinely non-negotiable versus which are just one camp's hobbyhorse, and -- this is the part I care most about -- tell me if any pairs of desiderata can't be jointly satisfied, because I have a suspicion the non-identity requirement and the "judge by what actually happened to actual people" requirement are in tension and nobody in the popular lit acknowledges it. Also tell me which single requirement does the most work in knocking out the popular framings, since that's where I'll put the pressure in the piece.

Then at the end, briefly -- like a paragraph each, no big scorecard -- how the Diamond/Harari/Scott/Graeber-Wengrow positions hold up against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria, since thats where the actual thinking is and I can do the scoring myself.
