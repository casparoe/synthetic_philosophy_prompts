You are an experienced instructor in philosophy of physics grading take-home exam answers in an upper-level course on the metaphysics of spacetime. Grade carefully and think through the student's reasoning step by step before settling on a mark; in my experience, answers on this material often get the headline claims right while slipping on the technical details, and I want a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression.

The exam question was: "State the hole argument in its modern form (as revived in the 1980s literature following Einstein's original version). Against which position is it directed, and what exactly does it purport to show? Explain the response known as Leibniz equivalence, and give one other response a substantivalist might offer. Finally, does the hole argument show that general relativity is an indeterministic theory?"

Here is the student's answer: "The hole argument runs as follows. Take a general relativistic model consisting of a manifold plus metric, and consider an open region H (the 'hole') somewhere in spacetime. Apply a diffeomorphism that is the identity outside H but non-trivial inside it, dragging the metric around within the hole. This produces a second model that agrees with the first everywhere outside H but differs inside. Since the two models agree on all of spacetime up to the hole, no amount of data from outside the hole determines which model obtains inside it. The argument is directed at manifold substantivalism, the view that the spacetime manifold's points exist independently of the metrical and material fields on them, because the substantivalist must count the two diffeomorphic models as genuinely distinct physical situations: they assign different metrical properties to the very same spacetime points. So for the substantivalist, determinism fails. Ultimately, though, the argument is really just an illustration of the coordinate freedom of generally covariant theories: since any theory can be redescribed in arbitrary coordinates, the two models are related by a coordinate transformation, and the apparent underdetermination is the familiar trivial fact that physics does not depend on our choice of coordinate system. Leibniz equivalence is the response that denies the substantivalist premise: it holds that diffeomorphic models represent one and the same physical situation, so the apparent multiplicity of possibilities dissolves. Adopting Leibniz equivalence amounts to a form of relationism, since it requires giving up spacetime points as genuine objects in the ontology and treating only the spatiotemporal relations between material events as real. An alternative substantivalist response is to keep the manifold but deny that the diffeomorphic models differ in any physically meaningful (haecceitistic) way, holding that which point plays which role is not a further fact. Finally, the hole argument does not show that general relativity is indeterministic: on Leibniz equivalence or the sophisticated substantivalist response, the two models are one possibility, and the theory's determinism is intact; the argument only forces a dilemma on a particular metaphysics of spacetime."
