I should say up front that I have no academic training — I work in logistics and my engagement with this topic is two years of popular books, long online threads, and a lot of thinking during night shifts — so I may mislabel things, and I would rather be corrected than flattered. The question that has gripped me is the gradual-replacement version of mind uploading: suppose a person's brain is converted to artificial components one neuron at a time, over the course of months, with the patient awake, conversing, and reporting an unbroken stream of experience throughout. Does the person who walks out at the end count as the one who walked in, or as a flawless successor to someone who died on the table?

What puzzles me is not which answer is right but why the argument never moves. As far as I can follow it, both sides have been holding the same positions for decades. The survival side leans on continuity: nothing is ever interrupted, the body already turns over its matter, and each single replacement is surely too small to kill anyone, so at no point could death have occurred. The death side leans on duplication: whatever the gradual process preserves could equally be preserved in two places at once, and since one person cannot be two, what the process preserves was never the person but only the pattern. Each side seems to find its own argument obviously decisive and the other's obviously confused.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that this is not a factual disagreement at all but a collision of bedrock intuitions about what a person is — and that each side's favorite thought experiment is precisely the case the other side refuses to count as evidence, which is why the debate can continue indefinitely without anyone conceding anything. But I may be wrong, and I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is the disagreement verbal, substantive, or some mixture? Is there a hidden empirical question underneath it — say, about the physical basis of consciousness — whose resolution would dissolve the dispute, or would both sides simply re-run the same arguments on whatever the neuroscience showed? Could any conceivable result, experiment, or conceptual distinction actually end this debate, and if not, is the question itself ill-formed, or merely beyond us? And given my own leanings — I find myself on the survival side — which part of the death side's case should I be taking more seriously than I currently do?
