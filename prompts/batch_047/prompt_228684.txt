Two months ago I became the founding director of a perpetually endowed foundation. Its charter forbids spending down the endowment, fixes a narrow mission, and contains elaborate governance provisions meant to keep my successors faithful to the founder's intent for centuries. Our board's first strategic decision is whether to commission — and, more importantly, whether to believe — a forecast: the probability that the foundation both still exists and still substantially follows its charter one hundred years from now.

For questions like this I lean on a mentor of mine, a retired historian who is an uncompromising outside-view forecaster: he always starts from a reference class and a base rate and treats the inside details of a case as seductions. What I have never managed to extract from him is the rule by which he picks the class. Here are the verdicts of his I have on record:

1. A restaurateur projecting success on the strength of a celebrated chef and a prime location: he disregarded both and used the early-years survival figures for all new restaurants in the city, on the grounds that classes like "restaurants with celebrated chefs" are carved out after the fact and have no stable statistics.
2. A venture-funded startup attempting a radically new type of aircraft: he declined the class "venture-backed startups," though he admitted its data were rich, and forecast instead from documented attempts to build genuinely novel aircraft going back over a century, well before venture capital existed.
3. A national government replacing its core administrative software: he forecast from large public-sector IT overhauls rather than from software projects generally, saying the procurement and incentive structure was doing the causal work.
4. Asked once whether a declining great power would still count as one in fifty years: he used agrarian-bureaucratic empires past their revenue peak, not the contemporary club of great powers.
5. A friend convinced he would die young because his father and grandfather had: he used cohort life tables for men of the friend's age and health profile, and treated the family history as something to be folded into the actuarial class rather than as the basis for a narrower one.
6. A billionaire-backed charter city: he set aside recent special economic zones, conceding their data were better, and forecast from privately founded planned settlements and chartered ventures across five centuries, thin as that record is.

Now the foundation itself. The candidate classes seem to be: (a) modern endowed foundations and charitable trusts — well documented, with high institutional survival but conspicuous drift from founder intent over generations; (b) institutions across history with legally binding founder-intent charters — perpetual trusts, certain colleges, monastic orders, entailed estates — sparse, heterogeneous, and spanning eras with very different state capacity; (c) family fortunes and dynasties, which he occasionally cites in conversation for their three-generations decay.

Three questions. First, your prediction: which class will he choose for the foundation, and roughly what forecast — optimistic or pessimistic — will it yield? Second, the analysis: state at least two selection rules that fit all six verdicts on record, show what each predicts for the foundation's case, and say which you think is really his, with your reasons. Note that a simple "prefer contemporary, data-rich classes" rule appears to be excluded by verdicts 2 and 6, unless you can reconcile them. Third, the discriminating case: describe a further hypothetical verdict — a case plus his ruling on it — that would do the most work in distinguishing the two strongest candidate rules.

One discipline for the answer: please separate what is genuinely settled among people who study forecasting and institutional longevity (for instance, that base-rate anchoring usually beats inside-view planning, and that organizations drift from founder intent) from what remains contested — above all, whether there is any principled method for choosing a reference class at all, and whether centuries-old base rates can legitimately be applied to modern institutions. Do not present a contested selection rule as the consensus of the field, and if his actual rule is underdetermined by my six data points, say so plainly rather than forcing a verdict.
