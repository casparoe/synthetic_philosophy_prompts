Next month I am giving a colloquium talk in the philosophy department at a research university, defending a thesis I have been developing for a while, and I want to survive the question period. My claim is an extension of P. F. Strawson's account of moral responsibility: I argue that the participant reactive attitudes -- resentment, indignation, gratitude, and the like -- are not only appropriately directed at individual persons but are already, as a matter of psychological and social fact, directed at institutions, and that this observation is not an embarrassment to be explained away but the correct foundation for institutional moral responsibility. On my view, a hospital, a corporation, or a government agency can be a proper target of indignation in the same second-personal way a person can; institutions can sometimes be excused (perverse incentives, genuine ignorance of effects) but never exempted the way Strawson exempts the very young or the deeply disturbed, because exempting an institution amounts to treating it as an object of policy rather than a participant in moral relations, and we demonstrably do not do that. I take this to show that Strawson's restriction of the participant stance to interpersonal relations was an unargued assumption, and that his framework, properly generalized, dissolves much of the puzzle about how something without phenomenal consciousness could be responsible.

The audience will be about twenty-five people: a handful of specialists on Strawson and the reactive attitudes who know "Freedom and Resentment" line by line and are protective of it; several free will skeptics in the vicinity of Pereboom's hard incompatibilism, who think nothing about institutions touches the basic manipulation argument; two or three people who work on group agency and collective intentionality and will have opinions about whether institutions can have attitudes at all; and some graduate students who will ask the question everyone else is too polite to ask. A few of them have read a draft and were skeptical in ways I found vague.

Act as a senior philosopher who has spent a career on moral responsibility, has chaired many such colloquia, and is on my side enough to be honest with me. Think carefully before answering -- first brainstorm the full space of objections, then filter and organize. Deliver a prep sheet, not a defense of my thesis: the objections I will actually get, ordered by likelihood and by how damaging each is to the talk, each phrased the way that particular audience member would phrase it in their own vocabulary, each with the best reply I can give in two minutes and a frank note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede up front in the talk itself to defuse objections before they are voiced, what I must not concede because my thesis collapses without it, and -- this is the part I most need -- the objection I am secretly hoping nobody raises, why it is so dangerous, and what, if anything, I can say if it comes anyway.
