I'm prepping discussion sections for a political philosophy seminar and we just covered Sen's liberal paradox, using the usual Lewd-and-Prude style example where one person is nosy and the other is a busybody and their meddling preferences about each other's private reading do all the damage. A student pushed back and said something that I think is right: this makes the paradox look like it depends on people having illegitimate or nosy preferences, and if we just told everyone to mind their own business the conflict would evaporate. So my question is whether the paradox really needs meddlesome preferences to get going.

Construct a thought experiment to test this: a realistic scenario (a household, a hospital, two friends -- no grand political setups, no more than three people, and keep it to a paragraph or so) in which the conflict between minimal liberal rights and weak Pareto optimality arises even though every preference in the story is benevolent. That is, nobody disapproves of anyone's private life, nobody wants to control anyone for its own sake, and each person's preference over what the other does is driven by genuine concern for that person's own good as they themselves understand it. If you think this is impossible, say so and explain why, but I suspect it's not.

Then walk me through the mechanics: state which choices each person is supposed to have decisive rights over, lay out the preference rankings (a small table is fine), and show exactly where the Pareto-better outcome gets blocked by someone's right and where the rights-respecting outcome is Pareto-worse. After that, tell me which feature of your scenario is doing the real work -- like, if I removed the benevolence and let people be slightly nosy, would the structure stay identical, and if I removed something else instead would the whole thing collapse back into no conflict at all? Finally, say a word about how the usual escape routes fare against your version: Sen's own suggestion that we sometimes willingly suspend rights, the proposal to restrict the domain of admissible preferences, and the move to recast rights as constraints on game forms rather than as decisive pairs over outcomes. My hunch is that making the preferences benevolent makes some of these responses look worse, not better, but I want to see if the case actually bears that out.
