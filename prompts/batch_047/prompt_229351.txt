I am setting the final examination for my upper-division undergraduate seminar, Technology and Political Order, and I need one essay question, answerable in class in 80 minutes, on Langdon Winner's claim that artifacts can have politics.

Here is what the students will bring to the room. We read Winner's essay closely, including his two routes by which artifacts are said to be political. On the first route, the design or arrangement of a technical system becomes a way of settling a community's affairs; his showcase case is the low parkway overpasses on Long Island, which Robert Caro's biography of Robert Moses presents as built with clearances too low for buses, so that poor and Black New Yorkers dependent on public transit were kept from Jones Beach — and Winner himself stresses that this route transcends the simple categories of intended and unintended. On the second route, some technologies are inherently political, requiring or being strongly compatible with particular forms of social organization, as nuclear power plausibly requires centralized, hierarchical control while rooftop solar is plausibly compatible with decentralized arrangements. We then spent a full session on the later historical scrutiny of the bridge story, associated especially with Bernward Joerges and the late-1990s social-studies-of-science literature, which concluded that the story as Winner retells it does not survive examination: the clearances were unremarkable by the engineering standards of the period, the relevant decisions were not Moses's to make in the way the story requires, and Jones Beach was in fact reachable by public transport. In discussion, several students concluded that Winner's thesis simply collapses with its example; others insisted the thesis was never hostage to the bridge story at all. That unresolved disagreement is what I want the exam to exploit.

The question should therefore be unanswerable by reciting the two routes. It should force students to take a stand on what the empirical failure of a theory's canonical example does to the theory — whether the bridge story functioned as evidence, as mere illustration, or as something in between, and what follows for the thesis in each case — and to state which version of Winner's claim, if any, survives in defensible form. To prevent retreat into abstraction, the question must also require students to test their repaired thesis against a case I supply for the occasion: a university housing office adopts an algorithm that assigns first-year students to dormitories by clustering questionnaire responses about tastes and habits, in the stated service of building community; because tastes and habits track family income, the algorithm reliably sorts students by class, although no one intended this and the office is dismayed by the outcome. Students should be asked to say whether this artifact has politics on their repaired reading of Winner, and whether the absence of intention matters.

Please deliver: (1) the question exactly as it would appear on the examination paper, with the housing-algorithm case stated within it in two or three sentences; (2) a model answer of roughly 600 words, written at the level of the best student I have taught; and (3) a marking scheme out of 20, organized around five required moves with the marks each carries, a one-line note for each on the characteristic way a weaker answer mishandles that move, and an explicit statement of what separates an A answer from a B answer. Conclude your whole response with a last line beginning "Verdict:" followed by one sentence stating the position your model answer takes on whether, and in what form, Winner's thesis survives the debunking of the bridge story.
