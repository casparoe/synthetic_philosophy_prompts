So a little backstory: I cover science and big ideas for a general-interest magazine, and last month my friend Dave, who teaches political philosophy at a small liberal arts college, came down with the flu right in the middle of midterm grading. I owed him a favor, so I ended up with a stack of blue books on my kitchen table. Most of them were easy calls, but one essay has me genuinely torn, and my own philosophy background is mostly stuff I've read on planes, so I'd like a second opinion before I slap a grade on it. The question Dave set was: "Is taxation theft? Critically assess this slogan, engaging with at least one argument for and one argument against it, and state what, if anything, follows about the limits of legitimate redistribution."

Here's the student's answer, verbatim: "The slogan 'taxation is theft' rests on an analogy. If a stranger took thirty percent of your paycheck at gunpoint, we would call it robbery, so why does it become morally different when the taker is the government? The strongest version of this argument comes from Robert Nozick in Anarchy, State, and Utopia. Nozick argues that taking a portion of someone's earnings is morally on a par with forced labor, since the state is effectively making you work some hours each week for other people's benefit. His Wilt Chamberlain example backs this up: if wealth is distributed according to some favored pattern, voluntary exchanges (like fans paying to watch Chamberlain play) will immediately upset the pattern, so preserving it requires constant coercive interference. On Nozick's view, then, taxation simply is theft, and the only legitimate state is a minimal one confined to protecting rights. The main counterargument comes from Liam Murphy and Thomas Nagel's The Myth of Ownership. They point out that 'theft' only makes sense against a background of legitimate property rights, and that those rights are themselves creatures of the legal system. There is no morally privileged 'pre-tax income' you were entitled to before the state touched it. Following the social contract tradition, they argue that citizens who accept the benefits of government have tacitly consented to its tax system. If property is a legal convention in this way, then the state may redistribute however it judges best, and no level of taxation could count as unjust. My own view is that the debate is partly verbal. Legally, taxation cannot be theft, since theft means unlawful taking; the real question is whether it is morally analogous to theft. On that question I find the libertarian analogy forceful but I think it underestimates how much our ability to earn anything at all depends on public institutions, so I conclude taxation is not theft, though governments can definately tax unjustly at the margins."

Grade this for me the way Dave would want: fairly, catching real problems rather than just rewarding fluency. Give me your response as exactly five numbered items, with no introduction or conclusion. Item one should be the letter grade plus a one-sentence bottom line, and items two through five should each be a single specific comment, a sentence or two long, pointing to something particular in the answer that justifies the grade (a strength, a weakness, a mischaracterization, whatever matters most). Don't just give me general vibes about the writing.
