A close friend of mine is a behavioral ecologist who now works as a management consultant, and she has an exasperating habit of sorting every display in nature and in business into one of two bins: honest signal or cheap talk. She insists her rule is nothing more than Zahavi's handicap principle carried into boardrooms — honesty is purchased with cost, full stop. Last night, over several hours and one bottle too many, I tried to convince her that her own verdicts don't actually support that rule, and the argument ended unresolved. What I want from you is a prediction: given her track record below, what will she say about a new case I intend to put to her when we resume, and what principle is she in fact running?

Her verdicts so far, as best I can reconstruct them: the peacock's train is an honest signal, because a malnourished male simply cannot grow one like it; stotting in gazelles is honest, because a slow gazelle cannot afford the wasted takeoff and because the display is the very athleticism it advertises; the roaring contests of red deer stags are honest, since only a stag in fighting condition can sustain the rate; and a live working interview, in which a candidate spends a full day solving real problems alongside the team, is honest, whereas a polished résumé and a rehearsed sit-down interview are not. On the other side of her ledger: she dismissed a luxury watch as cheap talk, since it can be leased for a weekend or financed by someone drowning in debt, and she said the same of apologies unaccompanied by restitution, and of a firm's boastful press releases about its own stability.

The new case is this: a small business applying for a loan offers its bank permanent, read-only access to its real sales ledger and live bank feed, verified on the bank's own systems so the data cannot be staged or curated. This costs the owner essentially nothing, yet it would seem to separate healthy businesses from unhealthy ones just as decisively as any peacock's tail separates fit males from starvelings. When I sketched the case she refused to classify it, which is when things got heated. Please give me your best guess as to which bin she will put it in, and — more important than the guess itself — lay out the candidate rules that are each consistent with every one of her earlier calls: whether honesty for her requires differential cost, practical unfakeability, or something subtler that I haven't seen. Say what each candidate rule predicts for the loan case, explain why you favor your prediction, and then tell me the one further case I should put to her next if her answer still leaves her underlying principle ambiguous.
