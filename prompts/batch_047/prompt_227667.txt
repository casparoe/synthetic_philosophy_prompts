So I'm part of a five-person collective that runs an open-source software project, and our setup is that any one of us can merge code and cut a release on our own. Last week one member shipped a feature that quietly collects usage stats from users, people got mad, it was a whole thing. At the emergency meeting I mostly stayed out of it, but two of the others (not me) got into a real argument and I want an outside verdict.

(1) Priya said the lesson is simple: nobody ships anything that affects the whole group without at least one other person signing off. The failure was one person acting alone, so the fix is a two-approval rule. Case closed.

(2) Tom said she's treating the symptom. He'd read about something called the unilateralists curse: if any single "yes" is enough to launch something, then stuff launches whenever even one person out of five thinks it's a good idea — and the situations where exactly one person says yes and four say no are disproportionately the ones where the lone yes is just wrong. So a group with our structure will systematically greenlight bad ideas even if everyone is honest, competent, and shares their real opinion. The problem isn't trust, it's statistics, and a sign-off rule is a bandaid on that.

(3) Priya replied that his argument proves way too much. By that logic the lone dissenter is always probably wrong, but history is full of lone voices being right — whistleblowers, scientists everyone dismissed, the one person in a meeting who says this plan is going to blow up. If his statistics were really so grim, groups could never benefit from individual initiative at all, which is obviously false.

(4) Tom said the whistleblower examples are survivorship bias: we remember the famous vindicated dissenters, not the thousands of cranks and alarmists who were just wrong. The base rate is what matters.

(5) Priya's last move: fine, but then his own implied fix is equally "statistical" in the other direction — requiring everyone to agree just means the group systematically blocks good changes whenever one person's judgment dips, trading one bias for another. So statistics alone can't pick the structure; you need to know the actual costs of false alarms versus missed launches in each case.

Can you go through move by move — who wins each step, which claims are actually right or wrong, and is there anything important both of them are missing?
