I work in model risk at a mid-sized bank, and my native vocabulary for talking about risk is the quantitative finance one: value-at-risk, expected shortfall (CVaR), spectral risk measures, distortion risk measures, Choquet integrals, the axioms of coherent risk measures. A philosopher friend recently told me that her literature has its own home-grown rival to expected utility theory -- risk-weighted expected utility theory, associated mainly with Lara Buchak -- in which an agent evaluates a gamble by weighting outcomes according to the probability of getting something at least as good, via a "risk function," rather than by their plain probabilities. My first reaction was that this sounds like a spectral risk measure wearing philosophical clothes, and that expected shortfall is just the special case where the risk function is linear up to a threshold and flat after. She insisted the theory is doing things my framework can't even state. I want to test that.

So: translate risk-weighted expected utility theory, component by component, into the vocabulary of distortion/spectral risk measures. Cover at least these components: (1) the rank-dependent weighting scheme itself and what it shares with the Choquet-integral form of a distortion risk measure; (2) the claim that the theory separates an agent's attitude to risk from the diminishing marginal utility of money, so that risk aversion is not just curvature of the utility function in disguise; (3) the resolution of the Allais paradox, where the theory claims to accommodate the common-sense preferences without dropping the ordering of lotteries; (4) the claim that risk-weighting captures something normative -- that a certain pattern of concern for worse-off outcomes is rational, not merely a taste; and (5) the standard complaint that the theory makes the evaluation of a gamble depend on global features of the outcome distribution in ways that generate trouble for sequential or dynamic choice. For each, be honest about the fit: do not smuggle in finance-side assumptions (coherence, law-invariance, subadditivity) and present them as translations. Assume I know the math cold but have read no decision theory, so flag where the philosophical literature uses familiar words in unfamiliar ways.

Structure your answer in five sections, in this order: "Shared machinery" (about 200 words: the exact formal overlap, stated precisely, including where expected shortfall sits as a special case if it does); "Component-by-component translation" (about 400 words, taking (1) through (5) in turn); "What translates only with distortion" (about 200 words: what has to be bent to fit, and what the bending hides); "What has no expression in the risk-measure vocabulary" (about 200 words: the residue, stated as claims, not gestures); "Where the residue points" (about 150 words: your verdict on whether the inexpressible remainder is a genuine blind spot of the finance framework, or something the philosophical theory only appears to say because it is not forced to be precise).
