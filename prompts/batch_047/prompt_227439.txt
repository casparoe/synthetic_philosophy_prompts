I am putting together the final examination for my upper-level undergraduate course in the philosophy of history, and I need one essay question that cannot be answered by reciting the standard vocabulary of "path dependence," "contingency," and "lock-in." Students will have 75 minutes. Please write the question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme.

The question should be built around the following pair of claims, which students must treat as both possibly true, and should force them to work out whether -- and how -- they are compatible. Suppose a historian argues two things about the industrialization of a particular region. First, that the region's adoption of a specific technical standard (say, a rail gauge or a power-grid frequency) was the accidental result of a single early decision, after which switching costs, network effects, and sunk investments made reversal prohibitive: had the early decision gone the other way, a different standard would have been locked in instead. Second, that the region's industrialization itself was overdetermined: given its coal deposits, labor supply, trade position, and institutional pressures, some form of industrial economy would have emerged under almost any sequence of intermediate events. The exam question should ask candidates to do at least the following: state precisely what each claim asserts and what evidence or counterfactual reasoning each one depends on; explain why the two claims are not in tension once we notice that "the outcome" can be picked out at different levels of description, while also explaining why that observation is not by itself a full resolution (because a defender of historical inevitability can simply restate her thesis at a coarser level, and a defender of contingency can restate hers at a finer one); and adjudicate the dispute by proposing a criterion for when a path-dependence claim is substantive rather than trivial -- for instance, whether the claim must concern outcomes that matter for the explanation at hand, or must be robust under some specified class of counterfactual interventions. Finally, candidates should apply their criterion to a fresh case they construct themselves, not one discussed in class.

The model answer should exhibit the moves you expect of a top candidate: clean statement of the two theses, the level-of-description observation together with a clear account of why it merely relocates the disagreement, an explicit criterion for substantiveness defended against at least one objection (e.g., the charge that any criterion smuggles in the very notion of "mattering" that historians disagree about), and an original case applied competently. It should reach a verdict on whether the historian's two claims can be jointly substantive, rather than ending inconclusively.

The marking scheme should be out of 25 marks, allocate the marks across the components above, and for each component include a one-line note on the mistake students most commonly make (for example: treating contingency and inevitability as contradictories simpliciter; assuming lock-in mechanisms like switching costs prove the *importance* of the locked-in outcome rather than merely its persistence; or offering the level-of-description point as if it dissolved the dispute). Please also add a short paragraph explaining what separates a first-class answer from a good-but-not-excellent one, stated in terms of specific moves rather than general impressions.
