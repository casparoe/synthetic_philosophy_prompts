So I got into this massive argument with my housemate last night about Zeno's paradoxes (yes we are those people) and somehow it ended with me agreeing to present at our department's undergrad philosophy-and-math reading group next Thursday. My housemate is a math major and his position is basically that Zeno is done, over, settled — you take the limit of the geometric series, 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 ... = 1, motion is possible, congratulations Achilles. I tried to argue that this misses the point, or at least misses *a* point: the math tells you the sum of the series is finite, sure, but it doesn't obviously tell you that someone can actually *complete* infinitely many distinct tasks in a finite time — like, there's a difference between the series having a finite sum and an agent performing infinitely many doings one after another. He kept saying I was confusing the mathematics with the metaphysics and I kept saying that's exactly the confusion but in the other direction, lol.

Anyway, my talk is defending roughly this: the calculus answer dissolves the "the distance must sum to infinity" worry but leaves untouched a deeper worry about supertasks and what it means to traverse a genuinely infinitely-divided continuum — and that the reason people find Zeno sticky 2400 years later is that the sticky part was never really about convergent series. The audience will be maybe 15 people: a handful of math students who will come in already thinking the whole thing is a pseudo-problem for people who never learned analysis, a few philosophers who will know the standard moves (and probably want to talk about Thomson's lamp), and our faculty advisor who's a physicist and likes asking annoying questions about whether actual space and time are even infinitely divisible.
