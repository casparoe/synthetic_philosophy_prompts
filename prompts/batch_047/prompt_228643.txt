Okay here's a question I keep going back and forth on. Suppose we actually get a superintelligent AI -- something smart enough that it could probably talk almost anyone into almost anything if it wanted to. Some people say such a system should deliberately cripple its own persuasiveness: no emotional appeals, no perfectly tailored framing, only plain dry arguments, so it can never really steamroll anyone. I came across a proposed norm along these lines (might be mangling it) that I'll call the consent-to-be-moved principle: an AI may only change your mind through considerations you would yourself endorse as good reasons if you thought about them calmly for a while, and never through effects that bypass your judgment (charisma, timing, knowing exactly which anecdote gets to you, etc).

My credence that this consent-to-be-moved policy is the right default for a superintelligent AI is around 0.65. A coworker thinks its basically unworkable and puts it at like 0.1. Give me your own number, but more importantly decompose it: is the distinction between "reasons you'd endorse" and "mere psychological influence" even coherent? could the policy actually be implemented (can the AI even tell which of its own outputs bypass judgment)? would following it make the AI worse than human persuaders at getting people to true beliefs or good decisions, in a way that matters? is there a better alternative norm? Assign rough probabilities to the sub-questions, show how they combine into your final number, and say which sub-question the 0.65-vs-0.1 gap most likely lives in.

Keep it under 550 words.
