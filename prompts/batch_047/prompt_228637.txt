I sit on the ethics committee of a regional health system, and we are rewriting our protocol for allocating scarce resources when we cannot treat everyone — one dose of a drug, one ICU bed, one surgical team during a mass-casualty event. The committee is split on a question with a philosophical literature behind it: when we must choose between saving a smaller group and a larger group, should the numbers count? One faction keeps invoking John Taurek's argument in "Should the Numbers Count?" that we owe each person an equal chance and should flip a coin rather than automatically save the many; the other faction finds that conclusion obviously wrong. Neither side has read the paper carefully, and I suspect both are caricaturing it.

Write a decision procedure the committee could actually adopt: the ordered questions to ask about a case (whether the competing claims are of the same kind and urgency, what Taurek's equal-chance proposal would require here, what considerations might justify weighting numbers without straightforwardly aggregating welfare, whether any individual's stake is so much larger that it changes the comparison), what counts as a settled answer at each step, and where the procedure must hand the decision back to the committee because philosophy genuinely has not settled the question. Be explicit about what Taurek actually argued versus the common misreadings of him, and do not present either the aggregating view or the anti-aggregation view as the consensus where there is none.

Then run the procedure on two cases: (1) a single dose of an antidote that could save either one patient or be divided to save five patients with identical prognoses; (2) the same dose, where it could save one patient's life or prevent five patients from permanent but non-fatal paralysis. If the procedure yields a verdict on either case that will be unpalatable to some members, say so rather than smoothing it over.
