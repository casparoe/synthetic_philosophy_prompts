Last night a close friend and I spent the better part of four hours arguing about rent control. We had read the same three empirical studies and the same policy report beforehand, specifically so that the disagreement would not be about who had seen what. I came away thinking the evidence, on balance, favors well-designed rent stabilization for incumbent tenants; he came away thinking the same evidence shows it harms the housing market overall. Neither of us moved an inch. Each of us regards the other as at least an equal on questions like this, and we parted — politely — with each privately concluding that the other was being slightly unreasonable. I then spent today thinking about the philosophical literature on peer disagreement, and I was struck by the suspicion that the literature is in exactly the condition my friend and I ended up in.

The positions are familiar. Conciliationism, in its equal-weight form, holds that discovering that a peer disagrees with you is higher-order evidence that requires substantial revision of your confidence, roughly toward the midpoint, and that this revision must be independent of the first-order reasoning that produced your view. Steadfast views hold that the disagreement is simply one more piece of evidence to be weighed alongside everything else, and that if your original reasoning was sound and you have no independent grounds to doubt it, you may rationally retain most of your confidence. The debate has run for nearly two decades, and it seems to me stalled in a recognizable pattern: a small stock of toy cases is recycled (shared mental arithmetic at dinner, judgments about the weather), and the same two objections are traded without movement. Steadfastness is accused of illicitly privileging one's own perspective; conciliationism is accused of self-defeat, since there is peer disagreement about conciliationism itself, and of enjoining spinelessness. New work mostly re-fences the old cases.

What I want from you is not a verdict on who is right, but a diagnosis of the impasse. Is the disagreement verbal — are the two sides answering different questions under one name, say "what credence does the total evidence support" versus "what credence is it rational for a fallible agent to hold"? Is there a hidden premise doing the work? Or is it bedrock intuition that neither side can argue for?

I will offer my own hypothesis, as a hypothesis. The impasse is downstream of a deeper, mostly unspoken disagreement about the epistemology of one's own reasoning: whether a thinker can rationally rely on the quality of reasoning she cannot independently and non-circularly certify. Conciliationists, on this reading, implicitly assume she cannot, which generates internalist pressure toward demotion; steadfasters assume she sometimes must, on pain of a regress that ends in skepticism. And the toy cases are selected, on both sides, precisely because they suppress the variable that matters: in mental-arithmetic cases the reasoning is fully displayable and auditable, so the only remaining question is whether to defer. In my argument with my friend — and in most real disagreements — the reasoning on each side is a large mass of judgment that cannot be laid on the table and checked, so the live question is not whether to defer but whether either party can even locate the other's error. A related suspicion: the literature models disagreement as one-shot and treats peerhood as fixed beforehand, though each exchange is itself evidence about peerhood, and iterated models might soften the sharp alternatives.

So: test my hypothesis against what each position's strongest arguments actually commit it to. Can conciliationism be run purely on higher-order evidence — the peer's contrary verdict as evidence about my reliability conditional on my reasoning — without any internalist premise? Does steadfastness need the externalist premise, or only the banal claim that evidence must be evaluated by someone? Then, given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress: a formal result about iterated disagreement or non-auditable reasoning, an empirical finding, a distinction, or nothing at all because the residue is self-trust all the way down. Finally, apply the diagnosis back to last night: once my friend and I had shared all the evidence, what were we actually disagreeing about, and is there anything either of us could have said that would have constituted progress rather than repetition?
