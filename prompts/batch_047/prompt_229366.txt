A friend and I are stuck on the opening of Montaigne's essay “Of Cannibals”:

“When King Pyrrhus invaded Italy, having viewed and considered the order of the army the Romans sent out to meet him; "I know not," said he, "what kind of barbarians" (for so the Greeks called all other nations) "these may be; but the disposition of this army that I see has nothing of barbarism in it."—[Plutarch, Life of Pyrrhus, c. 8.]—As much said the Greeks of that which Flaminius brought into their country; and Philip, beholding from an eminence the order and distribution of the Roman camp formed in his kingdom by Publius Sulpicius Galba, spake to the same effect. By which it appears how cautious men ought to be of taking things upon trust from vulgar opinion, and that we are to judge by the eye of reason, and not from common report.”

Compare these two ways of reading the passage:

1. custom-skepticism: labels like “barbarian” mostly express the observer's habits and ignorance, so we should suspend them until we have reasons that could survive contact with the unfamiliar practice; but reason can still condemn or approve across cultures.

2. code-relativism: a practice is morally right or wrong only relative to the norms of the society in which it occurs, so an outsider's use of reason is really just their own customs dressed up.

My friend says Montaigne obviously can't be a relativist because he explicitly appeals to “the eye of reason.” I think that's too quick: maybe Montaigne thinks what people take to be reason is itself trained by custom. At the same time, the Pyrrhus example seems to say that Greeks can recognize real military order among Romans, which would be weird if every judgment were trapped inside its own code.

Apply your answer to this case: a society practices funerary cannibalism, in which relatives ceremonially eat a small part of an honored dead person's body, and they regard embalming a corpse and keeping it intact underground as a disgusting failure to incorporate the dead back into the community. Europeans in the case feel immediate revulsion at the eating but see their own embalming as respectful. Does Montaigne's opening commit him to saying that neither side may criticize the other, or only that both sides should distrust revulsion until they understand what the practice means? If a practice involved eating dead enemies solely to humiliate them, would the same reading still block condemnation?

Please compare the two readings rather than just splitting the difference, and explain what work “barbarian,” “vulgar opinion,” and “the eye of reason” are doing in the passage. End with exactly one machine-readable line choosing the better reading: \answer{custom-skepticism}, \answer{code-relativism}, or \answer{mixed view}.
