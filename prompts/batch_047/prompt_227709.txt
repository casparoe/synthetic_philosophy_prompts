A friend and I have been arguing about this for weeks and can't settle it. Take the Greek debt crisis, roughly from Greece joining the euro in 2001 through the 2015 referendum, and analyze it as a Ulysses contract. Adopting the euro looks like a textbook precommitment: hand over the printing press, and no future Greek government can inflate or devalue its way out of trouble when tempted. But when the crisis actually hit, it turned out someone else was holding the ropes -- the ECB and the troika decided whether Ulysses stayed tied to the mast. So first: does the precommitment model even fit, and who exactly is Ulysses here -- the Greek state, the electorate, each passing government? Second, what does the July 2015 referendum do to the analysis, where voters rejected the bailout terms and the government accepted basically the same terms days later? Is that the crew rightly ignoring a captain gone mad, or a later self being overruled by an earlier one it never consented to be bound by? And run it in the other direction too: what does the episode show about precommitment theory itself? The theory usually assumes a single continuing self who binds itself voluntarily and knows what it's doing -- none of which seems clearly true here -- and it assumes the problem is time-inconsistency, which maybe wasn't even Greece's core issue. Finally, connect it to the democratic deficit debate: constitutional precommitment is normally defended as the people binding themselves, so what happens to that defense when the binding is designed in Brussels and Frankfurt and enforced against a population that keeps voting against it?
