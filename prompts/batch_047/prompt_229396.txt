Write a dialogue of roughly 900–1200 words between two colleagues -- an economist who is a strict subjective Bayesian and a decision theorist who defends ambiguity aversion as rational -- as they design the incentive scheme for a hiring committee. The committee must choose between two candidate pools for a fellowship, and the economist proposes settling it with the following device: one urn contains 30 red balls and 60 balls that are black or yellow in unknown proportion (the classic Ellsberg urn), a second urn contains 90 balls whose colors were assigned by a mechanical randomization device whose workings are fully documented, and fellowship applicants will be paid according to which ball is drawn. The Bayesian insists that any applicant who declines to bet on red from the unknown urn while betting on red from the known urn is simply confused, and that the committee should treat the unknown composition with a precise prior like any other uncertainty; the decision theorist wants the committee's payment scheme to respect applicants' reluctance to stake money on the unknown urn, and argues this reluctance is not a bias to be corrected but information about the quality of the agent's evidence.

The dialogue should proceed as a genuine argument, with each character answering the other's actual last point rather than restating their opening position. It should pass through at least the following beats, in whatever order the conversation naturally takes: (1) the Bayesian presses the point that refusing both the red bet and the black bet on the unknown urn -- when exactly one must win -- looks like declining a free option, and asks what coherent probability assignments could rationalize preferring the known urn in both comparisons; (2) the decision theorist concedes that no single probability distribution captures their choices but challenges the assumption that an agent with genuinely impoverished evidence owes the world a precise distribution, and distinguishes between uncertainty about an outcome and uncertainty about which model of the outcome is right; (3) the Bayesian deploys a dynamic argument -- e.g., that ambiguity-sensitive choices expose the agent to being dutch-booked across time or to regretting their own policy when the sequence of choices is laid out in advance -- and the decision theorist must respond to the *specific* version offered, not a generic one (responses might involve refusing the package deal, resolute choice, or questioning whether the axiom being violated deserves its normative status); (4) at some point the Bayesian asks whether the decision theorist would make the same choices if they had to run the committee's scheme a thousand times, forcing the decision theorist to confront the difference between one-off and repeated decisions; and (5) the conversation ends not with agreement but with each articulating, crisply and without caricature, what they take the other's view to get right and what fact about the world would change their own mind.

Both characters must be maximally competent: the Bayesian should know the strongest replies to ambiguity aversion (including the charge that the ambiguity-averse agent violates the sure-thing principle and can be turned into a money pump under suitable framings), and the decision theorist should know the standard Bayesian responses and have non-strawman answers ready. Neither should misrepresent Ellsberg's setup or the sure-thing principle. Avoid having either character quote real papers or cite results by name; the arguments should stand on their own. The tone is collegial but sharp -- two people who respect each other and therefore do not pull punches. Do not append any commentary, summary, or analysis after the dialogue ends; the last line of dialogue is the end of the piece.
