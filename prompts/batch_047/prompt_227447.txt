Write a dialogue of roughly 900–1,200 words between two philosophers hashing out a variant of the Ship of Theseus that I've never seen discussed in exactly this form.

The setup: two ships, the Kallipos and the Eudoxus, are moored side by side in the same harbor for a long refit. The dockyard's master shipwright, as a cost-saving exercise in standardizing parts across the fleet, swaps exactly one matched pair of components between the two ships each day — a mast for a mast, a plank for a plank, an oar for an oar — installing each removed part into the corresponding slot on the other vessel. After many months, every single original component of the Kallipos is now installed in the hull called the Eudoxus, and vice versa. At no point was either ship ever disassembled; both remained continuously seaworthy and in service throughout.

One interlocutor defends the view that each ship persists: the hull called the Kallipos is still the Kallipos, merely now built of different matter, because identity survives any sufficiently gradual change and no day on which it could have ceased to exist can be named. The other defends the view that the ships have exchanged matter and therefore exchanged what they are made of, so that the original Kallipos is now, in every material respect, the thing across the dock — and that a ship is nothing over and above its material constitution at each stage.

A few requirements. First, both characters should be competent and argue in good faith; neither should be a strawman. The gradualist must actually face the strongest pressure against their view — that the end state is, plank-for-plank, the reverse of the start state, and it seems perverse that moving the same parts to the same places all at once would swap the ships' identities while doing it one piece a day swaps nothing. The materialist must actually face the sorites argument against theirs — if replacing one plank can't destroy a ship's identity, and replacing a second can't either, the induction seems to run all the way through.

Second, I'd like them to converge at least once on a genuine crux: a place where they can state, precisely, what observation or principle their disagreement turns on. In the spirit of the rationalist tradition of tabooing contested words, have one of them propose at some point that they stop using the phrase "the same ship" and redescribe the situation in purely operational terms — who the harbormaster should charge the mooring fees to, which ship a sailor who served on the original Kallipos would recognize as the one she served on, which vessel's logbook continues which — and then have them examine whether anything substantive remains in dispute once the question is reframed that way, or whether the disagreement dissolves. It should not be obvious which character this maneuver favors.

Third, the dialogue may end either in resolution or in a sharply articulated disagreement, but either way it should end with each character able to state the other's strongest argument better than the other stated it.

Tone: the characters may be collegial and even enjoy the argument, but keep the prose tight and the philosophy load-bearing rather than decorative.
