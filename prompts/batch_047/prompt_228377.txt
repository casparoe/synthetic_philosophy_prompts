I am setting the final exam for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the ethics of promising. Please write one essay question that students answer in 75 minutes, together with a model answer of roughly 600 words and a marking scheme out of 20.

The question should resist being answered by a tour of the standard positions (Hume on artificial virtues, act-utilitarian and rule-utilitarian derivations, Rawls's practice account, Scanlon on expectations, Kantian universalizability). The specific crux I want it to force is this: expectation-based accounts locate the wrong of promise-breaking in the disappointment or manipulation of the promisee's reliance, but they seem to struggle with cases where the promisee never forms the relevant expectation — for instance, a deathbed promise made to someone too far gone to understand it, or a promise to a hardened cynic who fully expects to be let down. The question should ask students to diagnose whether these cases are a genuine counterexample to expectation accounts or merely an awkwardness, and what a rival account must commit to in order to do better — for example, whether treating promising as the exercise of a normative power explains the wrong without smuggling in the very obligation it was meant to ground.

The marking scheme should be built around five must-hit points, with marks allocated per point (totaling 20), and for each point a one- or two-sentence note on the characteristic way a weaker candidate mishandles it — e.g., treating the deathbed case as settled by the fact that the promisor still believes the promisee understood, or assuming that a normative-power account is circular without actually showing where the circularity enters. The model answer should take a definite position rather than surveying both sides evenly, while acknowledging the strongest objection to the position it takes and explaining why that objection does not settle the matter. The question itself should be a single prompt of at most three sentences, suitable for printing on an exam paper, and should not mention any philosopher by name — I want to see whether students know which machinery applies, not whether they can match names to views.
