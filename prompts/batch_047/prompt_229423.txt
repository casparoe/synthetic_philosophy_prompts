Here's roughly where we each stand, so the document has something to work with. Her view (the "no real difference" camp): lying and misleading are both just intentionally getting someone to believe something false, the saying-vs-implicating line is a fact about language not about morality, and the intuition that misleading is gentler is basically a self-serving myth people use to feel clean while doing teh same thing. My view: there's a genuine difference, because when you lie you stake your own authority on the falsehood, whereas when you mislead with literally true statements the false conclusion is something the listener infers — they're partly a participant in their own deception, and that changes what you've done to them, even if both are wrong.

Cases that came up, which the statement has to engage with:

- Cancelling plans with a false excuse ("my aunt's in town") vs a true-but-pointless one ("I have so much laundry").
- Answering "were you out with Dan last night?" with "I was home by ten" — true, but Dan was over before ten.
- The murderer at the door: is steering him wrong with truths any better than a flat lie, or is scrupulousness there just dodging responsibility while getting the same result?
- One I raised late in the night: a blatant lie nobody believes vs a subtle misleading that fully works. If lying is worse because of what the liar commits to, this comparison should come out a specific way — and Maya thinks it doesn't.
