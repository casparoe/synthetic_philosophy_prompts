I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums since the Overcoming Bias era, and the recent cycle of discourse about language models and the Turing test has left me frustrated in a specific way: everyone argues about whether some system "passed" or whether passing would even count, but almost nobody states what a behavioral criterion for mindedness is supposed to do in the first place. The debate has a standard-shaped hole in it.

On rereading Turing's "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (Mind, 1950), I was struck by a passage from section 2 that is doing more work than the famous parts of the paper:

"The new problem has the advantage of drawing a fairly sharp line between the physical and the intellectual capacities of a man. No engineer or chemist claims to be able to produce a material which is indistinguishable from the human skin. It is possible that at some time this might be done, but even supposing this invention available we should feel there was little point in trying to make a 'thinking machine' more human by dressing it up in such artificial flesh. The form in which we have set the problem reflects this fact in the condition which prevents the interrogator from seeing or touching the other competitors, or hearing their voices."

What I want is the specification. Write the desiderata that any behavioral criterion for mindedness -- any test that infers thought or intelligence from observable performance alone -- must meet to count as adequate, with the case or consideration motivating each requirement. Concretely:

- State each desideratum precisely, then give the case behind it. The list should at least engage with: a system that passes a loosely run imitation game by retrieval and pattern-matching rather than anything we would pre-theoretically call thinking (the giant-lookup-table construction); a locked-in patient who can produce no behavior at all; an octopus or corvid whose cognition is real but not conversational; a hypothetical alien or uploaded mind with no shared cultural context to imitate; and a present-day language model that fails the test only because it has been trained to announce that it is one.
- Distinguish explicitly between requirements that are genuinely non-negotiable for any behavioral criterion and requirements that are the signature commitments of particular camps (functionalists, biological naturalists, dualists about consciousness, people who think the test detects intelligence but say nothing about experience), and say which camp each of the latter belongs to.
- Respect the distinctions the list depends on: a criterion for detecting mindedness versus a definition of it versus evidence for it; competence versus performance; a machine passing versus an interrogator being fooled. If any of these collapses under pressure, say where.
- Flag any pair or subset of desiderata you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, and explain why -- this is the part I most care about, since an unsatisfiable corner of the list is itself a result.
- Identify the single desideratum that excludes the most candidate criteria currently in circulation, and say what survives it.

Then, second and more briefly: interpret the quoted passage. Turing is clearly smuggling at least one substantive desideratum into the design of the test under the guise of a convenience -- something like embodiment- and substrate-independence -- and the "artificial flesh" argument is his defense of it. Extract the desiderata implicit in the passage, assess whether the sharp line it draws between physical and intellectual capacities is a principled requirement or an artifact of the teleprinter technology available in 1950, and score Turing's own imitation game against your full list, including whether it meets requirements Turing himself never stated. I would guess 600-900 words for the desiderata section and 300-400 for the Turing analysis, but prioritize completeness of the requirements over hitting those numbers.
