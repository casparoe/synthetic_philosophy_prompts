I'm a senior software engineer who has spent most of my career on large, long-lived systems with many contributors -- the kind where the real enemy is not bad intentions but undocumented behavior, contradictory specifications, and implementations that quietly drift away from what the spec says. I have essentially no legal philosophy background, but I keep running into references to the Hart–Fuller debate and I want to understand it in terms I actually think in.

Here is what I'd like. Take the core dispute -- Hart's legal positivism and separability thesis on one side, Fuller's claim that law has an internal morality captured by his eight desiderata for legal systems (generality, publicity, prospectivity, clarity, non-contradiction, possibility of compliance, stability through time, and congruence between the rules as announced and the rules as administered) -- and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary of my world: specifications, documented versus actual behavior, deprecation policies, breaking changes, regression tests, undefined behavior, the difference between a system satisfying its spec and a spec being worth satisfying. I want each side's position rendered in those terms, including where Hart concedes a "minimum content of natural law" and whether that concession has a counterpart in engineering practice.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I care about most. For each of Fuller's desiderata, say whether it maps cleanly onto an engineering norm or whether something was lost. More importantly: Fuller insists his desiderata constitute a morality -- that a system failing them badly enough is not just bad law but not law at all, and that this internal morality connects to external moral goodness. Engineering has an analogue of the first move (a "specification" that is self-contradictory, retroactively rewritten, and divergent from the implementation arguably stops being a specification at all), but I suspect the second move has no counterpart, because nothing in my vocabulary plays the role of governing human beings or being backed by coercive force. Tell me whether that suspicion is right. Is there anything in the philosophical dispute that simply cannot be expressed in engineering terms -- and if so, is that because my vocabulary is missing something real, or because the philosophers are treating a feature of law's social function as if it were a feature of rule-systems as such?

One request about calibration: if this translation project rests on a confused premise -- if, say, the Hart–Fuller debate is really about something other than what the eight desiderata make it look like, or if I've misdescribed either side's position -- say so plainly and correct me before proceeding, rather than politely translating a misunderstanding. Likewise, if parts of my engineering vocabulary lead me to caricature one side, flag where the caricature comes from.
