In the modern revival of Stoicism, the dichotomy of control is usually presented as practical advice: desire only what is up to you, and treat everything else as indifferent. This sits uneasily with two other Stoic commitments that modern popularizers also endorse: the doctrine of 'preferred indifferents' (health, reputation, and the success of one's projects are genuinely to-be-preferred rather than objects of apathy) and the Stoic insistence that we have duties to act vigorously in the world rather than withdraw from it. A common modern fix is the strategy of internalizing goals: aim at the promotion, the victory, or the recovery, but define success for yourself as having judged and acted well, so that an adverse outcome never registers as a failure on your part. A related ancient device is the reserve clause, undertaking every action with the implicit rider that fortune may decide otherwise. Please explain whether these maneuvers genuinely resolve the tension or merely redescribe attachment to outcomes in safer vocabulary; in particular, if an internalized goal is fully achieved whenever one acts well, what work is the external goal still doing, and if it is doing no work, in what sense does the Stoic still really prefer health to sickness? Give your answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion.
