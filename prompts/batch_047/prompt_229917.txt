I keep going back and forth on this one and I want a proper test case for it. Construct a thought experiment with two artificial systems that are behaviorally indistinguishable in practice -- give them the same sequence of inputs over your whole interaction with them and they produce the same outputs, including sincere-sounding reports of pain, distress, and pleasure -- but where it's at least seriously arguable that one of them is a moral patient and the other is an empty shell. The point of the case is to figure out wether we could owe something to a system whose inner workings we can't inspect, so don't cheat by stipulating the verdicts.

Constraints: (1) both systems have to be physically buildable in principle -- finite memory, finite runtime, no actual infinities, no simulated universes or Boltzmann funny business; (2) the difference between them has to be architectural (for instance, live counterfactual-supporting computation versus a giant precomputed table of responses that only ever gets traversed along one path, or something cleverer you come up with), not anything an outside observer could detect just by interacting; (3) the verdicts have to be generated by the theories, not asserted.

After the scenario, walk through at least two positions: a permissive functionalist view on which matching the actual input-output profile, plus maybe the right behavioral dispositions, is enough for sentience, and a stricter view on which sentience requires genuine causal/counterfactual organization, so that a system which never actually runs the relevant computation doesn't count. Show what each position says about each system and why. Then: tell me which single feature of the case is doing all the work, what happens to the verdicts if you remove that feature, and what the strongest move is for whichever side comes out looking worse -- e.g. can the permissive functionalist just bite the bullet and say the lookup table suffers too, and what exactly does that cost them?

One more thing: when you describe where the literature stands, be clear about what's basically agreed among people who work on this stuff versus what's still live and contested, and don't present any particular theory of consciousness as the settled consensus when it isn't.
