I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm working on a column about the St. Petersburg paradox — the coin-flip game where the pot doubles every time tails comes up, so the expected payoff is infinite. A mathematician friend walked me through this argument over coffee and I can't find the hole in it, but the conclusion can't be right. Roughly as he gave it:

(1) Rational choice just is maximizing expected utility; that's the one thing in decision theory everyone signs off on.
(2) No sane person pays $25,000 to play the St. Petersburg game once, despite the infinite expected payoff. So rationality isn't about expected money — the utility of money has to diminish at the margins.
(3) But if utility is unbounded, you can rebuild the paradox: offer prizes whose *utilities* grow fast enough to outrun the halving probabilities, and you're back to a game with infinite expected utility.
(4) So utility must be bounded above.
(5) Bounded utility means that for any probability, however tiny, there's some guaranteed mundane outcome you'd prefer over a shot at the best outcome imaginable at those odds. One in a trillion: a guaranteed nice weekend beats a one-in-a-trillion chance at a century of perfect happiness for everyone alive.
(6) (5) is absurd. So either maximizing expected utility is wrong, or the intuition in (2) is wrong and you really should hand over your savings for one coin-flip game.

Neither exit seems live, and I definately don't want to tell my readers to mortgage their homes for a martingale. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? If I have to sacrifice something I'd rather keep (2) than (1), but tell me the price either way.
