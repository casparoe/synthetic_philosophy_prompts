Last night, over too much coffee, a friend and I spent the better part of four hours arguing about whether the world is becoming less violent. I defended the view that it is; he countered with an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept but whose individual steps I have been unable to fault. What follows is my reconstruction of his reasoning, rendered as fairly as I can manage, since I may need help finding the exit.

1. Comparing the level of violence in two eras is meaningful only if the same thing is counted as violence in both. Otherwise the two figures measure different quantities, and the comparison between them is empty.

2. What is counted as violence is not stable across history. Dueling, public execution, the corporal punishment of children and servants, and territorial conquest were, in their time, classified as honor, justice, discipline, and glory respectively; today they are classified as violence. Conversely, our era counts as violence things earlier eras barely registered as events worth recording at all: psychological abuse, marital rape, various forms of state coercion. The historical record itself was compiled under those older classifications.

3. Therefore any long-run statistics on violence pair numbers generated under our present classification with numbers generated under past classifications.

4. Quantities produced under different classificatory schemes are incommensurable. No amount of statistical care can repair a mismatch in what is being measured in the first place.

5. So there is no fact of the matter about whether violence has declined. Apparent declines are artifacts of redefinition: we are reading our categories back into records compiled under theirs, and the "trend" lives in the categories, not in the world.

Here is why I balk. Substitute "disease," "poverty," "unemployment," or "intelligence" for "violence" and every step goes through unchanged — disease categories, poverty lines, and the very concept of being employed have shifted at least as much as the category of violence has — so the argument equally establishes that there is no fact of the matter about whether health has improved or poverty has fallen since 1800. My friend cheerfully embraced this and called me naive for resisting, which is roughly when the evening stopped being enjoyable. The argument seems to prove that all long-run social measurement is impossible, and I cannot bring myself to believe that.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? For what it is worth: I would most like to keep (2), which strikes me as plain historical fact, and I would hate to surrender the thought that what contemporaries took their own practices to be matters to how we may legitimately describe them. If I must give up (1) or (4) instead, I want to know exactly what picture of measurement I am thereby committing myself to.
