I help run an internal forecasting tournament at my firm: each week, analysts assign probabilities to questions like whether a product launch will slip or whether a client will renew, and once the outcomes are known, we score everyone with the Brier score — the mean squared difference between the probabilities they gave and 0 or 1 for what actually happened. We chose it because we were told it is a "strictly proper" scoring rule, meaning an analyst maximises her expected score by reporting exactly what she believes rather than shading her probabilities strategically. Lately, though, our new head of analytics has proposed replacing it with a scheme that awards points only for predictions made at 90 per cent confidence or higher that come true, on the grounds that the business can only act on decisive forecasts. Something about the dispute feels philosophical to me, and I'd like it made explicit.

What conception of epistemic value does our existing practice presuppose? Scoring graded credences for their closeness to the truth seems to assume that credences, rather than outright beliefs, are the things epistemically assessable, that their goodness consists in accuracy, and that accuracy comes in degrees measurable on a single scale — and perhaps, in the insistence on strict propriety, that an epistemic norm is only acceptable if it never rewards insincerity. Which rival views does that quietly rule out: a picture on which only full belief can be rational or irrational, or one on which justification, rather than nearness to truth, is the relevant good? And what does my colleague's alternative presuppose — that confidence thresholds track actionability, or that accuracy below some level of boldness is worthless? Finally, is there a tension in the practice of one and the same organisation evaluating its people by graded accuracy while its public communications issue only categorical predictions? If we adopted a view on which only binary belief is epistemically evaluable, what would have to change in the tournament, and conversely, what would the categorical communications have to look like if we took the scoring-rule picture fully seriously?

Please give your answer in no more than 600 words, and distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely sits comfortably with.
