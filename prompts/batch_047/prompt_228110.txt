At a recent cognitive science seminar, a speaker ended a lively discussion about whether perception ever gives us the world as it actually is by saying, flatly, 'perception is controlled hallucination' -- and then took no further questions, as though that settled it. I've since noticed the slogan (alongside its cousin, 'the brain is a prediction machine') doing a lot of quiet work in this literature: sometimes it's offered as a striking empirical discovery, sometimes as a conceptual truism about any inferential system, and sometimes as a deflationary way of dissolving philosophical questions about veridicality. I'd like a precise disambiguation. Lay out, as an organized list, the distinct theses this slogan could be expressing -- ranging from the nearly trivial (e.g., that perception involves top-down processing at all) through intermediate claims (that perceptual content is dominated by priors, that perception differs from hallucination only in being causally constrained by sensory input, that there is no principled line between perception and confabulation) up to the genuinely radical (that the phenomenal world is a construction with no more claim on reality than a dream). For each reading, state: what exactly it claims; whether it is trivial, substantive-but-defensible, or implausible; what empirical or conceptual evidence would bear on it; and whether it would actually license the dismissive use my speaker made of it -- that is, whether it answers, rather than merely relabels, the question of whether perception can be veridical. Conclude by identifying which reading(s) you think people who repeat the slogan most often have in mind, and whether those readings are the ones that would support their rhetoric.
