I help run a small philosophy of physics reading group (mostly final-year undergraduates, plus a couple of graduate students who keep us honest), and this term we've been working through the Everett interpretation and the problem of what probability could possibly mean in a universe where every outcome occurs. Ahead of writing up my own position, I've tried to set down precisely what I take myself to believe, and I'd like you to audit the set for internal coherence. I'm not asking you to defend or attack Everett itself — assume, for the sake of the exercise, that the interpretation is true. My question is whether my particular package of commitments hangs together.

Here is where I've landed. (1) Everettian quantum mechanics is literally true: there is only unitary evolution, no collapse; after a measurement there exist several decoherent branches, each fully real, each containing a successor of me; and there is no further fact about which branch "I will end up on" — talk suggesting otherwise is at best a manner of speaking. (2) I know the quantum state and my own place in it completely, so before the measurement there is nothing relevant I'm ignorant of: what will happen is exactly everything the state evolves into. (3) Nevertheless the Born rule is rationally binding on me. The Deutsch–Wallace decision-theoretic argument shows that an agent whose preferences satisfy ordinary consistency constraints must act as if maximising expected utility using the squared amplitudes as weights — so that, faced with a measurement whose up-branch has amplitude √(1/3), I must be indifferent between a prize awarded to my up-successor and a one-in-three objective lottery ticket, whatever that latter phrase turns out to mean. Crucially, this derivation needs no notion of uncertainty: it extracts the weights from non-probabilistic physics plus rationality alone. (4) The Born weights are also genuine credences. I ought to be roughly one-third confident of spin-up in that same case, and it is because observed frequencies keep matching these credences that experimental evidence confirms Everett over its rivals. (5) Since my successors are all equally real and all equally continuations of me, I should care about each one's welfare equally, whatever the amplitudes: a low-amplitude successor is no less me, and amplitude differences cannot make one of my futures matter more than another.

Please give me your overall verdict first, in one or two sentences — does the package hang together? Then work through the tensions you find, in descending order of severity. For each genuine tension, identify exactly which commitments conflict, state the cheapest single amendment that restores consistency, and say what that amendment costs me. Also flag any pair of commitments that looks tense but is not, and explain why the appearance is misleading. I genuinely don't know where the weak joint is, so don't assume I've signalled it, and don't propose fixes that amount to abandoning the Everettian framework itself, since that is held fixed. Confine the whole reply to 600 words at most.
