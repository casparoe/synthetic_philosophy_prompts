I'm a journalist working on a long piece about the philosophy of vagueness, and I've talked myself into epistemicism: vague predicates like "tall" and "heap" draw perfectly sharp boundaries, classical logic survives intact, and our inability to say where the cutoff falls is ignorance on our side, not indeterminacy in the language. Before I defend this in print, I want to know how exposed it actually is. What would count against epistemicism -- not the standard grumble that a sharp last-tall-nanosecond is counterintuitive, which every defender has already swallowed, but genuine defeaters? Assess three candidates in particular: first, the argument that higher-order vagueness forces the epistemicist to posit unknowable boundaries upon unknowable boundaries until the view becomes indistinguishable from the supervaluationism it was meant to beat; second, a hypothetical psycholinguistic finding that competent speakers, under idealized conditions, each draw stable cutoffs that nonetheless drift from day to day within a single speaker -- would that embarrass the view, confirm it, or be irrelevant to it; third, a formal result showing that the margin-for-error principles the position relies on cannot all hold together in languages with certain common features. Which of these would an honest epistemicist concede as damaging rather than merely awkward, and is there anything the view genuinely risks at all -- or has the appeal to in-principle unknowability insulated it from refutation, and if so, is that a virtue or a symptom? Please answer in British English.
