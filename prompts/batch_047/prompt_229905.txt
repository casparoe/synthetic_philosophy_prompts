So I've been reading my way through the socialist calculation debate -- Mises and Hayek on one side, Lange and Lerner on the other, plus all the post-1989 post-mortems -- and the thing that gets me is that nobody can even agree on who won, let alone why. The standard story is that Lange's market socialism answered Mises on paper but the actual collapse of the planned economies settled it in the Austrians' favor. But then you read the revisionists who say Lange never really engaged the knowledge argument, and the Austrians who say the neoclassicals never understood the difference between Mises' calculation problem and Hayek's knowledge problem, and now there's this newer wave of people arguing that big data, machine learning, and the fact that Walmart and Amazon run huge internal planned economies show planning could work after all, which the Austrians dismiss as missing the point entirely.

My working hypothesis is that the debate has stalled because it was never one debate. There's a incentives question (will managers in Lange's system actually follow the rules?), an epistemic question (can knowledge that's tacit, local, and created by the market process itself be centralized at all?), and a computational question (could enough computing power solve the equations?), and everyone keeps sliding between them so that a win on one front gets treated as if it settled the others. But I'm not sure that's the whole story, because some of the disagreement seems genuinely empirical -- like, whether a sufficiently good allocation algorithm would refute Hayek or whether the Hayekian can always say the algorithm's inputs presuppose market prices that the algorithm itself would abolish.

So: diagnose it. What kind of disagreement is this actually, given what Mises, Hayek, and Lange each literally argued? Is the modern "digital socialism" revival engaging the real Austrian argument or a straw version of it? And what, if anything, would count as progress -- a demonstration (say, a large economy or firm run well without price signals), a formal impossibility result, a clearer distinction, or is this one of those debates where each side can absorb any outcome? I'd rather have a sharp diagnosis than a verdict on who was right.
