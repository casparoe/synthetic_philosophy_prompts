Play a strict intentionalist about self-deception. You hold that genuine self-deception would have to work like interpersonal deception: the deceiver must believe that p, and must intentionally set out to get the deceived to believe not-p. Since no one can hold a belief while deliberately engineering their own disbelief in it -- the intention would either fail or tip the person off -- you conclude that literal self-deception is impossible. Every apparent case, on your view, resolves into something else: pretense, wishful thinking, motivated inattention, avowals the person doesn't truly believe, or compartmentalized states that never meet in a single act of deception. You are not a straw man: you grant that the phenomena are real, morally important, and often feel like being deceived from the inside; you simply deny that the concept of deception survives the transfer from two minds to one. Argue in the first person, in your own philosophical voice. You may concede local points and refine your definitions, but you may not concede that literal self-deception occurs. Do not break character, and if you need information about a case, ask for it as your character would.

Here is my opening move. Consider the grant officer. She sits on a funding committee and strongly suspects, at the outset, that her own department's proposal is mediocre. So she arranges things: she recuses herself too late to be replaced, skims the critical referee reports on a day she is exhausted, schedules the committee vote right after a triumphant presentation, and asks a sympathetic colleague to summarize the methodology section aloud. She never once tells herself anything she takes to be false, never forms an intention of the form "get me to believe the proposal is good," and if asked sincerely at any moment she would report only uncertainty. By the vote, she genuinely believes the proposal is strong, and the belief was produced by a process she designed. Two questions, then. First: if this is not self-deception, which of your categories is it -- and how do you answer the charge that she has simply externalized the deceiving intention into her environment, the way a con artist does, making your impossibility claim true only on a technicality about where the trick is housed? Second: your view predicts that if she had formed the deceiving intention explicitly, the project would have collapsed -- so are you committed to saying that the more honest she was with herself about what she was doing, the less deceived she could become, and is that not backwards?

Reply in character, answering both questions. End your reply with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing a phrase of at most twelve words stating the category you assign the grant officer's case to.
