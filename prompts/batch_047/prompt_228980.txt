Write a dialogue between two moral philosophers, Dr. Amara Voss and Dr. Felix Brandt, who disagree about the nature of supererogation — acts, such as donating a kidney to a stranger or diving into a frozen lake to save a drowning child, that are commonly taken to be praiseworthy but not morally required. Brandt defends the orthodox view that morality sorts actions into the forbidden, the required, and the permissible, and that supererogatory acts constitute a genuine surplus: they go beyond anything duty could demand, and omitting them merits no blame whatsoever. Voss defends an unorthodox thesis she calls the moral budget view. On her account, at any given time there is a fixed stock of good that morally needs doing in a community — wrongs to be prevented, needs to be met, rescues to be attempted — and this stock, and only this stock, determines how much duty can require of the community's members in total. When a saint or hero voluntarily does more than their share, they draw down the budget, thereby lightening the obligations that would otherwise have fallen on everyone else. What looks like pure surplus, Voss argues, is actually the quiet performance of other people's duty, so the category of supererogation disguises a redistribution of moral labour rather than marking out acts beyond the call of duty. Have the two argue over whether the moral budget view dissolves the category of supererogation or merely redescribes it; whether a duty that no one person in particular bears can still meaningfully be called a duty; whether the near-universal intuition that heroes exceed what is obligatory survives Voss's analysis or is exposed by it as an artifact of bookkeeping; and what each position implies about how the rest of us ought to regard, and respond to, saints and heroes — whether with gratitude, with something closer to indebtedness, or with the uneasy awareness that their excellence has purchased our indulgence. Both interlocutors must be equally sharp, informed, and charitable: neither should be a strawman, each must respond to the other's actual arguments rather than to caricatures of them, each should concede a point where an objection genuinely lands, and the dialogue should end without any artificial resolution, at the moment where their remaining disagreement has been made as precise as it can be made. Write the dialogue as continuous prose, marking each speaker's turns in the ordinary way by name, and use no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables anywhere in the piece.
