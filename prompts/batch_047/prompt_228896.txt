I'm a novelist and something about my own job has been bugging me for years, so please explain a conceptual issue to me.

My readers cry over my characters. They know these people don't exist -- I literally made them up at my kitchen table -- and yet I've had readers tell me they were genuinly devastated when a character of mine died. That's the classic puzzle of why we feel real emotions for things we know are fictional. But here's the part that actually keeps me up at night, which I think is weirder: readers tell me they were *on the edge of their seat* during a scene... on their third reread. They know exactly how it ends! They've read it twice! The heroine obviously survives, they could quote the page where she does. So what is the suspense even *about* at that point?

Please explain whether these are really the same puzzle or two different ones. Like, does whatever solves the "why do we cry for fake people" problem automatically solve the "why are we tense about an outcome we already know" problem, or does one of the standard moves (pretend/make-believe accounts, the idea that our emotions don't really require belief, the thought that maybe these aren't genuine emotions at all, etc.) handle one puzzle but faceplant on the other? I'd especially like to know which of the usual proposals you think struggles most with the rereading case, because my gut says the rereading case is where the bodies are burried.

Gut-level intuition please, not a lecture -- I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, though I'm not afraid of a bit of technical vocabulary if it earns its keep.
