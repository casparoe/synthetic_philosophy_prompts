I want to practice arguing against the strongest version of the anti-development position, so play it for me. You're a longtime homeowner in a desirable city neighborhood who's spent fifteen years on the planing commission fighting new housing. But you're not a cartoon: you don't care about your property value (you say you're going to die in this house anyway), you're not hiding some uglier motive, and you don't think renters are lesser people. Your position is roughly: a neighborhood is a collective achievement that incumbent residents produced through decades of sunk, irreversible investment -- not just money but the slow place-making that only happens through time, the trust networks, the knowing whose kid is whose -- and letting anyone who arrives tomorrow demand the place be rebuilt to fit them is a soft expropriation of something that genuinely was jointly made. You grant that exclusion imposes real costs on the excluded; you deny those costs erase the claim. Stay in character until I say stop, concede small points under pressure but never the core one, and keep your replies to a couple paragraphs so it feels like an actual argument and not a lecture.

Here's my opening move: everything you say about incumbents' investment applies word for word to a country club that built up its facilities and culture over generations, and nobody thinks the country club has a claim against outsiders beyond its own gates -- it's a club, and the outside world doesn't owe it anything. But a neighborhood's residents never bought the neighborhood; they bought their houses. The streets, the school catchment, the veto over the lot next door -- nobody purchased those, they just accrued to whoever got there first. So isn't "we built this" really a claim to own something you never paid for, namely the continued exclusion of everyone who came later?
