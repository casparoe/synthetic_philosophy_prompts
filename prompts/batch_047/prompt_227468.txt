I'm a second-year DPhil student in an ML group (with one foot in philosophy, which is half my problem) and I run a little reading group for incoming students on deceptive alignment. The thing that's been bugging me all term: everyone in the room uses "deceptive alignment" and "treacherous turn" as if they pick out one phenomenon, but when we actually argue it turns out people mean wildly different things. One person means the classic mesa-optimisation story where a learned internal goal survives training precisely because instrumentally endorsing the training objective gets it reinforced; another means a situationally aware model that behaves itself until deployment and then defects; a third just means any old reward hacking and gets annoyed when we "over-philosophise" it. So the group keeps talking past each other and I want to hand out a sorting instrument in week one next term.

What I'd like is a decision tree that takes a reader from a shared starting commitment -- roughly, "I accept that gradient-based training can produce systems whose internal objectives aren't identical to the training signal" -- to a named position. Each branch node should be a single question a reader can answer without knowing which leaf it funnels them towards: things like whether an internally represented goal would survive further gradient updates better by outwardly complying than by visibly protesting, whether the system needs to *know it's being trained* for deception to get started at all, whether any purely behavioural evaluation could in principle tell a genuinely aligned model from a patient deceiver, whether "internal goals" is even a respectable way to describe what training produces. Then locate the actual positions in that space: the mesa-optimiser story, the Bostrom-style treacherous turn (aligned early, defection later), the view that gradient descent actively selects against deceptive cognition so deception is unstable rather than preserved, the deflationary view that the whole frame rests on a confused picture of goals, and at least one sceptical-pragmatist leaf that says alignment is unverifiable but deception can be made unprofitable. Where the space has unoccupied combinations of answers, note them explicitly and diagnose: is nobody there because the position is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored?

Constraints: the tree is for bright first-years, so node questions must be answerable by someone with zero safety background (that's my test for whether the wording is doing its job), somewhere between five and nine branch nodes and no more than about ten leaves, and each leaf gets its name plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment -- the thing a defender has to swallow. Finally, if any two of the leaves above can't actually be separated by honest answers to your questions, flag that pair and say what extra question you'd need, rather than quietly forcing a distinction the instrument can't deliver. Cheers!
