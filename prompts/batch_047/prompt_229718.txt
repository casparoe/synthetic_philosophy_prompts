Anglo-American criminal law generally refuses to punish bystanders who fail to rescue: a stranger who watches a child drown in a shallow pond, doing nothing, commits no crime, while someone who pushes the child in commits murder. Yet the same legal system treats certain omissions as full causes of death -- a parent who refuses to feed an infant, or a nurse who disconnects a ventilator out of spite, is guilty of homicide, not merely of failing to prevent a death. Meanwhile the civil law of negligence routinely holds defendants liable for harms they failed to prevent when a duty of care existed. What theory of causation, or of moral responsibility, does this pattern of doctrines presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view: does the practice assume that omissions are not really causes (with the special-duty cases as pragmatic exceptions), or that omissions cause but normally do not ground responsibility, or something else, such as a view on which causation itself is duty-relative? Say whether the three parts of the practice -- no bystander liability, homicide by omission under special duties, negligence liability for failures to act -- presuppose consistent commitments or pull in different directions, and what would have to change in the doctrine if we instead adopted either a purely moralized view (an omission is a cause whenever a reasonable person would have acted) or a purely metaphysical one (omissions are never causes, full stop).
