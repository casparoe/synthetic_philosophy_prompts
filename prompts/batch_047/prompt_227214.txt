I'm TAing for an undergrad phil of action course and while grading papers I talked myself into an argument that I now think must be broken, because the conclusion is insane. Quick setup for context: there's a famous experiment where people hear about a chairman who is told a new program will make money but also harm the environment; he says he doesn't care about the environment, he just wants profit, and approves the program. Most people say he harmed the environment intentionally. Flip "harm" to "help" -- he equally doesn't care, equally just wants profit -- and almost nobody says he helped it intentionally. This result has been replicated a bunch, in lots of cultures and with lots of variations, so please don't answer by telling me the experiment was bad or the effect isn't real. Assume the data are solid.

Here's my argument. (1) If two vignettes are judged differently and differ in only one respect, that one respect is what's driving the difference in judgment. (2) The harm and help cases differ in exactly one respect: whether the side effect is bad or good. The chairman's mental states are identical -- same foreknowledge, same indifference, same motivation, same decision procedure. (3) People apply "intentionally" in the harm case and withhold it in the help case. (4) So whether something counts as intentional depends partly on the moral status of the outcome, not on the agent's psychology. (5) Any concept whose application conditions include the moral status of outcomes is, to that extent, a moral concept rather than a descriptive one. (6) So "intentionally" is at least partly a moral concept wearing a descriptive disguise. (7) But huge amounts of our practice -- mens rea in criminal law, excuses like "I didn't do it on purpose," the whole of action theory's distinction between things I do and things that merely happen -- treat "intentionally" as a descriptive, psychology-only question. (8) So all of that practice rests on a confusion: there is no purely descriptive fact about whether a foreseen side effect was intentional, only our moral reactions doing the work.

Step (8) is where I get off the bus. It would mean two agents with literally identical psychologies differ in criminal liability only because of how we feel about the outcome, and that every "it was an accident" excuse is just an appeal to moral valence. If I had to keep something it would be (3) and the general idea that the experiment tells us something real about the concept of intentional action -- I don't want the answer to be that the data are just noise or that people are answering a different question than the one asked. So: which step do you reject? Explain why that step looked fine to me (it still does, honestly), and tell me what rejecting it costs -- especially whether I can keep a strong reading of (4) while denying (6), or whether those two stand or fall together.
