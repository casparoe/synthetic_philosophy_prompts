For years I have been collecting instances of a pattern I cannot find an established name for, and I would like to know whether one exists — but I will not be satisfied with a bare label. I want the phenomenon carved properly: what it is, what it is not, and where its edges are.

The pattern, roughly: a species of political discontent that arises in stable, prosperous liberal democracies, that intensifies as material conditions and formal equality improve, and whose object is not the distribution of goods but the distribution of significance. Three instances, at different scales.

At the level of mass politics: in a wealthy, peaceful country whose institutions work tolerably well, the most vigorous political movement of recent years draws its energy from people who are objectively comfortable, and its demands are not for redistribution but for restoration — of the country's standing, of its citizens' sense that their lives add up to something, of an order in which they are not, as they put it, looked down on by people no better than them. At the individual level: young men raised in uneventful middle-class suburbs abandon safety and prospects to join militant causes abroad whose doctrines they often barely understand; what the causes offer is not money, justice, or even belonging, but a life in which something is at stake and in which they would count. At the elite level: people who have already won every prize their society offers — wealth, credentials, fame — lend their support to projects that threaten the very arrangements that made their success possible, as though succeeding within the rules had come to feel worthless next to the prospect of greatness against them.

What strikes me is the common shape. The grievance is genuine, but it is not envy, and it is not the fear of falling; it afflicts the secure as readily as the precarious. Nor does comfort pacify it — if anything, comfort inflames it. I have seen adjacent terms: anomie, which concerns the dissolution of norms rather than the hunger at issue here; ressentiment, which is the souring of the powerless, whereas my pattern grips the powerful too; status anxiety, which fears losing a rank rather than asserting one. There is also the so-called Tocqueville effect — revolutions erupting when conditions improve — but that describes the timing of discontent, not its content.

Two questions. First: is there an established concept for exactly this, and how does it divide the territory from the neighbors I have listed — including the hard case of distinguishing it from legitimate protest by people whose dignity really is denied them? If nothing established fits, say so and coin a term with a definition and boundary cases. Second: a friend insists that this pattern is either a decisive refutation of, or precisely the thing predicted by, Francis Fukuyama's end-of-history thesis — which I know only in summary, as the claim that ideological history had ended in liberal democracy's victory and would presumably settle into contentment. On the summary version, my pattern plainly refutes the thesis. My friend maintains that the thesis contains my pattern. Which is right, and what follows for how the thesis should be read?
