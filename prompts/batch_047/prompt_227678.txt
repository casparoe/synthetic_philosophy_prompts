Here's an exercise I give my students that nobody's nailed yet. Construct a thought experiment where costly signaling theory (the handicap principle, Zahavi and all that) gets it wrong in the interesting direction: a signal that is cheap to fake but nonetheless reliably honest. Constraints: set it in ordinary human life -- hiring, dating, roommate selection, academia, whatever, no peacock tails -- and the honesty can't come from some external enforcement mechanism like contracts, audits, or legal penalties, since that's just smuggling the cost back in through the back door. One paragraph for the scenario itself. Then do the diagnostics: state exactly which feature of your setup replaces cost as the thing keeping fakers out, and show that removing that feature makes deception profitable again. Finally, the hard part: a defender of the handicap principle is going to respond that your scenario secretly contains a cost after all (foregone opportunities, reputational risk, whatever). Tell me whether that response is legitimate or whether it's the theory retreating into unfalsifiability, and what would count as a genuinely cost-free honest signal that the theory just can't accomodate.
