I'm planning the last two meetings of my grad seminar on Darwinian theories of culture — we've already done the memetics material (Dawkins's replicator argument, Dennett's defense of the meme's-eye view, the usual critiques) and a bit of the dual-inheritance literature — and I'm also trying to decide where the second half of a paper I'm drafting should push. While prepping I got stuck on something Darwin says in the preface to the second edition of The Descent of Man (1874), where he's pushing back against critics who read him as a pure selectionist: "my critics frequently assume that I attribute all changes of corporeal structure and mental power exclusively to the natural selection of such variations as are often called spontaneous; whereas, even in the first edition of the 'Origin of Species,' I distinctly stated that great weight must be attributed to the inherited effects of use and disuse, with respect both to the body and mind. I also attributed some amount of modification to the direct and prolonged action of changed conditions of life."

This kind of derailed me. The standard philosophy-of-culture debate fixates on whether cultural change is "genuinely Darwinian" — are there discrete replicators, is copying fidelity high enough, is variation blind or guided — and everyone seems to treat "Darwinian" as shorthand for selection acting on undirected variation. But here's the patron saint of the whole analogy saying that use-inheritance and direct environmental influence do serious work, for the mind included. If Darwin himself was a pluralist about mechanism, maybe the field's organizing question is less central than people assume, and I keep loosing the thread of what should replace it at the top of the pile.

So: given two seminar sessions and half a paper, which open questions in the Darwinian theory of culture matter most, and in what order should I take them up? Candidates I keep circling: whether the theory needs replicators at all or can run on cultural attractors instead; whether intentional, guided variation guts the selectionist framing or just adds another source of variation; what the units and levels of cultural selection even are; whether these models explain anything that plain social history doesn't. But don't just rank my list — tell me what should be on it. Defend the ordering: what makes a question upstream of the others, what's tractable for a philosopher rather than an empirical researcher, what's unduly neglected, and what result or argument over the next few years would change your ranking. And actually use the Darwin passage rather than waving at it — if the field's framing question really does rest on a misreading of Darwin, which question gets promoted in its place, and does anything get demoted that people currently waste time on?
