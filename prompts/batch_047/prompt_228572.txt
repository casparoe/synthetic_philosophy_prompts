So I just spent like three hours at dinner arguing with a friend about advance directives and I need help settling something, or at least making it less mushy. Her position was basically: the person with dementia in front of you right now is the person who matters, so if they seem content and don't want the thing their earlier self demanded, you go with what they want now, full stop. My position was that this makes the advance directive pointless -- the whole reason you write one is that you know future-you might not be in a position to be trusted. We went around in circles on the case where someone writes "if I get severe dementia, stop all treatment including antibiotics" and then years later is sitting in the garden, apparently enjoying the birds, and gets pneumonia.

Here's the thing though. I actually have skin in this game: I help out on my hospital's ethics consult service (I'm a nurse, I do maybe two or three of these a month) and we genuinely have no settled way of handling conflicts between an old directive and the patient's current behavior/wishes. Every consult turns into the same dinner argument I just had.

So: write me a decision procedure the consult team could actually follow for these cases. Things it needs to do:

- The steps in order -- what to establish first about the document itself, then about the patient now, then about the conflict. What counts as "established" at each step (we're not philosophers, we need to know when we can move on).
- How to handle the classic hard cases: the directive is clear but the patient now seems happy and resists; the directive is clear and the patient now seems miserable; the directive is vague ("no extraordinary measures") and current wishes are clear; the patient now wants something their earlier self explicitly forbade, like the person who wrote that they never want to be kept alive in a home but now begs not to be sent back to theirs.
- Somewhere in there it has to deal with the question my friend kept pushing: how much does it matter whether the current self still has enough understanding for their current wishes to count as a real preference vs just a reaction? Don't just say "assess capacity," tell me what the procedure should do with borderline cases.
- Mark the points where the procedure has to stop and a human (and who -- family? the attending? the courts?) makes a value call, because I'd rather have a shorter procedure that's honest about that than a long one pretending to settle everything.
- Where it should just say "honor the directive" or "follow current wishes" without further agonizing, if anywhere.

Then run the procedure on the pneumonia-in-the-garden case and on one more case of your choosing, and one of them should come out in a way I won't like (I'll let you guess which side I was on). Also tell me upfront what a procedure like this can't do that my dinner argument was trying to do, because I suspect part of the fight was really about what makes a person the same person over time and you can't put that in a flowchart.

We're usually working under time pressure -- a consult like this maybe gets a few hours of deliberation total, spread over days -- so don't give me anything that needs a philosopher in the room. And no reading list, I can find the Dworkin stuff myself if I want it.
