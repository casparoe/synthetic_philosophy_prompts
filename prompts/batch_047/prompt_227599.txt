Write me a sonnet about causation by omission -- the old gardener case, where the hired gardener who doesn't water the flowers thereby kills them, while the neighbor across the street, who also didn't water them, killed nothing at all, even though both of them did exactly the same thing (nothing).

Here's the constraint that makes it interesting: whenever the poem refers to the omitted act itself, the line has to come up short -- drop a beat from the pentameter, or leave the expected rhyme hanging with no partner, so the form literally performs the absence the line is about. (Flag for me after the poem which lines you did this in, because I want to check it worked.)

Structure-wise: use the octave to build the naive intuition that nothing can't do anything -- you can't bruise an apple by not touching it, an empty hand leaves no fingerprints. The volta should be the moment the duty walks in: the gardener was *hired*, there was a standing expectation, and suddenly the same nothing counts as a killing. The closing couplet should state what's still puzzling rather than resolve it -- something like: if it's the duty that turns an absence into a cause, then causation is partly made of norms, and that should feel weird for something we thought was pure physics. A specialist should be able to tell you know the norm-sensitive accounts and aren't just doing "absence is spooky." Also -- and this is the part I suspect is hard -- the neighbor has to appear somewhere in the poem, becuase the whole problem lives in the contrast between the two non-waterers.
