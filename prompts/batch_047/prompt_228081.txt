Some time ago I came around to a position I'll call dogmatism about counterevidence. The reasoning behind it is the familiar paradox: if I know that p, then any evidence against p is misleading evidence; and since I know it is misleading, I am rationally entitled to discount it and retain my belief. The conclusion strikes nearly everyone as absurd, but the derivation is two steps from premises I accept, and I have stopped being able to find the leak.

Tell me what would count against this view. I want a map of its exposure, organized along the following lines.

First, rank the candidate defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. For each one, say whether a committed dogmatist would concede that it counts against the view or instead dismiss it — and here I want you to confront the self-application problem directly: dismissing apparent counterevidence is, on the view itself, precisely what rationality requires of me. Does the position thereby render itself immune to refutation? If so, is that immunity a defect, or simply the predictable consequence of a thesis about how one should respond to evidence one knows to be misleading?

Second, the derivation runs on a closure principle for knowledge. If dogmatism falls, must single-premise closure fall with it? Distinguish defeaters that strike the dogmatic conclusion alone from those that would bring closure down too, and assess whether there is a stable position that keeps closure while refusing the dogmatic inference — say, by driving a wedge between knowing that the evidence is misleading and being licensed to ignore it. What does that wedge cost?

Third, the principled line. What distinguishes the rational dogmatist from the merely stubborn believer? Note the difficulty: any line the dogmatist draws will be drawn using the very belief under dispute. Determine whether this circularity is a genuine defeater or a feature that every epistemology shares when pressed.

Fourth, test the view against higher-order evidence: evidence not that p is false, but that my faculties or methods are unreliable — a trusted colleague telling me I have been drugged, say. If the view licenses dismissing that too, I regard the consequence as a near-fatal reductio; if it does not, explain what grounds the restriction without ad hoc patchwork.

On what I would personally find most persuasive: an argument showing the view commits me to an independently implausible verdict in a concrete, ordinary case will move me far more than restatements of the intuition that dismissing evidence is obviously irrational. That intuition is exactly what is under dispute.

Please write your response in American English.
