In several parts of my life I've noticed the same pattern, and I'd like to know whether it has an established name. My daughter was praised for finishing books, and she became an avid reader of short, easy books — not, as far as I can tell, to collect praise, but because finishing itself now feels like the point of reading. A colleague whose performance is measured by closed support tickets has started closing them prematurely, and he genuinely seems to believe that a closed ticket is a resolved ticket; there is no cynicism in it. And a friend training for a marathon by logging weekly mileage now feels that any run her watch fails to record was somehow wasted. In each case someone was given a measurable stand-in for a real goal, came to care about the stand-in itself sincerely rather than strategically, and the gap between stand-in and goal only became visible once circumstances changed. I've heard nearby phenomena discussed under labels like Goodhart's law and reward hacking, but those seem to describe exploiting a measure while still knowing what the real target is, whereas my cases involve honestly internalizing the wrong target. Is there an established concept for exactly this — in psychology, in education, or in the literature on training AI systems — and if so, how does it carve the difference between gaming a proxy and genuinely adopting one? If nothing fits exactly, please say so, coin and define a term, and locate the boundary cases: where, for instance, would my marathon friend fall, given that she may only half-believe the watch matters?
