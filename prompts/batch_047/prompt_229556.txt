I am a TA running a 75-minute section for an upper-level undergraduate game theory course (about 24 students, mixed philosophy and economics backgrounds). They know the definitions of strict and weak dominance, mixed strategies, and best responses, and they have done iterated elimination of strictly dominated pure strategies on paper. What they have not internalized -- and what the lecture alone does not get across -- is that pure-strategy elimination does not exhaust what rational play rules out: an action can be undominated by every pure strategy and still be a best response to no possible belief about the opponent, because some mixed strategy strictly dominates it. Design an in-class activity that makes the students run into this themselves before it is named.

The arc I want: students first analyze a game with the pure-dominance tools they already have and conclude that nothing can be eliminated; they then play it for points over several rounds and discover from the play itself that one action simply never pays off for anyone; only afterward do they construct (ideally themselves, with scaffolding) the mixture that dominates it and see why "never a best response to any belief" and "strictly dominated by some mixed strategy" are the same property in this game.

Specific requirements:

1. Give an explicit payoff matrix with simple numbers (integers or halves), sized so that hand calculation is feasible, and verify in your answer that (a) the target action is not strictly dominated by any pure strategy, (b) a stated mixture strictly dominates it, and (c) once the target action is removed, at least one further round of elimination succeeds, so the game partially unravels and later rounds have somewhere to go. Show the arithmetic for all three checks.
2. Provide rules, materials, a points-based payoff scheme with small stakes that do not involve money, a pairing or rotation scheme that keeps each player's beliefs about the opponent genuinely uncertain, and a round-by-round timeline for the full 75 minutes, including one deliberately premature mid-session poll of who still thinks the target action is playable.
3. Include a short warm-up that makes the eventual discovery land harder rather than giving it away.
4. Write the debrief plan: which moments from the rounds map onto which definitions, and how to lead students to show, for this specific game, that the target action loses against every belief they could hold -- not just against the beliefs that happened to occur in the room.
5. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (at least three concrete failure modes, e.g. mixture arithmetic errors, students playing the target action out of curiosity or spite, the premature poll backfiring) and how to handle each.
6. If time remains, sketch a ten-minute optional extension on order-dependence of weak-dominance elimination, with its own small matrix; if you include it, verify that two different elimination orders genuinely yield different surviving sets.
7. Close with one honest paragraph on what the activity cannot reproduce -- I am thinking of the full common-knowledge-of-rationality machinery behind iterated elimination and rationalizability, and the gap between observing that an action lost in a dozen rounds and proving it can never be a best response -- and what I must supply by lecture.

Keep the whole answer under 1,000 words, and do not include citations or a reading list; I will add references to the course text myself.
