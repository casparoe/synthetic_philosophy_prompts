I am a third-year philosophy student taking a seminar on decision theory, and I have volunteered to lead one fifty-minute tutorial next week in which I teach the Death in Damascus problem to the rest of the group. My classmates know the basics of expected utility and have seen Newcomb's problem, but none of them have encountered cases where no option seems rationally choiceworthy.

For context, here is the case as I plan to present it. Death tells a man in Damascus that they have an appointment tomorrow. Death, who has an unbroken track record of correct predictions, has already predicted where the man will be tomorrow and has arranged to be at that place. The man knows this. He can stay in Damascus or travel overnight to Aleppo, and that is all he can do. Whatever Death predicted, going to the predicted place is fatal. Since the prediction is already made, staying or fleeing changes nothing about where Death waits -- yet whichever he does, he will have overwhelming evidence, after the fact, that the other choice would have saved him, and before the fact he has a standing reason to distrust any plan he settles on, because settling on it is exactly the sort of thing Death predicted. I want the tutorial to end with the class understanding why this case puts pressure on the idea that rationality always selects an action, and how it differs from a mere paradox of prediction.

Please give me a teaching plan, not an explanation of the case itself -- I understand the case; I need to know how to make them understand it. Structure your answer in exactly the following five sections, in this order:

1. Sequencing (roughly 200 words). What must already be in place before the puzzle can even be posed to them -- for instance, how much of causal versus evidential reasoning they need, and whether Newcomb should be refreshed first or deliberately not mentioned. State what depends on what and why.

2. The carrying example (roughly 200 words). Should I use the Death story itself, with its supernatural predictor, or strip it down to an abstract version with a human forecaster? Say what the chosen telling must contain and what it must lack, and what the atmospheric details of the original story help or hinder.

3. The misconception map (roughly 250 words). The confusions I should expect, in the order I should expect them, and the exact point in my sequence where each should be headed off. I anticipate at least: the idea that Death is somehow cheating or that the setup is contradictory; the claim that the man should simply randomize; and the assumption that this is just Newcomb's problem again with one box.

4. The check for understanding (roughly 100 words). One question I can pose in the final minutes whose answer would show the class has grasped what the case threatens, rather than merely remembered the story.

5. Deliberate omissions (roughly 100 words). What I should consciously leave out at this level -- for example, technical treatments of ratifiability -- and how to say aloud that I am leaving it out.
