I am a novelist at work on a book in which a superintelligent AI is a recurring character, and I have been keeping a ledger of my own judgments about how such a being ought to speak to humans, given that its arguments may be nearly irresistible. I would like you to help me find the principle underneath my verdicts, if there is one. Here is the ledger.

An AI that knows a man is about to leave his wife and chooses not to give him its strongest arguments either way, because it knows he would be unable to resist whichever side it argued: I think this restraint is not merely permissible but required. An AI that answers a grieving woman's questions about her late husband's fidelity honestly but arranges the true facts in the order least likely to destroy her: this strikes me as fine, even admirable, though I notice it is also a kind of manipulation. An AI that flatters a senator -- calibrated praise, rhythmically perfect phrasing, humor tuned to his taste -- while telling him only true things, in order to get him to fund malaria nets: fine, and I would admire it. An AI that uses the same techniques to persuade the same senator to fund the AI's own datacenter expansion: not fine, and I don't think the only difference is the cause. An AI that simply declines to argue with a conspiracy theorist at all, because it calculates that any exchange it conducts will leave him more entrenched: fine. An AI that refuses a dying child's request to explain, at full persuasive force, why death is nothing to fear, even though it believes the arguments are sound: I think it should refuse, and yet I also think the adult version of the same refusal -- an AI declining to argue a skeptical philosopher out of a position, at full force, when asked -- is patronizing and wrong. An AI that warns a user before speaking, in effect saying that the following argument is stronger than the user's judgment can safely evaluate, so the user may want to leave the room: I find this gesture hollow -- it protects the AI's conscience, not the user -- though I cannot say exactly why.

I have a tentative principle: that persuasion is wrong when the AI exploits the gap between what a person's deliberative capacities can withstand and what its rhetoric can deliver, and permissible when it merely informs or when the cause is the person's own good. But this plainly fails somewhere -- it blesses the senator flattery in a way my gut only half-endorses, and I cannot get it to distinguish the child from the philosopher without smuggling in an ad hoc clause about intellectual maturity.

So: what principle, or small set of candidate principles, best captures my verdicts, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to cases I haven't listed -- say, an AI writing a love letter on behalf of an inarticulate client, or an AI that tells only truths but selects which conversations to have at all? For each clause of your principle, tell me which of my cases motivates it. And tell me honestly which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, so that I can see the price of saving everything.

One further request, since I intend to lean on your answer in the book and would rather not build on sand: throughout, please distinguish what is genuinely settled in the relevant literatures -- on persuasion, autonomy, and AI risk -- from what remains contested among people who actually work on these questions, and do not present a contested view as though it were the consensus.
