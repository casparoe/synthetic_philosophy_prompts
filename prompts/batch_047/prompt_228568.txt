My daughter and I have been arguing, half seriously, about the wasps she traps in jars and the ants she floods out of the patio: whether it matters, how much, and how we could possibly know. Around the same time, my book club landed on H. G. Wells's The Island of Doctor Moreau, and I was struck by how neatly the novel dramatizes the very continuum we keep stumbling over at home. I would like a serious critical reading of the novel through the lens of graded sentience and moral status, anchored in one well-known philosophical text: the famous footnote in Jeremy Bentham's An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (the chapter on the limits of the penal branch of jurisprudence), where Bentham argues that neither reason nor the power of speech should mark the boundary of moral concern, since a grown horse or dog surpasses a newborn infant in both, and proposes instead that the capacity to suffer is the only defensible criterion. Please paraphrase or describe the passage in your own words rather than quoting it.

Some questions to organize the reading, though you need not march through them mechanically. The Beast Folk are granted standing within Moreau's imposed order in rough proportion to how well they approximate human speech, posture, and self-restraint — precisely the criteria Bentham dismisses as beside the point. Does the novel thereby stage Bentham's boundary problem, and does it side with him in locating the moral fact in suffering (the laboratory scenes, the cries from Moreau's operating room) rather than in degrees of human-likeness? Consider the variation among the creatures themselves: some speak, some merely recite, some revert. If moral status tracks capacities and comes in degrees, does regression to animality revoke standing, and does the novel treat the reverted creatures as less, or does it suggest their suffering persists undiminished? Consider also Prendick: his revulsion at the island seems more aesthetic than ethical, and his later inability to stop seeing animality in London crowds extends the continuum in the other direction, onto ordinary humans. Does the novel end up vindicating Bentham's single-criterion view, a genuinely graded pluralism about status, or neither?

Please keep the reading faithful in both directions: don't flatten Wells into a Benthamite pamphlet, and don't stretch Bentham to fit the plot. Where the novel resists the reading — and I expect it does, somewhere around the role of disgust, or around whether suffering alone can account for what horrifies us about the vivisection — say so plainly. End with a paragraph on what the novel contributes to the philosophical debate about sentience as a matter of degree that argument alone does not.

After the reading, summarize your analysis as a table with the following columns: Creature or character; Capacities on display (reason, speech, self-command); Suffering the novel depicts; Treatment by others on the island; Moral standing the novel implicitly assigns; Bentham's verdict (whether the capacity-to-suffer criterion vindicates, widens, or unsettles that standing); Point of resistance (where, if anywhere, the text pushes back against the Benthamite reading for this case).
