I help run the little grants panel for our village community trust -- five volunteers, no professionals, and we meet twice a year to dish out about £40k between whoever applies. The applications are all over the shop: fixing the church hall roof, a bereavement counselling charity, new kit for the youth football team, rewilding a bit of meadow. We've been using a points system someone nicked from the council website, and honestly it feels like cheating. You end up pretending that "keeps 30 kids active" and "preserves a habitat" are the same kind of thing and can be scored out of ten, and whoever's best at writing forms wins.

I've been chewing on this in my spare time (no training in this stuff, just a bloke who reads too much at bedtime) and I've got a half-formed thought that some pairs of options might not be better, worse, or exactly equal -- they're just... on different scales, and that's a real fourth possibility, not me being indecisive. If that's right, it changes things: a tie between two genuinely equal things is different from two things you can't compare, and what you're allowed to do next should differ. I've also noticed our panel smuggles in rankings by arguing about "worthiness" until someone gives in, which seems worse than just admitting the comparison fails and having a fair way to break it.

So here's what I want: a proper step-by-step procedure we could actually run in one evening per meeting, that takes the incomparability idea seriously instead of papering over it. It should say: what questions to ask about each pair of applications before reaching for numbers; how to tell the difference between "roughly equal" and "can't be compared" (and what test you'd run to tell them apart, if there is one); what you're permitted to do in each case -- and specifically whether a coin flip between incomparable options is principled or a cop-out, because I proposed one last meeting and got laughed at; where a scoring system is fine and where it starts lying; and which bits can't be proceduralised at all and have to stay with human judgement, flagged honestly rather than hidden in step 4.

Constraints: five laypeople, about three hours per meeting, nobody can be made to read a book beforehand, and the decision has to be explainable to a grumpy applicant afterwards. Please work it through on two real-ish cases from our pile -- the roof versus the counselling charity, and the football kit versus the meadow -- and include at least one run-through where the procedure spits out an answer I won't like (say, the roof loses) and tell me why I should live with it anyway. Also tell me the main way this procedure will go wrong in practice, becuase I'd rather know now.
