I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm writing a piece on a dispute I witnessed at a workshop on automated fault detection. I want to get the substance right, so I'd like a careful adjudication of the exchange I recorded.

The background: a research group has built a system that monitors industrial equipment and flags "mechanistic anomalies" — cases where the causal process producing some behavior has changed, as opposed to cases where the same underlying mechanism is merely producing unusual outputs. A test case split the room. A pump assembly had a corroded valve replaced with a valve of a different design that performs the same regulating function, and the system flagged the post-replacement assembly as an anomaly.

(1) The first researcher, Dr. Halvorsen, defended the flag: a mechanism, she argued, is constituted by its actual components and their interactions; swap a component for one built on a different design and you literally have a different mechanism, so the detector did exactly what it should. (2) Her colleague Dr. Okafor replied that this individuates mechanisms far too finely. Components degrade and are swapped constantly; if every such change produced a new mechanism, anomaly detectors would flag everything and the concept would be useless. Mechanisms should be individuated by their causal organization and functional role, and since the new valve plays the same role in the same organization, the post-replacement assembly is the same mechanism running normally — a false positive. (3) Halvorsen answered that functional individuation is too coarse to do the work anomaly detection requires. Consider gradual replacement: swap one part, then another, then another, until nothing original remains. On Okafor's criterion there is never a change of mechanism at any step, yet the endpoint is plainly a different mechanism than the start — and a detector that can never flag any step will miss genuinely dangerous substitutions, like a counterfeit part that mimics the original's function under normal conditions but fails under stress. (4) Okafor said the gradual-replacement case just shows mechanism identity has vague boundaries, like baldness, and vague boundaries are no objection to a criterion. Moreover, he added, Halvorsen's worry about base rates and false positives is a point about what detectors need to be sensitive to, not a point about what a mechanism is; the practical demands of calibration cannot dictate metaphysics. (5) Halvorsen's final move was to deny that there's any metaphysics here to respect: "mechanism" is a scientist's term of art, not a natural kind, so if the detector's purposes call for fine-grained individuation, fine-grained individuation is correct, full stop.

I have my own leanings, but I'd rather not contaminate the assessment. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five moves, say whether it's clearly right, clearly mistaken, or defensible with qualifications, and justify the rating. Then say who is more right overall, and whether both parties are missing something — for instance, whether the dispute assumes a single correct answer where there might legitimately be several, or whether some shared premise deserves scrutiny.
