I'm TAing a grad seminar on practical rationality and the instructor has this ritual where in the final session everyone submits a short "credo" about intentions and the rest of the group gets to attack it. Here's mine, roughly as I plan to submit it. (1) In Kavka's toxin puzzle, no rational agent can win the prize. Once your intention has been registered, drinking the toxin would do nothing for you, and you can't rationally intend to do something you already know you'll have no reason to do when the time comes. (2) That said, I think deterrent intentions can be perfectly rational: it can be rational to form a genuine, settled intention to retaliate (say, launch the counter-strike) even while knowing that if the moment ever arrives, retaliating accomplishes nothing — the whole point is that the intention does its work beforehand. (3) I'm sympathetic to Bratman-style norms of intention stability: if you formed an intention rationally, reopening deliberation about it later without any new information is a rational failing, not just a harmless psychological habit. (4) Means-end coherence: intending an end rationally requires intending the means you believe necessary to it, and part of what it is to intend to X is believing, at least defeasibly, that you will in fact X. (5) Ought implies can, even for rational requirements: rationality can't demand of you what is psychologically impossible for you.

So: does this package hang together? Go through it and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only look like they are but dissolve once you're careful. I'm pretty sure there's at least one real problem in there but honestly I can't locate it myself. For each genuine tension, say how severe it is and what the cheapest repair would be (weaken one commitment, add a distinction, whatever), and order the tensions from most to least damaging rather than treating them all as equally bad. And if there's one single revision that would clean up the most trouble, tell me which it is — though note I have some attachment to all five, so "just drop two of them" is a last resort. One more thing: I'm not asking whether my individual views are correct, just whether they're jointly coherent given what motivates each of them. If two of them are each defensible on their own but rest on arguments that can't both be right, that's exactly what I want flagged.
