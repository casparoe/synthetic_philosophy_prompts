My cousin and I were listening to a philosophy podcast on a long drive last week, and afterwards she tried to explain this thing called Parfit's hitchhiker to me, because I'd never heard of it. Her summary went something like this: "Imagine you're stranded in the desert and you'll die without a rescue. A totally selfish driver shows up, and he'll only drive you out if you promise to pay him a hundred dollars once you're back in the city. But he's no idiot — he knows that once you're safely home, you've got nothing to gain from paying, so a rational person would just break the promise. Since your promise isn't credible, he drives off and leaves you. And that's the paradox: being perfectly rational gets you killed. Parfit's conclusion is that rationality is self-defeating. It's the same lesson as the prisoners dilemma, really — when everyone just follows their own rational self-interest, things go badly for everyone involved — so the rational move is to make yourself somewhat irrational, like taking a pill that makes you incapable of breaking promises."

Now, something about this rubs me the wrong way, but I can't put my finger on it. For one, I thought the prisoner's dilemma was about two people choosing at the same time without knowing what the other does, whereas this seems to be about whether you can bind your *future* self — but maybe that's a distinction without a difference? For another, "rationality is self-defeating" sounds way too dramatic as the takeaway — surely Parfit didn't think we should all just try to be less rational across the board. But I also haven't read Parfit myself, and the podcast people seemed like they knew what they were talking about, and my cousin is usually pretty careful.

So: is her summary fair? I'd like an accuracy audit I can actually use — which parts are basically right, which parts are subtly off (conflations, overstatements, technical terms used loosely), and in particular whether the big conclusion she draws — that rationality is self-defeating and the smart move is to become a bit irrational — actually follows from the case once you state it correctly. And if the prisoner's dilemma comparison is doing real work here or is just superficially similar, I'd like to know that too.
