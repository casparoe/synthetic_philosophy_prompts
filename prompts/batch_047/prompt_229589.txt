I am a PhD student in epistemology, and next month I am leading a seventy-five-minute seminar for advanced undergraduates on Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge and its consequences for epistemic closure. The students will have already encountered the tripartite analysis of knowledge and one or two Gettier cases, but nothing else in post-Gettier epistemology. I do not want an explanation of the theory for myself; I want a plan for how I should explain it to them.

A few constraints on the plan. First, the classic fake-barn case is off-limits as the central example, because half the class will have met it in an earlier course and will pattern-match rather than think. I want one bespoke carrying case, specified in the plan, that does the same work: it must be a situation where the subject's belief-forming method is reliable in the actual circumstances but would have delivered the same belief had the proposition been false, and it must be constructed so that sensitivity fails while adherence holds, without importing probabilistic noise that would muddy the counterfactuals. Second, I need the plan to make the denial of closure feel like a considered consequence of the theory rather than an embarrassing slip, since the students' first reaction will be that any theory forcing you to give up closure is thereby refuted. Third, the plan should reckon with the methods-individuation problem (that whether a belief tracks the truth depends on how finely we describe the method used to form it), but should advise me on how much of it to raise with this audience rather than simply solving it for me.

Please structure your response in the following sections, in this order:

1. Sequencing rationale (roughly 250 words): the order in which the components must be introduced — tripartite analysis, Gettier pressure, the counterfactual conditions of sensitivity and adherence, the definition of knowledge, and the closure denial — with a justification for each dependency, i.e., why each piece has to precede the next.
2. The carrying case (roughly 150 words): the example itself, stated compactly, plus a note on what it deliberately lacks and why those omissions matter pedagogically.
3. Misconception map (roughly 250 words): the misunderstandings I should expect — at minimum, that tracking is just reliabilism restated, that sensitivity amounts to a demand for infallibility, and that denying closure means licensing outright contradictions in what one knows — and the exact point in my sequence where each should be headed off, before it hardens.
4. The closure maneuver (roughly 150 words): how to stage the closure denial so it lands as principled, including whether to present it before or after the students have absorbed the sensitivity condition.
5. Deliberate omissions (roughly 100 words): which complications I should openly set aside at this level, and what one-sentence framing I should give students so they know material has been deferred rather than resolved.
6. Exit question (roughly 50 words): a single question I can pose at the end whose answer would reveal whether the theory has actually been understood, with a note on what a wrong answer would tell me.
