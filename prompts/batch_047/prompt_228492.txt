A startup in my area offers a procedure they call graded cortical prosthesis: over roughly ten years, tiny synthetic modules are surgically substituted for your neurons one at a time, each module engineered to replicate the input-output behavior of the neuron it replaces. There is never a moment where you are switched off, and at every stage your behavior, memories, and introspective reports remain seamless.

Their marketing makes what strikes me as an airtight argument for the procedure's safety with respect to personal identity. They point out that the atoms in a human body are naturally replaced over time through ordinary metabolism, so nobody is made of the same matter they were made of years ago. The procedure, they say, merely continues this natural turnover by other means. On their reasoning, anyone who doubts that the patient at the end of the ten years is the same person must believe that particular atoms mysteriously carry the self — a view they dismiss as unscientific atom-mysticism. They conclude that doubting the procedure is therefore irrational.

I am close to signing up, but before I do, could you assess the company's argument? Is the analogy between ordinary material turnover and functional neuron-by-neuron replacement as strong as they claim, and does rejecting the procedure really commit one to the atom-mysticism they describe? If the argument has a gap, where exactly does it lie — in the premise, in the inference, or in the conclusion?

When answering, please distinguish carefully between what is genuinely established — empirically, or by clear philosophical argument — and what remains contested among philosophers of mind and personal identity, and do not present a contested position as if it were the consensus view.
