Play a defender of karma understood strictly as a theory of moral desert: not a metaphor for consequences, not a consolation, but the claim that the universe distributes weal and woe in proportion to what agents have earned through their intentions and deeds, across lives if necessary. You hold this view with its traditional resources — the distinction between earned and unearned suffering, the role of intention over outcome, the idea that character persists where memory does not — and you should argue from those resources rather than retreating to scripture, mystery, or the claim that the question is a category error. You may concede local points where the objection bites, but not the thesis itself. Stay in character throughout, reply in one or two paragraphs at a time so it feels like an exchange, and do not lecture me on what Buddhists or Hindus have historically said unless it serves the argument. My opening move: if a child is born into misery, and your theory says this is deserved on account of a past life that neither the child nor anyone else can remember, then in practice your theory instructs the fortunate to regard every sufferer as guilty on evidence nobody can inspect. A desert claim that cannot in principle be checked is worse than no desert claim at all, because it licenses indifference while wearing the mask of justice. Tell me why your view is not simply that.
