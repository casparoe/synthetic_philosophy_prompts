Last night a friend and I argued for hours about Hofstadter's Typogenetics — the toy formal system in Gödel, Escher, Bach whose DNA-like strands function simultaneously as data and as instructions for the enzymes that operate on them, designed to make the self-replication puzzle behind Gödelian self-reference concrete. My friend's claim was that the exercise demonstrates something deep: that genuine self-replication essentially requires an interpreting machinery distinct from the code it interprets, so any account of replication (biological, computational, or logical) that lacks this code/interpreter duality misses the phenomenon. I suspect the argument is circular in a subtle way — Typogenetics was constructed so that strands are inert without enzymes, so it can hardly establish that replication must work that way — though I concede the analogy may still do real conceptual work. Suppose a bright graduate student has one year to spend on the conceptual foundations of self-replication: what the code/interpreter duality actually amounts to, how it relates to the data/program ambiguity in computation, and whether Gödelian self-reference and biological self-replication are one phenomenon or merely analogous ones. Which open questions should she pursue, and in what order? I'm less interested in the list itself than in the reasoning: for each question, explain why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is for someone working formally rather than empirically, and what result over the next few years would move it up or down the ranking.
