**Closed Loops: Can Human Kinds Settle?**

Ian Hacking's work on the "looping effect of human kinds" rests on a contrast that has become standard. Classifications of things are indifferent: a quark does not change its behavior upon being called a quark. Classifications of people are interactive: those sorted under a label may come to know it, and may conform to it, resist it, or reorganize their self-understanding around it. The classification thereby changes what it classifies; the changed population eventually fits the classification less well; the classification is revised; and the loop turns again. Hacking's study of the rise and decline of multiple personality is the canonical illustration: the diagnosis helped produce the phenomenon it described, and the transformed phenomenon helped undo the diagnosis.

The picture this suggests is of human kinds as permanently moving targets. I want to question that suggestion. Some loops, I will argue, appear to close. A loop is closed when the feedback between classification and classified settles into equilibrium: the kind becomes stable and predictable, but its stability is an artifact of the loop itself rather than a structure the classification merely discovered.

Three mechanisms could close a loop. First, saturation: once nearly everyone who fits a classification has been brought under it, and institutions -- clinics, insurance categories, support groups, self-help literatures -- route behavior into the expected pattern, the population stops drifting, and prediction becomes reliable. Second, semantic capture: the label becomes the standard vocabulary for the experiences it organizes, so experiences that do not fit become difficult to articulate, hence rarely reported, hence invisible to those who would revise the classification. Third, reflexive professionalism: classifiers trained on the stabilized population encounter mostly confirming cases, and anomalies are absorbed as comorbidities or subtypes rather than registered as counterevidence. The loop's outputs are, so to speak, catalogued as its inputs.

Closed loops pose a problem that I do not think has been adequately appreciated. The standard criteria for a good psychiatric classification -- inter-rater reliability, predictive validity, treatment response -- cannot distinguish a closed loop from a genuinely indifferent kind, because a closed loop will pass all of them. Our criteria therefore risk certifying manufactured stability as discovered structure.

Can we do better? Consider a counterfactual test: ask whether the kind would persist if the classification's institutional supports were withdrawn. If it would, the stability is not loop-manufactured; if it would dissolve, we have a closed loop. The test has obvious difficulties. We rarely get to run it. History supplies imperfect natural experiments -- diagnoses removed from the manuals, such as homosexuality, where the underlying behaviors did not vanish but the kind as a clinical object largely did -- but such cases are confounded by simultaneous changes in law and social attitude. More troublingly, the test may be uninformative precisely where closure is semantic: withdrawing the label does not restore the vocabulary people had before it, so the kind may survive the experiment for reasons that still trace back to the loop.

Where does this leave psychiatric classification? Two morals compete. If manufactured stability is a defect, classifications should be designed to keep loops open -- with sunset clauses on diagnostic categories, or periodic re-derivation of categories from data gathered outside the classificatory system. But if what psychiatry needs is stability by any honest means, then looping is not a defect to be engineered against but the very mechanism by which classifications succeed. On the second view, a closed loop is a classification that has finished its work.

**My requests for comments:**

1. Accuracy and fairness: Is my sketch of Hacking's looping effect, and of the standard interactive/indifferent contrast, correct as far as it goes? Have I attributed to the literature an assumption (that human kinds are perpetually unstable) that is not actually as prevalent as I imply?
2. Are the three closure mechanisms genuinely distinct, or do two of them collapse into the third? In particular, is semantic capture just saturation operating on vocabulary rather than behavior?
3. Does the counterfactual test survive my own objections to it, or should it be abandoned? If you think a better test for distinguishing closed loops from stable kinds is available, sketch it.
4. Is the closing dilemma real? That is, is there a coherent position on which psychiatric classification aims at manufactured stability, and would that position have consequences a psychiatrist could actually accept?
5. Which paragraph is weakest as a piece of writing, and what specific revision would you recommend?

Please organize your comments by these five questions, and note any additional problems I have not asked about.
