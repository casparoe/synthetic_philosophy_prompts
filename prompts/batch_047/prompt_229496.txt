Historians of the First World War routinely distinguish the trigger of the war — the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo on 28 June 1914 — from its underlying causes: the rigid alliance system binding Germany to Austria-Hungary and Russia to France and (loosely) Britain, the Anglo-German naval race, imperial rivalries, and the mobilization timetables, especially the Schlieffen Plan, which pressured general staffs to mobilize early lest they lose the race to deploy. In everyday historiography this is often expressed by saying that war was "bound to happen" sooner or later, with Sarajevo merely the spark.

Philosophers of causation have a notion that looks tailor-made for this talk: causal overdetermination, in which an effect has multiple independently sufficient causes, so that no single cause is necessary — subtract any one and the effect still occurs. Please analyze the outbreak of the First World War in August 1914 as a candidate case of causal overdetermination, in both directions.

Specifically: (1) State precisely what the overdetermination hypothesis commits one to, in counterfactual terms, and distinguish it from neighboring structures it is easily confused with — a conjunctive causal web in which each factor is individually necessary and only jointly sufficient, preemptive or "backup" causation, and something like Mackie's INUS conditions. (2) Test the historical material against these structures. Is it plausible that any single structural factor was individually sufficient for war? Does the record of the July Crisis — Austria-Hungary's ultimatum, Germany's blank cheque, Russia's decision to mobilize, the failure of last-minute mediation — instead suggest a highly contingent conjunctive structure, in which case the "war was inevitable anyway" rhetoric mimics overdetermination while describing something quite different? (3) Consider whether the individuation of the effect matters: overdetermination may be more plausible for "a major European war within a decade" than for "this war, starting in August 1914, with this alignment." (4) Say what the episode shows about the theory itself — for instance, whether "sufficient cause" is even well-defined where history cannot be rerun, and whether overdetermination talk is doing explanatory work or merely encoding a judgment about robustness.

Conclude with a last line beginning "Verdict:" followed by a single label — overdetermined, preempted, conjunctively caused, or indeterminate — and one sentence justifying it.
