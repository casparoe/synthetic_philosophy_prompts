Write a dialogue between two philosophers arguing about what makes pain bad. One defends the hedonic view: pain is bad simply because of what it feels like, full stop, and the aversion is downstream. The other defends the view that the badness of pain requires (or at least heavily depends on) the subject disliking or being averse to the sensation, so the raw feel alone isn't sufficient.

The whole thing should revolve around pain asymbolia: patients who, after certain brain lesions, report that they still feel pain -- they can identify it as pain, locate it, rate its intensity -- but insist it doesn't bother them anymore and they don't flinch away from it. Both characters should treat this as a live test case for their view and have to do actual work to accommodate it, rather than just dismissing the patients as confused. So the hedonic theorist should have to explain what's still bad about an unmotivating pain (or bite a bullet), and the aversion theorist should have to explain why we still instinctively think something has gone wrong for these patients, and why asymbolia isn't just... a cure.

Both characters should be equally competent -- no strawmen, and they should actually respond to each other's specific points instead of giving canned speeches. Feel free to give them real positions on things like whether asymbolia patients are genuinely better off, whether morphine's effect of "the pain is there but I don't mind" is the same phenomenon or different, etc.

Since this is a dialogue there's no verdict to give, but at the end have one character (or a brief authorial note) flag where the dialogue leans on a contested reading of the asymbolia literature -- i.e., separate what's fairly settled about the phenomenon from what's genuinely disputed among people who work on this, and don't present a contested interpretation as the consensus. Also, keep it in American English.
