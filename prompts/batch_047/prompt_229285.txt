I edit the science desk of a mid-sized general-interest magazine, and roughly once a week someone pitches us an evolutionary-psychology story: why we evolved to love gossip, why women are supposedly better at remembering where the fruit is, why teenagers take risks, that sort of thing. Some of it is serious science and some of it is a pub anecdote wearing a lab coat, and at the pitch meeting I have about ten minutes per pitch and no statistics training to lean on.

What I'd like is a decision procedure I can actually run at the meeting, grounded in whatever the philosophy of biology and the just-so-stories debate have produced that is genuinely usable by a non-specialist. By that I mean things like: the distinction between a trait being an adaptation, a byproduct of some other adaptation (what Gould and Lewontin called a spandrel), or just drift; whether the proposed explanation makes any specific risky prediction or just redescribes the observation in the language of the Pleistocene; what the comparative and cross-cultural evidence would have to look like; and what would count as evidence against the claim, given that 'it helped our ancestors on the savanna' seems to fit absolutely anything.

Concretely, give me:

1. The questions to ask, in order, with what counts as a passing answer to each and which answers should kill the pitch on the spot.
2. A way to handle the very common case where the underlying finding is real and replicated but the evolutionary gloss is doing all the work -- is that a green light, a kill, or a 'run the finding, cut the gloss'?
3. The points where the procedure has to hand the call back to me because it's a judgement about what our readers need, not a question the method can settle.

Then run the procedure on three invented pitches so I can see it working:

a) A researcher claims that women's interest in true-crime content is an evolved predator- and threat-monitoring adaptation, on the basis of a survey of podcast listeners.
b) A well-powered cross-cultural study (thirty-odd countries, decent methods) finds a stable sex difference in occupational preferences, and the pitch says this shows the difference is evolved and therefore fixed.
c) A journalist wants to argue that open-plan offices fail because our hunter-gatherer brains can't cope with them.

Please include at least one verdict I won't enjoy (I've got a soft spot for a good evolutionary yarn, and the procedure shouldn't care). And be honest at the end about what a checklist like this can't catch -- where a plausible-sounding story gets through anyway.
