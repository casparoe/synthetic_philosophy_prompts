I have come to accept Amartya Sen's entitlement approach to famine: on this view, famines are characteristically political and economic failures rather than natural disasters, occurring not because a society lacks food in aggregate but because particular groups lose their entitlements to food — their ability to command it through what they grow, earn, trade, or receive from the state. Sen's canonical cases are meant to bear this out: Bengal in 1943, where food was available in the region while rural laborers' purchasing power collapsed; Ireland in the 1840s, where food continued to be exported while the blight destroyed the subsistence crop of the poor; and China during 1959–61, where an authoritarian regime faced no electoral or press pressure to reverse catastrophic procurement policies. The position also carries a sharper corollary, namely that substantial famines do not occur in functioning democracies with a free press. What would count against this view? I want the defeaters mapped honestly: revised demographic or food-availability estimates for these three cases, apparent counterexamples of famine under democratic or partially democratic institutions, conceptual worries about whether the entitlement frame explains anything a food-shortage story cannot, and an assessment of whether the democracy corollary has been hedged — by qualifications about what counts as functioning, free, or substantial — into something that risks nothing. Present the response as a numbered list of exactly five items, ordered from most to least damaging to the position, each item a sentence or two identifying a potential defeater and how decisive it would be, with no introduction or conclusion.
