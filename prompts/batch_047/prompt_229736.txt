Okay so I'm a novelist, lit-fic midlist type, and I'm stuck on the central character of my next book and I want to outsource the philosophy part to you. Here's the character: a woman in her fifties who every three or four years detonates her life. Quits the career, ends the marriage, moves cities, picks up a new name or a new politics, and tells everyone -- and she means it, she's not performing -- that she's finally free, that the past is just a story she used to tell herself and she's under no obligation to keep telling it. My hunch is that she's in Sartrean bad faith, but at the pole that never gets the famous examples: not the waiter who's over-identified with his role and is fleeing his freedom into thinghood, but the reverse move, fleeing her facticity into pure transcendence, treating her past, her body, her promises as weightless.

Build me the scene. Specs:

- One scene, contemporary and realistic, and the two canonical setups are banned: no café, no waiters, no first date.
- The bad faith has to come through entirely in behavior and dialogue. I can't just narrate "she was in bad faith," and ideally a reader who's never heard of Sartre should still feel that something is off about her without being able to name it.
- She must be sincere. No con artist, no hypocrite, nobody lying to anyone -- and that's the hard part, because the scene should somehow make palpable the weird structure of bad faith, where there's no liar and no dupe and yet the whole thing is crooked.
- Her escape should fail not because the world blocks her (no villain, no debt collector at the door) but because her own past choices keep showing up inside what she does, facticity reasserting itself without her noticing. She's carrying the suitcase she claims never to have packed, that kind of thing.
- Cruellest constraint: the things she says should sound like textbook Sartrean authenticity -- owning her freedom, refusing to be defined by what's given. Her bad faith has to be invisible to the standard "just own your choices" advice. If a life-coach could endorse everything she says out loud, you've got it right.

After the scene, walk me through it: why this counts as bad faith on Sartre's actual terms rather than healthy reinvention or serial change of mind; which specific beats are carrying the philosophical weight; and how it differs structurally from the waiter case rather than just inverting it. Then the attack: someone tells me this isn't bad faith at all, it's just a person who keeps changing her mind, and Sartre has no principled line between the two -- answer that objection, and tell me honestly whether the case survives it. Also, what happens if I let her, just once in the whole novel, notice the pattern out loud? Does the whole structure collapse, or does it get worse?

No need for citations or quotes from Being and Nothingness, I'll dig those up myself -- I'd rather you paraphrase than misremember a line.
