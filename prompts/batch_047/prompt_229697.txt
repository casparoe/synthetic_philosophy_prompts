I host a long-form podcast on political theory, and my next guest is the author of a new book defending what he calls performance legitimacy: the thesis that a regime which durably delivers prosperity, public safety, and competent administration thereby acquires genuine legitimacy — not mere acquiescence or stability — even while denying its citizens competitive elections, a free press, and most civil liberties. Singapore and post-reform China are his exhibit cases, and he treats demands for liberal-democratic procedures as a parochial export rather than a universal standard.

Draft a sequence of eight to ten interview questions for him. I do not want questions that invite him to restate the thesis or rehearse his case studies; each question should turn on something his view is actually committed to and press where a careful critic would press. For every question, include a follow-up keyed to the reply he is most likely to give, so the exchange does not stall on his first deflection. At minimum, I want the sequence to cover:

1. The epistemic circularity problem: the surveys and quiescence he cites as evidence of legitimacy are produced under conditions the regime itself controls, so the claim risks resting on manufactured or falsified preferences — and his likely retreat to "hard outcomes" (growth, homicide rates) needs pressing on whether output levels, as opposed to normative standing, can ground legitimacy at all.
2. The counterfactual problem: performance claims require a baseline, and he owes us an account of how the relevant comparison — what a liberal alternative in the same country would plausibly have delivered — could ever be established rather than asserted.
3. The endogeneity problem: legitimacy is said to produce compliance, compliance produces governability, governability produces performance, and performance produces legitimacy — so the question is whether his central concept explains stability or merely renames it.
4. The fragility consequence: legitimacy pegged to performance is hostage to the next recession; he should have to say whether this is a feature (a form of accountability) or a confession that the view justifies a regime only until its luck runs out.
5. The moral floor: whether there is any level of repression no growth rate can offset. If yes, the view quietly imports the liberal commitments it claims to supersede; if no, he should be made to defend that explicitly.

End with one question about what development in his own model cases — what kind of downturn, succession crisis, or revealed preference — would make him abandon the thesis, and whether a view that risks nothing is a theory of legitimacy at all or simply a compliment paid to winners.
