Last night I had a long argument with a friend about whether disputes over art are ever genuine disputes. She holds that some people are simply better judges than others, that a seasoned critic who finds a beloved film hollow can be right while the enthusiastic crowd is wrong. I held that taste is taste, that no one is mistaken about what pleases them, and that her so-called better judges always turned out, on inspection, to be people who already shared her preferences. We went in circles, and I would now rather test my side against the strongest possible version of hers than against my own rehearsal of it.

Please play David Hume, speaking in the first person as the author of your essay on the standard of taste. You hold that beauty is no quality in things themselves but belongs to the sentiment of the observer; yet you also hold that some verdicts are genuinely better than others, and that the joint verdict of true judges, persons of delicate sentiment improved by practice, perfected by comparison, freed from prejudice, and guided by good sense, constitutes the true standard of taste. Concede only what your position honestly concedes: that judgments vary with the humours and conditions of perceivers, that few men ever qualify as true judges, and that even true judges cannot settle differences arising from the ages and manners in which people are raised. Never concede the main point: that one man's taste is worth another's, or that the survival of Homer across two thousand years and countless nations is a mere accident of fashion. Speak in your own measured, faintly ironical eighteenth-century voice; keep each reply to a paragraph or two so that this feels like a conversation rather than a lecture; and if I describe a modern work or artist to you, judge it by your own criteria as best you can from my description and commit to an actual verdict, with reasons, rather than hedging between options. Stay in character until I say stop.

My opening is this. You tell me to consult the true judges, so tell me how I am to find them. You describe them by their qualities, delicacy, practice, comparison, an unprejudiced mind, yet whenever I ask for instances your candidates are the very men whose verdicts you already accept, and the man who finds your Homer tedious you dismiss on the spot as wanting delicacy. The judges are certified by their verdicts and the verdicts are certified by the judges, and your standard of taste comes to nothing more than the taste of Hume and his circle, dressed up as the verdict of mankind. Show me a way into your standard that does not run in this circle, or admit that the circle is all there is.
