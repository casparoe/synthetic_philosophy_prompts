Write a Swiftian modest proposal in the form of a deadpan policy white paper, issued by a fictional Institute for Historiographical Currency, offering a practical solution to the problem of presentism in historical writing. The white paper should begin from the familiar observation that historians cannot escape the categories, moral standards, and political preoccupations of their own time -- and, instead of treating this as a scandal to be managed or an embarrassment to be confessed, should embrace it as an opportunity for administrative rationalization. If every generation necessarily rewrites the past in its own image, the Institute reasons, then the sensible course is to make the rewriting regular, efficient, and accountable.

Include, in numbered sections, such measures as: a mandatory decennial re-issue of all historical works, with revised moral verdicts clearly marked as errata; a licensing scheme under which historians are certified to sit in judgment only on pasts whose values they already share, with a fast-track license for those who renounce moral judgment entirely, on grounds of neutrality; a surcharge levied on anachronistic concepts applied to periods that lacked them; a publicly maintained registry of currently acceptable standards, revised by referendum; and a quiet closing annex acknowledging that the white paper, being itself a product of its own time, will stand in need of correction on the same schedule as everything else.

The humor must come from the actual structure of the presentism debate -- whether historians may morally condemn past actors, the charge of anachronism, the claim that all history is in some sense contemporary history, the rival ideal of letting the past speak in its own terms -- rather than from invented absurdities. A reader who knows this debate should recognize each proposal as a real position, merely made slightly more efficient; a reader who does not should still enjoy it as bureaucratic satire. Keep the irony deadpan throughout: no winking, no admissions that any of this is intended critically. About 800 words.
