Ok so I need a tiebreaker because I just spent three hours at the pub arguing with my friend Marco about Amartya Sen and we got absolutely nowhere. We're both in this political economy seminar and last week we covered Sen's 1970 paper The Impossibility of a Paretian Liberal, where he proves you can't have unrestricted domain, the Pareto principle, and even a super weak liberalism condition (basically that at least two people each get to decisively settle one purely personal pair of options, like whether they sleep on their back or their belly) all at the same time. Marco walked away saying the theorem shows liberal rights and efficiency are basicly on a collision course and one of them has to give. I think he's overreacting and missing the obvious fix.

Here's my arguement. The paradox only gets going because people have nosy preferences — in Sen's own example with the single copy of Lady Chatterley's Lover, Prude cares intensely about whether Lewd reads the book in the privacy of his own home, which is frankly none of Prude's business. But Sen himself is famous for trashing the idea that every preference is just self-interested maximization. In Rational Fools: A Critique of the Behavioral Foundations of Economic Theory he writes: "Where is the railway station?" he asks me. "There," I say, pointing at the post office, "and would you please post this letter for me on the way?" "Yes," he says, determined to open the envelope and check whether it contains something valuable. So if Sen thinks the self-interest picture of what people want is absurd, why can't we just launder the meddlesome preferences out — apply the Pareto principle only to what people want for their own lives — and watch the paradox dissolve? Marco says this "solves the theorem by redefining it," whatever that's supposed to mean, but it seems to me Sen hands us the escape hatch himself and wierdly nobody in the literature talks about it.

So which of us is confused, me or Marco? Keep it to 250 words max, and don't split the difference — I want an actual verdict with the reasoning, not "both of you have a point."
