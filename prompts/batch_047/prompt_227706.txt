I'm a novelist, mid-draft on a campus novel, and I've written myself into a corner. There's a scene where my university provost cancels a controversial speaker — a polemicist whose whole schtick is that a particular minority group is a danger to the country — and defends it to my protagonist with a little shrug and the line "you can't shout fire in a crowded theater." The protagonist is supposed to push back next chapter, and I want the exchange to be smarter than the usual shouting match, but while writing it I realized I honestly don't know what that sentence is even claiming. It's doing a lot of work for something that vague.

So give me a menu, not a lecture: what are the distinct precise claims that slogan could be making? Some flavors I half-sense floating around in it: some speech is regulable at all, so the absolutist loses; speech that produces an involuntary crowd panic is more like a physical act than like expressing an idea; false statements that cause harm get less protection than true ones; the danger has to be immediate and the mechanism short-circuiting, like a stampede, before the analogy kicks in; or just "no right is absolute," full stop. For each reading, tell me whether it's a platitude, substantive-but-defensible, or actually false — and what features the provost's case would need to have for the fire analogy to go through on that reading.

The crux for my scene: whatever harms my speaker causes, if any, run through audience members getting gradually radicalized over months or years, not a room stampeding in ninety seconds. Which readings does that kill outright, and which survive it? Tell me which version my provost is most likely actually running, so my protagonist's comeback hits the argument he needs and not a strawman. And definately flag which reading makes his line strongest and which makes it weakest — I haven't decided yet how sympathetic I want him to be, and that choice basically picks which version comes out of his mouth.
