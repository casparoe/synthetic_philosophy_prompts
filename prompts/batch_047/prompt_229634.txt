I TA for an undergrad political philosophy course, and for the session on dirty hands in politics the prof has banned me from lecturing -- she wants the ~20 students (75 minutes, one session) to run into the problem themselves before anyone names it. Design me a runnable activity. My rough idea: split them into small groups running a fictional city government through a crisis (a credible terrorist threat, or an epidemic, you pick whichever makes for better decisions), where they face a sequence of escalating choices. Each individual choice should be one a reasonable person could defend -- lying to the public to prevent a panic, letting an innocent low-level official take the fall, cutting a deal with someone awful -- but I want the design arranged so the defensibility decays slowly enough that they don't notice how far they've slid. Crucially, have each group keep a written ledger of everythign they authorized, without telling them why, so the reveal at the end is them reading their own record back. I'd also like a second phase where groups swap: each group becomes a tribunal reviewing another group's ledger and has to issue a verdict, so they feel the weird asymmetry between judging from the outside and choosing from the inside -- ideally at least one group condemns another group for a decision they themselves made earlier in the run.

A few requirements. First, the target insight I want them to arrive at experientially is not "should you do the bad thing" but the stranger claim that an action can be the right thing to do and still leave something behind -- that even students who stand by every decision should find their own tally sheet ugly to read, and the debrief should dig into what that leftover feeling is and whether a good politician ought to feel it or has a duty to learn not to. Second, if you can fit it in the time, I'd love a mechanic that contrasts one decision made by a single named "mayor" with the identical decision made by anonymous committee vote, because I suspect the diffusion changes how dirty anyone feels while changing nothing about the dilemma itself, and that gap is interesting. Third, the debrief section should map specific moments of the activity onto the conceptual structure (how this differs from an ordinary tragic tradeoff, what the "moral remainder" is supposed to be, where a pure utilitarian would deny there's any problem at all and what to say to them).

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- students gaming the scenario, contrarians who relish authorizing everything, groups that refuse to take the fiction seriously, that kind of thing -- and how to handle each. And be honest about the one thing a classroom game obviously can't reproduce (real stakes, real guilt, real consequences) and what I as facilitator need to supply to compensate for that. No upfront explanation of the concept; they should only hear the phrase "dirty hands" after the debrief has already happend.
