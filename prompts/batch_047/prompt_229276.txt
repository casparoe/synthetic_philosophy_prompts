I help run a university colloquium series in political philosophy, and next month's guest is a philosopher known for defending premise-based majority voting as the right way for committees, courts, and boards to reach collective judgments -- even after conceding that it can deliver inconsistent collective verdicts and that it is vulnerable to manipulation. I would like eight to ten interview questions that do real work rather than letting her restate the view. Specifically, I want:

- One question that makes her reconstruct the discursive dilemma in her own terms -- say, a tenure committee voting separately on whether a candidate meets the research bar and the teaching bar, and then on tenure itself -- and that presses her on why she treats the collective inconsistency as a feature to manage rather than a reductio of aggregating judgments at all.
- One question on the epistemic justification she typically offers (that majority votes on premises track the truth better than majority votes on conclusions), with a follow-up keyed to the worry that this justification depends on assumptions -- voter independence, better-than-chance competence -- that real committees rarely satisfy.
- One question on agenda-dependence and strategic voting: since the premise-based outcome can change if the chair reorders or repackages the premises, ask how she defends the procedure against the charge that it hands outsized power to whoever frames the agenda, with a follow-up anticipating her likely reply that framing power is a problem for every deliberative institution.
- One question contrasting her view with the conclusion-based alternative, asking what she thinks is lost when a multi-member court issues a bottom-line verdict without collectively endorsing any rationale, and a follow-up on whether that loss is a genuine cost or just an aesthetic preference for reason-giving.
- One question on whether her defense of premise-based voting survives the impossibility results in the judgment aggregation literature -- the fact that no aggregation rule satisfies all the attractive fairness and rationality conditions at once -- with a follow-up asking which condition she is prepared to give up and why.
- One question about a hard case: a hiring committee whose members sincerely disagree about how the criteria weigh against each other, such that premise-by-premise voting produces a collective judgment no individual member actually holds, and whether the collective can then act on a view that belongs to no one.

The remaining questions are up to you, but keep them in the weeds: no biographical or career questions, and each should come with a follow-up calibrated to the answer she is most likely to give.
