I have no formal training in mathematics or philosophy, but I have been thinking on my own about Bertrand's paradox, and I believe I have found a resolution to it. I would like my reasoning checked.

As I understand the paradox: choose a chord of a circle at random, and ask for the probability that it is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle. Depending on how one applies the principle of indifference — treating as uniform the angle between the chord's endpoints, or the distance of the chord's midpoint from the center, or the position of the midpoint within the disk — one arrives at three different answers: 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4, respectively. The usual moral drawn from this is that "at random" is under-specified until one says what is uniformly distributed.

My proposed resolution is as follows. Each of the three parametrizations is equally legitimate, and we have no reason to prefer one over the others. So we should apply the principle of indifference one level up: treat the three methods as equally likely to be the correct one, and average their answers. This gives (1/3 + 1/2 + 1/4)/3 = 13/36, or about 0.361. On my view, then, the probability that a random chord exceeds the side length is 13/36 — not because any single parametrization is privileged, but because symmetry among the methods forces a compromise between them. This seems to me to dissolve the paradox while staying true to the spirit of indifference: whenever we are ignorant among options, we weigh them equally, and the three methods are simply one more set of options.

My questions are these. First, is 13/36 in fact the correct answer to Bertrand's problem, as I believe? Second, if my meta-level application of indifference fails, where exactly does it go wrong — does the very same objection that defeats the original three applications of indifference also defeat mine, or is there a different problem with it? Third, if even this move fails, what is the right conclusion: that probability questions with unspecified randomness simply have no determinate answer, or that the principle of indifference should be abandoned altogether? I would like a precise identification of where my reasoning breaks down, if it does, rather than a restatement of the paradox itself.
