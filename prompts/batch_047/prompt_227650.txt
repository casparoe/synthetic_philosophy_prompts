So I run a philosophy-adjacent podcast (we do a lot of big-history and econ-anthro episodes) and next week I'm interviewing an anthropologist who's just published a book defending the strongest version of the thesis that agriculture was a catastrophic wrong turn for humanity: foragers were broadly affluent, farming made nearly everyone worse off for millenia, and inequality is basically downstream of stored surplus. She's fluent in the Sahlins-and-Diamond lineage and has clearly fielded every standard objection a hundred times, so I need questions that don't let her just rerun her greatest hits.

Write me 8-10 interview questions, ordered so they gradually close in on her. For each one, give me the follow-up keyed to the answer she'll most likely give. The pressure points I want covered:

- The affluence evidence: when she leans on low forager work-hours, I want a question pressing on how the accounting handles food processing, tool upkeep, firewood, and childcare -- the classic complaint is that the famous low-hour estimates clock time at camp but not the work that happens back at home. Follow-up should anticipate her replying that even fully loaded hours still come in below farm labor.
- The history/counterfactual problem: agriculture arose independently in a bunch of places, and as far as we know no population that adopted it ever voluntarily went back to foraging full-time. Her likely move is the ratchet line (population grows, return becomes impossible, so nobody *could* go back). I want a follow-up that presses what "mistake" even means here -- a mistake for whom, judged by what standard, from which vantage point, if the trap was invisible at the moment of decision.
- Inequality: Pacific Northwest foragers had rank, slaves, and hoarded surplus without farming, while plenty of early farming villages look pretty egalitarian for a long stretch. So is her real target agriculture itself, or storable surplus plus the state? If she concedes it's the latter, the follow-up is that her book seems to blame the wrong culprit.
- Health: stature decline, dental caries, zoonoses are her best cards, so make her weigh them against forager infant mortality and the violence-rate literature that makes pre-state life look considerably less rosy than her picture. The follow-up should push on whether she's cherry-picking quality-of-life metrics over lifespan, or has a principled way to trade them off.
- One question on her normative standard: is the claim about average welfare, and what does she say to the point that agriculture enabled orders of magnitude more lives to exist at all? Not a gotcha -- I genuinely want to hear whether she bites the bullet that the average is all that matters, or has some other criterion in reserve.

No warm-up fluff, nothing she can deflect with "that's chapter 4," and please don't have me ask her to just summarize the book -- my listeners will have read the blurb.
