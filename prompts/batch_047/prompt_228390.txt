I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I keep ending up at philosophy conferences where the same fight breaks out, basically unchanged since the early 90s: the fight over Richard Rorty. On one side you've got people who say his antirepresentationalism -- the idea that we should drop the picture of language as mirroring the world and treat vocabularies as tools for coping instead -- collapses into a view where the only thing you can say about why one belief beats another is that your peers happen to accept it. On the other side, Rortians who say the critics keep smuggling back the very notion of corresponding to reality that Rorty spent whole books arguing doesn't do any explanitory work, and that he answered the relativism charge a thousand times: he never denied there's a mind-independent world, he denied that the world tells us which vocabulary to describe it in.

What bugs me, as someone who's now watched this loop three times in person: both sides are smart, both have read everything, and the exchange always has the same shape. Critic says but then you can't claim science gets closer to the truth. Rortian says I can say everything you say, just re-described in my vocabulary. Critic says that's exactly the problem. Repeat forever.

So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? A hidden dispute about what a philosophical theory of truth is even supposed to do? A verbal fight over the word true that both sides would abandon if they tabooed it? Or bedrock intuitions about whether the question how do we know we're getting it right deserves a philosophical answer rather than a sociological one, where neither side can argue for its own starting point? Diagnose it using the actual moves each side makes -- not a survey of pragmatism -- and tell me what would count as progress: a distinction nobody's drawn yet, or an argument that the honest move is to just stop having this debate.
