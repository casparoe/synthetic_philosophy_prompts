I am writing a comparative policy paper and keep running into a pattern I cannot make philosophical sense of. In most of continental Europe — France, Germany, Italy, Spain — surrogacy is prohibited outright, in some places criminally, typically on the stated ground that neither the child nor the woman's body may be made an object of commerce. The UK and the Netherlands permit surrogacy but only if it is unpaid beyond expenses. Several US states, by contrast, enforce fully commercial surrogacy contracts and treat the intended parents as the legal parents from birth, while other US states void such contracts as contrary to public policy. Meanwhile the US has a largely legal market in egg donation in which compensation openly varies with the donor's appearance, education, and test scores, whereas European countries that allow egg donation cap payment at or near reimbursement of expenses and lost earnings. And in every one of these jurisdictions the official line is the same: payment compensates the donor or surrogate for her time, effort, and risk — it is never payment for the egg, let alone for a child, since selling a child is everywhere a crime.

What I want from you is the implicit philosophy. What view of the body, of labor, and of parenthood does each regime presuppose — for instance, what must be true about a person and her parts for gestation to be inalienable while blood plasma and dangerous wage labor are not, or for a child's genetic origins to be purchasable while the child is not? Which of the distinctions the law leans on — thing sold versus service compensated, gift versus commodity, parent by intent versus parent by biology — actually have to be coherent for these practices to make sense, and which rival views do the European bans quietly rule out? Where are the regimes internally inconsistent (say, a country that bans surrogacy but permits other intimate or risky uses of the body for pay), and where is each practice underdetermined, so that several different theories would license the same arrangement? Finally, if the compensation-not-purchase framing were dropped and we admitted openly that gametes or gestational labor are being bought, what would have to change in how either regime is structured?
