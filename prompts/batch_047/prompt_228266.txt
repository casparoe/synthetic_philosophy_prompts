The question set was: "Can a person face a genuine moral dilemma — that is, a situation in which they are morally required to do A, morally required to do B, and unable to do both? Present the strongest argument on each side and defend your own conclusion."

Here is the answer submitted:

"A genuine moral dilemma is a situation in which an agent is morally required to do A and morally required to do B, but doing both is impossible. This must be distinguished from a merely difficult choice, where the agent is uncertain what morality requires; in a genuine dilemma, the agent knows exactly what is required and still cannot comply in full.

The main argument against the possibility of such dilemmas rests on the principle that 'ought implies can.' An agent in a supposed dilemma cannot do both A and B, and since no one can be obligated to do what they cannot do, the agent cannot be obligated to do both. Genuine dilemmas are therefore impossible.

The main argument in favor comes from the phenomenon of moral residue, associated with Bernard Williams. In cases like Agamemnon's sacrifice of his daughter, the agent's guilt and regret seem fitting no matter what he does. If one option were simply the right thing to do, such residue would be irrational. Since the residue is fitting, both obligations must be genuine, and at least one goes unfulfilled.

Utilitarianism, however, faces dilemmas of its own: whenever two actions would produce exactly equal amounts of good, the agent is pulled in two directions and cannot fully satisfy morality.

My own view is that genuine dilemmas are possible. If morality always had a completely correct answer, feelings of guilt after choosing would never be appropriate — yet they plainly are in cases such as Sophie's Choice."
