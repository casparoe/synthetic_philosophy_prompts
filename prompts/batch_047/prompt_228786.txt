So I host a longform podcast about science and big ideas, and next month I'm interviewing a guest I'm genuinely stuck on how to prep for. He's a biologist-turned-philosopher with a new book arguing two things at once: first, that insects probably feel pain (bees, wasps, fruit flies, the lot), and second, that once you grant even a modest probability of that, the sheer number of insects on earth — quintillions, versus maybe 80 billion chickens a year — means insect suffering could be the biggest moral problem there is, dwarfing factory farming in expected terms. He's sharp, media-trained, and very good at sliding out of trouble with "we should stay humble about other minds" or "that's an empirical question for future research."

Write me a sequence of 8-10 interview questions, in the order I should ask them, and for each one give me the follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give. No "what got you interested in bugs" warm-ups. A few things I specifically want the sequence to do:

- Open with Darwin. In The Formation of Vegetable Mould Through the Action of Worms (1881) he starts the book like this: "The share which worms have taken in the formation of the layer of vegetable mould, which covers the whole surface of the land in every moderately humid country, is the subject of the present volume." That's it — a book announcing itself as being about dirt. And then a chapter or two later Darwin's got worms dragging leaves into their burrows by the narrow end, handling weird novel shapes like paper triangles, and he's concluding this shows some intelligence rather than blind instinct. I want a question that turns on this: does my guest think Darwin's inference from flexible behavior to mentality is basically the same inference he's making for insects, just with fancier tools? And when he says (as he will) that we now have better evidence — nociceptors, motivational trade-offs, self-administered analgesia — what's the follow-up that pins down whether any of that is evidence of *felt* pain rather than very clever reflex? Where exactly is the line, and does he think Darwin was over it, under it, or is the line itself the problem?

- Make him say where the probability comes from. His whole scale argument needs some number like "10% chance insects are sentient" or whatever, and I want a question on whether that credence is doing any real work or whether it's just a vibe dressed up as a probability. His likely move is "any reasonable person should assign at least some non-negligible probability" — the follow-up has to ask what makes a probability reasonable when there's no frequency, no model, nothing to conditionalize on.

- The reductio. If low probability times huge numbers is the master move, nematodes outnumber insects by orders of magnitude and C. elegans has 302 neurons and a fully mapped connectome. Does the argument extend to them? To rotifers? If he says there's a complexity threshold, the follow-up is what principled threshold doesn't look gerrymandered to land on insects. If he bites the bullet and says yes, nematodes too, the follow-up is whether he's recieved the memo that this now sounds like a worldview where microbiology is the main branch of ethics.

- The fanaticism problem, but without me having to say "Pascal's mugging" on air like a dork. Something like: any framework where a tiny chance of an astronomical stake dominates everything seems exploitable by any cause that can describe a big enough number. He'll say his probabilities aren't *that* tiny. Then the follow-up: so there's a probability below which the argument fails — where is it, and is insect sentience actually above it by a method he'd accept from someone else?

- Aggregation across tiny minds. Even granting pain, is one bee's bad minute the same kind of moral unit as one chicken's bad minute? He'll say suffering is suffering. Follow-up on whether he's smuggling in equal weight per individual while his opponents weight by capacity, and whether the numbers advantage survives if you weight even slightly by richness of experience.

- The "so what" question. What would he actually have us do — fewer pesticides? Different crop science? Nothing yet? He tends to retreat to "we're just raising the question." I want a follow-up that makes the retreat cost him something: if the stakes are as big as he says, isn't inaction on his own framework a catastrophe, and if it's not a catastrophe, was the framework ever doing anything?

- And a closing question about what evidence could count *against* the view — not just weaken it, but make him shelve the whole project.

Keep the questions in my voice where you can: direct, a bit skeptical but not hostile, comfortable saying "quintillion" out loud. And tell me which two questions in the sequence you think are most likely to produce a genuinely newsworthy answer, so I know where to let silence do the work.
