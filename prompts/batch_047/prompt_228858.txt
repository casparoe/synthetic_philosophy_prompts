So last weekend I got into this dumb three-hour argument with my friend Ben about the axiom of choice. He's a smart guy (engineer, not a mathematician) and his position was basically that Banach-Tarski proves mathematicians have talked themselves into believing nonsense, that doubling a ball is obviously impossible so whatever principle leads there must just be wrong, and the fact that mathematicians keep it anyway shows the whole field is vibes. I lost the argument, honestly, or at least I didn't win it, because I kept saying things like "well it's independent of ZF" and "everyone uses it" which I now realize are terrible arguments.

The annoying coda: I run a math circle for sharp high schoolers (they've seen proofs by induction, they know what a function is, a couple have done some real analysis, none have done set theory formally) and Banach-Tarski is coming up organically because one of them saw a video about it. I have about 50 minutes and a whiteboard. I do NOT want to just tell them the statement and move on, I want to actually teach the axiom of choice properly enough that the next Ben they meet doesn't roll them.

Don't explain the axiom to me -- plan how I should explain it. Specifically:

- The order the pieces have to come in and why. What has to be in place before the axiom itself even makes sense as a claim (I'm guessing: what it means to pick an element from a set, the difference between doing that finitely many times vs all at once for infinitely many sets, why the finite case is not an axiom at all but the infinite one is). If some piece I think is a prerequisite actually isn't, say so.
- The one carrying example. Everyone reaches for choosing one sock from each of infinitely many pairs of identical socks vs one shoe from infinitely many pairs of shoes. Is that actually the right example for this audience? What must the example have (and not have) so that students feel why a rule-based selection is fine but a ruleless one needs an axiom? If there's better than socks, give it and say why socks fail.
- The misconception map. Predict what they'll form -- my guesses: "so it's obviously true, why is it even an axiom," "but you can't actually make infinitely many choices in real life," "Banach-Tarski means it's false so why keep it," and the kid who saw the video will say something about cutting balls with scissors. For each one: where exactly in my sequence do I head it off, and how, and which ones do I let sit until the end because defusing them early would break the build.
- Where Ben's argument actually has a point. I want to be honest with them about what's genuinely weird here rather than defensive, because that's where I lost. Where in the plan do I concede what needs conceding without handing them "math is vibes."
- One question to ask at the end that tells me whether it landed. Not "state the axiom" -- something that would catch a kid who can recite it but still thinks the sock choices are physically sitting in a pile somewhere.
- What to explicitly refuse to cover at this level (Zorn's lemma, well-ordering, the independence proofs, how Banach-Tarski actually works?) and the one-line deflection for each so they feel deferred rather than blown off.
