I'm a journalist covering science and big ideas, and I'm working on a long feature about infinite ethics -- the tangle of problems that show up when moral theories have to handle infinite populations, infinite utility, or a possibly infinite universe. For the piece I need to figure out what the actual live frontiers are, not just rehash the intro-level puzzles.

Here's the framing I keep running into when I talk to people. A physicist I interviewed says the whole thing is downstream of cosmology: if something like eternal inflation is right, the universe really does contain infinitely many of everything, and every moral theory has to cope with that, so the physics is the crux. A philosopher friend says the opposite: the paralysis problems are a pathology of a specific way of valuing outcomes -- cardinal utilities summed or averaged over arbitrarily large sets -- and would mostly dissolve with a different axiology (non-Archimedean values, person-affecting views, whatever), so the value theory is upstream and the physics barely matters. And a third person told me both are missing the point, that the real problem is decision-theoretic: even if the world is finite, infinite payoffs in principle (tiny probabilities of astronomically large outcomes, say) already break the usual tools, so that has to be fixed first.

So: if you had to place a bet on which of these three -- the cosmology question, the axiology question, or the decision theory question -- is genuinely upstream, in the sense that progress on it would most change how the other two look, which would you pick and why? I want a dependency-aware take: what depends on what, which of these is even tractable in the next decade given the tools we actually have, and which one you'd deprioritize because resolving it wouldn't move the others much. If there's a fourth question you think I'm leaving out that beats all three, say that instead.

Also, be straight with me about how confident you are in your ordering -- rough confidence levels on the main claims -- and flag the single point in your answer you're least sure about, since that's probably the bit I'll want to double-check before it goes in print.
