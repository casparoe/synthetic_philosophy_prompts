I got into it with my uncle last weekend about the statue of a colonial-era figure that our city council wants to move from the main square into a museum, and he ended the discussion with the line everyone reaches for: "you can't judge people from the past by today's standards -- you have to judge them by the standards of their own time." It sounds obviously sensible when he says it, but the more I turn it over the less I understand what it's actually claiming, because taken literally it seems to make moral criticism of anyone before, say, 1900 impossible, which can't be right.

So here's my question: what are the different precise theses that slogan could be expressing? I can gestur at a few candidates myself -- maybe it's an epistemic point (people couldn't have known better, given what everyone around them believed), maybe it's a point about blame (we can still say the thing was wrong, we just can't hold *them* responsible), maybe it's a claim that moral facts themselves are relative to eras, or maybe it's just a reminder to check our own smugness because future people will find us monstrous too. Probably there are others I haven't thought of. For each reading, I'd like to know: is it a platitude, is it a substantive but defensible position, or is it actually false or incoherent? And crucially, which readings would actually do the work my uncle needed -- i.e., get him from the slogan to "leave the statue up"?

One thing I'd specifically like untangled: the figure in question was criticised *by his own contemporaries* for the stuff he's now controversial for, so "standards of his time" seems to do no work at all in this particular case -- his time's standards were contested, like ours. Does the slogan quietly assume that each era has one settled moral standard, and is that assumption itself part of what's wrong with it? Also, does anything change depending on what commemoration even *is* -- if keeping the statue is just recording history versus actively honouring him, maybe different readings of the slogan matter for each.

When you lay this out, please be clear about which parts are basically settled among people who've thought about this (e.g., whether the literal "moral facts change per era" reading is tenable) and which parts are genuinely live disputes (I suspect the blame/excuse stuff and the "what are statues for" stuff are), and don't present a contested philosophical view as if it were the agreed consensus. I want to go back to my uncle with a menu of positions, not just a counter-slogan.
