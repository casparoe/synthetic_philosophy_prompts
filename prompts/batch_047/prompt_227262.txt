So my roommate cornered me with this last week and I still don't have a good answer, which is annoying because the conclusion is obviously crazy. He calls it the "shrinking target" argument:

(1) It's only fair to blame someone for something if that thing was up to them -- blame is basically a verdict that says YOU could have done otherwise and didn't.

(2) Take two people who drive home equally drunk on the same night. One hits a kid who runs into the road; the other gets home fine because no kid happened to be there. Their choices were identical; only luck seperates them. So it's unfair to blame the first one more. (Everyone grants this about outcomes, he says.)

(3) So blame should only ever attach to what the person willed -- their intention, their choice -- never to how things turned out.

(4) But now apply the same move one level down. What you intend depends on what considerations occur to you in the moment, how compelling they feel, what kind of person you are. You didn't choose any of that. The careful planner of a murder didn't choose to be the sort of person whose cruelty feels natural to him, any more than the lucky driver chose to have an empty street.

(5) If you didn't choose the thing, it wasn't up to you in the sense (1) requires. That's just what "up to you" means.

(6) So intentions and choices aren't a fair basis for blame either.

(7) And there's nothing else blame could attach to -- everything about an action is either the outcome, the will behind it, or the character behind that. All three are swallowed.

(8) Therefore blame is never fair. Not for the drunk driver, fine, but also not for the cold-blooded planner who got exactly the result he wanted.

Conclusion is nuts. Punishment, resentment, apology, the difference between an accident and a crime -- all of it goes. And the argument seems to swallow praise too (he admitted this cheerfully), so it "proves too much" in both directions. But I can't find the step where it slips.
