I'm putting together the final for my upper-level metaphysics seminar (mostly juniors/seniors; we did a unit on modality covering Descartes, Hume's maxim, Kripke, and some of the recent conceivability literature). I want one essay question on conceivability as a guide to possibility, built around this passage from Descartes' Sixth Meditation (Veitch translation), which I want them to interpret closely rather than just recognize:

"But if I desire to think of a chiliogon, I indeed rightly conceive that it is a figure composed of a thousand sides, as easily as I conceive that a triangle is a figure composed of only three sides; but I cannot imagine the thousand sides of a chiliogon as I do the three sides of a triangle, nor, so to speak, view them as present [with the eyes of my mind]. And although, in accordance with the habit I have of always imagining something when I think of corporeal things, it may happen that, in conceiving a chiliogon, I confusedly represent some figure to myself, yet it is quite evident that this is not a chiliogon, since it in no wise differs from that which I would represent to myself, if I were to think of a myriogon, or any other figure of many sides; nor would this representation be of any use in discovering and unfolding the properties that constitute the difference between a chiliogon and other polygons. But if the question turns on a pentagon, it is quite true that I can conceive its figure, as well as that of a chiliogon, without the aid of imagination; but I can likewise imagine it by applying the attention of my mind to its five sides, and at the same time to the area which they contain."

Here's the crux I'm trying to test. Every one of my students can parrot that clear and distinct conception guarantees possibility because God can create whatever is distinctly conceived. What I want them to grapple with is what Descartes' conception/imagination contrast actually buys him against the standard modern objection that apparent conceivability just tracks our cognitive limitations or ignorance (the Hesperus/Phosphorus cases, water is H2O, the zombie argument). Notice the tension: Descartes himself admits you can confusedly represent a figure that is NOT in fact a chiliogon while thinking you're conceiving one. So the question should force a verdict: does the distinction give Descartes a reliable test for possibility, or does it merely relocate the problem, since nothing in the passage tells you whether your "pure conception" latches onto the thing itself or onto a confused stand-in?

Deliverables: (1) the exam question itself, phrased so it can't be answered without doing real work on the passage — something like a "does Descartes have the resources in this text to..." structure, doable in 75 minutes, worth 30 marks; (2) a model answer of roughly 600 words (the passage above doesn't count toward that) that COMMITS to a position — I dont want a balanced on-the-one-hand-on-the-other essay, it should say whether Descartes' test survives the objection and defend the verdict, including against the a posteriori necessities case; (3) a marking scheme naming the four or five specific moves an A answer must make, how many marks each is worth (total 30), and the specific mistakes that should cost marks — e.g. reading "imagination" as Descartes' term for any mental imagery whatsoever, or answering as if the passage were about the cogito. I'd also like the scheme to seperate what earns an A from what caps you at a B, in one line each.
