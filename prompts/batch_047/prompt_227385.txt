Background: the essay is about Ayn Rand's critique of altruism. To be clear about what I mean by that: Rand attacked altruism in Comte's original sense, the view that serving others is the standard of moral value and that acting for your own sake is at best amoral. She was not against kindness or generosity as such; she held that each person is an end in themselves, that rational self-interest is the proper code, and that neither sacrificing yourself to others nor sacrificing others to yourself is moral. So far so standard. My essay goes after what I think is a soft underbelly: her view on helping strangers in emergencies.

---

DRAFT: "The Standing Emergency: Rand's Exception Eats the Rule"

1. Rand's official position, from her writing on the ethics of emergencies, is roughly this. Helping strangers is fine, even good, in genuine emergencies: the drowning child, the car crash victim, the person lost in the desert. Three conditions keep such help within egoism: the situation is exceptional rather than the norm of human existence, it is temporary, and the helper must not make helping their central purpose or turn the rescued person into a permanent claim on them. Outside emergencies, the trader principle governs: relationships among adults are exchanges of value, not unilateral gifts.

2. Here is the problem. At any given moment, there are emergencies. Lots of them. Someone is drowning, starving, bleeding, or being crushed by rubble right now, and for a modest sum I can do something about some of them. From the victim's side, each of these cases meets Rand's three conditions perfectly: exceptional in that person's life, temporary, and nobody is asking me to adopt them forever. What has changed between Rand's writing and now is not the emergencies but my awareness of them. The drowning child is no longer the one I happen to walk past; she is one of thousands I can see on a screen.

3. So the emergency clause, which Rand treats as a small footnote, becomes for a well-informed agent a standing, open-ended, radically demanding permission, and permission slides into obligation quickly once you grant her premises: if human life and human achievement are objective values, and I can advance those values at trivial cost to values I hold higher, on what grounds do I decline? The exception eats the rule. The Objectivist who reads the news is in the moral position of Peter Singer's pond-walker all day, every day, forever.

4. The obvious Randian reply: you may only help where your own hierarchy of values is genuinely engaged, where the person or the cause falls within your context. Emergencies far away, involving people you have no connection to, are simply not your context. But notice what this reply needs. It needs "your context" to do real carving, and Rand never tells us where the boundary sits. Worse, the boundary looks like it is drawn by attention, i.e. by what you happen to know about. And that creates a perverse incentive structure: the less you know about distant suffering, the cleaner your conscience. Rand's philosophy treats evasion, the willful refusal to look at facts, as the root vice. Her emergency doctrine, on this reading, quietly rewards exactly that. Keep your eyes on your own life, and the exception never triggers.

5. So the dilemma. Either Rand's view permits (and arguably requires) a Singer-like level of engagement with distant emergencies, in which case the ethics of emergencies is not a footnote but a Trojan horse that smuggles demandingness into egoism. Or it restricts obligation to what falls in one's "context," in which case the whole theory secretly runs on an unanalyzed and self-serving notion of moral attention, and the famous clarity of the system is an illusion at precisely the point where it matters most.

6. My tentative conclusion: the real content of Rand's ethics of benevolence is an unstated theory of what you are obliged to notice. Somebody should write that theory. If it can be written consistently with her premises, the emergency doctrine survives; if not, altruism gets in through the back door wearing a lifeguard's whistle.
