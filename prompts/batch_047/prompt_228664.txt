I teach in a philosophy department, and next semester I am responsible for one ninety-minute session of an interdisciplinary graduate seminar on rational agency, co-taught with an economist. The students come from economics, political science, and cognitive science; they are fluent in utility maximization and game theory but have no training in the philosophy of action. My session is on Kavka's toxin puzzle (Gregory S. Kavka, "The Toxin Puzzle," Analysis, 1983), and I want the session to turn on a passage from David Gauthier's response to the puzzle ("Assure and Threaten," Ethics, 1994), in which Gauthier defends the rationality of following through on a rationally formed intention:

"The rational outcome of your deliberation tomorrow morning is the action that will be part of your life going as well as possible, subject to the constraint that it be compatible with your commitment—in this case, compatible with the sincere intention that you form today to drink the toxin. And so the rational action is to drink the toxin."

Do not give me the lecture; give me the plan for it. Specifically: (1) The order in which the pieces must go. My own view is that the distinction between reasons to act and reasons to intend has to be secured before the case is even stated, since otherwise these students hear the setup as a puzzle about poison and money rather than about what can be intended — but tell me whether that dependency is right, and what else must be in place before the Gauthier passage can be put on the table rather than merely quoted at them. (2) The carrying example. I do not want to run the whole session on the toxin case itself, because the vial and the billionaire invite showmanship; specify one mundane preparatory case, state what structural features it must share with the toxin case (reward keyed to the intending rather than the doing; a later choice that is transparent and incentive-free) and what it must lack (an external predictor, self-deception, the option of forgetting), and explain why the obvious candidate — resolving tonight to get up at six and run — smuggles in weakness of will and so teaches the wrong lesson. (3) The misconceptions, in the order this particular audience will produce them, with the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off: that the puzzle is ordinary time-inconsistency and a commitment device fixes it; that it is a re-run of Newcomb's problem; that an intention is just a strong desire or a decision to try; and, once the passage is on the table, that Gauthier has dissolved the puzzle by showing drinking to be rational — when the contested move is his quiet phrase "sincere intention that you form today," which assumes precisely what Kavka argues a clear-eyed agent cannot bring about. (4) A closing check that would show the idea has landed rather than been memorized: I am imagining handing them modified versions of the case — the payout depends on actually drinking; the toxin is painless; a hypnotist can install the intention for a fee — and asking which modifications dissolve the puzzle and which merely relocate it. Tell me what their answers should look like if the session worked. (5) What I should openly refuse to cover at this level: the experimental literature on whether people can in fact form such intentions, and the puzzle's origins in nuclear deterrence theory, both of which I would flag as provenance and then set aside.
