Got into it again at the Allais paradox with people at our local rationality meetup last week, and afterwards I figured I should finally write down my actual positions instead of freelancing in every argument. Context: I've been reading LW and the diaspora blogs for like a decade, I've read the decision theory sequences and most of the classic posts, and I want to post my positions as a shortform so people can poke holes — but before I do, I want to know if the holes are structural. Here's where I've landed:

(a) In the standard Allais setup — first choice is $1M guaranteed vs 89% $1M / 10% $5M / 1% nothing; second choice is 11% $1M vs 10% $5M, nothing otherwise — I take the guaranteed million in the first and the 10% shot at $5M in the second. I don't think this is a bias or a miscalculation. The gap between certainty and a 1% chance of walking away with nothing is a real thing that's worth paying to avoid, and just multiply doesn't capture everything I legitimately care about.

(b) I find money pump arguments decisive. If your preferences let someone repeatedly extract money from you while leaving you no better off, the preferences are broken. This is exactly why I'm an absolutist about transitivity — no exceptions, no "but the cycle is what I really want."

(c) I'm a one-boxer on Newcomb, and I'm fairly confident I'd pay in a counterfactual mugging. In general I'm sympathetic to the updateless-ish idea that you evaluate decisions as policies, including how the policy does across branches that don't end up being the actual one.

(d) I'm a consequentialist about evaluating decisions: what matters is what actually happens as a result of the choice. Arguments that lean on what would have happened in some branch that by hypothesis never occurs strike me as a bit spooky.

(e) For actual repeated small-stakes stuff — poker bankroll, index funds, whether the extended warranty is worth it — plain expected value is obviously the right tool and I'd laugh at anyone who used anything else.

(f) I don't buy the "in the long run expected value wins" argument for EU maximization; the ergodicity critique basicly convinced me one-shot decisions aren't ensemble averages.

So: does this package hang together? Tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only look like they are, ranked by severity, and for each real tension say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me. If there's a single revision that buys the most consistency for the least pain, name it. And please don't just relitigate which Allais answer is correct — I want an audit of my commitments, not a conversion attempt.
