Write a dialogue of roughly 1,400–1,800 words between two philosophers serving on a hospital ethics committee. They have been consulted about the following case, which you should treat as the shared subject of their exchange:

A woman in her late fifties, after receiving an early diagnosis of Alzheimer's disease, executed an unusually detailed advance directive. It refuses all life-prolonging treatment once she reaches advanced dementia, and it contains a clause she insisted on adding herself: anticipating that her future demented self might appear content, it instructs caregivers to treat any such apparent contentment as no evidence whatsoever about how she should be treated, since, as she saw it, the person enjoying that contentment would no longer be her. Eight years later she has advanced dementia. By every observable measure she is serene and often joyful. She also, with the help of a well-meaning volunteer, has begun drafting new "directives" most weeks — short documents expressing wishes about her own future care that contradict the original directive. Capacity assessments of her at the time of writing these new documents have been equivocal: some days she seems to grasp her situation, some days not.

One philosopher defends the view that the original directive, self-discount clause included, should govern: a person's authority over her own life is precisely the authority of her settled, reflectively endorsed values over time, and deferring to the later self's current states would not respect the patient but abandon her. The other defends the view that treatment decisions must answer to the interests of the person now in the bed, whose capacities, pleasures, and attachments are what they are; the earlier self's instructions are weighty evidence about who this person was, but treating them as binding commands confuses fidelity to a biography with care for a patient, and the self-discount clause in particular asks us to pretend away the only subject currently capable of being benefited or harmed.

Requirements for the dialogue:

- Both characters must be equally philosophically competent. Neither may be given a lazy or easily demolished position, and neither may be permitted to win by the other's oversight.
- The exchange must involve genuine back-and-forth: each character must respond to the other's actual arguments, concede points where concession is honestly forced, and press objections where they are not.
- The dialogue must at some point take up each of the following, in whatever order arises naturally: (a) whether the original directive's authority rests on the author's competence at the time of writing, on the psychological continuity between author and patient, or on something else — and what follows for the case given that the continuity here is severely diminished; (b) the self-discount clause specifically — whether one can rationally bind caregivers to ignore the future self's manifest states, and whether honoring the clause respects the author or merely indulges her contempt for a condition she had not yet lived; (c) the new weekly "directives" — whether the authority claimed for the original directive, whatever its ground, is also available to the present self during her lucid-seeming intervals, and whether the two philosophers' answers to (a) commit them to symmetrical or asymmetrical treatment of old and new documents.
- At least once, each character must articulate what they take to be the strongest version of the other's position before criticizing it.
- The dialogue need not end in agreement, but it must end in sharpened disagreement: the characters should close by stating, precisely, the one proposition on which they now see their dispute to turn — not merely restating their opening positions.

Set the exchange in a quiet room after the committee meeting, with no audience and no decision required of them that evening, so that the conversation can follow the arguments rather than the institutional pressures. No citations, names of real authors, or references to published literature are needed; the characters should argue from the case and from first principles.
