There's a result in decision theory I keep bumping into: for an idealized Bayesian agent, free information can never have negative expected value before you look at it (worst case, it doesn't change your decision), so turning down free info is always a mistake. But my gut verdicts on actual cases don't line up with that at all:

- A friend declines a free, reliable test for an untreatable late-onset disease because she'd rather not spend thirty years dreading it: seems fine to me.
- I don't check my investment portfolio daily, partly because I'd make panicky trades if I did: seems fine, maybe even smart.
- A judge refuses to look at a defendant's prior record before setting bail because it would bias her: seems right.
- My roommate won't open his credit card statements because it's stressful: not fine, he's being an idiot.
- A scientist running a trial refuses to peek at the data early: seems right, something about stopping rules.
- A hiring comittee member asks not to be told candidates' names to avoid bias: fine.
- My sister says she doesn't want to know whether her husband cheated years ago: honestly torn on this one.

Is there a principle that separates the fine cases from the not-fine ones, and can it be stated in a way that's compatible with the theorem -- e.g., the good cases of ignorance are exactly ones where the agent isn't the idealized info-processor the theorem assumes (bounded, biased, prone to making worse decisions *because* of how they'd react)? Note the portfolio and credit card cases look structurally similar to me, so a good principle should explain why they differ, or else convince me to flip one of them. And say what your principle predicts for my sister.
