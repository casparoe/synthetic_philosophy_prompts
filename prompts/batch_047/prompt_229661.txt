I am a PhD student working on existentialist ethics, and for a graduate colloquium on Sartre I would like you to write a satirical piece that lets me test whether a model has actually understood the structure of bad faith rather than just the slogans. Here is the brief.

Write a corporate onboarding manual, roughly 600–700 words, for a fictional firm whose leadership has decided to institutionalize Sartrean bad faith as official company policy. The manual should be written in the deadpan, procedural voice of a real HR document — welcome note, numbered policies, a short FAQ, perhaps a note on performance reviews — and should never wink at the reader or break character.

The humor must come from getting the philosophy right, so please build the manual's policies out of the actual mechanics of bad faith as Sartre describes them in Being and Nothingness:

1. Model the ideal employee on Sartre's waiter, the café server who performs his role a little too precisely, as though he were a thing whose nature is to wait tables. The manual should encourage this kind of total identification with one's function as a promotion criterion.

2. Include an HR case study modeled on Sartre's woman on a first date, who leaves her hand in her companion's hand while treating it as a mere object, postponing the moment of decision. Frame this as an approved technique for deferring uncomfortable choices, e.g. in salary negotiations.

3. Have the policies oscillate, as bad faith does, between the two forbidden reductions: treating employees as pure facticity (they simply are their job descriptions, with no freedom beyond them) and treating them as pure transcendence (their past record, training, and actual situation count for nothing). Ideally the manual contradicts itself on this point in a way that is left unresolved, since bad faith requires affirming and denying the same freedom at once.

4. Address what Sartre calls the spirit of seriousness — the view that values are inscribed in the world rather than chosen — as the company's official stance on its mission statement and core values.

5. Finally, and this is the part I care about most: the manual should quietly founder on the self-undermining character of bad faith. Sartre's point is that one cannot simply become a thing; it takes an ongoing, free effort to flee one's freedom, so the flight itself testifies to what is being fled. Let the manual's policies require ever more workshops, certifications, and compliance exercises in order to convince employees they have no freedom — so that the reader sees the absurdity without the manual ever acknowledging it.

Do not quote Sartre verbatim and do not mention him by name inside the document; the manual should read as though the company invented these policies itself. After the manual, add a brief coda of 100–150 words, outside the satirical voice, explaining which features of bad faith each policy was designed to exaggerate, and whether you think the satire is fair to Sartre or whether it distorts his position somewhere.
