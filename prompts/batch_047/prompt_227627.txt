Last night I had a two-hour argument with my friend Priya about whether viruses are alive. She said obviously not — no metabolism of their own, just chemistry that borrows a cell — and I said they reproduce and they evolve, and then she hit me with "so does fire, sort of" and I came back with the mule problem (if reproduction is your criterion, is a mule not alive?) and by midnight we'd dragged in prions, frozen tardigrades and a self-replicating robot her brother helped build at uni. Neither of us won, and I'm still annoyed about it. Anyway, I run a combined science-and-philosophy club at my school, about 14 students aged 15-17, one hour a week, and I want to turn this exact fight into next week's session — but as something they do, not something I tell them. No lecture up front, and I don't want them hearing the standard textbook list of the "characteristics of life" beforehand, because that just gives them the answers.

Here's what I'm after: an activity where the students have to act as some kind of classification board. My rough thought is a planetary-protection panel deciding which samples brought back from a mission get stamped ALIVE, with actual stakes attached — a limited quarantine budget, points for calls that survive challenge, something like that — so that "it's all just semantics, words mean whatever we want" stops feeling like a clever escape hatch, because in the game the classification costs you something. The structure I want is: they write their own definition of life first, as a group, then candidate cases arrive one at a time, each chosen to break whatever definition they've currently got, and they have to revise under time pressure. Ideally they end up generating the killer counterexamples themselves — someone in the room has their own mule moment — rather than me dealing the counterexamples from a deck. Give me the full rules, the materials, which cases to use and in what order (and why that order), a timeline for the hour, and a debrief that maps each case onto the criterion it breaks and what it costs to patch the definition at that point.
