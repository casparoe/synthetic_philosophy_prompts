So I'm in this grad reading group on 20th century moral philosophy and last week we did Bernard Williams's critique of impartial moral theories, including the famous case where a man can only save one of two drowning people and one of them happens to be his wife. Williams's complaint, roughly, is that any moral theory which requires the man to first establish to himself that saving his wife is morally permitted in situations of this type has given him one thought too many -- the motivating thought should just be that it's his wife and she's drowning, full stop. Our group got stuck on how far this objection actually reaches, and I want to write up something more careful on it.

Here's the case that broke our discussion. Imagine a triage nurse at a small hospital during a mass-casualty event, and her own child is brought in alongside a stranger with slightly worse injuries. Triage protocol says treat the stranger first, and it's hard to deny the protocol is justified -- impartial triage saves the most lives overall. But now apply Williams: if the nurse pauses to check whether the rules permit prioritizing her kid, does she have one thought too many, in which case Williams's objection seems to delegitimize basically every institutional role that demands impartiality (judges, triage officers, referees, journal editors)? Or is the wife-rescue case special, and if so, what exactly marks the difference -- intimacy, the absence of a professional role, the stakes? It can't just be "roles make the difference" without argument, since Williams's whole point is that moral theories shouldn't alienate us from the projects and attachments that make life worth living, and plenty of people experience their professional roles as among those very projects.

Write an essay of around 1200 words on whether Williams's one thought too many objection can be contained to intimate-rescue-type cases or whether it generalizes in a way that undermines impartiality-demanding roles. Take a definite position. Whichever side you land on, you need to (a) say precisely what feature of the original case does the work in Williams's argument, (b) address the strongest version of the opposing intuition -- for generalizers, why the triage nurse really would be objectionably alienated if she follows protocol; for containers, why the drowning husband isn't just being indulged -- and (c) engage with the reply that Williams's point is only about what moral theories ask agents to treat as *justifying* reasons, not about which thoughts should consciously occur to them in the moment, and say whether that reply actually saves impartial theories or just relocates the problem. Don't just summarize the literature positions; I want an argument.
