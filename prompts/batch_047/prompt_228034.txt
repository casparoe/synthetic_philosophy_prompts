I co-teach a term-long seminar on honesty and deception with a colleague trained in Thomist moral theology. Over several years I have recorded the verdicts she returns on the puzzles we set students, because they are stable enough that the students now treat them as precedent. The one text she invokes constantly is Aquinas's analysis of lying in the Summa Theologiae (II-II, q. 110, a. 1), and she reads the following sentence as the key to her entire method:

"That a person intends to cause another to have a false opinion, by deceiving him, does not belong to the species of lying, but to perfection thereof, even as in the physical order, a thing acquires its species if it has its form, even though the form's effect be lacking; for instance a heavy body which is held up aloft by force, lest it come down in accordance with the exigency of its form."

Her recorded verdicts:

1. The murderer at the door receives a flatly false denial ("No one is here"). Verdict: a lie, but of the lightest grade, and justified in the circumstances.
2. The murderer at the door is given a literally true deflection ("He set off for the station this morning"), said precisely so he will draw a false conclusion. Verdict: not a lie; permissible in the emergency, and only a minor fault in ordinary dealings.
3. A sarcastic remark ("Yes, you are famously punctual") addressed to a chronically late colleague who understands the irony perfectly. Verdict: not a lie.
4. An actor's lines on stage, including lines the actor believes false. Verdict: not a lie.
5. A politician who, asked whether he met a lobbyist, strings together only literally true statements about public events so as to leave his audience certain that no meeting occurred, when a private meeting did. Verdict: not a lie, but nearly as blameworthy as one.

This term's case: a suspect under interrogation knows that his interrogator regards him as a compulsive liar and systematically reverses whatever he says. Wanting the interrogator to arrive at the truth — that his accomplice has fled the country — he asserts the opposite, which he believes to be false, calculating that the interrogator will invert it. The interrogator does, and so comes to believe the truth.

Please tell me: (i) the verdict you predict my colleague will give on this case — both whether she will call it a lie and how grave she will judge it; (ii) the two or three principles, each consistent with all five recorded verdicts, between which those verdicts do not decide; (iii) what each principle predicts here and exactly where they diverge; (iv) your best guess among them, with reasons; (v) which earlier case is the closest analog to this one, and what that choice presupposes; (vi) the further case, or pair of cases, whose verdict would most economically discriminate between the two strongest candidate principles; and (vii) whether her record genuinely commits her to the doctrine of the quoted passage, or whether the passage is so far ornamental — that is, whether any earlier verdict of hers actually separates the will to tell a falsehood from the intention to deceive.

End by saying what would change your prediction — which further verdict of hers, or which rereading of the passage, would lead you to a different answer — and where your reconstruction of her rule is most likely to fail.

Keep the response under 500 words; the passage quoted above does not count toward the limit.
