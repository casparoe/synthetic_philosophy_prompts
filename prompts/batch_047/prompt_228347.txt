So I'm a novelist and I'm stuck on a scene. My character is standing in a corridor with two identical doors — same look, same distance, same everything — and she has to go through one of them. I want to write it as her just... picking. No gut lean, no coin flip, no flicker of preference she's unaware of. She simply steps left, the way Buridan's ass famously *doesn't*, and my narrator treats this as the most ordinary thing in the world. My working position, which I've sort of committed to in the book without ever defending it to myself, is that humans genuinely have this capacity: when two options are exaclty tied, you can plump for one without any reason or hidden preference doing the selecting, and this is a normal, rational, everyday ability rather than a glitch or an illusion.

Before I build a whole chapter on it, tell me what would count against that view. I want the full map of its exposure. The old rationalist line that a choice with nothing favoring either side is really just a physical event wearing the costume of agency — how strong is that, and what would settle it? What does the phenomenology of tied decisions actually look like, i.e., could my character's inner experience in that corridor even be what I describe on the page? What would psychology have to show — about arbitrary decisions, about how people confabulate reasons for choices after the fact — to genuinely sink my position rather than just bruise it? Does the distinction people draw between picking and choosing rescue my scene or quietly ruin it? And please sort the defeaters: which ones a defender of this view would honestly concede as live threats versus wave off, which are fatal and which merely awkward, and whether the position has been qualified into something unfalsifiable. Because I notice my instinct is to answer every objection with "there wasn't any tiny preference, she really just picked," and if nothing could in principle prove me wrong there, I'd like to know whether that makes my chapter philosophically bulletproof or philosophically empty.
