I teach third-year philosophy of mind to students with some programming and causal-inference background, and I have one seventy-five-minute seminar for Jaegwon Kim’s causal exclusion argument. Plan how I should teach it rather than simply explaining it to me. Specify the order in which causal closure, realization, property distinctness, causal efficacy, and exclusion must be introduced, and why each depends on the previous step. Choose one carrying example that makes the pressure vivid without either assuming reduction from the outset or making a merely epistemic distinction look like two causes; say why examples involving pain, thermostats, or computer programs would or would not work. Map the predictable misconceptions—such as “the argument denies that ordinary beliefs cause behavior,” “multiple realization automatically saves mental causation,” “the mental and physical causes are just two descriptions of one event,” and “interventionist causation dissolves the problem”—and indicate exactly where in the sequence to confront each. Finish with one diagnostic question that distinguishes students who understand the dilemma from students who have merely memorized its conclusion, and state what complications I should deliberately postpone.
