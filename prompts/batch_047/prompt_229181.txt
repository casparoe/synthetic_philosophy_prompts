In a history-of-biology book I am reading, the author summarizes Gould and Lewontin's 1979 paper on the spandrels of San Marco — the well-known critique of the adaptationist programme — in the following terms.

Gould and Lewontin charge that evolutionary biologists have grown too ready to explain each trait of an organism as an adaptation fashioned by natural selection, often resting the explanation on a plausible story rather than on evidence. Their central image is architectural. The spandrels of St. Mark's Basilica in Venice — the tapering triangular spaces between the dome and the arches that carry it — are covered with mosaics fitted so artfully to their spaces that one is tempted to suppose the spaces were designed to receive the images. In fact the spandrels are an unavoidable byproduct of setting a dome upon rounded arches; the decoration came afterward. Some traits, the paper argues, are like this: byproducts of an organism's architecture rather than direct products of selection, and when such a byproduct is later co-opted for a use, the authors call the result an exaptation. They conclude that most features of organisms are spandrels rather than adaptations, and hence that natural selection has played a much smaller part in evolution than their colleagues had assumed. Against the adaptationist programme they urge a pluralism in which developmental constraint, drift, and historical accident are entertained as serious rivals to selection, and adaptationist reasoning they compare to Dr. Pangloss's discovery that noses were made in order to bear spectacles.

I have seen this paper invoked constantly but have never read it, and I am trying to decide how much of this summary to trust. My own hunch is that the summary's report of the paper's conclusion — that most features of organisms are spandrels — goes further than the paper itself does, but I cannot say why. Would you go through the summary point by point and tell me which parts are accurate, which are subtly wrong and in what way, and what a defender of the paper would say has been misrepresented? Where the summary errs, I would like to know whether the error is one of emphasis or of substance. I am particularly interested in whether the book's later inference — that the paper demolished the methodological foundations of adaptationist explanation in general, so that the adaptive stories told by evolutionary psychologists may be dismissed wholesale, without case-by-case examination — is licensed by anything correct in the summary. Finally, once the inaccurate parts are corrected, what is the strongest conclusion the paper actually supports?
