I'm a retired chemist (40 years in materials science, so I know my way around a proof but nothing about political theory until recently) and I've rather rashly agreed to give an evening talk to my local electoral reform group. My thesis, roughly: the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem shows that any half-decent voting rule with three or more options can be gamed, so reformers should stop selling their favourite systems as ones where tactical voting disappears. Instead we should compare systems by what I'd call the price of strategy — how hard the optimal tactical move is to work out, and how badly it distorts the result when lots of people do it. On my scoring, some systems that are technically manipulable come out better than ones marketed as strategy-proof, and a bit of easy tactical voting might even be a useful signal rather than a pathology.

The audience: mostly long-standing PR/STV campaigners whose whole pitch to the public is that you can finally vote for who you actually want, plus two or three mathematically literate members who've definately read more social choice theory than I have, and a sceptical chair who thinks academics overcomplicate everything. Prepare me for the Q&A: the objections they'll actually raise, in the order they'll come, phrased the way this lot would phrase them (campaigners and hobbyist mathematicians, not journal referees), with what I should concede, a two-minute reply to each, and a note on where each reply is weakest. And tell me the question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks.
