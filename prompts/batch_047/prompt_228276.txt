I'm a software engineer on the evaluation team at a mid-sized AI company. Several times a quarter, we hit the same dispute during pre-deployment review: someone claims a model "knows" something it isn't telling us, and someone else claims the model is just a text predictor and talk of knowledge is anthropomorphism. Recent examples: a model that says it can't read a certain language but translates it correctly when the instruction is phrased as a continuation task; a model that gives a wrong answer confidently, then picks the right answer when shown its own wrong answer alongside the right one and asked to compare; a model whose chain of reasoning is gibberish but whose final answers stay suspiciously accurate. Management wants these resolved with something better than vibes, because the answer changes what we're willing to deploy and what we tell customers.

I'd like you to turn whatever the philosophy of mind and epistemology actually know about attributing knowledge to a system into a decision procedure my team can run. Relevant background: I have no formal philosophy training, but I can follow a careful argument. I'm vaguely aware that philosophers have thought about things like the difference between having a disposition and exercising it, whether knowledge requires the right kind of connection between belief and truth, and whether it even makes sense to attribute knowledge to something without a human-style mind -- please use whatever of that survives translation into practice, and tell me which distinctions have to survive the simplification and which can be dropped without distorting the verdict.

The procedure needs to be runnable in one working day by an engineer, using only things we can actually do: prompting, few-shot rephrasing, comparing behavior across contexts, consistency checks, fine-tuning on held-out data, and simple internal probes. It should specify the tests in order, what result at each step counts as sufficient evidence one way or the other, and where the procedure must stop and hand the question to a human because it's a judgment call dressed up as measurement. Be explicit about what each test can and cannot establish -- for instance, what it would show if reformatting a question flips the answer, versus what it would show if only fine-tuning recovers the behavior.

Then apply the procedure to the three cases above, and flag which verdicts are ones we'll find unwelcome (e.g., concluding the model has capability we've been underreporting, or that an apparent capability is an artifact of how we asked). Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do: the cases where "does the model know X" has no testable answer with our tools, and what we should write in the audit report in those cases instead of a verdict.
