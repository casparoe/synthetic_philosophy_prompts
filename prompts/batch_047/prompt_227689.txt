**The Dynastic View (draft)**

Population ethics has two famous ways of aggregating welfare. The total view sums everyone's wellbeing, and it implies the Repugnant Conclusion: a vast population of people whose lives are barely worth living is better than a smaller population of flourishing people, as long as there are enough of them. The average view avoids that but has its own disasters — it says adding people with decent lives can make things worse if they're slightly below average, and it seems to imply we'd improve the world by eliminating its least happy person.

I want to try out a third option, which I'll call the dynastic view. The idea: the basic unit of value isn't the person or the person-moment, but the *lineage* — a chain of lives connected by descent and care, stretching across generations. Here's how it works. First, compute the average welfare within each lineage across its whole history, weighting all generations equally. Second, take the average across lineages. A society is good insofar as its ongoing family-lines are good, considered as extended projects rather than as collections of individuals.

Why think this helps? Against the Repugnant Conclusion: flooding the world with billions of barely-worth-living people doesn't help, because either they join existing lineages, dragging down those lineages' averages, or they form new miserable lineages, dragging down the cross-lineage average. Adding wretched people is bad on this view, full stop. Against the average view's problem: adding happy new people within healthy lineages still counts in favor, because it raises those lineages' averages — we don't get the weird result that any below-average addition is a moral tragedy.

It also seems to dissolve the non-identity problem neatly. If the unit of evaluation is the lineage rather than the person, it doesn't matter *which* particular individuals get born — a choice that changes the identities of future people but leaves the lineage's average welfare intact is morally neutral. That seems right to me, since the non-identity problem always struck me as a technicality-generating machine rather than a real puzzle.

Practical upshot: this vindicates a moderate pronatalism. Having children who'll probably flourish is good because it extends and enriches a lineage. But the view is anti-natalist about bringing children into predictable misery, and it's neutral-to-negative about population growth via fragmentation into disconnected lives with no intergenerational continuity. So it threads the needle between the "growth at all costs" people and the anti-natalists.

Okay, so what I want comments on:

1. Is this actually a distinct position, or does it collapse into something already in the literature once you poke it? I have a nagging feeling it might be average utilitarianism wearing a costume, or maybe some kind of critical-level view, but I can't quite see the reduction.
2. Does the Repugnant Conclusion dodge actually work? My worry: nothing stops someone from just describing a world with astronomically many barely-positive lineages, and then averaging across lineages still gives you a positive number that might beat a small-world average. Did I just move the repugnance around?
3. What's the strongest counterexample? I suspect there's an ugly implication around eliminating struggling lineages (since that raises the cross-lineage average), which would be as bad as the average view's implication I complained about. Maybe worse. Am I right that this bites, and is it fatal?
4. The non-identity "dissolution" — I suspect I was too quick there. Does treating lineage-average as the thing that matters actually avoid non-identity issues, or do they just reappear as questions about which *lineages* get to exist?
5. Lineages are a mess biologically — they merge, they fork, go back far enough and everyone shares ancestors. Does the view need a cleaner individuation of its units to even be stated, and if so is there any non-arbitrary way to do it? If there isn't, is the whole thing dead on arrival?

Also general comments on whether the pronatalism upshot in the last paragraph follows at all, since that's the part people will actually care about and I'm least sure about it. Thanks!
