I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for well over a decade, and there is one argument I have watched recur in essentially unchanged form the entire time: whether cancel culture, deplatforming, and organized social sanction should be understood as punishment. The two positions are by now so standardized that I can predict each thread's course. One side holds that a coordinated campaign to get someone fired, deplatformed, or ostracized is the deliberate imposition of a harm in response to a perceived transgression, and therefore belongs to the category of punishment; from which it follows that such campaigns should be held to the norms we impose on punishment generally — proportionality, some connection to desert, consistency across cases, and at least an informal analogue of due process. The other side holds that none of this is punishment at all: criticism is speech, refusal to associate is a preference, no one is owed a platform, a job, or an audience, and the category of punishment properly applies only to penalties imposed by institutions with a recognized coercive mandate; from which it follows that importing the norms of criminal justice into the court of public opinion is a category error. What strikes me is that neither side disputes any of the descriptive facts — everyone agrees on what actually happens to the targets of these campaigns — and yet the disagreement has not moved in years.

Recently, rereading Locke, I came across a passage that made me suspect the whole dispute is running on rails he identified over three centuries ago. In Book II, Chapter XXVIII of An Essay Concerning Human Understanding ("Of Other Relations"), introducing what he calls moral relation, he writes:

"Fourthly, There is another sort of relation, which is the conformity or disagreement men's voluntary actions have to a rule to which they are referred, and by which they are judged of; which, I think, may be called moral relation, as being that which denominates our moral actions, and deserves well to be examined; there being no part of knowledge wherein we should be more careful to get determined ideas, and avoid, as much as may be, obscurity and confusion."

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is this. Locke's point is that a moral judgment is always relative to a rule — an action is judged by being referred to some standard — and that confusion proliferates when the rule in question is left unspecified. The cancel-culture debate, on my reading, stalls precisely because the disputants never specify the rule relative to which acts of social sanction are being assessed. The punishment side implicitly refers social sanction to the norms of the civil law, where deliberate harm demands justification; the no-punishment side implicitly refers it to the norms of private choice, where it needs none. Each side's conclusion follows trivially once its frame is granted, which would explain why the arguments all land and none of them move anyone. But I am not confident this diagnosis is right, and I would like it tested rather than adopted.

So here is what I want. First, a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it a merely verbal dispute over the extension of the word "punishment," of the sort Locke's demand for determined ideas would dissolve on inspection? Is it a substantive normative dispute about whether decentralized social sanction is punishment-like in the respects that matter — that is, whether the features of punishment that motivate proportionality and due-process constraints are present in pile-ons and deplatformings regardless of what we call them? Or is it something more strategic: a jurisdictional fight in which each side reaches for the categorization that imports the norms it favors, in which case the impasse is not confusion but rational positioning, and no amount of conceptual clarification would end it? Second, test my Lockean hypothesis against what the two sides actually say: does the frame-relative-to-a-rule diagnosis capture the real structure of the dispute, or does it flatten a genuine disagreement about the nature of social sanction itself? Third, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress. A stipulated taxonomy? A worked-out normative theory for the domain Locke elsewhere calls the law of opinion — that is, an account of what standards reputation-based enforcement owes its subjects, developed on its own terms rather than borrowed from criminal justice? Or should we expect no progress, because the category "punishment" is contested precisely insofar as the label carries normative freight, so that a neutral classification is unavailable in principle?

I am not asking for a verdict on cancel culture, a survey of the literature, or a bibliography — please reason from the positions as I have stated them and from the passage, and be explicit about where your diagnosis rests on intuition rather than argument. Something in the range of 900 to 1200 words.
