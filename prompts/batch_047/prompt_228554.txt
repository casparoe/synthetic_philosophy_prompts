Two friends of mine had a long row in the pub last week about karma, specifically about whether karma can work as a theory of moral desert -- i.e. whether it can be true that people get what they deserve because of it. I stayed out of it but I've been mulling it over since. Here's roughly how it went.

(1) Sam opened by saying desert needs a desert-*giver*: for someone to deserve something, there has to be an agent who judges, and an impersonal cosmic mechanism can't make anyone deserve anything any more than the weather can. Karma is just cause and effect dressed up in moral language.

(2) Priya said that's wrong because desert never needed a judge in the first place. When we say a cheat deserves to be caught even though nobody saw him, we're not imagining a cosmic referee -- we're saying the outcome would be fitting. Karma just says the universe is rigged so that fittingness actually cashes out, eventually.

(3) Sam came back with two points. First, if karma is an empirical claim about what actually happens to people, it's obviously false -- plenty of cruel people die comfortable. Second, and worse, the view commits you to saying every victim deserved it: the kid with leukemia, the tsunami victims, all of it. Any desert theory that implies that has gone wrong somewhere.

(4) Priya said he was conflating two things: karma *explains* why suffering lands where it does, but explanation isn't permission -- seeing someone suffer and shrugging because "they must have earnt it" would itself be bad karma, and anyway no living person can read anyone's karmic ledger, so no-one is ever in a position to actually apply the desert judgement. So the "blaming victims" objection targets a misuse of the doctrine, not the doctrine.

(5) Sam said "no-one can read the ledger" is doing all the work there -- a desert theory that can never be applied by anyone isn't a theory of desert, it's a promise that desert exists somewhere offstage. He also added that even if you grant the ledger, proportionality is a mystery: what's the exchange rate between, say, malicious gossip and a broken leg? A desert system with no conceivable proportionality principle is like a currency with no denominations.

(6) Priya's last move was about rebirth: she admitted the memory-loss across lives looks like a problem, but said we happily hold a 70-year-old responsible for what he did at 20 despite total cell turnover and a completely different personality, so continuity of memory clearly isn't required for desert -- and if it isn't required within one life, why demand it across lives?

So, can you adjudicate this properly, move by move? For each of the six moves, say whether it's basically right, basically wrong, or right-but-only-in-a-way-that-doesn't-do-what-its-speaker-needs, with a sentence or two on why. Then tell me who you think is closer to correct overall, and -- most importantly -- what *both* of them are missing. Is there a version of karma-as-desert that survives Sam's best points, and if not, is the failure specific to karma or does it infect other judge-free desert theories too?

Please write the answer in British English.
