During my sabbatical this year I decided to teach myself some Madhyamaka, mostly from secondary sources, and I've gotten stuck on the tetralemma. Background on me: I teach logic at a small college, and my comfort zone is non-classical systems -- many-valued logics (Łukasiewicz, Belnap's four-valued setup), paraconsistent and dialetheist stuff, intuitionism, the usual zoo. So when I read that Nāgārjuna considers, for a claim like "the self exists," the four options -- it exists, it doesn't exist, it both exists and doesn't, it neither exists nor doesn't -- and then rejects all four, my first reaction was: oh, that's just Belnap's four truth values (T, F, Both, Neither) plus a rejection of the whole framework. But the scholars I read insist it's NOT that, and when they try to say what else it could be, they get very hand-wavy very fast.

Here's what I want from you: translate the tetralemma, and the Madhyamaka rejection of all four horns, into my vocabulary as faithfully as you can -- and then give me the residue report. For each horn and for the rejection of each, what's the best formal rendering, what survives translation cleanly, what has to be distorted to fit, and what (if anything) has no expression in any logic I know? A few specific pressure points I care about: (1) Does the fourth horn just collapse into Belnap's N, and is rejecting it different from assigning N? (2) I gather the Indian tradition distinguishes two kinds of negation -- one that drags in a commitment to some alternative (like "not even" pushing you toward "odd") and one that's a bare refusal with no commitment attached, I think prasajya vs paryudasa is the terminology -- can that distinction be expressed in formal semantics at all, or does it live at the level of speech acts rather than truth values? (3) If all four horns are rejected, what stops "emptiness" from being just one more thesis that would get the same four-way treatment -- is the "emptiness of emptiness" move translatable as something like a type-level distinction or a fixed point, or is that me forcing it?

And then the verdict I actually care about: is the residue evidence that my logician's toolkit is missing something real -- that Madhyamaka is doing something with language that model-theoretic semantics genuinely can't capture -- or is it evidence that the position, once honestly translated, is alot less mysterious than the literature makes it sound? Argue for whichever you think is true, don't hedge.

At the end, give me a summary table with one row per horn plus rows for the rejection-of-all-four and the emptiness-of-emptiness move.
