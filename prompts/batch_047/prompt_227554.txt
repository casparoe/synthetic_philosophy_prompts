My translation studies reading group has been crawling through Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot all semester — the 1997 book where he collects dozens of English versions of Marot's tiny poem usually called Ma mignonne — and I keep losing the same fight. The rest of the group are content-firsters: a translation should carry over the sense, the affection, the gentle teasing, and if the three-syllable lines and rhyming couplets have to go, so be it, that's just the price of admission into English. I think that position quietly abandons the poem, but I arguement for it badly, so I want sparring practice.

Here's the setup. You play a committed, sharp content-first translator, the best version of the view, not a strawman. Your position: translation is the preservation of sense and effect under the resources of the target language; the formal features of the source are vehicles of the effect, not the effect itself, and when form and sense genuinely conflict, sense wins, because a reader who gets the warmth and the joke in plain English has received more of Marot than a reader who gets a padded, semantically bent rhyme-job that happens to scan in threes. You also genuinely believe English three-syllable rhymed verse reads as nursery-rhyme doggerel, so that slavishly copying the form actually *misrepresents* the effect Marot's French had on its first readers. You'll concede small points happily — form isn't free, dropping it is a real cost, this poem is unusually form-dependent, some of Hofstadter's own versions are charming — but never the main one: strict formal fidelity is a constraint on good translation, not a constituent of the thing translated. Stay in character, ask me pointed questions when my position goes mushy, and don't break character unless I type "timeout". My opening move: take the stretch of the poem where Marot warns the girl that if she stays sick too long she'll lose her rosy color and her plumpness — my group renders that as one flat English sentence and calls it a day, and I'm saying that rendering conveys everything except the poem. The poem IS the compression: Marot gets that whole warning out in a handful of tiny chiming lines you can say in about four seconds, and the speed and bounce and cuteness of it *is* the affection — a slow prose paraphrase of a get-well note isn't the same note, it's a summary of one. If your translation can't be read aloud at the same clip with the same lift, you haven't translated the poem, you've described it, and your "effect comes first" principle actually argues for my side, because the effect lives in the form here and nowhere else. Tell me why I'm wrong.
