So I've fallen down a rabbit hole of philosophy thought experiments lately — trolleys, famous violinists getting hooked up to you in a hospital bed, teleporters that scan you and rebuild you on Mars. Great fun. But I keep noticing something about how arguments using these actually go, and I can't figure out whether it's a known problem with a name or just me being difficult.

The pattern is this. Someone presents a thought experiment to get you to accept some principle, and the case is set up so the answer feels totally obvious. But then the obviousness seems to depend on details that, by the arguer's own standards, shouldn't matter at all. With the trolley thing, almost everyone says pull the lever to kill one instead of five, but swap it to pushing a large stranger off a footbridge and people flip — even though the person making the argument usually insists the numbers are all that count, and by that standard the cases are identical. With the teleporter, whether it feels like *you* survive seems to hinge on stuff like whether the original body is destroyed and how the story is paced, which feels like window dressing, not the point. And then the whole argument degenerates into bickering about which details are "essential" to the case, and nobody seems to have any rule for settling that.

So here's my question: is there an established term for this failure mode — where a thought experiment's persuasive punch comes from the dressing rather than from the principle it's supposedly testing, so an opponent can defuse it just by redescribing the case with different surface details? I know Dennett's "intuition pump" label, but that seems to be about the tool in general, not this specific weakness. And I don't think it's exactly a framing effect either, since the flip happens even when one person hears both versions side by side. If there's no name, coin one and define it so it's clearly distinct from those two, with a boundary case or two. Please answer as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, with no introduction or conclusion.
