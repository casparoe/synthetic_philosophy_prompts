The exam question was:

"Dennett describes both Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection and Turing's account of computation as 'strange inversions of reasoning.' Explain what a strange inversion of reasoning is, and show how Darwin's and Turing's ideas each count as one. Why did these ideas provoke so much resistance?"

Here's the student's answer:

"A strange inversion of reasoning is Dennett's term for the assumption, common before Darwin and Turing, that competence can only arise from comprehension -- that intelligence and design must flow from a prior, greater intelligence. Darwin inverted this by showing that mindless processes (random variation plus selection pressure) can generate the appearance of design without any designer at all. His theory showed that species gradually progress toward more and more perfect forms, which is part of why it was so threatening: it knocked humans off their pedestal as the pinnacle of creation.

Turing's inversion is parallel. Before him, people assumed you had to understand arithmetic in order to do it. The Turing machine shows that a device following simple mechanical rules can carry out any computation without understanding a thing -- competence without comprehension. This is what led Turing, in his 1950 paper, to argue that machines can genuinely think, since thinking is at bottom just computation.

Both ideas met resistance for the same deep reason: they eliminate the need for a 'first mind.' Paley's watchmaker argument depends on comprehension coming before competence, so Darwin's inversion undercuts it directly. And critics of AI resist Turing because they assume machines can't be conscious, but Turing's point is that consciousness is beside the point."
