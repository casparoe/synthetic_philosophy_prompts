Here is the exam question:

State Arrow's impossibility theorem precisely, including each of the conditions it places on a social welfare function and what each condition means. Then sketch the structure of one proof (a full formal proof is not required, but the key steps must be identified). Finally, describe one way of escaping the impossibility result by relaxing or modifying the framework, and assess whether the escape is philosophically satisfactory.

Here is the student's answer:

Arrow's impossibility theorem (1951) shows that no social welfare function can simultaneously satisfy a small set of seemingly reasonable conditions, given at least three alternatives and at least two individuals. A social welfare function takes a profile of individual preference orderings as input and outputs a single social preference ordering, which must itself be complete and transitive. The conditions are as follows. Unrestricted domain: the function must work for any logically possible profile of individual preferences, meaning that individuals may hold any ordering of the alternatives, including intransitive ones. Pareto (unanimity): if every individual strictly prefers a to b, then society must strictly prefer a to b. Independence of irrelevant alternatives: the social ranking of a against b must depend only on how individuals rank a against b, so that the introduction or removal of some third alternative c cannot reverse the social ranking of a and b. Non-dictatorship: there is no individual whose most-preferred alternative always wins regardless of everyone else's preferences. Arrow proved that no function satisfies all four conditions at once.

The classic way to see why is the pivotal voter argument. Partition the voters into two blocs and consider a sequence of profiles in which some alternative b moves from being universally worst to universally best, one voter at a time. By the Pareto condition, b starts ranked socially last and ends ranked socially first, so there must be a first voter whose switch lifts b off the bottom: the pivotal voter. One then shows that whenever this voter prefers any a to any c, society must rank a above c no matter what the blocs do, because independence of irrelevant alternatives lets us move b around in other voters' rankings without disturbing the relevant pairwise comparisons. Iterating this over all pairs shows the pivotal voter dictates every social ranking, violating non-dictatorship.

The Condorcet paradox illustrates the theorem from another angle: majority rule over the profile where voters rank abc, bca, and cab produces a cycle, which is a violation of independence of irrelevant alternatives, since the pairwise contest between a and b is influenced by where c sits in each voter's ranking.

The standard escape is to restrict the domain. If preferences are single-peaked over some shared ordering of the alternatives, majority rule yields a transitive social ordering, so Black's median voter result shows that Arrow's conditions can be jointly satisfied once unrestricted domain is dropped. Whether this is satisfactory depends on whether one thinks real electorates are plausibly single-peaked on the dimension that matters; for multi-issue politics the assumption is strained, so the escape may be more of a technical curiosity than a vindication of democratic aggregation.
