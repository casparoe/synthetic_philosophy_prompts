I'm co-hosting our undergrad philosophy society's discussion night, and the topic I've landed on is epiphenomenalism -- or rather, the self-defeat worry about it: if experiences cause nothing, then my uttering "this coffee tastes bitter" isn't caused by the taste, and my belief that I'm in pain isn't caused by the pain. So how could an epiphenomenalist ever know or even justifiably judge anything about their own experience? The audience is mostly second- and third-years who've done intro phil mind (they roughly know what qualia are, they've heard of zombies) but haven't read Chalmers or Jackson themselves. I don't want the session itself explained to me; I want a plan for how to run it. What has to be in place before the paradox even lands -- I'm guessing the causal closure intuition and a clean distinction between having an experience and judging that you have one, but tell me the actual dependency order. Pick one example to carry the whole thing (something where a judgment and an experience visibly share a common physical cause, without smuggling in Cartesian theater stuff) and say what it needs to have and avoid. Map the objections they'll raise in the order they'll raise them -- I expect "so you're saying experiences don't exist," "isn't this just the other-minds problem again," and "natural selection would fix our judgments" -- and where in the sequence each should get headed off. Also one question I can pose at the end to check they've grasped the paradox itself and not just concluded epiphenomenalism is silly, and what to explicitly bracket for the night (illusionism? panpsychism?). And if you think the paradox as I've framed it only bites certain versions of epiphenomenalism, or isn't really a paradox about judgment at all, say that up front rather than planning politely around a muddle.
