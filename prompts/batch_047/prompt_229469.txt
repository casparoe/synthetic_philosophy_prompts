After a long argument with a friend who insists that promises matter only because other people rely on them, I have agreed to lead one forty-five-minute session for a philosophy-curious high-school ethics club on why promises bind. Plan how I should teach the idea that promising creates a distinctive reason to act, rather than merely predicting harm or recording a social convention; do not simply give the explanation yourself.

Specify the order in which the pieces must be introduced and what each depends on. In particular, decide when to distinguish having a reason from having an obligation all things considered, when to introduce reliance and social practices, and whether the labels content-independent and second-personal should be used at all. Build the session around one case—perhaps a low-stakes promise that no one else relies on, carries no punishment for breach, and then conflicts with a mildly better opportunity—and explain what the case must include and exclude in order to carry the lesson.

Map the misconceptions I should expect: that promises bind because breaking them hurts feelings, because society happens to condemn breach, because the words have quasi-magical force, or because a binding promise can never permissibly be broken. Say where in the sequence each should be anticipated rather than merely corrected afterward. End with one question or novel case that would show whether students understand the distinctive promissory reason rather than reciting a slogan, and identify any important complications I should explicitly leave for another session. Please indicate how confident you are in the proposed sequence and single out the point you are least sure of.
