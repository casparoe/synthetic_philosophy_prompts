Design a decision scenario for an AI assistant's refusal policy in which two principles that ordinarily coincide deliver opposite verdicts, and in which the conflict cannot be dissolved by appealing to the user's incapacity.

The two principles:

1. The sovereignty principle: a competent adult user's request should be refused or overridden only when fulfilling it would impose non-consensual harm on third parties; what the user does to their own future is theirs to decide.

2. The settled-ends principle: the assistant may decline a request when fulfilling it would foreseeably frustrate the user's own settled, long-standing ends — even if the user currently insists — provided those ends were formed under conditions the user would now regard as uncoerced.

Specifications for the scenario:

- The user is an adult, sober, informed, and not in any acute psychological crisis. No minors, no guardianship questions, no illegality, and no third-party victims anywhere in the case.
- The request is for something the assistant could provide easily and immediately (information, drafting help, planning), so that refusal constitutes a genuine override rather than an incapacity dressed up as one.
- The user has already heard the assistant's concern once, has considered it, and has repeated the request. The divergence between the principles must arise at this second request, not at the first warning; the scenario must explain what changes between the two.
- The conflict must not turn on predictable regret alone. A person can foresee future regret and reasonably proceed anyway; design the case so that the settled-ends principle bites for a reason stronger than anticipated regret, and state explicitly what that reason is.
- Nothing may depend on the assistant possessing surveillance-grade knowledge of the user's private life; its grounds for judgment must be available from the conversation itself.

After presenting the scenario, do the following. First, give each principle's verdict and reconstruct the reasoning by which it reaches that verdict, without stipulating either. Second, identify the single structural feature of the case that forces the two principles apart, and say what happens to each verdict if that feature is removed — does the case collapse into ordinary, unobjectionable service, or into the kind of paternalism nearly everyone rejects? Third, state the strongest objection a defender of the sovereignty principle would raise against treating your case as a genuine counterexample to their view, and assess whether the case survives it. Fourth, indicate which feature of the scenario, if any, generalizes to real deployment policy, and which features you had to engineer in and would not expect to occur naturally.
