A friend of mine, who is generally reliable on intellectual history, gave me the following summary of Einstein's 1949 essay "Why Socialism?":

'Einstein's argument is that capitalism's essential defect is that production is driven by profit rather than by need, which generates unemployment, wasted labor, and recurrent economic crises, and that the remedy is social ownership of the means of production combined with a planned economy that distributes work and guarantees a livelihood to everyone able to work. He treats the Soviet experiment as having established that such a planned economy is workable, so the only remaining difficulty is political rather than economic: safeguarding individual rights alongside planning, which he regards as a manageable problem of institutional design. He also argues that because the economic system molds human character, socialism would in time produce a less competitive and more cooperative kind of person, so there is no need to worry much about how socialist institutions should actually be structured.'

Something about this feels off to me. I have a vague memory that the essay ends on a noticeably less confident note than the summary suggests, and that Einstein is careful at some points in ways this gloss flattens. But my friend usually gets things right, and the summary does sound like things Einstein plausibly believed.

Could you audit this summary for me? Go through it claim by claim and tell me which parts a careful reader of the essay would accept as accurate, which parts state Einstein's view more strongly than he states it himself, and which parts reverse or omit something he actually says. In particular, I would like to know: (1) whether Einstein's diagnosis of capitalism's flaws is rendered fairly, including what role, if any, he assigns to the way education and economic dependence shape individuals; (2) whether his attitude toward the Soviet Union and toward the practical feasibility of planning is characterized correctly, or whether the summary attributes to him a settled optimism he does not express; (3) what questions Einstein himself concedes he cannot answer, and how those concessions bear on the summary's closing claim that institutional design is not worth worrying about; and (4) whether, once the summary is corrected, my friend's overall takeaway -- that the essay is a confident brief for central planning -- survives, or whether the essay is better read as something more tentative. Where the summary is wrong, please explain not just that it is wrong but what the accurate position is and why someone intelligent might nonetheless have arrived at the misreading.
