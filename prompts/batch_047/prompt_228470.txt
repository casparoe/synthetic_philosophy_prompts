I am a PhD student in philosophy working on collective intentionality, and a chapter of my dissertation keeps running aground on the discursive dilemma -- the fact that a group whose members each reason consistently can end up with inconsistent collective judgments depending on whether you aggregate their views on the premises or on the conclusions. I have a tentative principle for when each aggregation method is appropriate, and I would like it stress-tested against my case intuitions rather than against the abstract impossibility results, which I already know. My tentative principle is this: a collective body should aggregate at the level of conclusions when it must act as one and answer to outsiders for the decision itself, and at the level of premises when the reasons, not just the decision, will do downstream work -- for instance, when they will guide future cases or constrain other bodies.

Here are my cases and my gut verdicts. Case 1: a three-member arbitration panel that issues only an award, no reasons. The contract is enforceable only if there was both a valid offer and a communicated acceptance. Arbitrator 1 finds offer but no acceptance; Arbitrator 2 finds acceptance but no offer; Arbitrator 3 finds both. My verdict: the award should go to the defendant, since two of three conclude the contract is unenforceable, and it would be perverse to enforce a contract that a majority of the panel thinks is invalid. Case 2: a five-member tenure committee where a candidate must clear a bar on research, teaching, and service. Each member fails the candidate on exactly one criterion, distributed so that each criterion individually passes by majority, but not a single member thinks the candidate clears all three. My verdict: no tenure, even though a criterion-by-criterion tally would grant it. Case 3: an appellate court with the exact vote pattern of Case 1, but this court must publish a reasoned opinion that will govern future litigants. Here my gut flips: I feel the court should vote on the premises and let the conclusion follow -- meaning the plaintiff wins -- because an opinion announcing the contract is unenforceable while containing no majority view on why seems worse than an uncomfortable outcome. Case 4, the one I keep going back and forth on: a three-member hiring committee where the overall vote is 2-1 to hire, but the candidate loses on every published criterion by majority, because the two supporters each weigh the criteria differently and think one outstanding strength compensates. Sometimes the bottom-line vote seems decisive to me; sometimes the unanimous-on-each-criterion rejection seems like it should be.

Please do the following. First, test my tentative principle against all four cases: does it actually deliver my verdicts, or does it merely appear to because I have described the cases tendentiously? In particular, press on whether the distinction I draw between Case 1 and Case 3 -- the presence or absence of downstream reason-giving -- is really what is moving my intuitions, or whether something else is doing the work (the stakes, the one-shot versus repeated character of the body, the fact that I happen to like the defendant's outcome). Second, repair the principle or replace it with a small set of precisely stated candidate principles, identifying which case motivates each clause. Third, apply your best candidate to cases I have not considered -- say, a criminal jury whose members agree a defendant is guilty but disagree about which of two acts constitutes the crime, and a central bank committee that votes both on a forecast and on the policy that depends on it. Finally, be honest about which of my four verdicts, if any, cannot be accommodated by a single clean principle, and tell me which one you think I should give up if forced to choose -- including a resolution of Case 4 rather than a shrug at it.
