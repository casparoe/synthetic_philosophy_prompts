You are a philosopher of history with experience coaching graduate students through conference presentations. Think carefully, and begin by briefly brainstorming what this particular audience cares about before drafting the prep sheet.

The situation: I am a history PhD candidate giving a forty-minute conference paper at a general philosophy-of-history workshop. The audience will be roughly a third working historians (mostly empirically minded, allergic to theory-talk), a third philosophers who take Hayden White's tropology seriously, and a third Carr-style moderates who think the objectivity debate was settled decades ago in Carr's favor. No Ranke specialists, but several people who know the secondary literature well.

My thesis: I defend what I call the "constrained narration" view. Roughly, Ranke was right that the past is fixed and recoverable in outline, and wrong that the historian can approach it without a shaping hand; White was right that every historical account is emplotted, and wrong (on my reading) that the choice between emplotments is essentially aesthetic or political. My claim is that the documentary record constrains narrative form the way a source text constrains a translation: many renderings are possible, but some are simply mistranslations, and we can say which without pretending there is one uniquely correct version. I use this to argue that objectivity in history is real but comparative -- a property of rankings between accounts, not a property of any single account's correspondence to the past.

Prepare my Q&A prep sheet:

1. The seven or eight objections I should actually expect from this mixed audience, ordered by how likely they are to come up and how much damage each would do to the paper. Phrase each objection the way that audience member would phrase it -- the empiricist historian's version of the underdetermination worry sounds different from the Whitean's version, and I need to recognize both.
2. For each objection, a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, plus a note on where that reply is weakest and what follow-up I should brace for.
3. What I should concede up front in the talk itself to take the wind out of the two most predictable attacks, and what I must not concede or the thesis collapses.
4. The strongest version of the objection that my translation analogy smuggles in the objectivity it claims to explain (since translation equivalence is itself contested), and whether I should address it preemptively or wait for it.
5. The one question I am hoping nobody asks.

Be concrete about the audience: a historian who has never read Metahistory will not object in White's vocabulary, so don't put it in their mouth. Where my position has a genuine soft spot, tell me it is a soft spot rather than inventing a reply that papers over it.
