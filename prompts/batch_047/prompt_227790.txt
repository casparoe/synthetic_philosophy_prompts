I am at work on a novel whose central marriage rests on a lie: before the wedding, the husband concealed from his wife that he has a child from an earlier relationship whom he secretly supports. The book's moral architecture depends on treating her consent to the marriage as no consent at all — consent obtained by deception is void, since a choice made in ignorance of the relevant facts is not really a choice. The structural problem I face is how far this voidness extends. If she never validly consented to the marriage, it would seem to follow that she never validly consented to anything premised on it: the joint mortgage, the relocation abroad, the intimacies of married life. My instinct is to write her as wronged in all of these respects at once, the original deception contaminating every downstream agreement like a poisoned well. But I want the architecture to be rigorous rather than merely intuitive. Given that deception voids consent, is there a principled way to fix the radius of the contamination — must I treat all of her subsequent choices within the marriage as unconsented, and if not, am I not conceding that consent procured by deception can be valid after all?
