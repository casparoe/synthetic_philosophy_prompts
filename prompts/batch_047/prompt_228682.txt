I play cello in a mediocre community orchestra and read philosophy on my phone during lunch breaks, and last week the two hobbies crashed into each other and I can't sort it out. Some background first. My relationship of six years ended in January, and my coping mechanism was basically sad music on repeat, Mahler slow movements, that kind of thing. Here's what I keep noticing: the grief in the music is completely vivid to me, sometimes MORE vivid than my own grief, but it doesn't hurt the way my own grief hurts. It's like the feeling gets taken out of me and set on a shelf where I can walk around it and look at it from all sides. Meanwhile a friend telling me a sad story at a bar, or a realistic breakup scene in a film, those genuinely hurt. And it's not about intensity, because the music is often the most intense thing in my week; the intensity is somehow welcome. Second instance: at rehearsal we were working through something stormy and furious right when I was panicking about a work deadline, and instead of winding me tighter it settled me, as if my agitation had been handed over to the viola section and no longer needed my participation. This isn't numbing and it isn't distraction, I'm fully inside the feeling the whole time. And I don't think it's catharsis either, at least as I understand that word, because nothing gets discharged or purged; the longing is all still sitting there when the movement ends.

So then I'm reading Schopenhauer, The World as Will and Idea, the old Haldane and Kemp translation, and in the last section of the third book (§52 of vol. 1) he describes more or less exactly this: he says music reproduces all the movements of our innermost being but entirely without reality, floating past us like a paradise that's familiar yet eternally remote, and cut off from pain. Which thrilled me for about a paragraph and then frustrated me, because as far as I can tell he just states the fact and moves on. Worse, it seems to actively clash with his own setup: a few pages earlier he argues music isn't a picture of things at all but a direct copy of the will itself. But if stormy music is a copy of striving itself, shouldn't it feed my striving rather than quiet it? Why would contemplating the will calm the will instead of inflaming it? He owes an explanation there and I can't find one.

So my actual question: does this specific trick have a name — the way a felt emotion keeps its full character but loses its sting when it's presented to you rather than undergone by you — either in aesthetics generally or in the Schopenhauer literature specifically? How is it different from catharsis, from the ordinary way time dulls things, and from just getting absorbed in a hobby? And if there's no established name for it, what would you call it, and where are its edges — does it cover tragic plays and sad films and paintings of shipwrecks, or is it something special about wordless music? I'd honestly rather have the careful carving than the label, so if the answer is "nothing fits exactly," tell me what comes closest and where each candidate breaks down.
