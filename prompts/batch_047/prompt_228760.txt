I teach a second-year philosophy of mind seminar, 14 students, one 80-minute session, and we're doing the color phi phenomenon and Dennett's Orwellian vs. Stalinesque revisions. Design me an activity where the students actually run the illusion on themselves -- two dots flashed in sequence, they see the moving dot change color halfway along its path, apparently before the second dot even shows up -- and then get pressed on *when* they experienced the change until the question starts to feel broken. I don't want to lecture first; they should be stuck in the timing puzzle before they hear either label.

Include: the practical setup (what intervals/distances make the illusion land on a laptop or projector, plus a no-tech fallback with cards if the projector dies), a couple of variations that sharpen the problem -- like catch trials where the second dot never comes, or trials where the second dot's position is settled by a coin flip only after the first flash -- and some small-stakes element, maybe they predict in writing what they'll be able to report about their own timing and then discover they can't. Then a structured bit where half the group has to defend the "your memory got rewritten afterwards" story and half defends the "the experience was pre-constructed before it reached you" story, using only what the demo gave them, and they should notice both fit the exact same first-person data.

Also: the debrief that maps each moment of the activity onto the two revision pictures and onto the stronger claim that there might be no fact of the matter about which one happened, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (someone won't see the illusion, someone will think the coin flip proves precognition, etc.), and an honest note about what the activity can't deliver -- e.g. it can't stop students feeling that there *must* be an answer to when the experience happened, which is sort of the whole trap.
