Please give your actual credence in the following proposition: some lexical threshold version of negative utilitarianism is the correct axiology for at least a substantial domain of moral decision-making — that is, there exists a level of suffering severe enough that no quantity of pleasure or happiness, however large, could morally compensate for a single token of it, while below that threshold pleasures and sufferings trade off against one another on a roughly cardinal scale. I do not want a survey of positions; I want a number between 0 and 1, followed by the derivation that produces it.

Decompose the credence into the sub-questions on which it depends, assigning a rough probability to each along with a sentence or two of justification. At minimum, please address: (i) whether pleasure and suffering are commensurable at all — whether there is a single hedonic dimension along which they occupy negative and positive positions, or whether the apparent trade-offs we make in everyday life are better explained in some non-hedonic currency; (ii) whether there is a phenomenological discontinuity anywhere in the intensity range of suffering — whether agony differs from ordinary pain in kind rather than merely in degree, such that a lexical break is tracking something real rather than legislating one; (iii) whether a threshold, if it exists, can be located in a non-arbitrary way, or whether every proposed cut-off collapses under sorites-style pressure toward either full lexical priority for all suffering or full commensurability; and (iv) whether the counterintuitive verdicts the view delivers — for instance, that a world containing arbitrarily many lives of near-maximal bliss, punctuated once per century by a single hour of suffering so intense that its bearer would accept immediate annihilation to end it, ranks below a world of mildly contented lives with no such episodes — should be read as a reductio of the view or as a bullet worth biting. Add any further sub-question you think the credence genuinely depends on, with its own probability.

Then combine the terms, state your final number, identify the sub-question to which the result is most sensitive, and tell me what evidence or argument — empirical (for instance, from the psychophysics or neuroscience of pain) or conceptual — would move that term, and hence the final credence, the most.
