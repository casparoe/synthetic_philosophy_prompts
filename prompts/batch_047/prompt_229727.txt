I chair the ethics panel of a hospital that performs staged neural replacement for patients with degenerative brain disease: failing tissue is swapped for synthetic components in sessions spread over months or years, with the patient awake and functional between sessions. My panel must certify, before each stage, that proceeding still counts as treating the same patient rather than as gradually replacing one person with another -- and at the end, whether the finished patient legally and morally is the person who enrolled. Philosophers disagree about this (psychological continuity, bodily continuity, and the worry that only *gradual, causally dependent* replacement preserves anyone), but I cannot hand my panel a literature review. Please turn whatever is most defensible in that debate into a procedure the panel can actually run before each session: the questions to ask in order (about rate of replacement, whether new components take over function because the old tissue shaped them, interruption and rollback options, the patient's own testimony, and what we do when memory and personality measures diverge), what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure halts with a refusal, and where it must hand the decision back to the patient or a court because the question is one of values, not facts. Then apply it to three cases: a five-year, two-percent-per-month replacement; the same total replacement compressed into six weeks; and a case where the neural measures say continuity is intact but the patient reports feeling like a successor to herself. Include at least one application where the procedure delivers a verdict my panel will find uncomfortable, and say what the procedure cannot settle at all. Finally, state what consideration -- philosophical or empirical -- would lead you to rebuild the procedure differently, and where its translation of the theory into steps is most likely to have distorted the theory.
