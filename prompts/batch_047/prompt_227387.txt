Next month I'm giving a talk in our department's logic seminar defending a thesis I've been developing: the so-called Löbian obstacle to self-trust — the family of results, flowing from Löb's theorem, showing that a sufficiently strong formal system cannot prove the soundness of an equally strong successor system, and hence that a formal reasoner cannot establish, within its own logic, that its self-modifications or future selves are trustworthy — has been effectively dissolved, rather than merely evaded, by recent work on probabilistic and bounded forms of reflection. My rough line of argument is that the obstacle arises only when trust is cashed out as formal provability of a soundness schema; once one permits a reasoner to assign calibrated probabilities to the statements it will later come to believe, or to trust an idealised limit of its own reasoning process (as in work on logical induction and self-referential probabilistic trust), diagonalisation loses its grip, and what remains of the obstacle is an artefact of an unnecessarily strict standard of trust. I'll concede that these constructions shift assumptions into the metatheory, but I'll argue every trust notion does that, and the question is only whether the shift buys you genuine self-modification capacity.

The audience will be mostly proof theorists and type theorists — people entirely at home with derivability conditions, reflection principles, ordinal analysis, and the difference between uniform and local reflection — plus one philosopher of mathematics who is friendly to formal epistemology and one visitor from an AI safety research group. Crucially, there will be almost no machine learning people, so I don't need prep for "but neural networks don't prove things"; I need prep for people who will press the formalisation itself. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to the thesis if I handle it badly. Phrase each objection the way a proof theorist would put it at the blackboard, not the way a journalist would — I expect things like whether the escape routes have merely changed the subject from proof-theoretic trust to something else, whether my gloss on the reflection results respects the quantifier structure, and whether probabilistic soundness claims quietly assume the consistency I'm forbidden to prove. For each objection, give me a two-minute reply I can deliver, and a candid note on where that reply is soft and what I should concede rather than defend.

Please also include: the objection I'm hoping nobody asks (and what I'd say if they do); one or two points where the proof theorists and the AI safety visitor will want opposite things from me, and how to answer without losing either; and a short list of terminological landmines — words I should avoid because this audience hears them differently than I intend. Write the whole thing in British English, and keep it usable as an actual prep sheet rather than a miniature essay.
