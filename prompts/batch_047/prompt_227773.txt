I retired last year after forty years as a research chemist and, to keep the brain from ossifying, joined a philosophy reading group that meets at the public library. Last month we were doing rescue cases, specifically the one where you're at the helm of the only boat: one swimmer is clinging to a rock to the north, five are clinging to a rock to the south, and you can reach only one rock before the tide does its work. Most of the room said save the five, obviously. Then one member, a retired lawyer, said flatly: the numbers shouldn't count -- flip a coin, that's the only way to give the one a fair shake, anything else treats him as expendable. It kind of shut the room down and we moved on, but it has bothered me ever scince.

Here's my problem. In my old life, counting was the whole game. Nobody flips a coin between a procedure that kills one lab animal and an otherwise identical one that kills five. So I can't even see what the slogan could mean that isn't either obviously wrong or obviously toothless. But the lawyer is not a stupid man, so I assume there's something precise underneath it.

Could you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses that "the numbers don't count, flip a coin" might be expressing? Here are my best guesses, to show you the level of precision I'm after: (a) a fairness thesis -- each endangered person is owed an equal chance of rescue, and weighted counting violates that; (b) a no-aggregate-victim thesis -- five deaths isn't worse *for anyone* than one death, since no single person suffers "five deaths," and only what's worse for someone matters; (c) an anti-instrumentalization thesis -- counting treats the lone swimmer as a mere resource to be traded off; (d) a modest thesis -- numbers aren't irrelevant, they just can't be decisive on their own; (e) a skeptical thesis -- "the worse outcome" is meaningless simpliciter, only "worse for so-and-so" exists, so there's no fact of the matter for counting to track. Feel free to correct my formulations or add readings I've missed.

For each reading, tell me: is it a platitude, a live substantive position, or just false? Does it actually support the coin flip, or something weaker? And stress-test each one by scaling -- does the reading survive when it's one swimmer versus a hundred, or versus the entire population of a city? Finally, which reading was my lawyer friend most likely invoking, given that he used it to end the discussion rather than open one?
