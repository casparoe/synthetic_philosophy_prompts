I don’t have any academic background, but I’ve been thinking about “commitment races” and got stuck on a distinction I can’t quite articulate.

Imagine competing actors — states, companies, or AI agents — can lock themselves into future policies in ways others can verify, like publishing a cryptographic commitment to a strategy or building a successor they genuinely can’t control later. Usually a commitment race is described as everyone rushing to lock in first. But suppose one actor commits to a policy saying, roughly: I will cooperate with any actor whose verifiable commitment meets these conditions, and punish or ignore everyone else. Others then rush not merely to commit first, but to committ in a form that the first actor’s policy will recognize.

Please explain what the conceptual issue is here. In particular, distinguish between:

- an ordinary credible commitment,
- a race to make such commitments first,
- a race to develop commitment-detection and verification technology, and
- a “meta-commitment” about how to respond to every possible future commitment.

Does the meta-commitment create a stable equilibrium, or does it just invite another race over whose meta-commitment gets recognized? Would better verification make the situation safer by preventing bluffing, or more dangerous by making irreversible commitments easier and faster? And is a commitment to cooperate with cooperators actually cooperative, or can it function as coercion by defining what counts as cooperation? A small made-up numerical example would help.
