Right, here's something that's been bugging me for ages and I'd love a proper answer rather than pub chat. I'm a novelist — crime mostly, with the odd bit of speculative stuff — and I keep tieing myself in knots over when two stories are pulling "the same trick". You'd think it would be obvious, but my own judgements are all over the shop. So here are my verdicts on some cases, and then what I actually want from you.

My verdicts:

1. Take a 1930s country-house whodunnit where the detective turns out to be the murderer, and take a space opera where the ship's computer investigating the sabotage is itself the saboteur. Wildly different books on the surface, but I'm convinced it's the same trick: the thing channelling the investigation to you is poisoning it.

2. Now take two books both set in a snowed-in manor with a dead peer in the library. In one the solution hinges on a doctored will, in the other on a secret identical twin. Identical dressing, but to me these aren't remotely the same trick.

3. A novel where the narrator turns out to be mad and has imagined half the events, versus one where the narrator is perfectly sane but has been lying to the reader to protect someone. These feel like different tricks to me, even though a mate of mine insists they're both just "unreliable narrator" and that's that.

4. But then: a story where a prophecy comes true because fate is genuinely real, versus one where it comes true because some schemer has quietly engineered it to. The causal machinery is completely different — destiny versus manipulation — and yet I can't help feeling these are the same old "prophecy fulfilled" business. Which sits awkwardly with verdict 3, I do realise.

So: what's the principle? What makes two twists the same mechanism, given that sameness clearly can't live in the surface trappings (verdict 2 kills that off) and can't just be a matter of sharing a genre label either? If there's one clean principle that delivers all four of my verdicts, give it to me — stated properly and precisely, with the case that motivates each clause of it. If there isn't, tell me which verdict has to go and why. My suspicion is the trouble is between 3 and 4, since I seem to want the *cause* of the dodginess to matter in one case and not in the other, but maybe you can see a way through that I can't. Either way, be honest about which of my instincts your principle can't accomodate.

Then two further cases to test it on, neither of which I've taken a view on. First: an "inverted" mystery where you're shown the murderer up front and the pleasure is watching the detective catch up — is that the same mechanism as a whodunnit run backwards, or something else entirely? Second: can a red herring in one book share a mechanism with the genuine solution in another, or does the narrative role (decoy versus answer) change the mechanism itself?

Oh, and British English in your answer please — I'll be reading bits of this out at my writers' group in Sheffield and they'll take the mickey out of any stray American spellings.
