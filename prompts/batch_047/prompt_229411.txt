I am a novelist by trade, and next month I have been asked to give the closing address at a small conference that brings together working fiction writers with philosophers and scientists of consciousness. The audience will be split roughly in half between the two groups, and I want to speak to both without patronizing either.

My subject is an idea that has quietly unsettled me for years, because it seems to cut at the root of my craft. In his essay on "quining qualia," Daniel Dennett describes two coffee tasters for a coffee company, Chase and Sanborn. One of them reports that the coffee tastes just as it always has but that he has somehow lost his pleasure in it; the other reports that his appreciation is unchanged but that the taste itself is no longer what it was. Dennett's point is that neither man — nor any conceivable test, from inside or outside — can settle which description is right: whether the taste changed and the reactions stayed fixed, or the reactions changed and the taste stayed fixed. And if we cannot in principle separate "the taste itself" from our whole complex of judgments, memories, and dispositions about it, then the taste itself, conceived as a pure, private, ineffable quale, may never have been a coherent notion at all.

Here is why this matters to me. Novelists routinely speak as though fiction's deepest achievement is to give readers access to what it is like to be another person — the redness of someone's red, the particular flavor of a grief. If Dennett is right that there is no separable, well-defined "what it is like" standing apart from a person's reactions, judgments, and self-interpretations, then either the novelist's entire self-image is a delusion, or the novelist's craft was never really about qualia in the first place. I want to argue the second: that good fiction has always worked in exactly the territory Dennett says is all there is — the tangle of reaction, memory, judgment, and redescription — and that the Chase and Sanborn case is, in fact, something close to a description of what a novelist does all day when building a character.

Please write this speech for me, about ten minutes at a natural speaking pace. It should take Dennett's argument seriously rather than dismissing it — the philosophers in the room will notice if I strawman him — but it may push back where pushback is honest. The central turn of the speech should be the move I've sketched: from the threat to the novelist's self-understanding to the recognition that the craft was never committed to what Dennett attacks. Use the coffee tasters as the speech's recurring spine, returning to them at least once near the end.

One discipline I want observed: Dennett's conclusion is contested. Many philosophers who know this material well hold that qualia are real and that the thought experiment shows, at most, something about the limits of our concepts or our access, not that there is nothing it is like to taste coffee. The speech should not present Dennett's eliminativist verdict as the settled consensus of the field, nor my novelist's reconciliation as obviously correct — it should mark, in language an audience can feel rather than merely be told, which claims are widely accepted and which are live disputes. After the speech itself, append a short note — a few sentences, not part of the spoken text — flagging any places where you had to lean on a contested reading of Dennett or of his critics, so that I can decide for myself whether I'm comfortable saying those lines aloud.
