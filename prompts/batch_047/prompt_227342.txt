I'm in a graduate seminar on applied ethics, and we've spent the last three weeks on trolley cases and the doctrine of double effect. For our final session each of us has to submit a short "position statement" that the others will try to break, so I'd like a stress test before I hand mine in. Here's where I've landed:

(1) In the standard switch case, diverting the trolley onto the side track where it will kill one person is permissible; in the footbridge case, pushing the large man off the bridge to stop the trolley is impermissible. I regard both verdicts as close to non-negotiable data points.

(2) The best explanation of the contrast in (1) is the doctrine of double effect: in the switch case the death is a merely foreseen side effect of diverting the trolley, whereas in the footbridge case the man's death (or at least his being struck) is intended as a means, and intending harm as a means is what's prohibited.

(3) In the loop variant — where the side track loops back to the main track, and the runaway trolley is only stopped from returning to kill the five because the large man's body on the side track blocks it — diverting is still permissible, just as in the plain switch case. The added causal complexity doesn't change my verdict at all.

(4) I'm deeply suspicious of saving moral principles by redescribing actions at a finer grain. If someone defends a verdict by insisting that "intending that the man's body stop the trolley" is a genuinely different intention from "intending his death," I think they're playing games with the closeness of descriptions, and I don't think the distinction between those two intentions is morally real.

(5) Relatedly, I don't think the permissibility of what someone does should turn on their private mental states. Two agents who do the same thing, with the same foreseeable results, are equally justified or unjustified; what they happened to be aiming at "on the inside" is a matter for assessing their character, not the act.

(6) I'm not a consequentialist — I accept that there are constraints on harming — but my reason for diverting in the switch case is simply that one death is better than five, and I think that reason is a perfectly good one to act on.

What I want from you is an audit, not a lecture on trolleyology. Tell me which of these commitments genuinely pull against each other and which only look like they do, and order any real tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, say precisely which commitment has to go or be weakened, and what that revision costs the rest of the package. If there's a single change that cleans up the most problems, identify it — but I haven't decided which of these I'm most attached to, so don't assume you know which one I'll want to sacrifice.
