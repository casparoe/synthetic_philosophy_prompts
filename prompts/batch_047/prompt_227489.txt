I have no academic training in philosophy or political theory, but some time ago I came across a free online copy of Kant's 1795 essay Perpetual Peace, in the old Campbell Smith translation, and one of its lesser-discussed passages has been nagging at me ever since. In the fourth of his preliminary articles for a lasting peace among states, Kant holds that states should not pile up debt in connection with their dealings with one another. His reasoning, as I understand it, is roughly twofold: a credit system gives governments an easy, almost invisible way to finance war without asking their peoples to feel its cost up front; and the accumulation of financial reserves is itself a kind of weapon, one whose mere existence alarms neighbouring states and can provoke the very conflict it was meant to prepare for. Money, in other words, is not neutral: it is one of the instruments of power, and treating it as harmless bookkeeping is a mistake.

Here is my puzzle. Kant wrote before anything like modern economic statecraft existed. Today, the standard response of the Western democracies to aggression is not (in the first instance) military: it is financial. Central-bank reserves are frozen; banks are cut off from international payment-messaging systems; exports of strategic goods are banned; third countries are pressured to comply; and governments borrow heavily and routinely for defence as a matter of ordinary fiscal policy. All of this seems to sit squarely in the territory Kant was marking out, yet I cannot tell which side of his line it falls on.

So: what would Kant have made of sanctions and financial warfare? I can see the case that he would condemn them as exactly the abuse he warned against — wealth and credit deployed as weapons of coercion, turning commerce into an extension of war by other means, and (in the case of reserve freezes) treating another state's treasure as a spoil. But I can also see the case that he would welcome them, at least in some forms: they are, after all, a way for a federation of law-governed republics to coerce an aggressor without shedding blood, and Kant clearly hoped that mutual commercial entanglement would make war irrational — which suggests he might accept deliberately cutting those ties as a lawful, temporary punishment, provided it is done publicly, by a league of states, and under something resembling international law rather than unilaterally by the strongest power. Relatedly, does his stricture against war debt catch the modern practice of ordinary peacetime borrowing for defence, or only debt contracted specifically to fund aggression?

I would like a careful answer that does three things: first, reconstructs what the fourth preliminary article is actually doing within Kant's overall scheme, so the extrapolation rests on his reasons rather than on the letter of the rule; second, gives a best honest guess at his verdict on each of the modern practices above, distinguishing cases where his text genuinely guides us from cases where we are merely speculating in his name; and third, notes where Kant's framework might simply be outmatched by facts he could not have anticipated — for instance, that modern finance is so deeply integrated that freezing reserves is itself experienced by its target as an act of war, which rather undercuts the hope that economic pressure is a peaceful alternative to one. Please write your answer in British English.
