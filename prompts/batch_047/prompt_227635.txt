A friend and I were arguing about zombies (the philosophy kind) over beers and she walked me into a corner I still can't think my way out of. Here's the argument as she ran it on me, cleaned up:

(1) A creature physically and functionally identical to me, down to the last neuron, but with no experience going on inside -- no redness of red, no felt ache, nothing -- is at least conceivable. I can describe it in as much detail as you like without contradicting myself.
(2) Whatever distinguishes me from this zombie twin makes zero difference to anything either of us says, writes, or does, because by construction the zombie's behavior, including all its speech, is physically identical to mine.
(3) My beliefs and reports about my own consciousness -- including the belief I have right now that I'm conscious -- are part of that speech and behavior, or at least are produced by the very same brain processes that produce it.
(4) So my zombie twin also sincerely believes and reports that it's conscious, and it does so for exactly the same underlying reasons I do.
(5) If a belief held for those reasons can be false (and the zombie's is false, since it has no experience at all), then being held for those reasons doesn't make a belief knowledge. The process churns out the same "I'm conscious" verdict whether or not it's true, so it can't be trusted on this topic.
(6) So my belief that I'm conscious isn't knowledge either. I stand in exactly the same epistemic position as the zombie, who is dead wrong about itself.
(7) But I know I'm conscious. If I know anything at all, I know that. If I could be wrong about this the way the zombie is, then the one thing I was supposed to be certain of turns out to be a coin flip.

Conclusion she drew: the conceivability of zombies seems to prove that nobody can know they're conscious, which is absurd -- so the zombie idea must refute itself somewhere.

If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (1) and (7) than any of the middle steps, but I genuinely can't locate the move that breaks. My suspicion is something slips between (4) and (6) -- the zombie "believing for the same reasons" bit feels load-bearing in a way I can't articulate. So: which premise or step do you think I should reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it actually cost me (i.e. what else am I now committed to)? Please walk through the obvious escape routes, including any that keep both (1) and (7), and don't just tell me the one you'd pick without pricing the others.

Also, if the whole argument as I've set it up is confused -- if I'm smuggling something into "conceivable" or "same reasons," or if (7) is doing something sneaky -- say that plainly rather than politely playing along with a broken setup.
