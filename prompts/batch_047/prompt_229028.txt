There's this fight that's been grinding on in wild-animal-welfare circles for well over a decade and I keep tripping over it, so I want someone to tell me what kind of fight it actually is. Roughly: one camp argues that most wild animals live short brutal lives — massive juvenile mortality, parasitism, starvation, predation, the whole r-selection picture — and that for many of these lives the bad plausibly outweighs the good, so since habitat destruction is one of the biggest levers on total wild animal populations that exists, and it's already happening at scale, shrinking habitat might actually reduce total suffering. The other camp finds this horrifying and replies that wild lives contain plenty of positive welfare we're ignoring, that we have no real evidence about hedonic balances in the wild, and that something of value (biodiversity, flourishing, whatever) gets lost when you reduce ecosystems to a sum of pains. Both sides cite the same handful of arguments back and forth and nothing ever moves.

My hypothesis, which I'm not attached to: the debate looks empirical (what are wild lives actually like, hedonically?) but it's really about aggregation and measurability — about wheter "the hedonic balance of a life" is even a well-defined quantity you could in principle measure, let alone sum across trillions of lives, and both sides quietly assume it is (one says it's net negative, the other says it's net positive or unknowable). On this reading the stall isn't waiting on better ecology data at all, it's waiting on someone to say what would even count as evidence that a typical wild rabbit's life is net negative. But I also can't rule out the duller hypothesis that it's a pure values dispute (does only suffering matter, or do flourishing and ecosystems matter independently?) wearing an empirical costume.

So diagnose it. Is this a factual disagreement, a conceptual one about what welfare is and wheter it aggregates, a values disagreement, or three different arguments stacked in a trench coat? What exactly is each side committed to that the other denies — like, spell out the load-bearing assumptions on each side. And given the diagnosis, what would progress even look like: a specific kind of study, a distinction nobody's drawn yet, a change of question? Or is this one of those debates where nothing could settle it because the camps are answering different questions under one heading? I want the diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of positions, and be honest if the answer is "this is unfixable and here's why."
