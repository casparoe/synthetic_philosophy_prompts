After forty years as an experimental psychophysicist — a career spent on sensory thresholds, just-noticeable differences, and the formalisms (semiorders, interval orders, tolerance relations) that we use precisely because indiscriminability fails to be transitive — I have retired and, like many retirees, wandered into philosophy. I am currently reading the value-theory literature on spectrum arguments and the claim that 'all things considered better than' can be intransitive: Temkin's and Rachels's arguments, and the sequential-choice version of the problem in Quinn's self-torturer puzzle. I keep having the reaction that my own field settled a version of this question decades ago, and I would like that reaction tested rather than indulged.

Please act as a professional philosopher with genuine expertise in value theory who is also conversant with the mathematical theory of measurement. Think carefully. Before composing the main answer, briefly sketch for yourself — and show me — what you take to be the hardest points of contact between the two vocabularies, so that I can see where the difficulties lie before you resolve them.

Then do three things:

1. Restate the debate over the transitivity of 'better than' in measurement-theoretic vocabulary — weak orders, semiorders, interval orders, just-noticeable differences, intransitive indifference, representability theorems. This includes the spectrum argument itself, the standard rejoinder that only indifference (or rough equality) is intransitive while strict betterness remains transitive, and the suggestion that some goods are 'essentially comparative' in a way that blocks the spectrum's smooth progression. Say, precisely, what each philosophical claim comes to in the target vocabulary.

2. Give me a residue report. Which moves translate cleanly — I suspect the intransitivity of indifference maps onto tolerance relations without remainder, but tell me if I am wrong? Which translate only with distortion, and what exactly is distorted? Which have no expression at all? Two cases particularly interest me. First, in a semiorder, strict preference is transitive by construction, so genuinely intransitive strict betterness seems to have no home in the formalism: does that show the measurement framework is too weak for the philosophical claim, or that the claim rests on conflating discriminability with betterness? Second, my just-noticeable differences concern perceptual discrimination, an empirical matter; betterness is not something one perceives. Does this disanalogy dissolve the comparison, or does it sharpen what the philosophers are actually disagreeing about?

3. Close with a verdict: is the residue a defect of the measurement vocabulary (value is not a stimulus), or a sign that parts of the philosophical debate are less substantive than they appear? Wherever your answer depends on a contested premise, flag it rather than papering over it.

One ground rule carried over from my old trade: do not smuggle measurement-theoretic assumptions into the translation and then present them as discoveries about value. If the target vocabulary forces a choice that the philosophers deliberately leave open, say so explicitly.
