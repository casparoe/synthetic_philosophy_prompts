I spent my career as an experimental physicist, and in retirement I have been reading widely in the philosophy of science and, more recently, the philosophy of history. What prompted this question is something I noticed while following public debates about foreign policy over the past few decades. Whenever governments deliberate about confronting a rival power, the discussion reliably turns to precedent: the failure of appeasement at Munich is invoked to argue for firmness against dictators; the Vietnam War is invoked to argue against military entanglement in civil conflicts; and the rising-power-versus-ruling-power dynamic of the Peloponnesian War is invoked to frame relations between the United States and China. What strikes me is that this practice coexists, within the very same institutions, with a strict evidential culture elsewhere: a drug agency demands randomized controlled trials and would laugh at a pharmaceutical firm that defended its product by analogy to a single historical case of a chemically similar compound, yet the same state treats one or two loosely analogous episodes from decades or centuries ago as near-decisive evidence about what to do now. I would like you to act as a professional philosopher with expertise in epistemology and the methodology of the historical sciences, and to think carefully and step by step about what this practice of analogical reasoning from history presupposes, philosophically. Please begin with some unstructured brainstorming before settling into your analysis.

More precisely, here is what I am after. First, reconstruct the implicit epistemology: does the practice treat history as a repository of quasi-experimental data from which causal generalizations can be extracted, or as something else -- a store of exemplars for pattern recognition, a source of salience and framing rather than evidence, a rhetoric of persuasion rather than inference? Which of these readings best fits how the analogies are actually deployed, and which would be needed to make the practice rational? Second, tell me which rival views of historical knowledge the practice quietly rules out: for instance, it seems to presuppose that the relevant features of 1938 and of some present crisis can be identified independently of the outcome one is trying to justify, but I am unsure whether that presupposition is even satisfiable, since the description of a past event is already theory-laden. Third, I want you to be honest about underdetermination: which parts of the practice strictly require a particular epistemology and which parts are compatible with several, including a deflationary view on which the analogies do no evidential work at all and merely license decisions reached on other grounds. Finally, tell me concretely what would have to change in the practice if we replaced its implicit theory with a straightforwardly statistical or model-based epistemology of the kind I am accustomed to -- would the analogies have to be abandoned, downgraded to hypothesis-generators, or could they be rehabilitated by being treated as data points in some explicit reference class? If you find that no single coherent epistemology underwrites the practice as it stands, say so plainly and diagnose the inconsistency rather than papering over it.
