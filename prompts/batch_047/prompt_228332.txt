I've been in a philosophy of probability reading group this term and last week we spent the whole session on Bertrand's paradox -- the 1889 one where you ask for the probability that a random chord of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and you get 1/2, 1/3 or 1/4 depending on which parametrisation you treat as uniform. What got me is that the room split into two camps that didn't seem to even disagree with each other so much as talk past each other. One side said the paradox kills the principle of indifference outright: there's no fact of the matter about what "random" means until you specify a method, so any principle that assigns equal probabilities to "equally possible" cases is just incoherent in continuous spaces. The other side -- the Jaynes-ish types -- said the paradox actually vindicates a refined principle: once you demand that your prior be invariant under the right group of transformations (translation, rotation, scale), only one of the answers survives (they claimed 1/2), so indifference works fine as long as you apply it to the symmetries of the problem rather than to whatever parametrisation you happened to write down first.

Both camps seemed to think they'd won, and honestly I can't tell what they're even fighting about. Is this a substantive disagreement -- e.g. about whether there's such a thing as a uniquely rational prior -- or is it a verbal one where "principle of indifference" just means different things to the two sides (a rule about descriptions versus a rule about physical symmetries)? Or is it a disagreement about what probability is for: the anti-indifference camp seemed to be thinking of probabilities as properties of physical mechanisms (how the chord is actually generated), while the other camp was clearly treating them as degrees of belief constrained by ignorance, and I suspect those two framings make Bertrand's setup into two different problems entirely. There's also a third possibility niggling at me, which is that the whole dispute dissolves once you notice that the circle problem is under-specified as a physical question but perfectly well-posed as a question about what a rational agent should believe given certain invariance commitments -- but then the "paradox" stops being a paradox and becomes a demand for more information, and maybe neither side wants to admit that.

So: diagnose this disagreement for me. Take a definite position on what kind of dispute it actually is and why it's lasted over a century, rather than just laying out the options for me to pick from. And given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as settling it -- a formal result, a conceptual distinction, or nothing at all because it's bedrock -- and be honest if you think it's unresolvable.
