I am a junior in high school writing my extended essay on the regress problem in epistemology, and I have noticed that the three standard answers are almost always introduced through pictures rather than arguments. My textbook presents foundationalism as a building: our beliefs form a structure that will collapse unless it rests on bedrock needing no further support. Coherentism is introduced through Neurath's raft — we rebuild our body of beliefs plank by plank while staying afloat on it, because there is no dry dock — or as a spiderweb whose strands hold one another in place. Infinitism gets a tower that descends storey after storey without ever reaching ground. My teacher insists these are only illustrations, but the choice of illustration seems to settle the debate before it begins: bedrock sounds obviously secure, the raft sounds either heroic or hopeless depending on the paragraph, and the tower sounds absurd on its face.

The images matter to me because of a passage I keep returning to in Aristotle's Posterior Analytics (Book I, Chapter 3). There Aristotle sets up a dilemma: on one side are those who argue that since knowing anything requires knowing what it rests on, and that in turn would require knowing something further, there can be no knowledge at all; on the other side are those who try to escape by allowing demonstration to bend back on itself, so that everything turns out demonstrable after all. Aristotle rejects both horns. What strikes me is that the dilemma seems to presuppose a picture already — justification as something like a load-bearing chain or column — before any of the three modern positions even gets a hearing.

Here is what I would like you to do. Treat each of the three analogies as an argument and audit it. For each one, give me an explicit correspondence table: in the building analogy, what plays the role of the bricks, the foundation, the bedrock, and the gravity that brings down an unsupported structure; in the raft or web, what plays the role of the planks, the sea, buoyancy, and the mutual tension of the strands; in the tower, what plays the role of the storeys and of the ground it never reaches. Then identify where each mapping breaks — the features of buildings, rafts, and towers that the images quietly import but that justification may simply not have. For example: a physical foundation bears weight whether or not anything supports it in turn, but a basic belief seems to need some reason in its favor to be credible at all; a web's strands genuinely support one another symmetrically, but can one belief justify a second while being justified by that very belief without the pair floating free of the world; a tower with infinitely many storeys fails under real physical constraints, but no person ever actually traverses an infinite chain of reasons, so what exactly is the objection supposed to be? Finally, tell me for each position whether the verdict its analogy nudges us toward survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the picture was carrying the conclusion the whole time. And come back to the Aristotle passage at the end: does his dilemma depend on the same unexamined picture, and if a better analogy exists for any of the three views — one that captures its actual commitments rather than its rhetorical flavor — propose it and show that it earns its keep.
