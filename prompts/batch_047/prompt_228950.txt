My brother-in-law develops commercial property, and over dinner he defended his work with an argument I haven't been able to answer. Clearing a stretch of meadow or scrub woodland for a warehouse park, he said, is a mercy to animals rather than a harm. Wild land supports dense populations of small creatures -- rodents, ground-nesting birds, frogs, insects beyond counting -- nearly all of whom live short lives dominated by hunger, cold, parasites, injury, and the constant fact and fear of being eaten; of the offspring produced each season, the overwhelming majority die before maturity, many of them miserably. A paved industrial site supports almost none of this. So, he argued, every acre he develops prevents far more animal suffering than it causes, and people who oppose development out of concern for animals have things exactly backwards: they are protecting the scenery, not the animals. I came away with the uneasy feeling that he might be right and no way to tell. Is his reasoning sound? If it fails, where precisely -- in its picture of what wild animals' lives are actually like, in what it assumes happens to the animals a development displaces, or in how it weighs preventing future lives against ending present ones? And if it succeeds, does it commit him to conclusions he would not accept, such as that we ought to pave over as much habitat as we can?
