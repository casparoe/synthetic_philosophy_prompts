I sit on the investment committee of a small grant-making foundation, and we must decide whether to accept an unusual gift. A retired mathematician has offered a $10 million endowment whose ordinary investment income (roughly $450,000 a year under our spending policy) would be ours, subject to one annual chance mechanism: each year, in perpetuity, a fair coin is tossed until the first head appears, and if that takes n tosses, a ledger entry of (−1)^(n+1) · 2^n/n dollars is booked — positive entries flow to our grant budget, negative entries to a reversion account for the donor's heirs. Our actuary notes that each outcome occurs with probability 2^−n, so the expected contributions, taken in their natural order, sum to the alternating harmonic series — ln 2, about 69 cents a year — but the positive terms alone diverge to infinity and the negative terms to minus infinity, so the total depends entirely on the order of summation, and she has refused to certify any expected value. The gift instrument turns this into a governance problem: it stipulates that the board may fix by resolution the order of summation used in all official valuations, and that whatever figure our chosen order yields — and any number whatsoever can be produced this way — shall be definitive in our reports to auditors, grantees, and the heirs. Our chair wants to accept and simply book ln 2; one trustee wants to decline, arguing that accepting money whose reported value exists only by our own say-so is a small fraud on everyone we report to. Draft the committee's decision memo to the full board: accept, accept with conditions (state them precisely), or decline. The memo should give a clear bottom line, say what spending figure, if any, we may honestly budget against the gift and what we should tell our auditors, and engage the strongest version of the losing side's argument rather than dismissing it.
