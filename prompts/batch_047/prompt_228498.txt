I'm a software engineer who has been reading about the knowledge argument against physicalism — the thought experiment about Mary, the scientist who knows every physical fact about color vision but has never seen color — in my spare time, with no formal training in philosophy. I'd like you to build me a variant of that thought experiment engineered to a specification I haven't found handled in the standard presentations.

The two physicalist replies I understand best are these: (1) on leaving the room Mary gains no new factual knowledge, only new abilities — the ability to recognize red things by sight, to imagine red, to remember what red looks like (the "ability hypothesis"); and (2) she gains no new factual knowledge either, only a kind of direct acquaintance with a phenomenal property she already possessed complete factual knowledge of (the "acquaintance hypothesis"). In everything I've read, these two replies are treated as more or less interchangeable escape hatches, deployed in the same breath.

Build me a version of the thought experiment in which the two replies come apart — specifically, one where the acquaintance reply succeeds but the ability reply fails: Mary gains something that plausibly counts as acquaintance with a phenomenal character, but which cannot plausibly be redescribed as the acquisition of any new ability (recognitional, imaginative, mnemonic, or otherwise). If you find the reverse separation easier to engineer — a case where the ability reply succeeds but the acquaintance reply fails — you may build that instead, but explain briefly why you switched.

Constraints:

- Preserve the architecture of the original case: a subject with complete physical information about some domain, an epistemic gap produced by a controlled deprivation, and a release event. No new physics, no magic, no stipulated superpowers beyond what the original already grants (total physical knowledge is itself an extreme stipulation — keep it at that level and add nothing further).
- The separation must be generated by the scenario rather than asserted. Don't just write "and she gains no new abilities"; build a situation where no ability attribution is plausible.
- One scenario only, ordinary-feeling rather than science-fictional, and the scenario itself under 300 words.

After the scenario, do the following:

1. State exactly what Mary knew before release, what changes at the moment of release, and how the acquaintance theorist and the ability theorist each classify that change — with the verdict each is forced into on this case and why.
2. Identify the single feature of the scenario doing the work of separating the replies, and say what happens to the separation if that feature is removed.
3. Give the ability theorist's best counter-attack on the case (for instance, proposing an ability I failed to notice), and assess whether it succeeds.
4. Flag where your construction leans on a contested reading of either reply rather than pretending those readings are settled.

One more thing: if the specification as I've posed it can't be satisfied — for example, if you think the two replies can only ever differ verbally, so that any case purporting to separate them rests on a confused premise — say so plainly and explain why, rather than politely producing a scenario that doesn't actually meet the spec.
