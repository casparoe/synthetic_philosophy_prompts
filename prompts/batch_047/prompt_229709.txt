I'm a philosophy PhD student and I TA a weekly section for an undergraduate normative ethics course: 16 students, one 80-minute meeting, and they have just finished a unit on Kantian and consequentialist moral principles. I want an activity for that section that makes them feel the pull of moral particularism — the claim that there are no true, exceptionless moral principles — before anyone names the view. Here is the structure I have in mind, and I would like you to develop it into something runnable: students work in small teams; each round they receive short case cards and must issue a verdict, then publicly state the rule they used to reach it; the deck is stacked so that a feature they relied on (say, that someone kept a promise, or that an act caused pain) reappears in a later card with reversed moral polarity, so every stated rule gets counterexampled within a round or two. I would also like a scoring twist: teams earn points for correctly predicting the majority verdict on each card. My hope is that this produces the real dialectic — their principles keep failing, yet their predictions mostly succeed — so the debrief can use that tension, rather than a lecture, to introduce the generalist's reply and what each side must give up. Please give me the full rules and flow, several sample cards including the polarity-flip pairs, and a debrief plan that maps moments in the game onto the particularism/generalism dispute, anticipates where students will predictably get stuck, and is honest about which part of the philosophical position the game cannot reproduce.
