Earlier this year I was put in charge of revising my firm's certification standards for building materials, and I was surprised by how much of the job turned on words like flammable, waterproof, and corrosion-resistant. A treated timber panel is called fire-retardant even though the retardant coating would be stripped away by exactly the conditions of the standard burn test; we still certify it, and I could not initially say why that seemed defensible rather than contradictory. A colleague pointed me to the philosophy literature on dispositions, where I learned this kind of case has a name -- masks, alongside finks -- and that it has been discussed continuously for roughly three decades.

My understanding of the state of play is this. An early analysis said a thing is disposed to respond to a stimulus just in case it would respond if stimulated. Counterexamples followed: a live wire connected to a device that would instantaneously remove its conductivity the moment anything touched it is conductive yet would not conduct if touched, and a fragile glass packed in protective wrapping would not shatter if struck yet remains fragile. A revision followed, holding that the object is disposed when it has some intrinsic basis such that it would respond if stimulated while retaining that basis. But the debate did not settle there: critics produced cases where the interfering mechanism operates precisely through the intrinsic basis, or where the basis itself is what gets removed, and defenders of the analysis answered with further qualifications, each met by further cases. Meanwhile a sizable camp concluded that dispositions are simply irreducible to conditionals or to categorical properties, so the counterexample industry was attacking a project that should never have been attempted.

My working hypothesis is that the dispute is not really about the logic of a few tricky cases but about a deeper disagreement over what a semantics for disposition ascriptions owes us: the analysts want a reductive account that eliminates dispositional vocabulary in favor of counterfactuals and categorical bases, while their opponents think such vocabulary is doing indispensable and ineliminable work in science, so that the endless back-and-forth of cases and patches is two sides talking past each other about the purpose of the analysis rather than its details. But I may be wrong: perhaps there is a genuine, narrow technical question here -- for instance, whether any refinement of the intrinsic-basis clause can survive all known interference cases -- whose answer would objectively vindicate or kill the reductive project regardless of philosophical temperament.

Please diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it verbal, with the camps using disposition and analysis to mean different things? Is it substantive but covertly normative, i.e., a fight over the standards a successful account must meet? Is there a hidden formal or empirical question doing the real work? Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually argues, not in my summary, and then tell me what progress would look like given your diagnosis: a decisive class of counterexamples, an agreed taxonomy of finks, masks, and antidotes, evidence about how working scientists deploy these terms, or a formal result about what conditionals can and cannot capture. If instead the right conclusion is that no resolution should be expected because the question as posed is malformed, argue for that directly.
