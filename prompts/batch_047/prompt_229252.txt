I'm a PhD student working on practical rationality and I've been stuck on this argument for a chapter of my thesis, so apologies in advance for the length. Everyone knows the hitchhiker setup: you're stranded in the desert, a driver will rescue you only if she believes you'll pay her £100 once you're safely home, and she's a good enough judge of character that you can't fake it. Here's the argument I've built out of it, and it seems to prove way too much.

(1) For a purely self-interested agent, an act is rational iff it serves the agent's interests at least as well as any available alternative.
(2) Once you're safely home, paying the driver costs you £100 and gains you nothing.
(3) So paying is irrational.
(4) But only people genuinely disposed to pay get rescued; the resolute non-payer is left in the desert.
(5) So the disposition to pay -- a disposition to perform an act that (3) classifies as irrational -- is one a self-interested agent should rationally want to have.
(6) Generalising: whenever possessing a disposition is advantageous, self-interest requires acquiring it, even where exercising it would be irrational by (1).
(7) Now my friend's variation, which is where it all goes wrong for me. A wealthy eccentric will give you £10 million tonight if at midnight you genuinely intend to burn down your neighbour's shed at noon tomorrow. You'll be paid either way; nobody will stop you tomorrow; and at noon tomorrow burning the shed would be pointless, costly, and horrible for everyone involved.
(8) By (6), self-interest requires making yourself genuinely so disposed. But a disposition isn't genuine unless it's the kind that would actually issue in the act. So by noon tomorrow, rationality has installed in you a commitment to do a thing that (2)-style reasoning condemns.
(9) And now the machinery licences anything: dispositions to retaliate when retaliation is futile, to honour threats when carrying them out is ruinous, to never back down in a negotiation. Any disposition whose mere possession someone will reward, however monstrous the acts it produces.
(10) But self-interest surely can't require me to turn myself into someone who burns sheds for nothing.

Which step do I reject? If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (1) through (3) -- I like the clean verdict that paying is irrational while being a payer is rational -- than accept that rationality can demand shed-burning. But I suspect the clean verdict is exactly what's doing the damage, and I can't locate where. If the argument is valid, tell me which premise costs least to abandon and what I'm actually paying for it; if it isn't, tell me where the slide happens and why (6) looked like an innocent generalisation of (5) when it apparently isn't.

Please answer in British English.
