A few months ago, in a reading group on modal metaphysics, I found myself talked into a view I initially regarded as obviously too strong: the necessity of origins. The rough claim is that a person's origins are essential to them -- that I could not possibly have originated from a different sperm and egg than the ones I actually came from, so that any possible individual with even slightly different genetic origins would be someone else, not me. What moved me was the transitivity worry my friend pressed: if I could have come from a slightly different gamete pairing, and that pairing's product could have come from a slightly different one again, then by iteration I could apparently have originated from entirely different parents, which seems absurd; the only non-arbitrary place to stop the slide seemed to be at the beginning. So I've provisionally accepted the view, along with the Kripke-style intuition that possibilities are stipulated rather than discovered, which is supposed to explain why my contrary feeling -- surely I could have been born to different parents! -- is just a confusion between epistemic and metaphysical possibility.

Now I want to stress-test the view, and I'd like a systematic map of its exposure rather than another round of the seminar debate. What would actually count against origin essentialism? Please distinguish several kinds of potential defeater: (1) intuitions or imagined cases that the view must simply reject -- which ones does it reject, and how costly is each rejection (I'd like these ranked from fatal to merely awkward); (2) results in adjacent domains the view stands or falls with -- for instance, how it interacts with views about the persistence of organisms over time (a zygote becoming a fetus becoming an infant already involves massive material turnover, so why should a difference at the very start be identity-destroying?), with the sorites-style arguments it was invented to block, and with the question of whether artifacts get the same treatment as organisms or whether the view fragments by category; (3) formal or structural objections -- does the view require an unacceptably coarse-grained or fine-grained individuation of gametes, and does the boundary between a slightly different sperm and a numerically different sperm become metaphysically loaded in a way nothing in biology supports?

For each defeater, I'd like to know whether a committed origin essentialist would themselves concede it as damaging -- that is, what the view's own defenders acknowledge as the real cost of admission -- versus what only its critics regard as a cost. I'd also like a verdict on the insulation question: has the view been qualified into a position where no possible finding about biology, no intuition pump, and no result elsewhere in metaphysics could dislodge it, and if so, is that a mark of depth or of vacuity? One more thing: I personally find the biological-chimera and twinning cases the most unsettling, since there the notion of an organism's origin is empirically messier than the tidy one-egg-one-sperm picture -- if you think I'm wrong to find those cases especially threatening, tell me why.
