I work on AI safety and, over the past few years, I've become fairly committed to a particular bet: that a substantial part of what we want when we say an advanced AI system should "avoid side effects" can be captured by formal impact measures defined over the system's model of the world, without needing anything close to a full specification of human values. The family of proposals I have in mind penalizes an agent for making the future unusually different from some counterfactual baseline (typically one in which the agent does nothing, or executes a sequence of inactions), whether the penalty is computed via changes in the attainability of many auxiliary objectives, via the agent's own future option set, or via coarse reversibility criteria. I know the standard failure modes -- offsetting behavior (an agent that breaks something and then restores it scores better than one that never acted, and an agent might prevent others from fixing its messes), catastrophes invisible to a coarse world-model, agents that manipulate the baseline itself -- but I've been treating these as engineering problems: pick a better measure, a better baseline, a better trade-off against task performance. A colleague recently told me I'm confusing the difficulty of an instance with the soundness of the program, and I want to take that challenge seriously.

So: what would count against the research program itself, rather than against any particular measure in the family? I'd like the candidate defeaters distinguished by their logic. Some would be technical obstacles that a better formalism could plausibly absorb; others would be structural objections showing the whole framing is misguided -- for instance, the claim that "impact" is inherently value-laden, so that any quantitative proxy either ignores changes humans care about or penalizes changes we'd endorse, and that fixing this requires smuggling in exactly the value specification the approach promised to avoid; or the claim that the choice of counterfactual baseline is a disguised normative choice, since the inaction baseline treats omissions as safe even when withholding help is catastrophic, and any baseline that doesn't do that has already taken a stance on which of the world's default evils the agent is responsible for preventing. I'd also like to hear about defeaters of a different shape: possible behaviors that score low on every reasonable impact measure yet are plainly unacceptable (I've wondered about multi-agent settings, where each system's individual impact is small but the equilibrium is disastrous, and about agents whose low measured impact is achieved by subtly shaping human behavior rather than the physical environment), and conversely, cases where a plainly acceptable action is penalized so heavily that the measure makes the agent useless.

Finally, I want an honest verdict on the program's dialectical health. Which of these defeaters would thoughtful defenders of impact measures themselves concede as genuinely damaging, and which would they wave away as demanding too much of a heuristic that was only ever meant as a cheap proxy? Please rank the objections from fatal to merely awkward, and say explicitly what observation, argument, or formal result -- a proof that distinguishing harmful from harmless large-scale change requires value information, say, or a demonstrated environment where every natural impact measure fails simultaneously -- would make you abandon the bet if you held it. I also want you to assess whether the program has quietly become unfalsifiable: every counterexample I raise in discussions gets met with "that just isn't the right measure yet," and if the family of possible measures is broad enough to absorb any failure, I'm not sure the position is still saying anything. If it has been insulated that way, tell me whether that's a fatal flaw for a pragmatic safety heuristic or a normal feature of engineering intuitions before they've been pinned down -- but make the case rather than gesturing at it.
