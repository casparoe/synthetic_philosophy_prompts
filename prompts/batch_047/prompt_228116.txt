I'm a third-year philosophy undergraduate currently taking a course on medical ethics and the philosophy of death, and our last few seminars have been on decision-making for patients who can no longer decide for themselves. I've come to hold a fairly strong position, which I'll defend in my assessed essay, and I'd like you to stress-test it rather than help me defend it. The position is this: a competently drafted advance directive should retain binding authority over the medical care of the person who wrote it, even when that person, now significantly cognitively impaired, appears content or even expresses wishes that contradict the directive. My underlying thought is that the impaired person's current preferences are typically shallow -- moment-to-moment comfort and discomfort, without the capacity to situate them in a life-narrative or weigh them against long-held values -- whereas the directive expresses the most fully formed, reflectively endorsed preferences the person ever had. On my view, deferring to the later, diminished self over the earlier, reflective one is a kind of injustice to the person considered as a whole: it treats their least authoritative moments as sovereign over their most authoritative ones. I should add that I don't think this authority is unlimited -- I'd grant, for instance, that a directive cannot licence the active suffering of the present self for purely symbolic ends -- but within those limits I take the earlier self to have something like a veto.

What I want from you is a map of this position's exposure: what would count against it? Please identify the observations, thought experiments, and theoretical results that would give me genuine reason to abandon or substantially weaken the view -- not the textbook objections I can already recite, but the cases where my own framework does the worst work. For instance: what happens to my "shallow preferences" claim if it turns out that some severely impaired patients do exhibit stable, value-inflected preferences over time? What would follow if the best available psychology showed that narrative self-conception is far less stable across a healthy life than my view needs -- that the "earlier self" who wrote the directive is itself discontinuous with the person who presented for treatment five years later? Which defeaters would an intellectually honest defender of my position concede as counting against it, and which would they try to absorb through qualification -- and at what point do those qualifications render the view immune to counterexample in a way that should worry me? I'd like the potential defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with an explanation of what the world, or the patient, would have to look like for each one to bite.

Finally, I'd like an honest verdict on whether my position, as I've framed it, actually risks anything. If every apparent counterexample -- the blissfully demented patient who'd be distraught by the treatment she once demanded, the family torn apart by enforcing it -- can be handled by a rider about the present self's suffering, then perhaps I've defined the view into safety, and I'd like to know whether you think that's what I've done and whether it's a defect for a normative view of this kind. Where relevant, please note what a defender would say in reply and whether that reply is a genuine answer or a retreat. Please write your answer in British English.
