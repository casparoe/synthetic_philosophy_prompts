I am drafting a short position paper for a political theory reading group on the retrospective assessment of historical figures, and before circulating it I would like a consistency audit of the commitments I currently hold. Please reply in American English. Here is where I stand:

1. Wrongness is not indexed to time or place. Chattel slavery was as wrong in 1780 as it is today, and it would have been wrong even in a world in which no one had ever suspected it. I want no truck with the idea that a practice becomes wrong only once its society comes to see it as such.

2. Blame, unlike wrongness, is governed by an accessibility condition: a person is blameworthy for a wrong only if they could have recognized it as wrong, given the concepts, evidence, and intellectual community actually available to them. I take this to be the defensible core of the "man of his time" defence — not that the past's wrongs were not wrong, but that blame requires an epistemic opportunity some past agents genuinely lacked.

3. Jefferson merits serious personal blame for slaveholding, not merely participation in a wrong institution, because the relevant considerations were available to him — he articulated versions of them himself — and his failure looks motivational rather than epistemic: he understood the case and organized his affairs so as not to act on it.

4. An unremarkable, reasonably reflective person embedded in a society where a wrong practice is universal and unquestioned — a minor slaveholder of no particular intellectual gifts, say — deserves little or no blame for participating, though the participation was wrong.

5. My own diagnostic, which I call the counterfactual peer test: historical blame is appropriate if and only if an epistemically conscientious person, placed in the same society with the same evidence and the same practical stake in the practice, could have come to see its wrongness. I treat (2) and (5) as one condition stated twice.

6. Decisions about commemoration — statues, building names, honors — should track blameworthiness rather than wrongness: what matters is how culpable the person was, not simply that they participated in something we now recognize as evil.

Please audit this set rather than survey the literature. Which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension, and which only appear to be? Order the genuine tensions by severity, and for each, state precisely what would have to give and what that concession costs the overall position. Then identify the least costly repair that leaves the set jointly tenable. Where a tension is merely apparent, show the distinction that dissolves it. I would rather be told that two of my commitments cannot both survive than be given a diplomatic verdict in which everything is fine.
