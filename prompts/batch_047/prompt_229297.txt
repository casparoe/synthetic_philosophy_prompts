Here's an argument a couple of my grad students cooked up in reading group, and it's been bugging me because the conclusion is nuts but I can't find the step to cut. Background: the special composition question asks when some things xs compose a further thing y.

(1) Any answer has to draw the line using only relations among the candidate parts -- contact, bonding, functional integration, joint causal work, whatever. Appealing to facts about the whole (like "they compose when they're caught up in a life") is circular, since whether the whole exists is exactly what's at issue.
(2) Every such relation comes in degrees: stronger or weaker bonding, more or less integration, tighter or looser causal coupling.
(3) So any line drawn in terms of those relations gives you a sorites series -- cases shading by tiny increments from clear composition (the atoms in a hamster) to clear non-composition (my left shoe plus the moon).
(4) No two adjacent cases in the series can differ in whether composition occurs. That would make whether something exists turn on an arbitrarily small physical difference, and arbitrariness like that is intolerable -- existence isn't the kind of thing that can be vague or gerrymandered.
(5) So composition happens either always or never: universalism or nihilism.
(6) Universalism says there's an object made of my shoe and the moon, and there plainly isn't.
(7) So nihilism. But I'm a plurality of simples if anything is, and I exist. Contradiction.

They concluded the special composition question has no true answer because its presuppositions are inconsistent, which feels like giving up rather than solving anything. Tell me which step you'd reject and why it looked ok in the first place. If forced, I'd rather give up the shoe-and-moon intuition in (6) than accept that tables and I are on par, but I want to know what the cheaper exits cost -- e.g., what you pay for letting composition be vague, or for a brute sharp cutoff, or for denying (1). Write in American English please.
