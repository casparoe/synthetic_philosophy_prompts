I'm in a reading group on free will and we've spent the entire term going in circles over Frankfurt cases and the principle of alternative possibilities (PAP), and honestly everyone is tired of it. Two of the regulars have finally agreed to stop arguing and try an adversarial collaboration instead. One of them thinks Frankfurt's late-60s cases basically settled that moral responsibility doesn't require the ability to do otherwise; the other thinks PAP survives, either through the flicker-of-freedom strategy or the dilemma defence. Write the joint statement the two of them could both actually put their names to.

Structure it as follows. First a numbered list of claims both can endorse: about how the standard Black-style case has to be set up to be fair (the counterfactual intervener never actually acts, the agent's choice is genuinely his own in the actual sequence, the prior sign has to be reliable enough), about what the debate is and isn't a debate over, and whatever common ground they share about what moral responsibility even is. Then a table of the remaining disagreements, each one worded so both parties would accept the formulation of the other's view — no 'begs the question' smuggled into the statement of the position itself. The heart of the document should be the cruxes. I suspect one is the dilemma about the prior sign (if the sign is deterministic, the incompatibilist says the case assumes what it needs to prove; if it isn't, some alternative seems to remain after all), and another is whether a flicker of freedom too thin for the agent to ever act on could really be what responsibility was tracking all along — but have them identify any further cruxes themselves, and for each one give a concrete case or argument both sides accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

To keep things concrete, our group invented a case the two of them must take a position on in the statement, which I'll call the Ulysses case: Jones, worried he'll cave under pressure and betray a friend at tonight's meeting, spends the afternoon installing a device that would force his hand to sign the betrayal document if and only if he started to waver. Come the meeting, he never wavers at all — he signs entirely on his own, cooly and deliberately, while the armed device watches and does nothing. Each party should record whether Jones is responsible for the signing, whether it matters that Jones installed the intervener himself rather than some meddling neuroscientist, and whether the answer changes in the variant where he does waver and the device does the signing. Around 800–1000 words, and keep the register of an actual joint statement rather than a referee report or a debate transcript.
