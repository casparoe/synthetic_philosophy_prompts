I have been reading LessWrong and the surrounding rationalist blogosphere since the late 2000s, and the side-effect effect is a fixture there — one of the standard exhibits in arguments about whether intuitions are evidence, whether ordinary concepts are as hygienic as armchair analysis assumes, and how much of philosophy survives contact with survey data. Here is the exam question, exactly as set:

Question 4 (25 points). In Knobe's side-effect experiments, participants' willingness to say that an agent brought about a side effect intentionally varies with the side effect's moral valence. (a) Describe the experimental design and the asymmetry it revealed. (b) Explain what Knobe took the asymmetry to show about the ordinary concept of intentional action, and why the result has been treated as methodologically significant. (c) Present one proposed explanation of the asymmetry and evaluate it against one rival account or one objection.

Here is the student's answer, unedited:

Knobe's original study, conducted in several countries, gave participants one of two versions of a vignette. In the harm condition, the chairman of a company is told that a new program will increase profits but will also harm the environment. He replies that he does not care at all about harming the environment and only wants to maximize profit, and he starts the program; the environment is harmed. Asked whether the chairman intentionally harmed the environment, a large majority, roughly four fifths, say yes. The help condition is identical except that the side effect is beneficial, and there only around a fifth say the chairman intentionally helped the environment. Knowledge, beliefs, desires, and skill are held fixed across the two conditions; only the valence of the side effect changes. The asymmetry therefore shows that whether people judge a side effect to be intentionally brought about depends on whether it is good or bad.

Knobe concluded that the folk concept of intentional action is partly normative: ordinary competence with the concept draws on evaluative considerations rather than on a purely descriptive theory of mind. This matters for moral philosophy because intentionality ascriptions are the input to moral assessment — since people judge the harm intentional, they are then in a position to blame the chairman for it, which illustrates how moral evaluation rests on prior judgments of intentionality. Methodologically, the result is a centerpiece of experimental philosophy's negative program. Traditional action theory, in the tradition of Anscombe and Davidson, treats intentional action as a purely descriptive concept to be analyzed from the armchair in terms of the agent's reasons, beliefs, and desires. The side-effect effect suggests that the intuitions consulted in that project respond to factors the analysis itself deems irrelevant, so armchair intuition is an unreliable evidential instrument at least in this domain.

One influential explanation, developed by Knobe in his later work, is the deep self model: judgments of intentional action track whether an outcome flows from the agent's deep self, the evaluative attitudes and cares that constitute who the agent really is, rather than from surface states like momentary desire. The chairman's indifference to the environment means the harm expresses what he values, so it is attributed to him intentionally; the help expresses nothing about his values, so it is judged unintentional.

A rival account, due to Adams and Steadman, holds that the effect is pragmatic rather than semantic. On their view, intentionally retains a purely descriptive core meaning, but saying that someone intentionally brought about an outcome pragmatically implicates that they are blameworthy, or creditworthy, for it. That implicature fits the harm condition but not the help condition, so attributions of intentionality diverge even though the concept itself contains no normative component. The deep self model handles the chairman cases, but it has difficulty with results suggesting that the asymmetry extends to violations of merely conventional norms, where nothing about the agent's values seems to be expressed. The pragmatic account may have the better resources there, since conventional violations still license blame.
