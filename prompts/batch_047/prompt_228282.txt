I was arguing with a friend about whether money is “real” or just something everyone agrees to treat as real, and we got stuck on India’s 2016 demonetization. The basic facts, as I understand them: on November 8, the Indian government announced that the existing 500- and 1000-rupee notes would cease to be legal tender almost immediately, even though those notes made up most of the currency in circulation by value. People could deposit the old notes or exchange limited amounts at banks for a while, and the stated goals included attacking unreported wealth and counterfeit currency. There were long lines, cash shortages, disruptions to businesses that relied on cash, and disputes over whether the policy achieved its aims. Please don’t get sidetracked into evaluating the policy’s economics.

Analyze the episode through John Searle’s account of socially constructed institutional facts: status functions, collective acceptance, constitutive rules, and the idea that something acquires a function because people collectively treat it as having that function within a system. Be precise about the mapping. Who exactly has to accept the notes as money for them to be money—the state, the central bank, commercial banks, shopkeepers, borrowers, or everyone together? What changed at midnight: the notes’ legal status, their purchasing power, their value as assets, their practical usability, or their identity as money? Also address the complication that the government could apparently turn legal tender into non-legal-tender by declaration, but could not simply declare a smooth transition into existence; banks, queues, replacement notes, public confidence, and everyday willingness to accept payment still mattered. Does that show that Searle puts too much weight on official status, or does it actually illustrate how institutional facts depend on layered forms of collective acceptance?

I want a committed verdict, not a survey of possible interpretations. Pick a definite position on whether demonetization vindicates Searle’s theory, exposes a serious gap in it, or shows that it needs to be combined with a more practice-centered account of money. Give reasons, and identify the single feature of the episode that most threatens your chosen reading.
