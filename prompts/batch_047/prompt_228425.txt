I'm a student in an upper-year epistemology seminar that has spent the last few weeks on the ethics of belief, mainly Clifford-style evidentialism and James's defense of passional belief in cases like the alpine climber who must leap a crevasse and whose confidence partly makes the leap succeed. For the final paper I have to predict how our professor would rule on a case she has never discussed, based on the verdicts she has given all term. I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher with expertise in epistemology and the ethics of belief. Please think carefully and begin by brainstorming the candidate principles her record could embody before committing to a prediction; I'm interested in the reasoning, not just the bottom line.

Her verdicts so far (all on whether the belief in question is epistemically permissible):

1. Clifford's shipowner, version where the ship sinks: impermissible.
2. Clifford's shipowner, version where the ship happens to make the crossing safely: still impermissible.
3. James's alpine climber, where self-confidence causally contributes to success: permissible.
4. A lonely man who adopts, on no evidence, the belief that the universe has a purpose for him; he could simply suspend judgment, the belief comforts him, and it harms no one: impermissible.
5. A juror who convicts on ambiguous evidence partly because believing in the reliability of the justice system comforts her: impermissible.
6. A coach who, on genuinely neutral evidence, firmly believes her athlete will win the final, knowing that her visible confidence improves the athlete's performance: permissible.

The new case: a mother whose child is seriously ill faces a prognosis on which the medical evidence is genuinely silent. Believing that the child will recover is live for her, and arguably forced, since she cannot sustain engaged, hopeful caregiving while suspending judgment, and her despair would be visible to the child. But unlike the climber and coach cases, her belief does not appear to contribute causally to the child's recovery; the illness will take its course regardless. It harms no one else.

Please answer the following. First, state at least two candidate principles that are each consistent with all six verdicts, including at least one that reads her as a disciplined Jamesian and one that reads her permissiveness in cases 3 and 6 as turning on self-fulfilling or performance-constituting beliefs rather than on James's live-forced-momentous criteria. Second, show how each candidate principle rules on the mother's case. Third, give your best prediction of her verdict, with reasons for preferring one reading of her record over the others. Fourth, if you think the record genuinely underdetermines the case, say so plainly, and describe one further hypothetical verdict of hers that would discriminate between the remaining candidate principles.
