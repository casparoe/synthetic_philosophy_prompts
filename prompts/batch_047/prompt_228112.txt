So I run a little philosophy reading group at work (we're a fintech, mostly backend engineers, no-one has any formal philosophy training) and we've just finished a six-week block on vagueness and the sorites paradox. I said I'd put together an end-of-block written assessment, the way you would for an internal training course, and I'm now realising that writing a good exam question is way harder than writing a good code review.

Here's the angle I want. Early on we read Bertrand Russell's 1923 paper "Vagueness", and one bit really stuck with me because it sounds like something out of an engineering spec review:

"The metre, for example, is defined as the distance between two marks on a certain rod in Paris, when that rod is at a certain temperature. Now the marks are not points, but patches of a finite size, so that the distance between them is not a precise conception. Moreover, temperature cannot be measured with more than a certain degree of accuracy, and the temperature of a rod is never quite uniform. For all these reasons the conception of a metre is lacking in precision."

My instinct as an engineer reading this is: fine, vagueness is just tolerance bands and measurement error, and the fix is to pick a cutoff and enforce it -- we do this constantly, e.g. a monitoring alert fires when latency exceeds 500ms, full stop, no paradox. But the group pushed back and said that just relocates the sorites rather than solving it, and that Russell's metre point is about something different from the bald-man case anyway. I want an exam question that forces people to actually take a position on this rather than summarise both sides.

So please write me:

1. The exam question itself. It should quote or paraphrase the Russell passage (that bit shouldn't count towards the word limit), ask candidates to say whether the engineering-style "just set a threshold" response dissolves the sorites or merely moves it, and require them to explain whether Russell's metre example and the standard heap/baldness cases are the same kind of vagueness or different kinds. Answerable in about 45 minutes, aimed at smart people with zero philosophy background, and it should be impossible to answer well by just reciting "a heap minus one grain is still a heap".

2. A model answer of around 500 words, written at the level of the sharpest person in the group.

3. A marking scheme, out of 20 marks.

For the marking scheme, give it to me as a JSON object (I'll paste it straight into our internal tool) with this exact structure:

{
  "total_marks": 20,
  "criteria": [
    {
      "name": short label for the criterion,
      "marks": integer,
      "full_marks_requires": the specific move an answer must make to get full marks on this criterion,
      "typical_failure": the specific way a weaker answer usually falls short on it
    }
  ],
  "grade_boundaries": {
    "distinction": minimum mark for a distinction,
    "pass": minimum mark for a pass
  }
}

Between four and six criteria please. The criteria should reward the actual conceptual moves (e.g. distinguishing vagueness from mere measurement imprecision, explaining why a stipulated cutoff has to be either arbitrary or itself vague) rather than rewarding people for namedropping philosophers, which half the group will definitely try.
