I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a follow-up piece about how governments should weigh the interests of future generations. Earlier this year I interviewed a moral philosopher who works on population ethics, and I have her verdicts on a set of hypothetical cases in my notebook. First, a couple knows that due to an unavoidable genetic circumstance, any child they can ever conceive will be born with a serious hearing impairment but will have a life clearly worth living; she said such a child is neither harmed nor wronged by being conceived, since it could not have existed without the condition. Second, a teenager can either have a child now, knowing the child will face real disadvantages but still have a worthwhile life, or wait ten years and have a different child with much better prospects; she said the teenager ought to wait, and that having the child now would be wrong even though that particular child is not made worse off than it otherwise would have been. Third, comparing two public health programs of equal cost -- one that screens women before conception and one that treats women during pregnancy, each preventing the same number of cases of the same condition, but where the first changes which children are born while the second benefits children who would exist anyway -- she said the programs are morally on a par. Fourth, a society choosing between conserving its resources and spending them freely, where the choice gradually changes which people come to exist and the spendthrift path leaves future people with somewhat worse but still worthwhile lives; she called the spendthrift choice wrong, full stop.

My follow-up concerns a policy debate that raises a case my interview never touched. A government is choosing between two long-run energy strategies. The first supports a future population of several hundred million people living comfortable but unremarkable lives; the second supports a population of a few tens of millions living genuinely flourishing ones. On any reasonable accounting, the first future contains considerably more total wellbeing, while the second has a much higher average quality of life -- and, as in the earlier cases, nearly everyone in either future owes their very existence to which strategy is chosen, since the policies will shape patterns of life and family for centuries. I want to predict her verdict before I call her, and I'd like your help reasoning it through. Which general principles are jointly consistent with all four of her recorded verdicts -- I'm assuming at least an impersonal total view and an impersonal average view both fit, and I'd like to know whether any person-affecting principle could fit them too -- and what does each of those principles predict about the energy case? Which verdict should I predict, and why? Finally, since I only have fifteen minutes with her, what single further hypothetical would most efficiently settle which principle she's actually running?
