My friend is a punishment-skeptic naturalist type, and I've been keeping track of her verdicts on cases, trying to figure out what rule she's actually running. So far:

1. A violent offender who's still dangerous: lock him up, but treat him decently -- justified.
2. A first-time nonviolent shoplifter: restitution and counseling, no prison.
3. Executing a murderer who could be safely imprisoned for life instead: absolutely not.
4. Heavy fines on polluting companies purely to change their incentives: fine.
5. Prison for a corrupt executive, mostly to deter other executives: yes, though she called it regrettable.

Next case, which I haven't asked her about: an elderly con man who ruined hundreds of retirees, now fully rehabilitated, no access to money ever again, and the fraud scheme exploited a loophole that's since been closed so nobody else could copy it -- meaning punishing him would deter nobody and incapacitate nobody. But the victims are furious, and the question on the table is whether the sentencing should include public condemnation and moral blame -- the judge officially denouncing him, victim statements expressing that he's to blame -- even stipulated to have zero deterrent effect. It's purely about whether blame is *fitting* here.

Predict her verdict on the blame/condemnation part, and tell me which rule she's implicitly running: a Caruso-style public health/quarantine view where he's treated like a carrier of a disease and blame is out of place, a Dennett-style view where apt blame and denunciation are a legitimate (even owed) part of keeping the responsibility system alive, or a flat forward-looking consequentialism that drops the question entirely. Also name the one further case that would let me tell the top two candidate rules apart.

Give me your reasoning, then finish with a last line of the form \answer{<blame justified or not> -- <rule name>}.
