For the final meeting of each semester, my decision-theory reading group has a tradition of setting aside the journal articles and having each member contribute a piece of satire on something we studied, on the theory that you only really understand an argument once you can make it funny without misrepresenting it. My assigned topic is the unilateralist's curse: the observation that when a momentous action can be taken by any one of many agents acting alone, the action grows more likely as the number of agents grows, since it will be taken by whichever agent most overestimates its value or urgency. The restraint of the cautious majority leaves no trace in the world, pooling everyone's assessments only partly repairs the problem (people keep their own priors and their own eagerness), and so a rational agent facing the choice should discount her own conclusion precisely in proportion to how many others face the same choice holding the same power.

Please write, in about 600 words, the official welcome packet given to new members of the Custodians of the Meridian Engine, a society founded to guard a machine whose late inventor believed it would either abolish scarcity or extinguish the species. She died before finishing the calculations that would have settled which, and so built the Engine to be started by a single key — then, deciding no one person could be trusted with that power, commissioned several hundred identical keys and distributed them to carefully vetted guardians. The packet should be written in the warm, deadpan voice of institutional self-congratulation: a welcome letter celebrating the growth from seven founding custodians to four hundred as a triumph of safety; a FAQ answering the obvious questions (surely more key-holders means more deliberation before anyone acts?) with serene confidence; a fond memorial note on the so-called Founding Skeptics, members who resigned in protest at each expansion and are remembered as well-meaning eccentrics; excerpts from the minutes showing the deliberation committee's attendance thinning year by year as its more cautious members quietly drift away; and an announcement of this year's Most Vigilant Custodian award.

The humor must run on the real mechanics of the curse rather than a caricature of them: the selection effect by which the keenest members come to staff the committees, the invisibility of all the keys that are never turned, the fact that the society's information-sharing sessions genuinely do take place and genuinely do not reconcile divergent priors, and the member whose perennial motion to discuss discounting one's own judgment in proportion to total membership is perennially tabled for lack of time. Every reassurance the packet offers should sound reasonable on its face — the satire fails if the institution sounds stupid rather than merely wrong — and no character may voice the objection from outside. The packet must never break character to explain the joke, and ideally the closing line lands the irony without stating it, perhaps by looking forward with pride to next year's membership drive.
