A friend and I argued until late last night about who ought to have the vote, and I left convinced that she was applying a principle she never stated -- and that, on the one case that mattered most, she departed from it. I want to reconstruct her principle from her verdicts and check. As best I can reproduce them, she held:

1. A tourist in the country for two weeks gets no vote, even though the law applies to her for as long as she is there.
2. A citizen who emigrated twenty years ago but kept her passport, still owns a flat in the capital, and comes back most summers to see family keeps her vote for life.
3. A non-citizen who has lived in the country for ten years, works, pays taxes, and has children in the local school should vote in every election, national ones included, even if he never naturalises.
4. No vote for a six-year-old, obviously; pressed about a fifteen-year-old she said almost certainly not, while conceding she could not justify where the line falls.
5. A prisoner serving a long sentence for a serious crime keeps his vote; taking it away struck her as indefensible.
6. The residents of a neighbouring country whose air is fouled by a power station just across the border get no vote here, though she allowed that they are owed something -- a hearing, compensation, consultation; not the franchise.

The case we actually fought over was asylum. The government is redesigning its border and asylum policy -- who is detained on arrival, who is turned back, who is admitted -- and I argued that the people this policy is applied to, none of whom has ever legally set foot in the country, should have some say in shaping it, since no other electorate will ever speak for them. She said no, quickly and confidently: a polity decides its own membership rules, and they are not members. I thought I caught her contradicting her own earlier verdicts, but I could not prove it on the spot.

So: what principle or principles would generate verdicts 1 through 6 as she gave them? I assume at least two distinct rules fit the record equally well -- identify them, note the riders each one needs (the tourist, the teenager), and say what each predicts about the asylum case. Then tell me whether her "no" is the verdict her track record actually commits her to, which of the six precedents is the closest analog to the asylum case and why, and -- if the record genuinely underdetermines the answer -- what principle she would be adopting by saying yes and what principle by saying no. Finally, name a case, different from all of the above, whose verdict would force the two candidate rules apart.
