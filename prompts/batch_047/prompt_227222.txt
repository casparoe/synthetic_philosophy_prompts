Next month I am defending, in a departmental seminar, the thesis that evidential cooperation in large worlds gives agents like us a strong reason to act cooperatively: if the universe plausibly contains vast numbers of agents deliberating much as I do, then my decision is evidence about theirs, and by choosing as they would wish I can influence, in expectation, far more value than anything I could causally affect on Earth. The audience is mostly decision theorists and formal epistemologists committed to causal decision theory — people who regard evidential decision theory as having lost the Newcomb debate decades ago — plus a couple of moral philosophers sceptical of anything to do with infinite ethics. Prepare me for the question period: the half-dozen objections I will actually face, ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased the way this particular audience would put it, with my best two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is weakest. I want definite recommendations rather than a menu of options: for each objection, tell me plainly whether I should concede, reframe, or stand my ground, and why. Finish with the objection I should be hoping nobody raises, and what I will say if they do.
