I am not asking you to adjudicate algorithmic fairness; I want the specification first. Suppose we need a formal criterion of fairness for statistical risk scores — the sort used in pretrial detention, lending, or medical triage — good enough to serve as the standard a regulator could audit against. Write the desiderata list such a criterion must satisfy: for each requirement, give the intuition or case that motivates it (for instance, the public dispute over recidivism scores in which one side documented unequal false-positive rates across racial groups and the other replied that the scores were equally well calibrated), say whether the requirement is genuinely binding or merely the dogma of one camp, and identify which requirement does the most to exclude candidates. Two complications must shape the list. First, the established impossibility results: absent perfect prediction or equal base rates across groups, no score can be simultaneously calibrated within each group and equal in both false-positive and false-negative rates across groups. The list therefore cannot simply conjoin the natural desiderata; state which must be sacrificed and by what principle, and whether the impossibility shows that some demands were never genuine requirements of fairness or instead that every deployable criterion chooses whom to wrong. Second, assess two requirements often smuggled in without argument: that fairness is a property of the score itself rather than of the process that produced it, and that an adequate criterion must be invariant under relabeling the outcome, so that its verdict does not depend on which class is coded as positive. Keep any scoring of existing contenders — calibration, equalized odds, demographic parity — brief and subordinate to the criteria themselves. Close by stating how confident you are in the framework as a whole and singling out the single point you are least sure of.
