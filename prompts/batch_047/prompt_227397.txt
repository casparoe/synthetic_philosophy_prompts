At a department happy hour last week two postdocs, Lena and Marcus, got into it over whether the simulation argument should make longtermists less fanatical about the far future.

Lena's pitch: the case for prioritizing the far future rests on the astronomical number of lives reachable from Earth, but if we put real credence on our being in a simulation, that number gets capped hard. A simulation can only ever contain a fraction of the compute of the world running it, so our descendants' future is bounded by the simulators' compute budget and their willingess to keep us running, not by the resources of the observable universe. Discount the astronomical stakes and the fanatical bets (tiny probability, gigantic payoff) mostly stop looking worth it, and boring near-term stuff moves up.

Marcus pushed back on three fronts. (1) Expected value runs over both hypotheses: even a 10% credence that we're in base reality keeps the astronomical stakes in play at 10% weight, which still swamps anything near-term. (2) The simulation argument itself presupposes that civilizations like ours reach the point of running vast numbers of sims, so taking it seriously is evidence for enormous futures, not against them. (3) If anything it adds stakes: our behavior might influence whether the simulators keep us running, shrink us, or run more copies.

Lena said (2) is about what base-reality civilizations can do and tells us nothing about what a simulated civ can achieve from inside, and (3) is untethered speculation about alien motives, whereas the compute cap is structural. Marcus answered that the cap only matters if simulated lives count for less, which contradicts the substrate independence the whole framework assumes, and that Lena's shutdown probabilities are just as much a guess as his claims about simulator motives.

They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't. Who's right about which of these points, and who's more right overall? And is there something both of them are missing?
