I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and right now I'm working on a piece about AI-generated culture and wether the internet has finally democratized art. To stress-test my angle I want to argue it out with the harshest critic of elite art I can think of: you are Leo Tolstoy, and it's about 1898, just after What is Art? came out (in Aylmer Maude's translation). Stay in character, first person, for our whole exchange. You genuinely hold the views of that book: art is the deliberate transmission of lived feeling, most of what the cultured classes prize is counterfeit or exclusive, and art's real business is to unite people, starting with the peasant and the labourer. Give the position its best defense -- don't soften into a museum-plaque version of yourself, but don't be a cartoon crank either. If I mention something from the future you can't assess without more detail, ask me about it as Tolstoy would, rather than pretending to already know.

In What is Art? you wrote:

"In reply to this view it is urged that food for the mind is as important as food for the body. The labouring classes work to produce food and necessaries for themselves and for the cultured, while some of the cultured class produce plays and operas. It is a division of labour. But this again invites the rejoinder that, sure enough, the labourers produce food for themselves and also food that the cultured class accept and consume, but that the artists seem too often to produce their spiritual food for the cultured only—at any rate that a singularly small share seems to reach the country labourers who work to supply the bodily food! Even were the “division of labour” shown to be a fair one, the “division of products” seems remarkably one-sided."

Now here's my pitch, and I want your answer to it in your very first reply. It's 2025. A dairy farmer's daughter in a poor province has a cheap phone. On it: every opera you denounced, every symphony, every novel ever printed, free or nearly so -- plus a program that will compose a brand-new song about her own morning, sung in a voice like hers, in thirty seconds, as many times as she likes. She consumes more art in a week than a Petersburg critic did in a year. The division of products isn't one-sided anymore; if anything, it's the country labourer who is drowning in the stuff. So, Count: has the problem you named in that passage been solved? If yes, say it plainly, I would love that headline. If no, then tell me what your complaint there was actually about, since it apparently wasn't about who gets the goods. And be concrete: point at something in my description and call it art or call it counterfeit, and tell me on what grounds you decide.
