I am a programme officer at a mid-sized endowed charitable foundation, and I need your help as a professional philosopher with expertise in the history of economic thought and the ethics of philanthropy. Our founding charter, written by a donor who was an avid reader of Keynes, instructs the trustees to make grants that prepare society for the moment when, in Keynes's phrase from his 1930 essay Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren, mankind's permanent problem replaces its economic one. The trustees have asked me to translate that instruction into something my team can actually apply, and I want the translation to be philosophically serious rather than a vague nod at wellbeing.

The passage I keep returning to is Keynes's statement of method early in the essay:

My purpose in this essay, however, is not to examine the present or the near future, but to disembarrass myself of short views and take wings into the future.

As you will know, the essay goes on to argue that the economic problem -- the struggle for subsistence -- may be solved within a century or so; that the love of money will then come to be recognised as a somewhat morbid propensity rather than a virtue; that what Keynes calls purposiveness, the habit of valuing an action for its remote future consequences rather than for its own quality and its immediate effects, is a disposition we are currently forced to cultivate but will eventually need to outgrow; and, crucially, that we cannot simply skip ahead: Keynes insists that for some time yet we must continue to treat accumulation as useful, because the money-makers will carry the rest of us towards abundance. The good life, on his picture, involves the cultivation of ends over means and the art of living itself, but arriving there is a transition with stages, and what is appropriate at one stage is pathological at another.

Please think carefully about this before answering, and begin by brainstorming the full range of distinctions in the essay that a grant-making procedure might need to preserve, before settling on the ones that matter. Then write me a reusable decision procedure that my team can run on any grant application. Our situation, which the procedure must respect, is as follows: we run four funding rounds a year, with roughly forty applications per round; a programme officer has about ninety minutes for a first assessment of each application; grants range from fifty thousand to two million pounds; final sign-off rests with a board of trustees none of whom has philosophical or economic training; and the charter's instruction is binding, so we cannot simply fall back on generic cost-effectiveness criteria.

The procedure should specify, in order: the screening questions to ask of each application; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; how to classify an application by the stage of Keynes's transition it addresses, and how that classification affects whether we may fund it; a test for what you might call purposiveness-capture, meaning proposals that dress up further accumulation, productivity growth, or status competition in the language of the good life; the points at which the procedure must stop and hand the decision to the trustees because the question is one of values rather than of classification; and an explicit statement of which of Keynes's distinctions had to survive the simplification and which you judged safe to drop, and why.

Then run the procedure on three hypothetical applications, showing the working at each stage: first, a pilot of universal basic services in a prosperous British city; second, a national programme of community arts and leisure education aimed at people retiring early; third, a research institute developing automation technology whose application argues, on explicitly Keynesian grounds, that hastening abundance is the best contribution to the good life now available. At least one of these should be a case where the procedure delivers a verdict our trustees will find unwelcome or counterintuitive, and you should not soften the verdict to please them.

Finally, be honest about the procedure's limits: where it will misfire, which judgments it merely dresses up as classification, and what it cannot settle even in principle. Interpret the quoted passage as part of your answer -- say what disembarrassing oneself of short views concretely demands of a funder, and whether it licenses discounting the needs of the present generation of applicants.

Please write in British English throughout, in a formal register suitable for circulation to the board, in roughly 2,000 to 2,500 words (the quoted passage does not count towards this).
