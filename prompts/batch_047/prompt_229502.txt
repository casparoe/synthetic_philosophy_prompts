I serve on the safety board of a county deciding whether to close an aging bridge while planning a major repair. The bridge is still used by commuters, school buses, and emergency vehicles; closing it for six months would create long detours and serious economic costs, while a structural failure could kill dozens of people.

Three independent engineering teams reviewed the same inspection records, load measurements, traffic data, and material tests. None accuses the others of making a calculation error. They differ only over which structural assumptions are reasonable, and each reports a range rather than a single annual failure probability:

- Team A estimates somewhere between 1 in 900 and 1 in 350.
- Team B estimates somewhere between 1 in 700 and 1 in 250.
- Team C estimates somewhere between 1 in 1,200 and 1 in 450.

The county’s safety rule requires closure if the annual failure probability exceeds 1 in 500. Every team’s range crosses that threshold, but none claims that its range can be narrowed without adding assumptions it considers unjustified by the present evidence.

Our chair has proposed converting each range into a representative number and combining the three results. That procedure produces an estimate just below the threshold, so she recommends keeping the bridge open. Another board member argues that closure is required whenever even one defensible assessment crosses the threshold. A third proposes keeping the bridge open for three more weeks while installing additional sensors, on the ground that the new data may eliminate much of the disagreement. Waiting would improve the decision, but it would also expose the public to three additional weeks of whatever the actual risk is.

What should the board do? More importantly, how should we reason about the threshold when the available assessments do not support one precise probability? Is the chair’s single-number compromise a principled estimate or an artificial removal of the very uncertainty that matters? Is the strict worst-case approach required by ordinary caution, or does it give unlimited authority to the most pessimistic defensible model? And how should we weigh a short delay intended to reduce this kind of uncertainty against the risk incurred during the delay? Please give a concrete recommendation and a decision procedure the county could defend afterward, including if the bridge fails or if it is closed unnecessarily. Please answer in American English.
