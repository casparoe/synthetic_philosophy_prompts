So I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm writing a sidebar to accompany a feature on the whole modern Stoicism boom -- the tech founders doing negative visualization, the apps, the sold-out conferences, all of it. My editor wants something a little playful to close the sidebar, and I had this idea for a villanelle but I can't write verse to save my life. Write me one from the point of view of a burned-out startup founder who picked up Epictetus on a long layover and is now, at 5am in a hotel before a make-or-break investor meeting, actually trying to use the dichotomy of control for the first time. For anyone who needs the concept spelled out: the Stoic move is to sort everything into what's up to you (basically your own judgments, intentions, and responses) and what isn't (your reputation, other people's choices, outcomes, the weather, whether the deal closes), and then to stake your peace of mind only on the first pile.

Here's the part I care about and where most attempts I've seen fall flat: the form has to do the work. The two refrains should be the narrator's sorting formulas -- one refrain for things going into the up-to-me column, one for things going into the not-mine column -- and across the stanzas things should migrate between the columns: the meeting, the investors' moods, the company's survival, eventually even his own fear and his body (it's 5am, he's shaking, the shaking isn't up to him, but what he makes of it is). The tricky bit the poem needs to land, and the thing I want to stress-test, is that the sorting isn't a one-time decision -- items keep trying to sneak back into the up-to-me column, which is exactly why the practice is a practice. Then in the closing quatrain, where a villanelle's two refrains finally stand side by side, the poem should let us see what's left once nearly everything has been surrendered to the not-mine pile: the two refrains meet over the one thing that never migrates, and it should feel like both a loss and a relief. The tone should be wry and contemporary (room service, Slack notifications) but genuinely Stoic underneath -- a reader who knows the doctrine should nod, and one who doesn't should just get a good poem about a man letting go of things in a hotel room. Roughly 19 lines, obviously. Thanks!!
