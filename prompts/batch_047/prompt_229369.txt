A friend who just finished an introductory epistemology course summarized the Gettier problem for me like this: "Gettier refuted the traditional analysis of knowledge as justified true belief, an analysis that had gone essentially unchallenged since Plato. He constructed cases in which someone has a belief that is both justified and true yet intuitively isn't knowledge, because its truth is a matter of luck: in each case, the person reasons from a justified but false belief to a conclusion that happens to be true for unrelated reasons. The natural repair, which most textbooks endorse, is to add a fourth condition requiring that one's belief not be inferred from any false premise. So the Gettier problem was effectively solved within a few years of the paper, and its real legacy is that it shifted epistemology's attention away from the analysis of knowledge and toward the theory of justification."

I have the sense that some of this is right and some of it is off, but I can't locate the seams. Going claim by claim: which parts would a careful epistemologist accept, which are subtle misrepresentations or contestable glosses presented as settled fact, and which are simply wrong? In particular, does the proposed fourth condition actually fix the problem, and does the closing claim about the problem's legacy follow from anything accurate earlier in the summary?
