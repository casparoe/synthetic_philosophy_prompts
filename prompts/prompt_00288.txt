I've been preparing a seminar on formal decision theory, and one worry about updateless decision theory (UDT) keeps nagging at me, though I suspect I may be misconstruing the proposal. As I understand it, following Wei Dai's original suggestion, an updateless agent fixes its policy once and for all "before" it has any information, precisely so as to avoid the kind of updating that gets ordinary Bayesian agents into trouble in cases like Newcomb's problem or counterfactual mugging. But this seems to imply that such an agent should perform systematically worse than an ordinary Bayesian reasoner in perfectly mundane sequential problems, since it can never let subsequent evidence change what it does. Take the Monty Hall problem: wouldn't an updateless agent simply stick to whatever door-choosing policy it started with and refuse to switch after the host reveals a goat, on the grounds that switching would require updating on new information? If that's right, I'm struggling to see why decision theorists treat UDT as a genuine advance over causal or evidential decision theory, rather than as a proposal that cures one disease by inducing a much more obviously fatal one. I'd be grateful for help seeing where this line of reasoning goes wrong, since I assume it must, given how seriously UDT is taken in the literature.
