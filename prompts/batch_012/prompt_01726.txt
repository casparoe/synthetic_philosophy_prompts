My roommate and I recently had a lengthy argument about the symbol grounding problem. She argued that words like "cat" get their meaning through causal relations to the world—when I perceive a cat, my brain enters a state caused by that cat, and that state has the content "cat." I countered that this doesn't solve the problem at all, because a brain state is just a physical pattern. Why is that pattern *about* a cat rather than merely correlated with cats? She responded that the key is the causal history: the state acquired its meaning through being produced by cats over evolutionary and developmental time. I pressed further: imagine a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of my brain created in a vat with no causal contact to the world. That brain state would be physically identical to mine, yet she seems to say it wouldn't mean anything. If meaning depends on external history rather than intrinsic properties, then two physically identical states could have different meanings—or one could mean something and the other nothing. That seems to place meaning outside the physical world. She acknowledged this but said it's the right conclusion: meaning is relational and historical, not intrinsic to physical states. I objected that this makes it mysterious why a vat-bound duplicate should lack thoughts if it's functionally and physically identical to me. If thoughts are real, they should supervene on physical facts. Neither of us shifted. Can you work through each stage of this exchange and explain who has the stronger position at each point? What is each party overlooking?
