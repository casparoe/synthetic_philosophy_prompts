I've been reading through the Lucas-Penrose debate on whether Gödel's incompleteness theorems show that human minds can't be (or can't be simulated by) Turing machines / formal systems, and I keep getting tripped up on one specific move in the argument that seems to be doing a lot of work but isn't always spelled out clearly.

The basic Lucas/Penrose claim is that for any consistent formal system F powerful enough to do arithmetic, there's a Gödel sentence G(F) that is true but unprovable in F, and that "we" can somehow see that G(F) is true even though F cannot prove it — supposedly showing our reasoning outstrips any single formal system. But critics (Putnam, Benacerraf, and others) object that this "seeing" isn't free: to conclude that G(F) is true, you don't just need to understand what G(F) says, you need to already accept that F is consistent (since G(F) is true iff F is consistent, roughly). And by Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, F itself cannot prove its own consistency — so if we (as reasoners) are implicitly relying on some assumption of F's consistency to assert G(F), we're bringing in an extra premise from outside F, and it's not obvious this premise is something we know rather than something we merely assume or hope is true.

Could you walk me through exactly why this "consistency" issue is considered the central weak point of the Lucas-Penrose argument, in a fairly gut-level way? I'm especially interested in: (1) why the argument seems to require that the human (or the machine, if it's meant to model us) doesn't just prove G(F) but somehow "knows itself to be sound" or "knows F is consistent" in a way that goes beyond formal provability, and (2) why critics think this same difficulty could just as easily apply to human mathematicians, undermining the claim that we have some magic insight machines lack. I've seen gestures at "maybe humans are inconsistent formal systems too" but I want to understand the actual logical mechanics of why that's a live escape route for the mechanist.
