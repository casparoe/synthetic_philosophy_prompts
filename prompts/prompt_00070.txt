I've been working through Andy Egan's counterexamples to causal decision theory, and I keep getting stuck on how "Death in Damascus" style cases interact with the sensitivity of an agent's credences to the very act of choosing. The basic Death in Damascus case has the ratifiability condition doing a lot of work—Death finds you wherever you decide to go, so no matter what you pick, you immediately have reason to regret it and switch, and CDT seems to spin in circles or else needs some patch like ratifiability to settle on a mixed strategy.

What I'm looking for is a novel variant: a case structurally like Death in Damascus (an agent facing a predictor or correlation-tracking process, where the agent's credences about the world are sensitive to which act they're deliberating toward) but set in an ordinary, non-mythological context—something like a hiring decision, a medical choice, or a financial bet—where the same instability arises. I want the example to make vivid exactly why the "act-state dependence" is doing the damaging work, and ideally to show why neither a naive causal decision theorist nor a naive evidential decision theorist gets the intuitively correct answer, so that the case actually motivates something like Egan's own revisions or a ratifiability-based fix. Can you construct such a case in detail, spell out the payoff structure, and explain precisely where standard CDT reasoning breaks down?
