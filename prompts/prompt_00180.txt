I'm preparing a graduate seminar on consequentialism and its critics, and I keep getting tangled up in the literature on cluelessness—specifically the distinction, drawn most carefully by Hilary Greaves, between "simple" and "complex" cluelessness. The rough idea seems to be that in cases of simple cluelessness (say, the far-future effects of buying a cup of coffee), our uncertainty about consequences is symmetric in some relevant sense, so it can be bracketed or "washed out" without threatening the action-guiding capacity of consequentialism. In cases of complex cluelessness (say, the long-run effects of donating to a specific charity), we allegedly have asymmetric but stubbornly unquantifiable evidence pointing in some direction, and this is supposed to generate a much deeper problem for expected-value reasoning.

What I'd like help with is an intuitive, gut-level explanation of two things: first, why the symmetry consideration is thought to neutralize simple cluelessness but not complex cluelessness—what exactly is doing the work in the "symmetry" argument, and why doesn't it generalize? Second, I'd like to understand why several critics (Lenman among them, though I think others have pressed similar points) regard the simple/complex distinction itself as unstable or question-begging, rather than a principled carving of the cluelessness problem. I'm less interested in a survey of the literature than in getting clear on the underlying structure of the disagreement, ideally with an example or two that brings out why reasonable people might come down differently on where the line falls.
