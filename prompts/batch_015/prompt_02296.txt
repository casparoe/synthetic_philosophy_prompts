I'm several drafts into a novel set in a city under siege, where a state oracle delivers one unambiguous prophecy every dawn and has never once been wrong. My protagonist, a naval officer with an unwelcome sideline in logic, refuses to accept that this means tomorrow's battle is already fated, and I want the argument she makes when she publicly confronts the oracle's priests to rest on real philosophical bones rather than hand-waving about destiny. Naturally this sent me back to Aristotle's sea-battle passage in De Interpretatione, and from there down a rabbit hole about a curious historical wrinkle: two quite different theories, from two very different centuries, both go by the name "Ockhamism," and I still can't tell whether the second is a faithful descendant of the first or simply borrowed its name.

The first is William of Ockham's own fourteenth-century response to the problem of divine foreknowledge: on this view, God already and truly knows today whether tomorrow's battle will occur, so future contingent statements remain fully bivalent, yet human freedom survives because God's "foreknowing" something is merely a soft fact about the past — parasitic on what free agents still go on to do — rather than a hard, settled fact of the kind that could genuinely constrain them. The second is the "Ockhamist" semantics that Arthur Prior devised centuries later for branching-time logic, on which a future-tensed sentence is assigned a truth value only relative to a moment together with an entire possible history, or "chronicle," running through it, so that, independent of a choice of chronicle, there is simply no fact of the matter about whether the battle will occur.

What I need from you is a real comparison rather than a potted history lesson: what is each theory actually trying to secure, and are they securing the same thing? Does Prior's formal apparatus genuinely capture what Ockham was after, or does calling it "Ockhamist" lend it a lineage it hasn't quite earned — is there something essential in the medieval picture, such as a single true future already settled for God even while hidden from us, that quietly disappears once the theory becomes truth-relative-to-a-chronicle? And because I need my logician to land somewhere by the end of the scene, tell me which of the two views gives her the more defensible grounds for denying that the battle is already fated without paying too steep a price in ordinary logic — and if you think neither survives scrutiny intact, say so plainly and defend that verdict too.
