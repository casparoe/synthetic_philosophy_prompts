I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, currently revising a dissertation chapter on the unity of consciousness. My advisor sent the last draft back with a fairly blunt comment: I had been treating split-brain cases the way most survey articles do, as though Sperry and Gazzaniga's classic experiments straightforwardly vindicate the old "two minds in one skull" reading often associated with Nagel's 1971 discussion. She wants the chapter to actually engage with the more specific theoretical options on the table rather than gesture at the popular version of the debate, so I'm trying to work through this properly before I revise further.

The two accounts I keep getting stuck on are Tim Bayne's "switch model" (Journal of Philosophy, 2008, developed further in his 2010 book The Unity of Consciousness) and the interpretation favored by Yair Pinto and colleagues in their 2017 Brain paper. Bayne's model denies that split-brain patients ever have two simultaneous streams of consciousness; instead, a single stream of awareness rapidly toggles between the two hemispheres, which he argues is the only view that can accommodate both the disunity patients display under lab conditions and their coherent, unified behavior in daily life. Pinto and colleagues, by contrast, report that their patients could correctly indicate the presence, location, and identity of a stimulus no matter which visual half-field it appeared in and no matter whether they responded with the left hand, right hand, or verbally - even though the very same patients still failed at explicitly comparing stimuli presented simultaneously to the two half-fields.

What I'd like from you is a genuine comparison rather than a literature summary: how would each of these two accounts explain that specific dissociation (intact identification and localization across the disconnected hemispheres, but failed cross-hemisphere comparison), and which explanation do you find more convincing? I'm also curious whether you think Pinto et al.'s data leaves Bayne's switch model intact, or whether it actually points to something more radical than switching between two hemisphere-bound streams - i.e., to there never having been two separable streams to begin with. A direct verdict, with your reasoning spelled out, would be more useful to me here than a balanced survey of the whole debate.
