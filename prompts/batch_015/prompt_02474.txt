I'm a PhD student in biostatistics, and this term I've been sitting in on a causal inference reading group that has spent an unusual amount of time re-litigating Simpson's paradox. Everyone in the room already knew the canonical cases going in: the 1973 Berkeley graduate admissions data, where men were admitted at a higher overall rate than women even though women were admitted at equal or higher rates within almost every individual department, and the 1986 kidney-stone study by Charig and colleagues, where the less invasive treatment looked superior once the data were pooled but was actually the worse option for both small-stone and large-stone patients once the data were broken out by stone size. What derailed the discussion was a disagreement about the general moral we're supposed to draw from these cases. Half the room defended the standard applied-statistics heuristic that, when in doubt, you should trust the stratified numbers over the pooled ones, on the grounds that pooling is precisely what let the confound sneak in. The other half, channeling Judea Pearl, insisted that this is really just a heuristic that happens to work in cases like the kidney-stone one, and that it fails outright in other reversals that look numerically identical but rest on a different underlying causal structure.

I'd like your help adjudicating this properly. Please compare these two ways of deciding which numbers to trust once a Simpson's-style reversal turns up: the classical "control for the third variable" heuristic, versus Pearl's causal-graph-based resolution of the paradox. In particular, explain why the raw joint distribution over the three variables is, on its own, never sufficient to tell us whether the pooled or the stratified association is the one that reflects the real causal effect, and what further piece of information -- one that isn't recoverable from the data alone -- actually settles the matter. A brief worked contrast would help enormously: could you construct a scenario, structurally parallel to the kidney-stone case and reproducing essentially the same numbers, in which stratifying on the third variable would actually be the wrong thing to do, so that the pooled figure is the one we ought to act on? Finally, I'd value your own verdict on whether the classical heuristic is best seen as a special case of the causal criterion, one that merely happens to hold whenever the third variable is a genuine pre-treatment confounder, or whether it embodies a more basic mistake about what stratification accomplishes. Please justify whichever position you take.
