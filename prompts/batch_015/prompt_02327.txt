I'm finalizing the reading notes for a graduate seminar that pairs classical liberal political philosophy with the contemporary literature on nudging and libertarian paternalism, and I keep getting stuck on the same paragraph from Chapter V of Mill's *On Liberty* — the one that everybody in this literature cites, but that I suspect few people actually parse with much care. A colleague and I went back and forth on it over coffee last week without reaching agreement, so I'd like a careful outside reading before I commit myself to an interpretation in front of the class. Here is the passage:

"Again, it is a proper office of public authority to guard against accidents. If either a public officer or any one else saw a person attempting to cross a bridge which had been ascertained to be unsafe, and there were no time to warn him of his danger, they might seize him and turn him back without any real infringement of his liberty; for liberty consists in doing what one desires, and he does not desire to fall into the river. Nevertheless, when there is not a certainty, but only a danger of mischief, no one but the person himself can judge of the sufficiency of the motive which may prompt him to incur the risk: in this case, therefore, (unless he is a child, or delirious, or in some state of excitement or absorption incompatible with the full use of the reflecting faculty,) he ought, I conceive, to be only warned of the danger; not forcibly prevented from exposing himself to it."

Two things about this puzzle me. First, Mill declares that forcibly turning the man back involves no "real infringement" of his liberty, on the grounds that "liberty consists in doing what one desires, and he does not desire to fall into the river" — but Mill has no actual evidence about what this particular man desires; the man might be in a desperate hurry, might already know perfectly well that the bridge is unsafe and be willing to gamble on it anyway, or might even want to fall in. It looks as though Mill quietly substitutes what a reasonable, fully informed agent would want for what the man in front of him actually wants, and then uses that substitution to define the coercion out of existence. If that is the right reading, doesn't it hand a very ambitious paternalist all the license she needs — any interference can be redescribed as "no real infringement" as long as we are confident enough that we know the agent's "true" desire? Second, I don't understand why mere certainty about the danger is doing so much philosophical work here: the very same act, crossing a possibly unsound bridge, apparently flips from a legitimate object of forcible prevention to an illegitimate one purely because our epistemic confidence about the outcome changes, and not because anything changes about the agent's own capacities, information, or consent. Is that a principled distinction for a liberty-based view to be drawing, or is Mill smuggling in, under cover of the certainty/uncertainty language, some other premise about what no rational person could possibly want?

If you can place the passage within the wider argument of the chapter, I would also value your view on how it sits alongside Mill's much stricter treatment, a little later on, of a person who contracts to sell himself into slavery — a case he settles not by speculating about what the man "really" wants, but by appeal to the principle that "the principle of freedom cannot require that he should be free not to be free." Are the bridge case and the slavery-contract case applications of one and the same rationale for limiting self-regarding choice, or is Mill quietly running together two distinct justifications for overriding a person's own judgment about her own good? I ask partly because defenders of libertarian paternalism are fond of citing the bridge passage as a distinguished ancestor of their own view, and I would like to know whether that lineage actually holds up or is more of a flattering retrospective attribution.
