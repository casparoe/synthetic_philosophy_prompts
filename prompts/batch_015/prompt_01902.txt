Okay so a coworker sent me this blog post about "ergodicity economics" (some physicist named Ole Peters) and it's been living in my head rent free for like a week now. The setup: you flip a coin, heads you gain 50% of your current wealth, tails you loose 40%. Expected value per flip is obviously positive (average multiplier works out to 1.05), so classic decision theory says take this bet as many times as you can. But Peters points out that if you actually simulate one person doing this over and over, their wealth goes to zero almost surely, because what matters for an individual living through time is the time-average growth rate, not the average across a huge population of parallel copies of them, and those two things are only equal when the process is "ergodic," which this one isn't. From there he argues this isn't just a cute paradox - it means expected utility theory has basically been quietly conflating two different kinds of average this whole time, and that maximizing long-run geometric growth (i.e. Kelly betting) isn't just one legit choice among many for people who happen to have log utility, it's the objectively correct thing to do, full stop.

Naturally I went down a rabbit hole and found out this is apparently a decades-old fight, because Paul Samuelson hated the "just always use Kelly" crowd so much that in 1979 he published an entire paper in the Journal of Banking and Finance written only in one-syllable words, basically to mock the idea that maximizing log wealth is THE uniquely rational strategy rather than just one utility function among many equally valid ones. So there's clearly some real substance here and not just a physicist reinventing a spreadsheet formula and feeling smug about it.

I don't have any economics or philosophy background at all (day job is backend systems, closest thing to "probability" I do is writing load-testing scripts), so I honestly can't tell if Peters is pointing at a genuinely deep hole that mainstream econ somehow missed for decades, or if this is really just "log utility has a nice property" dressed up in physics language to sound more objective than it actually is. Could you break down what the actual crux of that disagreement is, at a level where I could explain it to a friend over drinks, instead of just handing me the Kelly formula again?
