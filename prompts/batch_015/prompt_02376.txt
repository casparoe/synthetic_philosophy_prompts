I spent my working life as a particle physicist and only took up the serious study of philosophy after retiring a few years ago, working through the analytic tradition in a somewhat haphazard, self-directed way. Lately I have been stuck, productively I hope, on Bradley's regress: the argument, going back to his 1893 book Appearance and Reality, that a relation cannot do the job of unifying a proposition or a fact, because tying the relation to its relata would itself require a further relation, which would in turn need tying to what it relates, and so on without end. I understand why this is supposed to matter: without some solution, it seems we have no way to distinguish the fact that Desdemona loves Cassio (an example that keeps recurring in this literature) from a mere unordered heap consisting of Desdemona, the loving relation, and Cassio, sitting side by side and doing nothing in particular.

What I cannot get past is that the standard replies all seem to stop one step too soon. Frege says a concept is inherently "unsaturated" and so simply fits together with an object without further glue; Russell distinguishes a relation "actually relating," as it occurs in a genuine proposition, from that same relation taken merely as a term on a list; and Armstrong says a state of affairs holds its constituents together by a "non-mereological" form of composition, which is exactly why aRb can differ from the plain sum of a, R, and b. As a physicist, I was trained to be suspicious of this kind of move. If I told a colleague that two particles attract because there is a "force" between them, that would not yet be physics; it would only become physics once the force was cashed out as a field, a coupling constant, an exchange boson — something with content independent of the very attraction it was invoked to explain. Reading Frege, Russell, and Armstrong side by side, I cannot shake the sense that "unsaturated," "actually relating," and "non-mereological" are doing exactly the job "force" would be doing if I stopped there: each names the very unity in question and then presents the name as though it were an account of it.

I would like you to argue for the following claim: that these three proposals, and any others with the same structure, do not solve Bradley's regress but merely halt it by decree, since each rests on a technical term whose entire content is "whatever it takes to produce unity here," with no independent story about why that is enough to do the job. I am not looking for a survey of the debate, which I have already read in outline; I want the strongest and most precise case you can build for this specific charge, taking each of the three proposals seriously on its own terms rather than dispatching them together with a single generic objection.
