I'm a junior on the elections-reform subcommittee of my high school's student government, and for the past few weeks we've been debating how to fix the way we choose our student body president. Last spring's election was decided when a candidate won with only about 34% of the vote, because the two more "mainstream" candidates split the remaining ballots almost evenly; a lot of students afterward felt that the outcome didn't really reflect the school's preferences. Several committee members, myself included, proposed switching from simple plurality voting to ranked-choice voting (instant runoff), since a couple of nearby schools already use it and it seems designed to avoid exactly this kind of vote-splitting problem.

Our faculty advisor, who studied economics in college, cautioned us against treating ranked-choice voting as a cure-all before we vote on the proposal. She mentioned something called the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem, which she summarized as proving that any reasonable voting rule for three or more candidates can be strategically manipulated by at least some voters, unless the rule is an outright dictatorship. I've since found a few short explanations of the theorem online, but I'm having trouble translating that abstract, rather bleak-sounding mathematical result into practical guidance for an election with roughly 850 students choosing among four or five candidates.

Could you walk me through the most important implications of the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem for a decision like ours? I'd particularly appreciate your thoughts on: (1) whether adopting ranked-choice voting is genuinely worth the transition costs and the extra confusion it might cause among younger students, given that the theorem guarantees it will remain manipulable in principle; (2) which specific strategic behaviors, such as "burying" a strong rival near the bottom of one's ballot or "compromising" by ranking a more electable candidate above one's true favorite, are actually realistic to worry about among a student body that mostly isn't coordinating its votes; and (3) whether an alternative like approval voting, where students simply check off every candidate they find acceptable rather than ranking anyone, genuinely escapes the theorem's conclusion or merely runs into an equivalent problem in a different guise. I would find it much more useful if you could ground your answer in the practical trade-offs of our situation rather than restating the theorem in purely abstract terms.
