I've recently been rereading Stuart Armstrong's 2017 LessWrong sequence on acausal trade, especially the post arguing that acausal trade between two agents behaves like ordinary trade under supply and demand, and therefore converges on the Nash bargaining solution — his running example is an AI called Alice, who only values apples, trading with an AI called Bob, who only values oranges. It's a clean argument, but it has nagged at me ever since I reread Daniel Kokotajlo's "commitment races" post, which observes that when two parties can each simulate, or otherwise reliably predict, how the other reasons, staking out a bargaining position costs essentially nothing — there's no way to burn a bridge or otherwise pay a cost to make a threat credible, as real-world negotiators do — and bluffing is effectively impossible, since your counterpart can read your reasoning off directly rather than infer it from behavior.

Putting those two ideas together worries me. Nash's theorem singles out a unique trade only relative to a specific disagreement point and a specific set of axioms (symmetry and independence of irrelevant alternatives among them); rival solution concepts, like Kalai-Smorodinsky, drop one of those axioms and can recommend a different trade from the very same starting point. Nothing about the acausal setup itself seems to force either party to adopt the axioms, or the disagreement point, that happen to favor its counterpart — and if staking out a bargaining stance is both free and unbluffable, each party seems to have every incentive to just insist on whichever solution concept favors it most.

Could you make this concrete for me? I'd like a fully worked example of two acausal trading partners — specified utility functions and feasible trades, in the spirit of Armstrong's apples-and-oranges case — such that: (a) some trade is a Pareto improvement over no trade for both parties; (b) the Nash solution and at least one other defensible bargaining solution concept recommend different trades from that same setup; and (c) each party has some private fact about itself — its values, its resources, or just its credence that the other party even exists — that gives it a self-interested reason to insist on whichever solution concept and disagreement point favor it. Once the numbers are on the table, I'm curious whether mutual non-trade starts to look like a live risk rather than a merely logical possibility, and whether you can see any principled way to break the tie that doesn't just amount to stipulating that both parties are being "reasonable." This all reminds me a little of the Myerson-Satterthwaite result on trade under private information, except acausal traders don't even get a shared mechanism designer or communication channel to fall back on; that comparison may be a red herring, so feel free to discard it if it doesn't lead anywhere.
