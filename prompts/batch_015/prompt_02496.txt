Random question but it's been rattling around in my head all week, so bear with me. This is my second novel, and since my first one undersold pretty badly, my publisher has gotten way more "data-driven" about everything this time round, including the title. A couple weeks ago they put together a poll and sent it out to my newsletter list and social pages - something like 6,000 people got to vote between the two finalists my editor and I had narrowed things down to. Me and my editor were actually fully aligned on one of them ("A Quieter Kind of Fire"), for reasons that felt pretty solid after months of back and forth - it echoes a line from chapter one, it doesn't give away the twist, it doesn't sound like every other book in the genre right now. The poll itself just asked people which of the two titles they personally liked better, not which one they thought would actually sell the book, and it closed last night with the other title ("The Weight of Ordinary Days") winning by something like 70/30. When I pointed out that liking a title more isn't quite the same question as predicting whether it'll work commercially, my publisher's response was: sure, but any single person's opinion is full of idiosyncratic noise either way, and if you average six thousand of those opinions together the noise cancels out, so the crowd's pick is mathematically more trustworthy than two people's opinions, no matter how many years of experience those two people have between them.

Honestly the noise-cancelling argument sounded pretty convincing to me even after that - isn't that basically the logic behind things like prediction markets? But I brought it up with my writers' group last night and one of them said something like "sure, but that only works if the crowd's actually competent to judge the thing you're asking it to judge," and now I can't stop turning that over, especially given the question-mismatch thing. Part of me just wants to fight for our original pick, but I don't want to loose that fight on the strength of an argument that might not even hold up. So: is "a big enough poll beats a couple of experienced opinions" actually true in general, and if not, what would need to be true about the voters and the question for it to actually apply to a situation like this one?
