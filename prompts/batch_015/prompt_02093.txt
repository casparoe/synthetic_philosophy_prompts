I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on free will and moral responsibility, and I keep coming back to a textual connection I haven't seen properly spelled out anywhere. This term's reading group worked through Galen Strawson's "Basic Argument," which turns on the idea that nothing can be its own cause (nothing can be causa sui): to be truly or ultimately responsible for what one does, Strawson argues, one would have to be responsible for the way one is "at least in certain crucial mental respects," which would in turn require having already made oneself that way — a regress that never gets started, and one that Strawson insists holds whether or not determinism is true.

Separately, for a seminar on nineteenth-century philosophy, I've been rereading Beyond Good and Evil, where Nietzsche, in the aphorism on causa sui (§21), ridicules exactly this ambition, describing the wish to be one's own cause as amounting to wanting "to pull oneself up into existence by the hair, out of the swamps of nothingness." The overlap is close enough that I can't decide whether Strawson is doing anything more than restating Nietzsche's point in the idiom of analytic philosophy, or whether something genuinely different is going on.

A few things push me toward the latter view: Strawson wants a conclusion that is neutral on the determinism debate, whereas Nietzsche's aphorism is bound up with his wider polemic against the Christian-moral picture of an accountable, punishable subject; and Strawson proceeds by a fairly austere regress argument, while Nietzsche's method looks more like diagnosis or unmasking than proof. I'd like help thinking through how much these differences actually matter. Concretely: is there anything Strawson's argument secures that Nietzsche's aphorism doesn't, or vice versa, and if you had to judge which of the two mounts the more convincing case against ultimate moral responsibility, which would it be and why? I'd rather you engage with what each text is actually doing than simply treat Nietzsche as a rhetorical anticipation of Strawson.
