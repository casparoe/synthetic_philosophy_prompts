I teach a graduate seminar in formal and social epistemology, and this week's session was built around Aumann's 1976 paper "Agreeing to Disagree," and in particular the result that two Bayesian agents who share a common prior cannot have common knowledge of differing posteriors for the same event. After class, two of my students, Owen and Maya, kept arguing about it in the hallway long enough that I ended up transcribing most of it, since I was too busy locking up the seminar room to referee in real time. I'd like your help adjudicating before we pick the thread back up next week.

Owen opened by insisting the theorem has real bite outside the seminar room: if two people are honest and reasoning correctly, and each has fully explained their evidence to the other, then their continuing to report different credences about some contested empirical question shows that at least one of them is failing to be rational, or isn't being fully candid about what they believe. Maya replied that this is too quick, since the theorem's conclusion is conditional on a common prior, and nothing guarantees that two careful, well-meaning people start out with the same prior probabilities -- especially in cases where the actual disagreement concerns which background assumptions or reference classes are the right ones to bring to the evidence in the first place. Since the antecedent isn't satisfied, she said, the theorem simply has nothing to say about the disagreements we actually have. Owen answered that this concedes more than it denies: if two equally careful, equally informed reasoners end up with different priors, that difference is itself a fact calling for explanation, and absent some story about why the difference is legitimate rather than a mistake, a bare appeal to "different priors" is not an escape from his conclusion but a restatement of it. Maya's rejoinder was that this last step quietly imports a much stronger and highly contested thesis -- that a given body of evidence rationally permits only one doxastic state in response to it -- and that this thesis is doing all of the real work; Aumann's proof is a conditional result about a stipulated model, silent on where priors come from, and doesn't get Owen that further thesis for free.

Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each stage, rather than just whether Owen or Maya ultimately wins overall? I'd also like you to flag anything both of them seem to be taking for granted that doesn't actually hold up -- for instance, about what common knowledge of a posterior would really require in a case like theirs, or about what the common-prior assumption is supposed to represent in the first place. Please close with a verdict on its own line, in exactly this format: \answer{Owen} or \answer{Maya}, whichever of them you think is more right overall.
