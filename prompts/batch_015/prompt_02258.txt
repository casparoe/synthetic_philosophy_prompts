I've been getting into some effective altruism and rationalist-adjacent reading lately (mostly 80,000 Hours articles and a few old LessWrong threads), and I keep running into the idea of "moral uncertainty" — the claim that, quite apart from being uncertain about facts, we should also hedge our actions against the possibility that we've got the wrong ethical theory (utilitarianism, Kantian deontology, virtue ethics, and so on). From what I can tell, this is treated as a serious, unresolved problem in academic philosophy, with whole papers devoted to it. But the way it's described sounds to me like something my own field already knows how to handle.

At work, when we don't fully trust any single model, we don't just pick our favorite and go all-in on it — we ensemble. Each model gets a weight, based on something like validation performance or a prior over which one best reflects the true data-generating process; each model outputs a score or recommendation for the option in question; and we combine those into a single expected-value estimate, which is what we actually act on. The better a model has performed historically, the more it dominates the blend, but no single model gets to unilaterally decide the outcome unless we're placing all our credence in it.

This seems like a ready-made template for moral uncertainty: put a credence on each ethical theory, have each theory rate the available actions, weight those ratings by your credence, and act on whatever comes out highest in expectation — Bayesian model averaging, just applied to ethics instead of statistics. So I'm genuinely puzzled about why philosophers who work on this treat it as such an open, contested problem, with rival frameworks and no consensus solution, rather than as something ordinary decision theory already settles. Is there something about moral theories in particular that breaks this analogy, or is academic philosophy just behind the curve here?
