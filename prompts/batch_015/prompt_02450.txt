I spent about 35 years as an observational astrophysicist before retiring, and these days I fill the time reading philosophy, of all things (my wife thinks its just a phase, we'll see). A friend from my old reading group, she did her PhD in logic, got me hooked a few months back on the anthropic reasoning literature, specifically the fight between the self-sampling assumption (SSA) and the self-indication assumption (SIA), and Bostrom's presumptuous philosopher case that's suppose to be the knock-down argument against SIA. It rang a bell because back in the 90s and 2000s I sat through plenty of cosmology seminars where people were quietly leaning on this exact kind of anthropic reasoning to deal with the measure problem in eternal inflation, usually without ever naming SSA or SIA out loud, they'd just pick whichever assumption spat out a sensible-looking number for the calculation at hand.

What I'm trying to work out now is this: what are the most important implications of favoring SIA over SSA (or the other way round) for how confident we should be in "civilizations like ours are common" versus "civilizations like ours are vanishingly rare" answers to the Fermi paradox, given that all we know for sure is that at least one such civilization, us, exists? I don't want the version of this answer I keep running into online, which basically amounts to "SIA avoids the doomsday argument, but then Bostrom nails it with the presumptuous philosopher, the end" - I've already got that part. What I can't find spelled out anywhere is whether SSA has an equally embarrassing skeleton in its closet, something to do with the reference class problem, or those Adam and Eve cases Bostrom himself cooks up, that ought to make us just as suspicious of SSA-flavored Fermi paradox reasoning as we are of the SIA-flavored kind. And if you actually think one of the two views is better supported overall, just say so plainly instead of handing me a diplomatic "reasonable people disagree" shrug.
