I'm taking a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of dispositions this term, and the last couple of weeks have been devoted to the conditional analysis and its classic problem cases: Martin's electro-fink, Johnston and Bird on masking and antidotes, and Lewis's attempt to patch things up with his "reformed" conditional analysis. For our short response paper, we each had to invent a new case that tests one of these ideas, and at a study session last week two people in my cohort, Nadia and Theo, spent the better part of an evening arguing over whether Nadia's proposed case actually worked. The rest of us ended up split down the middle, and since office hours aren't until next week, I'd like an outside, independent verdict.

Nadia's case: take an ordinary smartphone whose glass screen is, by any reasonable standard, fragile -- dropped from waist height onto pavement, it shatters. Now suppose the same phone is fitted with a rugged case whose padding absorbs shock, such that the cased phone will never shatter from any drop within the range it's rated to survive. (1) Nadia argued the cased phone is still fragile: the glass panel's molecular structure -- its causal basis for shattering -- is completely untouched by the case, so the disposition persists even though its manifestation is masked. (2) Theo objected that this drains "fragile" of content: if the phone can never actually be made to break under any condition we could produce, we should just say the cased phone isn't fragile, full stop, and reserve the word for the bare panel. (3) Nadia replied that this has an absurd consequence: peeling the case off would then have to create fragility out of nothing at the instant of removal, since nothing about the glass itself changes when the case comes off. (4) Theo answered that nothing gets created -- we've simply been specifying "fragile" too loosely all along, the way "poisonous" really means "lethal if ingested without an antidote"; here "fragile" should be read as "breaks if struck without a protective case," so the correction belongs in the stimulus condition, not in the object. (5) Nadia countered that this move can't be made in advance: nobody could have written "without a protective case" into the meaning of "fragile" before rugged cases existed, and if some wholly new protective material is invented next year, Theo would have to say our definition had been quietly wrong the whole time -- which suggests masking is a genuine phenomenon, not just an artifact of a lazily specified stimulus. (6) Theo then proposed a variant: the case has no padding at all, only an accelerometer that detects free fall and fires internal shock-absorbing struts a fraction of a second before impact. He claimed this is strictly worse for Nadia's position, since the phone's own physical state now changes in direct response to the very process that would otherwise test it, so a view resting on "the intrinsic basis stays fixed" cannot afford to treat this the same way it treats an ordinary mask. Nadia hadn't managed a reply before we had to clear the room.

Could you go through the six moves in order and say who has the better of the argument at each one? They needn't split evenly -- either side could be right, or wrong, more than once -- and please flag anything you think both of them are simply missing. I'd also value your own take on move (6) specifically: is the accelerometer variant best classified as a fink, a mask, or something that resists that distinction altogether, and is Theo actually right that it poses a worse problem for Nadia's position than the original padded case did?
