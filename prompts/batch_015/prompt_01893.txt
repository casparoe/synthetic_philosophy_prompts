I am putting together the session on ancient theories of knowledge for my graduate seminar this term, and I find that I still don't teach one particular stretch of the Meno as well as I would like. The stock story is familiar enough: Socrates gets Meno to agree that a true opinion about the road to Larissa guides a traveler just as reliably as knowledge of the road would, Meno then asks why knowledge should be prized more highly than correct opinion if that is so, and Socrates answers with the simile of Daedalus's runaway statues. Most of us, myself included, tend to compress the payoff into "Plato is anticipating the justified-true-belief analysis of knowledge" and move on to the next dialogue. Sitting with the passage itself, though, in Jowett's translation, I think it is doing something more specific than that compression suggests, and I would like help thinking it through properly before I teach it again next week.

Here is the passage (Meno, 97a-98a, trans. Benjamin Jowett):

SOCRATES: I will explain. If a man knew the way to Larisa, or anywhere else, and went to the place and led others thither, would he not be a right and good guide?

MENO: Certainly.

SOCRATES: And a person who had a right opinion about the way, but had never been and did not know, might be a good guide also, might he not?

MENO: Certainly.

SOCRATES: And while he has true opinion about that which the other knows, he will be just as good a guide if he thinks the truth, as he who knows the truth?

MENO: Exactly.

SOCRATES: Then true opinion is as good a guide to correct action as knowledge; and that was the point which we omitted in our speculation about the nature of virtue, when we said that knowledge only is the guide of right action; whereas there is also right opinion.

MENO: True.

SOCRATES: Then right opinion is not less useful than knowledge?

MENO: The difference, Socrates, is only that he who has knowledge will always be right; but he who has right opinion will sometimes be right, and sometimes not.

SOCRATES: What do you mean? Can he be wrong who has right opinion, so long as he has right opinion?

MENO: I admit the cogency of your argument, and therefore, Socrates, I wonder that knowledge should be preferred to right opinion—or why they should ever differ.

SOCRATES: And shall I explain this wonder to you?

MENO: Do tell me.

SOCRATES: You would not wonder if you had ever observed the images of Daedalus; but perhaps you have not got them in your country?

MENO: What have they to do with the question?

SOCRATES: Because they require to be fastened in order to keep them, and if they are not fastened they will play truant and run away.

MENO: Well, what of that?

SOCRATES: I mean to say that they are not very valuable possessions if they are at liberty, for they will walk off like runaway slaves; but when fastened, they are of great value, for they are really beautiful works of art. Now this is an illustration of the nature of true opinions: while they abide with us they are beautiful and fruitful, but they run away out of the human soul, and do not remain long, and therefore they are not of much value until they are fastened by the tie of the cause; and this fastening of them, friend Meno, is recollection, as you and I have agreed to call it. But when they are bound, in the first place, they have the nature of knowledge; and, in the second place, they are abiding. And this is why knowledge is more honourable and excellent than true opinion, because fastened by a chain.

MENO: What you are saying, Socrates, seems to be very like the truth.

Two things about this stretch of text genuinely puzzle me. First, Socrates flatly identifies the "fastening" of true opinion by "the tie of the cause" with recollection itself: "this fastening of them, friend Meno, is recollection, as you and I have agreed to call it." That sits oddly next to what he says a little earlier about the slave boy's newly recovered true belief about the diagonal, which he describes as merely "stirred up in him, as in a dream," adding that only repeated questioning would let the boy "know as well as any one at last." That remark already seems to distinguish a dream-like, unstable recovery of true opinion from some further process that would turn it into secure knowledge—which sounds very like what "fastening" is supposed to do in the later passage. So is recollection, on Plato's own account, already graded or two-stage, with a "dream" phase yielding mere true opinion and a completed phase yielding knowledge, so that the Daedalus image just re-describes that same difference in figurative terms? Or is Socrates using "recollection" more loosely across the two passages than such a tidy reading would allow—and if so, what, if anything, does the Daedalus passage add to what we were already told about the slave boy?

Second, I would like help pressure-testing the "proto-JTB" reading itself. The phrase behind "the tie of the cause" is aitias logismos, and I am no longer confident that "giving an account of the reason why" is best read on the model of modern epistemic justification—having adequate grounds or evidence for a belief—rather than on the model of grasping an explanation of why the belief is true, which sounds closer to a causal theory of knowledge than to a justification condition. Does anything in the passage, or in how the Larissa example sets it up, favor one of these readings over the other? And does the choice actually matter for what we take "the Meno problem" to be a problem about—the addition of justification to true belief, specifically, or something closer to explanatory or causal grounding?
