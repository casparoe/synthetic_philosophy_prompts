I'm an econ minor, and this semester I'm taking an elective on market design that's still pretty abstract to me most days (lots of toy examples involving colored balls in urns) — but it did get me thinking about a pattern that's honestly bugged me for years in my fantasy football league, and I'd love an outside opinion on it.

Every trade season, I've noticed that whether I actually go through with a trade I've proposed depends a lot on how the other manager reacts, not just on the players being swapped. If someone takes a while to respond, or counters with something slightly worse for me, I feel totally fine pulling the trigger. But if they accept almost instantly, or come back offering to sweeten the deal even more than I asked for, I get this immediate gut feeling that I'm about to get fleeced, and I'll usually back out or try renegotiate — even though nothing about my own team, or what I know about the players, has actually changed in that moment. (It's not just fantasy football either: I get the same jumpy feeling trading cards with people, or that one time I tried to offload a concert ticket in a group chat and the buyer got weirdly enthusiastic.)

My roommate Deepak thinks this is basically superstition. His argument is that a trade is either good for my roster or it isn't, based on the actual players changing hands, and that how eager or happy the other guy seems when he hits "accept" is just noise that can't retroactively change the value of what I'm receiving — so factoring it in is just self-sabotage dressed up as instinct. I kind of see his point, but it still feels like their eagerness is telling me something real, since presumably they know things about their own roster, plans, and needs that I don't. So who's actually right here: is Deepak correct that the other person's reaction is strategically irrelevant noise and I should just evaluate every trade purely on its own terms, or is my gut onto something that's actually defensible?
