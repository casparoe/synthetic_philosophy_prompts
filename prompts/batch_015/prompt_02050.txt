I spent thirty-five years as an experimental physicist before retiring, and I've since been working through philosophy of science on my own, which is how I ended up thinking hard about Popper's notion of verisimilitude. At a reunion dinner last month, two former colleagues fell into an argument about it that I haven't managed to settle myself, and I'd like your help going through it properly.

(1) Elena opened by claiming that Rutherford's nuclear model of the atom is straightforwardly closer to the truth than Thomson's plum-pudding model, which it replaced: both retain the electron and the atom's overall neutrality, but Rutherford corrects Thomson's mistake about where the positive charge and mass actually sit, and that, she said, is exactly what Popper's verisimilitude was coined to capture.

(2) Marcus replied that she doesn't get this for free: Miller and Tichý proved in 1974 that Popper's formal definition only ranks two theories when both are true, so between any two false theories — which is all we ever actually have in real science — it delivers no verdict at all. 'Closer to the truth' was doing no formal work, he said, when she invoked Popper by name.

(3) Elena answered that this makes scientific progress collapse into an illusion, since almost no theory we've held has turned out to be strictly true, and that a piece of formal bookkeeping shouldn't force us to call Thomson and Rutherford equally mistaken.

(4) Marcus granted that the intuition survives but said the honest position is that no successor definition proposed since 1974 commands anything like consensus, so what Elena has is a strong hunch dressed in borrowed technical vocabulary.

(5) Elena closed by accusing him of an isolated demand for rigor, since notions like 'simpler' or 'more precise' also lack finished formal analyses, and nobody treats that as disqualifying.

Could you go through the five moves and say who has the better of it at each step, not just overall, and flag anything you think both of them are missing?
