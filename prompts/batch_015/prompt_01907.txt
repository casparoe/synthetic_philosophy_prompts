In my decision theory seminar, we treated the St. Petersburg paradox as essentially closed once expected monetary value is replaced by expected utility: a strictly concave function like Bernoulli's logarithmic utility renders the gamble's expected utility finite, since concavity just encodes diminishing marginal utility of wealth. That seems to imply that any risk-averse agent, in this technical sense, should be automatically immune to the whole family of St. Petersburg-style problems. But I recall coming across suggestions in the literature that this isn't actually the end of the story. What exactly is still unresolved here, and why wouldn't mere concavity be enough?
