A friend and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about whether it can ever be rational to prefer not knowing something. The trigger was her ongoing indecision about taking a genetic test for a late-onset hereditary condition that, even if the result came back positive, she would have no way of treating or preventing. I argued that her reluctance to test sat uneasily with a result I had come across a while ago: I. J. Good's 1967 proof that, for a Bayesian decision-maker, the expected value of free and relevant information can never be negative, and is generally strictly positive whenever the information is even slightly likely to change what one would otherwise do. On my reading, this seemed to settle the matter in favor of testing.

She was unconvinced, and pushed back with a handful of cases that, the more I thought about them, seemed like genuine trouble for my glib appeal to the theorem rather than mere emotional resistance to it: a mutually beneficial insurance contract that would unravel the moment either party learned a fact that current ignorance is quietly protecting; a negotiator who is better off not learning her counterpart's true reservation price, since she can then never be tempted, or pressured, into revealing it; and simply the fact that people who receive an accurate terminal prognosis often report being made worse off by the news even when it changes nothing about their treatment options. Neither of us could say with any precision whether these are real counterexamples to Good's theorem, cases the theorem was never meant to cover, or situations where the theorem is technically vindicated but somehow still misses the point.

I would like your help thinking this through more rigorously than we managed over dinner. Could you first explain, with enough precision that the assumptions are fully visible, what Good's theorem actually shows and for whom? Then, rather than stopping at the formal result, I would like you to set out what you take to be its most important implications and limits for how a person should actually reason about seeking out information, in personal decisions like my friend's as well as in professional or strategic ones. I would especially appreciate it if you could work through my friend's three examples along the way and tell us plainly where each one lands.
