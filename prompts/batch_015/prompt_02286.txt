For my school's Victorian literature elective, I've spent the past couple of weeks working through Middlemarch and Silas Marner alongside a handful of George Eliot's essays and letters, and what has really caught my attention is the particular shape of her unbelief. She was raised quite evangelical, translated Strauss's Life of Jesus as a young woman, and later produced the first English translation of Feuerbach's The Essence of Christianity—and yet by 1873 she could apparently tell F. W. H. Myers, on a walk through the Fellows' Garden at Trinity, that of the three words "God, Immortality, Duty," the first was inconceivable and the second unbelievable, while the third remained "peremptory and absolute." She seems to have wanted to keep all of religion's moral seriousness while relocating it entirely onto human relationships.

This term, a few people in my school's ethics bowl group have gotten really into effective altruism—GiveWell-style cost-effectiveness comparisons, the drowning-child thought experiment, earning-to-give, and so on—and I couldn't help wondering how Eliot would have reacted to it, since it also presents itself as a rigorous, quite demanding, non-religious way of taking morality seriously. What complicates my guess is a line from her 1856 essay "The Natural History of German Life," where she argues that "the greatest benefit we owe to the artist... is the extension of our sympathies," and adds that "appeals founded on generalizations and statistics require a sympathy ready-made, a moral sentiment already in activity"—as if statistics could never generate moral concern on their own, only ever presuppose a sympathy that already exists. On its face, that line reads almost like a rebuttal aimed in advance at a movement that leans so heavily on spreadsheets and impartial cost-effectiveness calculations to redirect people's charity.

So here is what I would like help working through: given what you know of Eliot's actual moral outlook—her Feuerbach-inflected humanism, her insistence that sympathy has to be cultivated through particular, imagined lives rather than demanded by abstract argument, and even the ending of Middlemarch, where Dorothea's goodness works through "unhistoric acts" and an influence that is "incalculably diffusive" rather than anything you could tally up—do you think she would have recognized effective altruism as a continuation of her own project of extending sympathy past "the bounds of our personal lot," or would she have thought it had the psychology of moral motivation backwards, trying to manufacture concern for strangers out of statistics rather than out of sympathy that has to be cultivated first? I would rather see the reasoning worked through in detail, with reference to specific aspects of her novels and essays, than get a quick verdict either way.
