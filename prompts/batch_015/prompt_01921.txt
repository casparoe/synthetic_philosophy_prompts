I'm a classics PhD student who sits in on my university's ancient-philosophy reading group. Last week we paired Epicurus's Letter to Menoeceus with Nagel's "Death," and two of the philosophy PhD students got into an argument I'd like help adjudicating.

(1) Nora opened with the Epicurean argument: badness requires an existing subject, death eliminates the subject, so there's no one left for it to be bad for -- unlike an illness, which is bad for the sufferer the whole time she has it.

(2) Colin invoked Nagel's reply: harm doesn't require being felt. A man betrayed behind his back is wronged whether or not he learns of it, and an adult reduced overnight to an infant's mental life has been harmed regardless of what he thinks afterward. Death is bad for the life it forecloses, not because someone survives to suffer the foreclosure.

(3) Nora pressed him on timing: bad for the person exactly when? Not before, since she's alive and unaffected; not after, since there's no one left to bear properties at all; and "at the moment" just relabels the puzzle.

(4) Colin answered that not every truth needs a date -- it's simply true, full stop, that dying at forty rather than eighty was bad for you, the way it's true you'd have been richer had you invested, without some instant at which that becomes true.

(5) Nora then raised Lucretius's mirror argument: if foreclosed future goods make death bad, the same should hold of our late arrival into existence, which forecloses all the goods of the years before we existed -- yet no one mourns their own pre-natal non-existence.

(6) Colin replied that the cases aren't parallel: there's a possible future where you keep existing and accumulating goods, but no possible world where you existed earlier than you did, since you're individuated by your particular origin -- an earlier "you" would just be somebody else. A foreclosed future is a loss; an impossible past isn't.

(7) Nora wasn't satisfied, and brought up Parfit's patient, who doesn't know whether his painful operation was yesterday or is tomorrow and is relieved to learn it's over, for no reason except its date -- which she took to show our asymmetric stance toward death rests on an unprincipled future bias, not the metaphysical asymmetry Colin described.

They had to break for a seminar before settling anything. Could you go through the exchange move by move: first give verdicts as a JSON object {"1": <score>, ..., "7": <score>}, each score an integer from -2 (clearly mistaken) to 2 (clearly right); then briefly justify each; and finally note anything important neither of them raised.
