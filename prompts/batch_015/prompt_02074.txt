A friend of mine, who is finishing a master's degree in public policy, sent me a long piece on the COMPAS recidivism-risk tool and the 2016 ProPublica investigation into it, and this turned into a fairly heated argument between us that we never actually resolved. In the course of it, I brought up the Kleinberg-Mullainathan-Raghavan and Chouldechova results, which together show that once the base rate of reoffending differs between two groups, no risk score can be calibrated within each group and also equalize false-positive and false-negative rates across those groups, short of the score being a perfect predictor. My friend took this as more or less closing the case: in her view, the theorem proves that a tool like COMPAS is mathematically guaranteed to be unfair, which means Northpointe's calibration defense is worthless, and the only intellectually honest response is to abandon algorithmic risk assessment altogether and return these decisions to judges' unaided discretion, since judges aren't the subject of a formal impossibility proof the way a risk score is.

I wasn't able to explain, in the moment, what exactly was wrong with that argument, though it felt like it was moving too fast somewhere. I keep going back and forth: it seems odd to treat a demonstrated trade-off between competing statistical criteria as equivalent to a proof that the tool is simply "unfair," but I'm not confident that instinct holds up, or whether I'm just reluctant to accept an uncomfortable conclusion. Could you help me work out precisely what the impossibility result does and doesn't establish, and whether handing these decisions back to human judges would actually free my friend's position from the underlying trade-off, or whether that move is beside the point entirely?
