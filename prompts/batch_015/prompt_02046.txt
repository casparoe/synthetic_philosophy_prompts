I'm about four drafts into a novel where the narrator spends the whole book insisting he "didn't have a choice" about leaving his wife for his brother's fiancée, and I want his self-deception to feel philosophically real rather than just "he's lying to himself," so I've been back in the bad faith chapter of Sartre's Being and Nothingness for research. Made the mistake of bringing it up over dinner with two old friends from my philosophy MA, Naomi and Julian, and it derailed the whole evening into an argument neither of them would let go of.

(1) Naomi opened by saying the whole concept is incoherent on Sartre's own terms: bad faith is supposed to be lying to yourself, and lying requires a deceiver who has the truth and a victim who doesn't, so if deceiver and deceived are literally the same consciousness, the "victim" already has the truth the instant it gets hidden, meaning nothing has actually been concealed -- the project fails before you even get to the waiter or the coquette. (2) Julian came back with the standard reply: Sartre isn't claiming you reflectively hold and reflectively deny one fact at the same moment, he's saying you can have a merely non-thetic, pre-reflective awareness of something -- the coquette registering, without dwelling on it, what it means that she's left her hand in her date's -- that never gets promoted to an explicit, reflective belief, so "knowing" and "not knowing" sit at different levels instead of flatly contradicting each other. (3) Naomi said that just relocates the paradox rather than closing it: to avoid reflecting on that fact specifically, instead of just not reflecting on anything, the evasion has to be aimed at it, and aiming your avoidance at one particular target requires already picking that target out clearly enough to steer around it, which smuggles back in exactly the reflective grip on the fact that the whole pre-reflective story was suppose to let you avoid needing. (4) Julian said that's precisely why Sartre calls it a "metastable" project rather than a settled state, something you have to keep re-choosing moment to moment instead of deciding once and simply having -- and it's also the whole basis of his objection to Freud, since a censor that "decides" what to repress would already have to recognize the very material it's hiding well enough to hide it, which just hands the paradox to a little person inside your head instead of solving it. Julians point was that at least Sartre's version doesn't need the homunculus, even if it needs constant upkeep instead. (5) Naomi's closer was that this concedes her point: if bad faith is an endlessly-renewed attempt that never actually lands, nobody is ever simply *in* it, which sits badly against Sartre constantly talking about the waiter and the coquette as people who just are in bad faith, not as people perpetually almost-failing at deceiving themselves -- swapping "state" for "project" quietly changes the phenomenon instead of rescuing the account of it.

Nobody at the table budged and it's been rattling around in my head since. Could you go through the five moves and say, at each step, who actually had the better of it and why -- not just an overall winner -- and flag anything you think both of them are missing that a real Sartre scholar would bring up. If any of it also helps me figure out whether my narrator can be honestly, coherently self-deceived in the Sartrean sense, great, but that part's on me -- I'm mainly just curious who's actually right here.
