I spent my career as an evolutionary biologist and have only taken up philosophy in earnest since retiring, so this may be a familiar puzzle to those better versed in the literature than I am. Working through Popper on natural selection, I keep running into what looks like a genuine fault line in his thought. In his intellectual autobiography and related essays, he argues that natural selection, on its standard formulation, comes uncomfortably close to the tautology "survival of the fittest = survival of the survivors," and on that basis downgrades Darwinism from a testable theory to a "metaphysical research programme" -- a verdict he only partially retracted in the paper later published as "Natural Selection and the Emergence of Mind." Yet elsewhere Popper builds his entire account of how scientific knowledge grows on explicitly Darwinian foundations, with bold conjectures standing in for blind variation and criticism standing in for natural selection, weeding out error much as predation or famine weeds out the unfit.

Could you compare these two roles that Darwinian thinking plays in Popper's philosophy: his skeptical verdict on natural selection's scientific credentials, and his own selectionist model of how science itself progresses? Do the two sit together consistently, or does his epistemology quietly rely on exactly the sort of theory his philosophy of biology treats with suspicion? And does his later recantation genuinely dissolve the tension, or does some version of the original tautology worry simply resurface once "conjecture and refutation" is itself cast in selectionist terms?
