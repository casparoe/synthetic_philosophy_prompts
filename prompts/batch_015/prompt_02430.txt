I'm working on a piece for the ideas section about philosophers whose technical work seems to secretly power their politics, and Michael Dummett keeps coming up as the perfect case study. Most people who've run into him at all know him as "the anti-realism guy" — the view that the meaning of a sentence is fixed by our capacity to verify or assert it, not by some mind-independent truth-condition that holds regardless of what we could ever check. But he was also one of the fiercest philosophical campaigners for migrants and refugees in Britain, co-founding activist groups back in the 60s and eventually writing a whole book about it late in life — On Immigration and Refugees, I think it's called.

My editor loves the idea that there's a straight logical line running from one to the other: if what's true isn't fixed by mind-independent facts but by what we can actually verify or justify, doesn't that undercut the whole idea that there's some mind-independent fact of the matter about who "really" belongs inside a countries borders? That would nicely explain why he thought the burden of proof always sat with the state, not the migrant, whenever it wanted to turn someone away. I ran this by a philosopher friend at a dinner party and she just got a funny look on her face but couldn't say what was wrong with it before we got pulled into another conversation. I want to open the article with this connection — can you tell me whether that's actually how Dummett's argument works, or whether I'm just bolting two unrelated parts of his career together because they happen to share an author?
