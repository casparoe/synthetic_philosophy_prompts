I've spent a long time following debates about consequentialist cluelessness on the EA Forum and LessWrong. The distinction that has stuck with me most is Hilary Greaves's split between "simple" cluelessness — unforeseeable effects that plausibly cancel out by symmetry and so can reasonably be set aside — and "complex" cluelessness, where we have concrete, structured reasons to expect an action's downstream effects to be large, yet those reasons pull in opposite directions with no principled way to weigh them against each other. (Her own example: averting malaria deaths might be good or bad for the far future depending on its effect on population size.) I'd like to see complex cluelessness dramatized rather than argued over. Please write a short story, under about 1,200 words, in which a character who takes this problem seriously has to make an ordinary, non-cosmic altruistic decision — funding a charity, having a child, intervening in a stranger's life — rather than a trolley problem or a version of Lenman's own "ancestor of Hitler" example. Let the character's awareness of complex cluelessness genuinely shape the plot and their choice, rather than merely appear as philosophical monologue, and resist giving the story a tidy verdict on whether they acted rightly.
