ok so I fell down a rabbit hole a while back reading old LW threads on Pascal's mugging and unbounded utility, and someone linked Nover and Hajek's "Pasadena paradox" as supposedly even worse than St Petersburg. The setup (they call it the "Pasadena game"): flip a fair coin til first heads; if that happens on flip n (prob 2^-n as usual), you get paid (-1)^(n-1) * 2^n / n, so the payoffs blow up in size and alternate between wins and losses forever. Unlike St Petersburg, where the expected value is just +infinity, this one's expected value is apparently undefined, full stop -- because the resulting series (1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 ...) only converges conditionally, so you can reorder the terms to sum to literally any value you like, or to +/- infinity, or to nothing at all.

I definitly get the math part (Riemann rearrangement and all that), but I don't have any real intuition for why it should matter to someone just deciding whether to play the game once -- the payoffs and their probabilities are completely fixed no matter what order I happen to add them up in my own head. Why does an arbitrary fact about summation order translate into genuine arbitrariness in what the gamble is actually worth? And why do people treat "undefined" as a worse verdict for decision theory than St Petersburg's plain "infinite," rather than a more forgiving one? Give me the intuitive version of what's really going on, not just "it's conditionally convergent so Riemann's theorem applies."
