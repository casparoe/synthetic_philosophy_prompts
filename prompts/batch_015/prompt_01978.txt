I spent my working life as a physicist, and John von Neumann's name has followed me since graduate school -- through game theory, computer architecture, and the mathematical foundations of quantum theory. Since retiring I've begun reading philosophy in earnest, and I recently returned to his 1955 Fortune essay "Can We Survive Technology?" Two of its claims sit uneasily together. Von Neumann warned that global climate control was coming and would inevitably be turned to advantage in great-power rivalry, writing that "we should not deceive ourselves: once such possibilities become actual, they will be exploited." Yet elsewhere in the same essay he rejected, as a general strategy, the idea that technological danger could be managed by singling out one alarming capability for suppression, arguing that the crisis "will not be resolved by inhibiting this or that apparently particularly obnoxious form of technology," precisely because a technology's usefulness and its danger tend to be inseparable.

Today more than 600 climate scientists and governance scholars have proposed exactly the kind of remedy his second claim seems to rule out: an international non-use agreement banning both research on and deployment of solar geoengineering -- the same global climate-control technology von Neumann had in mind, now pursued as a possible fix for a crisis rather than as a weapon. Working only from what von Neumann actually argued, and not from his general reputation as a Cold War hawk or a techno-optimist, tell me how you think he would have judged this proposed moratorium, and why. I want a definite verdict, defended -- not simply a survey of considerations on both sides.
