I'm a software engineer, and a few of us at work run an informal lunchtime reading group that works through papers and blog posts on machine learning theory. A couple of weeks ago we were going through the standard material on Solomonoff induction and Kolmogorov complexity (the idea that a hypothesis's prior probability should track the length of its shortest description on some universal machine), and it set off a real argument between two of the regulars, Vikram and Elena, that the rest of us couldn't referee and that has stuck with me since.

(1) Vikram opened by claiming that Kolmogorov complexity finally puts Occam's razor on rigorous footing: the invariance theorem guarantees that for any two universal machines, the complexity each one assigns to a string differs by at most a constant that depends on the two machines but not on the string, so "simplicity" is a machine-independent property of a hypothesis rather than a matter of taste. (2) Elena objected that the constant is doing a lot of hiding: it can be made as large as you like, and for any two specific hypotheses you actually care about, say two rival candidate physical laws, you can always build a machine that has one of them wired in as a primitive so it gets complexity O(1) while the other stays complex; the invariance theorem is a bound that holds for every string, yes, but nothing stops the bound itself from being astronomically large for the one pair you happen to care about, so the promised objectivity isn't there exactly when it would matter. (3) Vikram replied that this proves too much, since nobody seriously proposes using gerrymandered machines: any two general-purpose programming languages or Turing machine encodings that people would actually use can simulate one another via a compiler or interpreter of fairly modest length, so among the languages anyone would seriously propose, the complexity rankings of everyday hypotheses agree up to small constants, and Elena's worry only bites for machines built after the fact specifically to favor one string. (4) Elena's rejoinder was that "reasonable" or "not gerrymandered" is exactly the extra ingredient Kolmogorov complexity was supposed to make unnecessary: if you need an independent, prior notion of which machines count as natural to get a determinate ranking at all, then it's that notion, and not the complexity measure, doing the real work, and it quietly reintroduces the very judgment about which theory "looks simple" that the whole apparatus was invoked to formalize away.

Neither of them budged, and everyone else in the group had an opinion but nobody could actually settle it. I can follow the math well enough, but I have no background in philosophy of science and honestly can't tell how much weight the more philosophical moves deserve relative to the technical ones.
