Over the past few months my family has been dealing with something I still haven't worked out how to feel about. My younger brother spent roughly two years lying to our parents about money, disappearing for days at a stretch, and, on one bad night, shoving our father hard enough that my mother called it assault at the time — behavior so unlike him that we had more or less stopped including him in family life. This spring a scan turned up a meningioma that had apparently been pressing on his frontal lobe for years; it has since been removed, and by every outward measure he is back to being himself. My parents have already filed the whole episode under "that wasn't really him," and I want to feel the same way, but I've noticed that my resentment about certain specific things — the money, and especially that night with my father — hasn't actually dissolved just because I've revised my beliefs about how much he's to blame for it.

A friend who is finishing a philosophy PhD pointed me toward P. F. Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" when I tried to explain this to her, and it has clarified the problem for me even though it hasn't solved it. Strawson distinguishes excusing a particular act (he didn't know, he was pushed) from exempting a person more broadly from the whole system of what he calls "participant" reactive attitudes — resentment, indignation, gratitude, and their relatives — in favor of the detached "objective" attitude we adopt toward small children, the severely mentally ill, or others whose capacities for ordinary interpersonal relations are compromised. What strikes me is his claim that stepping wholesale into the objective attitude toward someone, even on good evidence, is not simply available to us at will, and isn't obviously something we should want even when it is available, since adopting it means treating the other person as someone to be managed or worked around rather than someone you can blame, thank, or reason with as an equal.

I'd like you to write a short story of somewhere around 1,000-1,500 words built around exactly this tension, rather than a generic "was it really him" narrative. Feel free to invent your own characters and situation rather than borrowing mine — a hospital waiting room, a courtroom, a family dinner years later, whatever serves the story best. What matters to me is that the piece earns its philosophical content: its central character should be caught between a well-supported, third-personal reason to exempt someone from responsibility and a first-personal reactive attitude that refuses to simply defer to it, and the story should make felt why this isn't merely a failure of emotional housekeeping on the character's part, but a case where something Strawson thought genuinely valuable is at stake in whether the shift to objectivity gets made at all.
