Two weeks ago I started as a research assistant in a lab that studies disorders of consciousness—mostly EEG and fMRI work with patients in the minimally conscious state—and my supervisor has asked me to draft the background section of a grant renewal motivating our reliance on higher-order theories of consciousness as an interpretive framework (the rough idea being that a sensory state only counts as a conscious one when it's targeted by a suitable higher-order representation, which is what we're ultimately trying to find neural correlates of). Since I'd only ever encountered these theories secondhand, through review articles, I decided to track down the historical sources people cite as ancestors of the view instead of just recycling someone else's paraphrase. Several footnotes pointed to Locke, so I pulled up the Essay Concerning Human Understanding myself and landed on this, from his discussion of whether the soul always thinks (Book II, Chapter I, §§10–11):

"I do not say there is no SOUL in a man, because he is not sensible of it in his sleep; but I do say, he cannot THINK at any time, waking or sleeping, without being sensible of it. Our being sensible of it is not necessary to anything but to our thoughts; and to them it is; and to them it always will be necessary, till we can think without being conscious of it. I grant that the soul, in a waking man, is never without thought, because it is the condition of being awake. But whether sleeping without dreaming be not an affection of the whole man, mind as well as body, may be worth a waking man's consideration; it being hard to conceive that anything should think and not be conscious of it. If the soul doth think in a sleeping man without being conscious of it, I ask whether, during such thinking, it has any pleasure or pain, or be capable of happiness or misery? I am sure the man is not; no more than the bed or earth he lies on. For to be happy or miserable without being conscious of it, seems to me utterly inconsistent and impossible. Or if it be possible that the SOUL can, whilst the body is sleeping, have its thinking, enjoyments, and concerns, its pleasures or pain, apart, which the MAN is not conscious of nor partakes in—it is certain that Socrates asleep and Socrates awake is not the same person; but his soul when he sleeps, and Socrates the man, consisting of body and soul, when he is waking, are two persons: since waking Socrates has no knowledge of, or concernment for, that happiness or misery of his soul, which it enjoys alone by itself whilst he sleeps, without perceiving anything of it; no more than he has for the happiness or misery of a man in the Indies, whom he knows not. For, if we take wholly away all consciousness of our actions and sensations, especially of pleasure and pain, and the concernment that accompanies it, it will be hard to know wherein to place personal identity."

My supervisor wants one paragraph explaining why this counts as an ancestor of higher-order theories, but the longer I sit with it the less sure I am that it supports the reading I've been told to give it. Locke elsewhere describes reflection as a kind of "internal sense" that takes the mind's own operations as its objects, which sounds like a genuine higher-order-perception view, on which a thought becomes conscious only when it is the object of a further, numerically distinct act of inner perceiving. If that's what's going on in this passage too, then isn't Locke's own argument already exposed to the regress objection that people now raise against Armstrong- or Lycan-style inner-sense theories—if being conscious of a thought requires a distinct inner perceiving of it, what makes that perceiving itself conscious, rather than just an unnoticed sub-personal scan? But nothing in this particular passage actually says that "being sensible of" a thought is a separate act rather than simply a feature of the thinking itself, so maybe it's more honest to read Locke here as gesturing at something closer to a one-level, self-representational view—more Brentano than Armstrong—which was never on the hook for the regress in the first place, since there is no numerically distinct higher-order state to set the regress going.

Could you help me work out which of these readings the passage actually supports, or whether Locke's text is genuinely too thin to decide the question either way? And if you think I'm reaching for the wrong passage entirely—if there's a more standard locus classicus in Locke for the "ancestor of inner-sense theory" claim than this one—I would much rather find that out now than put the wrong citation into a grant document.
