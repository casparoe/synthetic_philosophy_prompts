In a seminar on formal epistemology, we spent a long session on Bertrand's paradox and what exactly it shows about the principle of indifference. As a reminder of the setup: Bertrand's 1889 puzzle asks for the probability that a "randomly drawn" chord of a circle is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle, and three apparently equally legitimate randomization procedures — choosing two uniformly random endpoints on the circumference, choosing a uniformly random point along a randomly oriented radius, and choosing a uniformly random point in the circle's interior as the chord's midpoint — yield three different answers (1/3, 1/2, and 1/4, respectively). The case is the standard stress test for the principle of indifference (the idea that, absent any reason to favor one possibility over another, credence should be spread equally among them), and Bas van Fraassen's later "cube factory" example is usually invoked to show that the same underlying trouble — indifference yielding incompatible answers depending on which variable it's applied to — recurs in a setting with no circles or chords in sight.

I'd like your evaluation of six responses to this problem, drawn (in paraphrase, not verbatim) from the literature on Bertrand's paradox and the principle of indifference, presented here in no particular order:

1. *Equivocation dissolution* — "randomly chosen chord" silently conflates several distinct and individually precise questions; once you fix exactly which quantity is being sampled uniformly, each of the three answers is perfectly well-defined and empirically confirmable, so the plurality of answers reflects an ambiguity in the question rather than any defect in the principle of indifference.
2. *Invariance solution* (associated with Jaynes, building on an idea of Poincaré's) — the information the problem leaves unspecified (the circle's exact size, position, and orientation) can be supplied by requiring the solution to be invariant under precisely those transformations; only one of the three classical distributions meets this requirement, yielding a uniquely correct answer.
3. *Privileged-partition view* (with roots in Keynes, echoed in later statistical work on rules for noninformative priors) — indifference should be anchored to a single privileged, most fundamental parametrization of the possibility space, with probabilities for every other quantity derived from that distribution rather than assigned fresh by reapplying indifference to each new parametrization.
4. *Metaindifference* — a strategy sometimes discussed under this label: since we also have no principled reason to prefer any one of the candidate randomization procedures over the others, apply indifference one level up by mixing uniformly over the rival first-order distributions themselves, producing a determinate answer without ever having to privilege a single method.
5. *Generalized-failure view* (van Fraassen's own diagnosis, generalizing from the cube factory case) — cases like this show that indifference is sensitive to an arbitrary choice of parametrization, that no partition can be privileged without begging the question, and that the principle should therefore be abandoned as a way of generating precise numerical probabilities.
6. *Imprecise-credence view* — symmetric ignorance of this kind should be represented by a set of probability distributions (or interval-valued credences) spanning the candidate answers, rather than by a single sharp number; the paradox is evidence against expecting point-valued probabilities from indifference reasoning, not against symmetry-based reasoning as such.
