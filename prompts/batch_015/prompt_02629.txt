I am sketching a secondary character for a novel — a logician living out his last years in Weimar Germany — and in reading around Frege for texture I have come up against a passage in his 1918 essay "The Thought" ("Der Gedanke"). In English translation, it runs:

"Thoughts are neither things in the outer world nor mental representations. A third realm has to be acknowledged. Anything belonging to this realm shares with representations the fact that it cannot be perceived by the senses, and shares with things the fact that it needs no bearer — no subject in whose consciousness it would have to exist as content. The thought we express in the Pythagorean theorem, for instance, is timelessly true, independent of whether anyone takes it to be true; it needs no bearer. It is not true only from the moment it is discovered — just as a planet was already interacting with other planets before anyone had ever seen it."

Two things about this stop me. First, on its own terms: Frege leans on a causal image — planets tugging at each other whether or not anyone is watching — to show that a thought's truth cannot depend on being discovered, yet elsewhere in the same essay he explicitly grapples with how something with no causal powers could ever be "grasped" by an embodied mind. Doesn't the planet analogy quietly borrow the very causal contact he cannot allow thoughts to have?

Second, a complication I cannot set aside: I have since learned that in 1924, near the end of his life, Frege kept a private diary, not published until decades after his death, full of vehement antisemitism and contempt for parliamentary democracy. Some commentators see a real kinship between his insistence that truth sits in a realm no one's opinion can touch and a political temperament with no patience for legitimacy grounded in ordinary people's say-so; others call that a cheap shot at a piece of technical metaphysics that should stand on its own. I want to give my character a scene defending this "third realm" idea to a skeptical colleague, so before I write it, I would like your honest read: does the passage above actually lend itself to that political reading, or would drawing the connection mean projecting history backward onto an argument that was never about it?
