So a random rabbit hole from work this week: we had a job that gets retried by our queue with no idempotency key, so it genuinely can't tell if the current run is the first attempt or a retry of itself. A coworker said "oh that's basically the absent-minded driver problem" and linked me a summary of the old Piccione & Rubinstein paper (I'm not about to parse a Games and Economic Behavior article for fun). Setup: a driver has to take the second of two exits off a highway to get home. Take the first exit by mistake and the payoff is 0, sail past both and it's 1, nail the second one and it's 4. Catch is the two intersections look identical and he has zero memory of whether he's already passed one, so all he can commit to is a single probability p of "continue straight," applied at whichever intersection he happens to be standing at. Treated as a plain optimization before he ever sets off, expected payoff as a function of p is maximized at p = 2/3 (expected payoff 4/3). Fine, I can derive that part myself.

Here's what's actually bugging me. Apparently if you instead imagine yourself standing at one of the intersections, not knowing if its the first or second, and redo the expected-value calc from that vantage point using some honest belief about which one you're at, you do NOT get back p = 2/3 — you get a different answer, for basically any belief short of "I'm 100% sure this is the first intersection." Piccione & Rubinstein apparently treated this as a genuine paradox between "planning" reasoning and "in the moment" reasoning, and then Aumann, Hart, and Perry wrote a whole rebuttal insisting there's no real contradiction, just a subtler notion of optimality ("action-optimality") that happens to coincide with the plan here but is conceptually doing something else — and from what I can tell, people still argue about who's right.

In literally every planning/DP problem I've dealt with, re-deriving the optimal move from inside a decision point via backward induction is supposed to just spit back the policy you already committed to — that's the whole point of Bellman's principle of optimality. So can you give me an intuitive, non-algebra-first explanation of why self-locating uncertainty (not knowing which intersection, or which "timestep," you're currently at) breaks that normally bulletproof move here? Like, what is the "inside view" calculation implicitly assuming that turns out to be false? I've got the arithmetic fine, I just don't have the gut-level feel for where the reasoning actually goes wrong — or whether it's secretly the plan that's the weird one.
