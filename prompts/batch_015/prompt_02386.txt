As a software engineer who mostly skipped the pure-math track in college, I've been trying to fill in some gaps on the side, and this week I finally worked through the standard proof that every vector space has a basis, even an infinite-dimensional one like the real numbers viewed as a vector space over the rationals. The proof goes through Zorn's Lemma: take the poset of all linearly independent subsets of the space, ordered by inclusion; note that the union of any chain of such subsets is still linearly independent, so every chain has an upper bound; and conclude that a maximal linearly independent set exists, which must span the space and is therefore a basis. I mentioned to a friend from my old algorithms study group, who went on to do a math PhD, that as far as I can tell nobody has ever written down an explicit example of such a basis for the reals over the rationals, and we ended up arguing about what that actually means.

My instinct was that this has to be the same situation as the non-constructive existence proofs I already know from CS — say, a counting argument showing that some Boolean formula has a satisfying assignment without exhibiting one. There, "exists" and "can in principle be found" come apart only trivially, since brute-force search over all assignments is a perfectly well-defined, choice-free procedure that's guaranteed to turn one up eventually. I figured a Hamel basis (that's apparently the technical term) has to be the same deal, just with a search space that's uncountably infinite rather than merely exponential: nothing should stop you, at least in principle, from well-ordering the reals and running that same kind of search transfinitely, checking each real in turn and adding it to your candidate set unless it's already in the span of what you've collected so far. My friend insisted that this fix doesn't work, and that treating Zorn's-Lemma existence proofs as "brute-force search, just really long" reflects a basic misunderstanding of what the axiom of choice actually buys you, but she couldn't quite pin down why before we had to get back to work. Is she right, and if so, what exactly breaks down in the transfinite-search picture, given that Zorn's Lemma is supposed to let us well-order the reals in the first place?
