I write about science and ideas for a living, and lately that's meant writing a lot about AI hallucinations — including the weirder flavor where a model confidently confabulates something that turns out to be true anyway. I brought up one case like that at dinner with two friends, Naomi and Theo (both did philosophy before "real" jobs), and it turned into a proper argument I can't fully referee myself. The case: someone asks a chatbot what year the footbridge in the park near his old apartment was built. The bot says 1932, confidently. That's the correct year. But when he later checks the sources the bot cites, none of them actually give a date — the model basically pattern-matched to a plausible-sounding year that happened to be right. Naomi and Theo could not agree on whether the guy knew the bridge was built in 1932 at the moment he believed it, and the argument ended up being, more or less, a live re-run of the Gettier problem.

Roughly reconstructed: (1) Naomi said it's a textbook Gettier case — the belief is true, and it's justified, since the model is right about this sort of thing way more often than not, and he'd count as justified if a well-read friend had told him the same fact off-hand. But the truth of the belief has nothing to do with why he held it, and that gap between justification and truth is exactly the structure Gettier used to break the "justified true belief" definition of knowledge. (2) Theo said you don't even get to "justified" here — "the model is usually right about this kind of thing" justifies something like "this answer is probably correct," not the specific belief "the bridge was built in 1932." If a friend told you his sense of direction is usually good and then confidently sent you the wrong way, you wouldn't say you'd been justified in following him just because he's generally reliable — you'd say he gave you a bad reason dressed up as a good one. (3) Naomi answered that we accept exactly that level of justification from people constantly — if a well-informed friend states a fact off the top of their head, nobody demands they show their work first; testimony from someone generally reliable is a completely normal source of justification, arguably most of what any of us "know" rests on it. (4) Theo said there's a difference between a generally reliable source producing an answer via a reliable process on this occasion, and producing one via an unreliable process that happens to land on the truth — a knowledgeable friend recalling a fact is at least attempting to retrieve something true, whereas the model isn't retrieving anything, it's pattern-completing to a plausible-sounding number, and that's what should matter, not the source's general track record. (5) Naomi said that standard proves too much, since a lot of human memory is reconstructive — people confabulate details constantly without any idea they're doing it, and it feels exactly like real recall from the inside, so if a belief gets disqualified whenever the process behind it "actually" turns out to be confabulation rather than genuine retrieval, even though there's no way to tell the difference from the inside, you end up denying that ordinary people know all sorts of everyday things they obviously do know. (6) Theo said that's a bullet worth biting, not a reductio — the whole point of saying knowledge depends on the process behind a belief is that it can depend on facts the believer has no access to, and you don't get to keep the comfortable answer just because the diagnosis is unsettling.

Neither of them budged. Could you go through it move by move — who's got the better of it at each point, and where (if anywhere) one of them is just talking past the other — rather than just calling an overall winner? And seperately, I'd be curious what you think directly: does this actually work as a Gettier case, or is Theo right that it doesn't even clear the justification bar in the first place?
