A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about money-pump arguments: the idea that an agent with cyclic preferences (preferring A to B, B to C, and yet C to A) can be induced, one seemingly reasonable trade at a time, to pay a small toll at each step while cycling through all three options, ending up back where it started but strictly poorer. She thinks this only indicts a peculiarly short-sighted way of choosing: a more self-aware version of the same agent would see the loop coming and simply refuse the last trade. I suspect that the fact that each individual trade still looks sound in isolation is precisely what makes the argument troubling — that whether a whole sequence of choices is rational (its diachronic rationality) can't always be settled by checking whether each choice was rational on its own. Write a short story that dramatizes a money-pump scenario through a human character, in whatever setting you like (literal trades are fine, but a cycle of relationships, jobs, houses, or possessions could work just as well), such that a sequence of individually tempting exchanges leaves the character worse off than when they began. Let the story itself stake out an implicit position on the question my friend and I never settled, rather than resolving it through authorial commentary.
