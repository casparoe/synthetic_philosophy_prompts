One thread that keeps resurfacing on the rationalist forums I read is the claim that Eliezer Yudkowsky's old metaethics sequence was, in effect, quietly reinventing arguments that professional philosophers had already made, more carefully, decades earlier. Chasing that claim down sent me back to two primary texts I had previously only encountered in secondhand summaries: W. V. Quine's short essay "On the Nature of Moral Values" (collected in Theories and Things, and, so far as I can tell, the only piece he ever devoted entirely to ethics), and "Ethics and Observation," the opening chapter of Gilbert Harman's The Nature of Morality. The pairing felt natural once I remembered that Harman wrote his Harvard dissertation under Quine's supervision.

Quine's complaint is that moral disagreements reveal a kind of methodological infirmity in ethics relative to science: a scientific prediction can be tested against the independent course of observable nature, whereas a moral judgment about an act can only be tested against our own moral standards, so that, in his words, "a coherence theory is evidently the lot of ethics" rather than a correspondence theory. Harman's argument looks similar on the surface but seems to me to be doing something different: he happily grants that we make something like moral observations (the immediate judgment, say, that a group of teenagers setting a cat on fire for fun is wrong), and asks instead whether any moral fact needs to be posited to explain why we make them, in the way that positing an actual proton is needed to explain a physicist's observation of a vapor trail in a cloud chamber. His answer is that a purely psychological story about the observer's upbringing and sensibilities explains the observation just as well, which makes the moral fact explanatorily idle.

I would like your help working out how much real distance there is between these two arguments. Are Quine and Harman ultimately pressing the same skeptical point about the epistemic standing of moral belief in different vocabularies, so that a realist's reply to one automatically answers the other, or could someone coherently accept one challenge while resisting the other? I would also be curious whether Quine's own position sits comfortably with his general holism about confirmation elsewhere in his work: if, on his view, no individual sentence faces the tribunal of experience alone, what exactly earns science, rather than ethics, its title to a correspondence theory of truth? And, setting that tension aside, which of the two arguments do you think poses the more serious obstacle for someone who wants to defend a fairly robust, cognitivist moral realism, and why?
