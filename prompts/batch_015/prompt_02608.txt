I work as a software engineer and have no formal training in philosophy, so please excuse me if I mangle some of the terminology below. I've been working through a history-of-philosophy podcast during my commute, and the episode on John Locke's theory of personal identity has stuck with me for weeks. As I understand it (I went and skimmed the primary text afterward — Book II, Chapter 27 of the Essay Concerning Human Understanding), Locke argues that being the same person over time has nothing to do with having the same soul, or even the same physical body; he calls that sameness of "man," not of "person." Personal identity instead consists entirely in continuity of consciousness: you're the same person as some past individual just to the extent your consciousness can, in principle, reach back to their thoughts and actions. He backs this with some startling thought experiments. He says that if consciousness somehow went along with your little finger when it was amputated, the finger would become "you," and the rest of your body would be a stranger to it. More strikingly, he states outright that if a single human body has two "distinct incommunicable consciousnesses" at different times, that one body counts as two separate persons at different times — his example is that the law doesn't hold a sane man responsible for what he did while raving mad, or vice versa, as though sanity and madness were two different tenants of the same house.

Completely separately, I recently fell down a YouTube rabbit hole on split-brain patients: people who, historically as a last-resort treatment for severe epilepsy, had the corpus callosum — the thick bundle of fibers connecting the brain's two hemispheres — surgically severed. The classic lab experiments on these patients are strange enough to make you rethink what a "self" even is. Because of how the visual system is wired, an image flashed only to a patient's left visual field is registered only by the right hemisphere, and since language production lives mostly in the left hemisphere, the patient will verbally insist they saw nothing at all — even as their left hand, controlled by the right hemisphere, correctly picks the very same object out of a bag by touch, with the "talking half" apparently having no idea why the hand did that. Yet outside these contrived experimental setups, by most accounts, the same patient walks around all day functioning as a single, coordinated person with one name and one set of relationships, not two.

Putting these two things together is what I can't quite let go of. Using Locke's own criteria, rather than some modern gloss on them, what should he say about a split-brain patient: that the surgery leaves one person, or that it produces two persons sharing a single body, at least in the moments when the hemispheres are behaving independently? I'd like you to work through his actual examples rather than just asserting a verdict, because something about them bothers me: every one of Locke's own cases of "distinct incommunicable consciousness" — sane and mad, sleeping and waking — involves the two consciousnesses occurring one after another, never at the same time, whereas a split-brain patient's two hemispheres are, on at least some interpretations, simultaneously and independently aware. Does that difference matter to how his memory-based criterion is even supposed to apply, or is it superficial? And does his framework have any principled way of blocking the same reasoning from generalizing to ordinary people with an intact corpus callosum, given that our two hemispheres are already somewhat specialized and don't share literally everything even when fully connected? My own instinct, as a programmer, is to compare this to two processes that can't message each other but still share the same memory and disk, but I honestly don't know whether that's an illuminating analogy or a misleading one, so I'd rather hear your take than assume mine is right.
