I've been mulling over Bostrom and Ord's "parliamentary model" for moral uncertainty ever since I first ran into it on Overcoming Bias, and later watched people try to formalize it in that old Polymath-style thread on LessWrong. I want to write a post arguing that the model doesn't actually buy you anything over "maximize expected choiceworthiness" (MEC) plus a normalization scheme like MacAskill's variance normalization — it just relocates the problem rather than solving it. Before committing to a full post, I'd like comments on the argument, which really only takes four steps.

1. MEC's classic difficulty is intertheoretic comparison: to take a credence-weighted expectation over "choiceworthiness" across theories, each theory's choiceworthiness function has to live on a common cardinal scale, and it's unclear what licenses treating a unit of utilitarian value as commensurable with a unit of Kantian "wrongness." Variance normalization is the best-known attempted fix, and it has taken a real beating in the recent literature.

2. The parliamentary model is usually sold as sidestepping this entirely: instead of comparing theories directly, credence-weighted "delegates" bargain, and you act on whatever deal results. No common scale required — allegedly.

3. But take a single up-or-down choice between A and B, where a theory with 51% credence weakly prefers A, while the remaining 49% goes to a theory that regards A as a catastrophe relative to B. A purely ordinal parliament — majority vote, no cardinal information — picks A, which seems like exactly the wrong answer. The natural fix is to let intensity of preference count: give delegates von Neumann–Morgenstern utility functions, so the 49% bloc can outbid the 51% bloc when it cares far more.

4. But now you need, first, some way of extracting a cardinal utility function from theories that don't obviously come with one (what is deontology's utility function over lotteries?), and second, a disagreement point and a bargaining solution concept (Nash? Kalai–Smorodinsky?) to actually pin down a deal. Reasonable choices here move the answer around in ways the credences alone don't fix. When people have actually run the numbers on toy cases — I have in mind both the old LessWrong exchange and the later Greaves and Cotton-Barratt paper formalizing the model via an asymmetric Nash solution — the bargaining-derived verdict doesn't obviously coincide with what a normalization procedure delivers on the same case. So we've swapped "how do you compare theories' units" for "how do you cardinalize each theory, and pick a bargaining solution and a disagreement point," and I don't see why the second problem is smaller, or why it isn't the same arbitrariness wearing a game-theoretic costume.

I'd appreciate comments on: whether I've got the Nash-bargaining requirements right, since that's the part I'm least sure of; whether there's a standard move available to defenders of the parliamentary model that blocks step 4; and whether the parallel with competing normalization conventions in interpersonal utility comparisons (where similar indeterminacy is usually shrugged off) actually rescues the model, or is beside the point.
