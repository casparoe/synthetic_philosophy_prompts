I've been reading about Johannes Kepler's rather particular relationship with astrology and have a question that builds on it. Kepler spent much of his career reforming astrology rather than rejecting it outright: he dismissed the twelve houses and sign-based rulerships as baseless, and argued instead that only certain harmonic aspects between planets create a real resonance in the "soul" of the earth and of newborn humans — a claim he tried to ground empirically, tracking correlations between aspect patterns, weather records, and his own archive of roughly 800 birth charts.

Three centuries later, Michel Gauquelin ran a strikingly similar reform with real statistics: he discarded the zodiac, houses, and transits, and instead reported a correlation between a single planet's position in its daily rising-and-culminating cycle and eminence in certain professions — most famously Mars and athletes — triggering decades of unresolved controversy over data selection and replication.

Given how differently Gauquelin's key variable relates to the heavens compared with Kepler's own harmonic aspects, would Kepler have seen this as vindicating his reform project, as a fundamentally different enterprise, or as something else entirely? And given his own exacting empirical standards, how do you think he'd have reacted to a statistical controversy this sensitive to how the data were selected?
