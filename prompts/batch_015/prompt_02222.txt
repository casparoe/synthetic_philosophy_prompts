I've spent the past couple of months reporting a feature on how the vocabulary of Bayesian reasoning -- "priors," "updating," "credences" -- has migrated from statistics and formal epistemology into everyday science writing. While researching it, I sat in on a philosophy of science reading group at a local university, and one session, ostensibly about confirmation theory, ended in a long argument between two of the regulars that never made it into my piece but that I haven't been able to let go of. The dispute concerned what's sometimes called the problem of old evidence: if a scientist already assigns some fact E credence 1, because E was observed and accepted long before, then strict Bayesian conditionalization seems to entail that E cannot raise the probability of any later theory H that happens to explain it, since P(H|E) is mathematically guaranteed to equal P(H) whenever P(E) already equals 1. The textbook case is the anomalous advance in Mercury's perihelion, a discrepancy known for decades before Einstein arrived at general relativity, which is nonetheless remembered as one of the most celebrated confirmations general relativity ever received -- even though naive Bayesian bookkeeping seems to say it should have provided none at all.

One participant, whom I'll call Ingrid, defended what she called the standard fix: what really got confirmed was never the old fact of Mercury's precession, which Einstein already believed, but the new logical discovery, made only once he finished the calculation, that general relativity entails that exact figure; since no working scientist is logically omniscient, learning that a theory has some consequence is itself fresh information, and conditionalizing on that discovery lets probability theory do its normal job after all. Rafael, another regular, objected that this fix is bought at too high a price: the axioms of probability already require every logical truth to receive credence 1, so a framework flexible enough to let "general relativity entails E" be uncertain has quietly stopped behaving like a probability function in the textbook sense -- and, worse, nobody in this literature has produced a non-arbitrary rule for exactly how much a derivation like Einstein's should move his credence, as opposed to simply picking whatever number matches the verdict we already wanted history to deliver. Ingrid replied that giving up idealized logical omniscience is an overdue correction rather than a cheat, since real scientists obviously fall short of it independent of this puzzle, and that demanding an a priori exchange rate for logical discoveries holds Bayesian confirmation to a standard no rival account of evidence meets either. Rafael pressed a further objection: on Ingrid's view, whether Mercury's perihelion counts as evidence for general relativity depends on the accident of when, in one physicist's notebook, a derivation happened to occur, so two theories that both quietly entail the very same old fact could end up with wildly different degrees of confirmation depending on whose derivation came first or was noticed at all -- which seems like the wrong sort of fact to determine how well-supported a theory is. Ingrid's answer was that Bayesian confirmation is already unapologetically relative to an agent's actual epistemic state -- two scientists with different background beliefs are allowed to extract different degrees of confirmation from identical evidence -- so there is nothing newly embarrassing about its also depending on the timing of a particular derivation, provided the updating rule stays consistent for any one agent.

Neither side budged, and the group moved on to the next paper before it was settled.
