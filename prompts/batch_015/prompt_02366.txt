I've been working through the Carnap–Hirsch–Sider debate in metaontology for a seminar paper, and there's one question in it I can't settle to my own satisfaction, so I'd like a carefully argued answer rather than a quick gesture at one. In "Empiricism, Semantics, and Ontology" (1950), Carnap distinguished "internal" questions, posed and answered using the rules of some already-adopted linguistic framework (his own example: "is there a prime number greater than a hundred?" within the framework of arithmetic), from the "external" question a metaphysician wants to ask about whether the framework's entities really exist, full stop. Carnap held that external questions, so understood, are not factual questions with correct answers at all, only practical questions about whether to adopt a framework, to be settled by fruitfulness and convenience rather than truth. This deflationary picture is the direct ancestor of Eli Hirsch's contemporary "quantifier variance" thesis, on which many seemingly deep ontological disputes (say, over whether, in addition to particles arranged tablewise, there also exists a table) are merely verbal, because each side tacitly means something different, and equally admissible, by "there exists."

After Carnap's death in 1970, though, Theodore Sider mounted an influential realist rejoinder using a resource Carnap never had available to him: David Lewis's idea that some properties are objectively more "natural" than others, better at carving reality at its joints, and that this naturalness can act as a kind of "reference magnet" fixing otherwise indeterminate meaning. In "Ontological Realism" and in Writing the Book of the World, Sider extends this idea from properties to logical vocabulary itself, arguing that one candidate meaning for the existential quantifier may be objectively the most joint-carving of all, so that the question of what exists in that privileged sense (what Sider calls doing ontology in "Ontologese") is substantive after all, whatever one thinks about the variance of "there exists" across ordinary languages.

Suppose Carnap were somehow confronted with Sider's move. Would it actually change his mind about there being a substantive external question, or does his own framework already contain the resources to resist it, for instance by treating "joint-carving" itself as either a pseudo-concept lacking clear cognitive content or as just one more pragmatic desideratum internal to framework choice, on a par with simplicity? Give a definite, well-justified prediction of how Carnap would most plausibly respond, engaging concretely with the mechanics of Sider's naturalness-based argument rather than simply restating Carnap's general anti-metaphysical attitude and leaving it at that.
