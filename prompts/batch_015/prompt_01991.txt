Gödel's ontological proof of God is often mentioned on rationalist blogs as a neat logical curiosity, but a more interesting piece of Gödel theology rarely comes up: the argument for personal survival after death that he laid out for his mother, Marianne, across four letters in 1961. In outline: the world is rationally organized, as shown by the fact that a thoroughgoing principle of sufficient reason holds throughout nature and underwrites all of science; human beings are essentially creatures whose capacity for rational self-improvement is realized, within any single lifetime, to only the smallest fraction; and a rationally organized world could not have built in so much unrealized capacity only to let it go to waste. Hence, he concludes, we must persist after death in order to complete it.

Please argue that this inference does not actually go through: that nothing about a world satisfying a thoroughgoing principle of sufficient reason — one in which everything has a discoverable cause — entails anything about whether any individual's potential gets fulfilled rather than squandered.
