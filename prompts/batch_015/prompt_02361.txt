I'm several drafts into a novel whose emotional and legal climax turns on a single irreversible act. My protagonist spent her childhood and adolescence inside an isolated community built around one man's teaching that the will must be entirely surrendered. She left ten years ago, and now, during a visit she insists was purely personal, she sets a fire that kills the community's current leader. Part of what I want the ending to establish is that nothing she could have done differently in that moment would have changed the outcome — not because anyone was watching her, ready to intervene if she wavered, but because years of conditioning had already closed off, long before that night, every psychological route to any other decision. I deliberately avoided the more familiar device of a hidden watcher who steps in only if the character falters, since that felt too mechanical for this book; I wanted an inevitability that came from inside her, with no one holding a switch.

Trying to satisfy myself that the premise could bear the moral weight I want to put on it, rather than just feeling dramatically convenient, I read around in the free-will literature and realized I had, in effect, backed into what philosophers call a "blockage" Frankfurt-style case. It's the sort of case developed by David Hunt, and in a related form by Alfred Mele and David Robb, specifically to get around the "dilemma defense" that Robert Kane, David Widerker, and Carl Ginet had raised against Harry Frankfurt's original counterexamples to the principle of alternative possibilities. As I understand it, the dilemma says that whatever prior sign a would-be intervener relies on in order to decide whether to step in is either sufficient to bring about the agent's choice, in which case the example has covertly imported determinism and begs the question against the incompatibilist it's meant to persuade, or it is not sufficient, in which case the agent could, after all, have chosen otherwise. Blockage cases try to slip between both horns by dropping the sign and the watcher altogether: the alternative pathways are simply closed off in advance, while the agent's own deliberation is left, by design, completely untouched.

What sent me further down this road was a single line in something I read, mentioning almost in passing that Ginet and Kane later turned this same dilemma back on blockage cases themselves, without spelling out the argument in any detail. Before I decide whether my ending actually earns the philosophical weight I want it to carry, I would like to see that argument made properly: please argue that blockage-style Frankfurt cases fail to escape the dilemma defense they were designed to avoid — that closing off an agent's alternatives in advance, however exactly this is engineered, cannot in the end be sharply distinguished from simply determining her choice, so that the very problem blockage was meant to solve reappears in a new guise.
