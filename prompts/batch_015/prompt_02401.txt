For a science-and-ideas column I write, I've spent the past few weeks reporting on the revival of interest in quantum indeterminacy as a possible physical basis for free will — the line of argument that runs from Penrose and Hameroff's "Orch-OR" proposal through more recent work suggesting that stochastic noise at the level of ion channels gets amplified into macroscopic neural events. To give the piece some philosophical ballast, I went back to Galen Strawson's 1994 paper "The Impossibility of Moral Responsibility," where he lays out what he calls the Basic Argument: since nothing can be causa sui, and genuine moral responsibility for an action would require being causa sui with respect to one's own character and motives, "ultimate" moral responsibility turns out to be impossible for anyone.

As I understand the argument, it works by tracing any action back to the agent's character, then asking where that character came from, and showing that at every stage it was shaped by factors — genes, upbringing, earlier choices made by a self that was itself never chosen — that the agent didn't author. That reads to me like a more rigorous version of the familiar hard-determinist worry that our choices are just the last link in a causal chain stretching back before we were born. If that's the right way to understand it, then Robert Kane's libertarian model should be exactly the kind of empirical possibility that would defuse Strawson's regress: on Kane's account, at moments of internal conflict — what he calls "self-forming actions" — amplified quantum noise genuinely breaks the causal chain, which would mean our characters aren't simply handed to us by an unbroken deterministic lineage after all.

Before I put that framing in front of my editor, I'd like to sanity-check it: is that a fair account of how the Basic Argument bears on indeterministic, Kane-style responses to it, or is there something in Strawson's own construal of the argument that this reading misses?
