I've been taking a seminar on early modern political philosophy and we spent a whole class on Pascal's fragment about justice and force from the Pensées (I think its numbered 298 in the Brunschvicg edition, if that means anything to you). Everyone quotes the famous line, "Justice without force is powerless; force without justice is tyrannical," but what actually stuck with me was the harsher move right after it: since people can never agree on what's just but can always tell who's strongest, we never managed to make the just strong, so instead we just made the strong just. Basically, on this view, legitimate authority is just force that got dressed up after the fact and started calling itself justice.

What I cant stop thinking about is how weird this looks next to stuff Pascal obviously never saw coming — international courts and human rights bodies that condemn states, issue arrest warrants for sitting heads of state, or rule that some war was illegal, all while having basically zero independent capacity to make anyone comply. No army, no police, nothing but reputational damage, frozen assets, sanctions, and the hope that other countries cooperate anyway. It's like humanity tried to build "justice" at a scale where there's no possible sovereign to hand the "force" part to, and just went ahead with it regardless.

So here's what I'm actually curious about: if you sat Pascal down and walked him through how the ICC, UN human rights mechanisms, or something like the "Responsibility to Protect" doctrine actually function, would he say it's all still just force wearing a fancier disguise (it "works," on the rare occasions it does, only because it's backstopped by the economic or military muscle of powerful states) — or would he have to admit that reputational cost and interdependence are a genuinely new kind of "force," one that could let justice and force converge even with no sovereign in sight? Try to actually reason from the structure of his argument rather than just giving a generic modern take on power and justice, and say specifically which parts of his view would have to bend and which wouldn't.
