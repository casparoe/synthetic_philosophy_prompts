In English class we just finished reading Voltaire's *Candide*, and after watching poor Pangloss get endlessly mocked for insisting that this is "the best of all possible worlds," I got curious about what Leibniz himself actually argued, as opposed to the satirized version. So I found an old public-domain English translation of his *Monadology* online and read through it. I followed most of it well enough, including the famous bit in section 7 about monads having "no windows through which anything may come in or go out," but I got stuck trying to fully understand sections 78 through 81, where Leibniz explains how the soul and the body relate to each other given that, on his view, they cannot causally affect one another at all. Here is how my translation gives it:

"78. These principles have furnished me the means of explaining on natural grounds the union, or rather the conformity between the soul and the organic body. The soul follows its own laws, and the body likewise follows its own laws. They are fitted to each other in virtue of the preestablished harmony between all substances since they are all representations of one and the same universe.
79. Souls act in accordance with the laws of final causes through their desires, ends and means. Bodies act in accordance with the laws of efficient causes or of motion. The two realms, that of efficient causes and that of final causes, are in harmony, each with the other.
80. Descartes saw that souls cannot at all impart force to bodies, because there is always the same quantity of force in matter. Yet he thought that the soul could change the direction of bodies. This was, however, because at that time the law of nature which affirms also that conservation of the same total direction in the motion of matter was not known. If he had known that law, he would have fallen upon my system of preestablished harmony.
81. According to this system bodies act as if (to suppose the impossible) there were no souls at all, and souls act as if there were no bodies, and yet both body and soul act as if the one were influencing the other."

Section 81 is the part that really trips me up, because it seems to assert three things that do not obviously fit together: bodies behave exactly as they would if souls did not exist, souls behave exactly as they would if bodies did not exist, and yet somehow both also act "as if" each were influencing the other. If the first two clauses are true, doesn't that mean my decision to, say, raise my hand contributes nothing at all to explaining why my hand actually goes up, since my body was already going to do that on its own by the laws of efficient causes? When I mentioned this to a friend who is more of a science person, he said it sounds like Leibniz is an epiphenomenalist who is too polite to say so. Is that a fair reading, or is there something about "pre-established harmony" that actually lets Leibniz avoid saying the mind is causally powerless?

I am asking partly because we recently discussed a much more recent argument in my school's philosophy club, called the "causal exclusion argument," which, as I understood it, says that if the physical world is causally closed (meaning every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause), then there is no causal work left over for distinct mental properties to do, so a theory that wants to keep the mental distinct from the physical seems forced to collapse into either reduction or epiphenomenalism. It struck me that Leibniz's claim that bodies follow only "the laws of efficient causes" among themselves sounds like an early cousin of that same closure assumption, just dressed up in theological language about a harmony arranged in advance rather than in the language of physics. Could you help me pin down exactly what sections 78-81 commit Leibniz to about mental causation, and tell me whether that comparison to the modern exclusion argument actually holds up, or whether Leibniz's system has some resource for escaping the modern problem that I am not seeing?
