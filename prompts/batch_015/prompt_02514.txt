Ok here's a rabbit hole I've been in lately: the old philosophy debate over whether genuine moral dilemmas are even possible -- not just "hard choices" but cases where an agent is truly, bindingly obligated to do A and also genuinely obligated to do not-A (Sartre's student torn between joining the resistance and staying with his mom, or Agamemnon being obligated to sacrifice Iphigenia while also obligated not to kill his own daughter). The "no real dilemmas" camp leans on two deontic logic principles: agglomeration (ought-A + ought-B implies ought-(A&B)) plus ought-implies-can. Chain those together and a genuine dilemna would mean you're obligated to do something impossible, which is absurd -- so at least one of the "oughts" must never have been fully binding after all. The pro-dilemma camp (Williams, Marcus) says look at the moral residue instead: the guilt and felt need to make amends that lingers no matter which horn you pick, which is only appropriate if the ought you didn't satisfy was real all along.

can you write me a dialogue between two philosophers hashing this out, both equally sharp? I want something like an actual double crux to emerge -- the anti-dilemma character needs a real comeback to the residue argument instead of just brushing it off, and the pro-dilemma character has to directly engage the agglomeration/ought-implies-can argument rather than just insisting dilemmas feel real. Something like 10-14 exchanges, doesn't need to resolve neatly at the end.
