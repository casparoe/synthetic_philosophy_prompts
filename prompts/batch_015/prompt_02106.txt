I spent close to four decades as a nuclear physicist, measuring decay rates and half-lives, and I always took the probabilities involved -- the chance that a given nucleus decays within its half-life, say -- to be about as objective a feature of the world as any other physical quantity, rather than merely a reflection of my own ignorance. Since retiring I have been reading my way into the philosophy of probability, partly to see whether that old working intuition can be made rigorous, and over the last few weeks I have worked through David Lewis's two central papers on the subject, "A Subjectivist's Guide to Objective Chance" and the later "Humean Supervenience Debugged." I think I have a workable grip on the Principal Principle itself -- that a rational agent's credence in an event should equal its known objective chance -- but I have run aground on what Lewis calls the problem of "undermining," which arises once that principle is combined with his own view that chances are nothing over and above patterns in the complete history of the world, future included. It was a source of some wry amusement to find that the standard illustrations of this problem in the literature are built out of exactly the kind of system I spent my career measuring: an ensemble of radioactive particles and their decay chances.

What I would like from you is not the formal derivation, which I can work through on my own, but an intuitive, almost physical feel for why undermining is supposed to be a serious problem for the Humean, rather than a bookkeeping nuisance that a slightly more careful restriction on admissible evidence should dispose of without residue. Concretely: why does the mere existence of possible future courses of events -- ones that would, if they occurred, retroactively make some different chance distribution the true one at earlier times -- threaten to make today's chance distribution self-undermining, or the Principal Principle outright inconsistent? And, staying at that same intuitive level, do you think Lewis's own repair in "Humean Supervenience Debugged" -- the "New Principle," which he credits to ideas of Ned Hall and Michael Thau published alongside his own paper in the same issue of Mind -- actually gets underneath the difficulty, or does it merely relocate the bump in the rug, as some of the subsequent commentary suggests? I would like to be able to explain the crux of this to a former colleague of mine over coffee, so please keep the emphasis on the underlying picture rather than the machinery.
