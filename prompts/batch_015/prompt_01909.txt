I'm a PhD student in behavioral ecology, currently drafting the introduction to a thesis chapter on honest signaling in sexual selection, and I've run into a conceptual snag I can't quite untangle on my own. The textbook story, going back to Zahavi's original handicap principle, is that a display like a peacock's train is honest precisely because it is costly: only a genuinely high-quality male can afford to keep paying that ongoing survival tax, and it is this cost that polices honesty. But when I work through Grafen's 1990 formalization, which is routinely cited as having put Zahavi's idea on rigorous footing, what seems to be doing the real work is not the size of the cost an honest signaler actually pays at equilibrium, but the fact that the marginal cost of escalating signal intensity is higher for low-quality individuals than for high-quality ones. Számadó and others have pushed this further, arguing that the realized cost paid by an honest signaler at equilibrium is, strictly speaking, neither necessary nor sufficient for honesty, and that "signals must be expensive" amounts to a misreading of the very model usually invoked to establish it.

What I'd like help with is the intuition, not the algebra: how can an account of honest signaling genuinely rest on cost while not actually requiring the honest signaler to pay much of anything at the stable equilibrium? I can follow the mechanics of a separating equilibrium well enough in the abstract — Spence's job-market signaling model, with its single-crossing condition, is the version I remember most clearly from a labor economics course — but I keep failing to produce a clean, non-circular explanation of why "cost is what keeps signals honest" and "the honest signal need not itself be very costly" aren't actually in tension. Could you walk me through why this isn't a paradox, ideally with a concrete illustration, in language I could use with the first-year graduate students in a seminar I'm TAing this term, rather than one that just relocates the puzzle into the equations? I'd also be curious whether, in your view, this amounts to a substantive correction to how the handicap principle is usually taught, or more of a dispute about terminology that the underlying models were never really unclear about.
