I'm on my school's team for the Philosophy Olympiad, and while preparing for the essay round I've been reading through the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's entries on higher-order evidence and epistemic self-doubt. The example almost everyone in this literature uses is a doctor who has just confidently diagnosed a patient with an embolism when a colleague reminds her that she's been awake for thirty-six hours, a condition well known to impair clinical judgment. What I can't quite work out is the three-way disagreement about what she should do next. Almost everyone agrees she can't just shrug off the reminder and stay exactly as confident as before (that "steadfast" option seems, at least to me, too dogmatic to take seriously). But there's a real split between people who accept something like an "Enkratic Principle" -- the idea that she has to bring her confidence in the embolism diagnosis itself down into line with her new doubts about her own reasoning -- and "level-splitters," who think she can rationally keep her confidence in the diagnosis high while separately, and consistently, believing that her evidence probably doesn't support that confidence. A few of my teammates and I spent an entire lunch period arguing about this without getting anywhere.

Could you write a dialogue between two philosophers defending these two positions against each other, set in the hospital corridor right after the doctor has been reminded how long she's been awake? I'd like each side to press the specific objection I've seen raised against the other, rather than just restating their own view: the Enkratic Principle's defender should have to explain why keeping her two levels of belief in line doesn't require the doctor to irrationally disregard first-order clinical evidence (the actual test results and symptoms) that hasn't changed and still points to embolism just as strongly as before, while the level-splitter should have to answer the charge that, if it's ever rational to stay confident in a conclusion while doubting that your evidence supports it, the doctor could use a string of such "lucky" correct diagnoses to bootstrap her way into thinking she's more reliable than the evidence about sleep deprivation says she is. Please make both characters equally sharp, have them genuinely engage with each other's objections rather than talking past one another, and don't let the dialogue resolve neatly in favor of either side -- I'd rather see exactly where the real pressure points are so that I can decide for myself which position holds up better.
