I'm a retired analytical chemist, and since I stopped working I've been filling the gap with a lot of amatuer reading in moral philosophy, which is a whole new world after forty years of mass spectrometers. Something happened over Thanksgiving that I can't stop chewing on, and I suspect its actually a real philosophical puzzle dressed up as a family squabble.

Since retiring I've been volunteering a couple afternoons a week doing hands-on chemistry demos at the local high school, mostly because I was bored and it makes me feel useful again. At dinner my daughter said that since I obviously have the time and the skills, and it does a real amount of good for basically no cost to me, it would actually be wrong of me to cut back, and that I should probably take on the community college too once a spot opens up there. My old labmate, who was visiting for the holiday, pushed back hard, saying that logic proves too much: if any good thing you're capable of doing instantly becomes something you owe people, then the whole idea of a good deed being "above and beyond the call" stops making sense, since eventually everything a capable person does would count as owed rather than admirable.

I keep going back and forth on who's right, and I'd love your take, not just a verdict on my particular case but on the underlying disagreement. Is there some actual principled way of separating the stuff we're genuinely on the hook for from the stuff that's praiseworthy precisely because it isn't required? Or does that distinction fall apart once you really push on it, in which case my daughter has a point and the rest of us are quietly shirking every time we don't do more good than we're doing?
