I'm a software engineer, and one of the concepts I deal with constantly is the difference between two references pointing at a single object in memory and two distinct objects that simply happen to have identical field values — reference equality versus value equality, or in database terms, a case where two rows are literally the same row (same primary key) versus two rows that just happen to be indistinguishable across every column. A friend of mine who's finishing a philosophy PhD mentioned recently, half as a joke, that this exact distinction was the subject of a serious, decades-long argument among metaphysicians, applied not to data structures but to physical objects, and I haven't been able to stop turning it over since.

The argument, as best I understand it (I have no formal philosophy training, so forgive any imprecision), starts from Leibniz's Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles: the claim that there cannot be two distinct things sharing literally all of the same properties — if x and y agree on every single property, x and y are really one thing, not two. In 1952 the philosopher Max Black attacked this principle in a short dialogue by describing a universe containing nothing but two solid iron spheres, perfectly alike in size, mass, composition, and every other intrinsic respect, positioned symmetrically some fixed distance apart — two miles, in his example — with nothing else in existence to break the symmetry. Even the spheres' relational properties end up matching: each one is, say, two miles from an iron sphere just like itself, which is exactly as true of the first sphere as of the second. Since the scenario seems perfectly coherent to imagine, and yet obviously involves two spheres rather than one, Black took it to refute Leibniz's principle. Some later philosophers have tried to rescue the principle by arguing the spheres are still "weakly" discernible, since each stands in an irreflexive relation to the other (being two miles from it) that nothing stands in to itself — but that move has always struck me as a technicality rather than a real difference between the spheres, and apparently the same puzzle resurfaces in current debates over whether physically indistinguishable particles, like two photons in exactly the same quantum state, count as genuinely distinct individuals.

What I'd like is a short story that takes this scenario and dramatizes it rather than merely stating it as a puzzle. Feel free to set it wherever seems most alive to you — a deep-space survey mission that stumbles on a genuinely symmetric structure, a laboratory that deliberately builds a miniature version of Black's universe, or something else entirely — but I'd like the plot to turn on a moment where some character, human or artificial, has to act as though there are definitely two of something (or definitely only one) and discovers that nothing about the objects themselves could ever justify that assumption. I'm not expecting the story to resolve the philosophical question so much as to make a reader feel the vertigo of it.
