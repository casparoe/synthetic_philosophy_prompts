I'm about 40k words into a novel where the protagonist, Nadia, gets formally diagnosed with ADHD at 34, and I want the diagnosis itself to be a turning point in the book, not just a label that explains stuff that was already obviously true about her the whole time. My sister, who's doing a PhD in philosophy of science, pointed me toward Ian Hacking's stuff on "looping effects" a while back — the idea that psychiatric categories are "interactive kinds" that loop back onto the people they're applied to (unlike, say, being classified as a quark, which doesn't change what quarks do), so the category and the person kind of co-evolve over time. I've been chewing on this for months and finally wrote up a little craft note trying to apply it to Nadia's arc, and I'd love your honest take before I get further into the draft.

Here's the note: "Before her diagnosis, Nadia just thinks of herself as flaky and lazy, and so does everyone around her — that's the folk category she's been living inside. The diagnosis doesn't just redescribe her, it hands her a new autobiography: she starts combing back through old school reports, relationships, the degree she never finished, reorganizing all of it around 'ADHD' as the secret protagonist of her own past. Meanwhile she starts, half-consciously, performing the diagnosis a bit — picking up the vocabulary of 'executive function' and 'masking' from online communities, which changes how she acts in ways her doctor then reads as confirming evidence. By the end I want it to be a genuinely open question whether the diagnosis found something true about her that was always there, or partly built the thing it claims to describe, which I think is close to Hacking's own refusal to just declare human kinds 'real' or 'not real.'"

Two things I want your take on. First, does that note actually track what Hacking argued, or have I flattened it into a more generic "labels become self-fulfilling prophecies" idea that's honestly a much older and less interesting point? My sister also insists this is basically just repackaged 1960s labeling theory (Scheff, Goffman) with fancier vocabulary, and that Hacking didn't really add anything — is she right, or is the "looping"/human-kinds framing doing something genuinely different? And second, seperately from the accuracy question — does the discovered-vs-manufactured ambiguity I'm going for sound fresh, or is it such a well-worn move in "diagnosis novels" by now that I should be hunting for a sharper angle?
