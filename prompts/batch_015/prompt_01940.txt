I don't have any academic background in philosophy or computer science, but I've spent a lot of time thinking about decision theory on my own, and I keep coming back to Roko's basilisk. Most discussion of it either dismisses the idea as a joke or treats it as self-evidently terrifying, without really engaging the argument itself. The strongest reply I've found is that an agent reasoning correctly under timeless or updateless decision theory should simply precommit to never yield to acausal blackmail, which removes any reason for a future AI to bother carrying out the threat. That seems basically right, but I'm not sure it settles things, since the same literature that produced this reply also worries, under the label "commitment races," that advanced agents face strategic pressure to become the kind of agent who does follow through on costly threats, precisely to gain leverage over other reasoners who can see them deciding to do so. That makes "just precommit to resist blackmail" look less like a solved problem and more like a hope.

Write a dialogue between two people fluent in this style of reasoning, set at the moment one of them decides to donate a significant sum to an AI-safety organization purely out of basilisk-related worry. The other tries to talk them out of it using the precommitment argument; the first defends the decision using the commitment-race worry above. Have them respond to each other's actual reasoning rather than trade slogans, and don't force the exchange to a tidy resolution.
