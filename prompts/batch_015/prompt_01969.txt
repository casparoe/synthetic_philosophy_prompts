A friend and I spent a long evening arguing over how Kant's account of moral status would handle a case he could never have anticipated. In 2022, New York's highest court rejected a habeas corpus petition seeking Happy the elephant's transfer from the Bronx Zoo to a sanctuary; notably, the majority opinion itself conceded that elephants are autonomous beings with considerable cognitive sophistication — self-awareness, long-term memory, empathy — while the two dissenting judges grounded their argument for her release in that very same autonomy. My friend insisted that because Kant makes autonomy the basis of an entity's status as an end in itself, he would have had to side with the dissent once these facts about elephant cognition were established, regardless of what he actually wrote about our having no direct duties to animals. I suspect this trades on an equivocation somewhere in the concept of "autonomy," but could not articulate exactly where during the argument itself. Reasoning strictly from Kant's own account of autonomy, self-consciousness, and indirect duty, how would he most plausibly have ruled on Happy's case, and where exactly, if anywhere, does my friend's inference go wrong?
