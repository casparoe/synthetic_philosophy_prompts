I'm taking a seminar on philosophy of language this term, and for the past couple of weeks we've been working through Grice's reductive analysis of speaker-meaning: "Meaning" (1957), the revisions in "Utterer's Meaning and Intentions" (1969), and the relevant chapters of Schiffer's book Meaning (1972). We spent an entire discussion section on the regress problem that eventually pushes Grice's framework toward an appeal to common knowledge, and a friend in the seminar insists that this move is really just definitional bookkeeping rather than a substantive fix. I want to know whether her reaction can be turned into a rigorous argument, or whether I'm missing why the standard defense of the common-knowledge move is supposed to work.

Here's the problem as I understand it. Grice's original three-clause "M-intention" — roughly, that an utterer U intends to produce the belief that p in an audience A, intends A to recognize that intention, and intends that recognition to function as A's reason for believing p, with the last clause usually credited to Strawson's amendment of Grice's original formulation — survives Grice's own test case of Herod displaying John the Baptist's severed head to Salome, since Herod doesn't thereby mean that John is dead: Salome's reason for believing it is the head itself, not any intention of Herod's that she recognizes. But Schiffer shows that any such finite specification is hostage to a further deceptive counterexample. In his "Moon Over Miami" case, Alice sings loudly and annoyingly to drive off her unwelcome guest Bob while intending that he not recognize part of her plan; patching the definition to require Bob's recognition of that further intention just generates a new case ("Tipperary") in which an accomplice tips Bob off in advance, defeating the patch as well. Schiffer concludes that no finite hierarchy of "the audience should recognize intention n" clauses can be made immune to this maneuver, and that what's really needed is common knowledge, between U and A, that U has the relevant intention — an in-principle infinite, but only dispositionally so, hierarchy of mutual belief. Grice resisted this move rather than accepting it, and the standard reply to the natural objection that common knowledge demands actually-infinite cognitive states is that only a finite generative capacity is required of the parties, not any completed infinite regress of occurrent beliefs.

What I'd like from you is a rigorous defense of a fairly aggressive thesis: that the appeal to common knowledge does not actually block Schiffer's regress but merely relocates it one level up, and so is no real advance over the iterated-intention framework it replaced. Concretely, argue that whatever "way" is supposed to generate common knowledge that U M-intends p for A is itself exactly the kind of thing a sufficiently devious Alice could counterfeit in a further Moon-Over-Miami-style scenario, engineering mutual belief in her intention through a route other than the open, reason-giving one that common knowledge is meant to guarantee — so that "common knowledge" ends up naming the result we want rather than picking out a mechanism that actually excludes the deceptive cases. Please engage with the fine structure of Schiffer's examples and with the finite-generative-capacity defense specifically, rather than resting on a generic worry that regresses are vicious, and don't hedge your way into a "there's something to be said on both sides" conclusion: I want an actual verdict, argued for in detail.
