I'm in a philosophy of religion seminar this term, and this week's unit on divine hiddenness paired Schellenberg's argument from non-resistant nonbelief with the following fragment from Pascal's Pensées (no. 430 in the Brunschvicg numbering, trans. W. F. Trotter), which our professor described as an early anticipation of — and possible reply to — the modern argument:

"God has willed to redeem men and to open salvation to those who seek it. But men render themselves so unworthy of it that it is right that God should refuse to some, because of their obduracy, what He grants others from a compassion which is not due to them. ... It was not, then, right that He should appear in a manner manifestly divine, and completely capable of convincing all men; but it was also not right that He should come in so hidden a manner that He could not be known by those who should sincerely seek Him. ... [W]illing to appear openly to those who seek Him with all their heart, and to be hidden from those who flee from Him with all their heart, He so regulates the knowledge of Himself that He has given signs of Himself, visible to those who seek Him, and not to those who seek Him not. There is enough light for those who only desire to see, and enough obscurity for those who have a contrary disposition."

I can more or less follow the general idea, but I'm stuck on two things. First, is the "obduracy" language compatible with the later, more dispositional talk of seeking "with all their heart," or is there a real tension between explaining hiddenness as merited punishment for sin and explaining it as a real-time calibration to present sincerity? Second, when Pascal says the signs are "visible to those who seek Him, and not to those who seek Him not," is he making an evidential claim — that the very same evidence is literally undetectable to the non-seeker — or a broadly motivational one, on which the evidence is available but non-seekers are unwilling or unable to read it correctly? Schellenberg's argument seems to require quite different responses depending on which of these Pascal intends, and I don't think the passage settles it on its own. I'd appreciate help disentangling these readings, and any sense of which is more textually defensible.
