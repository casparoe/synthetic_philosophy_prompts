Ok so I've gone down a bit of a rabbit hole. I was arguing with some crypto people online about whether bitcoin counts as "real" money (they love calling it digital gold), and that somehow led me to reading about the history of money in general, which is how i stumbled on this philosopher named Berkeley - I think he's the "if a tree falls in the forest" guy? Turns out before all that he was also a bishop in Ireland who wrote this whole weird pamphlet in the 1730s, called something like The Querist, that's basically just page after page of questions about economics. The main thing I got out of it is that he didn't think money was ever really a "thing" with value built into it, more like a ticket or counter just for keeping track of who owes who what - he even asks at one point whether you could yank all the actual gold and silver out of a country and people could still buy and sell stuff just fine as long as everyone kept using the same names for the units. He was also big on setting up a national bank that could basically create credit out of thin air to get industry moving, instead of everyone waiting around for enough gold to show up first.

Now I can't stop wondering what this guy would of made of bitcoin specifically, because it feels like his idea and the total opposite of his idea at the same time. On one hand its not backed by any government or physical stuff at all, so in that sense its the ultimate "money is just an abstract ticket." But then the whole pitch from bitcoin people is that its valuable BECAUSE its scarce and capped at 21 million coins, basically digital gold, which sounds a lot like the "money has to be some special valuable substance" view he spent that whole book arguing against. Plus theres no bank or authority behind it that can expand credit to wherever the economy actually needs it, which was like his other huge point. So would this 1730s bishop have been a bitcoin maximalist, or would he just see it as gold-bug thinking with extra steps? I don't really have anyone to ask about this stuff in real life so figured i'd throw it out here.
