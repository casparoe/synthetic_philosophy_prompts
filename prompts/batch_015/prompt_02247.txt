Quick philosophy sanity-check for a novel I'm about a third of the way through — the plot hinges on a teleporter (I promise its not just a Star Trek knockoff) and I've been reading around Derek Parfit's teletransporter thought experiment so I don't write something a philosophy grad student would roll their eyes at. As I understand it, Parfit basically settled the "but is it really still me" question: personal identity just is psychological continuity, so if the person who gets rebuilt on Mars has all your memories, personality quirks, and general sense of being you, then she isn't a copy of you, she just straightforwardly IS you, and worrying about the original "dying" in the machine is a leftover confusion from assuming there's some extra soul-stuff that has to survive too.

My protagonist is a philosophy postdoc, so I want her to give her sister exactly this reassurance before the sister uses the machine for the first time — something breezy like "there's no mystery left here, identity just reduces to continuity, you'll step out the other end no less you than you are right now." Before I commit to that speech though, I want to check I'm not giving her a watered-down, internet-summary version of Parfit's actual argument. Did he really land somewhere that clean and reassuring, or is his real conclusion weirder and less comforting than "the copy is you, case closed" — and if so, would my postdoc, if she'd actually read the whole book, say something a bit different (and maybe more interesting) to her sister?
