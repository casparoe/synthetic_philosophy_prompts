Since retiring after a long career as a research pharmacologist, I have finally been reading philosophy in earnest, and Plato's Laws recently gave me pause. I had expected the usual philosopher's suspicion of bodily pleasure, but his treatment of drunkenness turns out to be far more considered than that: he argues that wine, drunk under the eye of a sober symposiarch (a designated overseer of the gathering), offers a safe and inexpensive way to test whether a person's composure survives the loosening of ordinary inhibition — a crude but recognizable controlled experiment for the soul — and that this same loosening can be used deliberately to train the self-control needed to withstand it. He is just as precise about dosage and eligibility: no wine whatsoever before the age of eighteen, since it would only inflame an already excitable temperament; moderate use short of real intoxication up to the age of thirty; and considerably greater latitude after forty, when he regards wine as a legitimate remedy for the stiffness of an aging character.

Given this specific and carefully graded theory, how do you think Plato would have judged two modern developments with no real counterpart in his own world: clinically supervised psilocybin- or MDMA-assisted therapy for conditions such as depression and PTSD, and the broader contemporary movement to decriminalize or legalize recreational psychedelic and cannabis use without any comparable oversight? Would his own framework lead him to endorse the former while resisting the latter, or does his actual reasoning cut against that neat division?
