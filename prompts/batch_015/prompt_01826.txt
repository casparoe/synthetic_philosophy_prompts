I'm taking a grad seminar on early modern women writers and had to do a close reading of Sor Juana's "Respuesta a Sor Filotea" (1691) this week. Everyone always goes to the "Hombres necios" poem or just the general "women deserve an education too" takeaway, but I got kind of fixated on one narrower move she makes about two-thirds through the letter, and I wrote up a paragraph trying to pin down what's actually happening argumentatively there. Would love a sanity check before I turn it into part of an actual paper.

Here's the move: Sor Juana cites a theologian, "Doctor Arce," who concludes that women may not preach publicly or lecture from a university chair, but that private study, writing, and teaching ("estudiar, escribir y enseñar privadamente") is, in his words, "no sólo les es lícito, pero muy provechoso y útil." Fine, thats the standard public/private carve-out you'd expect from someone defending herself to a bishop. But then she gets sneakier: she argues the real danger to girls isn't too much learning, it's the shortage of "ancianas doctas" (learned older women) to provide it, because when parents want their daughters taught beyond the basics, that shortage forces them to hire male tutors instead — which, she says, leads often enough to "lastimosos ejemplos de desiguales consorcios" (scandal, ruined matches) that everyone quietly treats it as an accepted risk. The conclusion is almost syllogistic: male tutors are dangerous; they're only used because there's no supply of learned women; therefore the absence of learned women just is a serious harm to the community — so the properly "protective," reputation-guarding move is to educate more women, not fewer. She even re-historicizes Paul's "Mulieres in Ecclesia taceant," claiming (via Eusebius) it targeted a specific first-century nuisance of women's chatter interrupting sermons rather than banning private teaching, and sets it against Paul's other, way-less-quoted line that older women should be "bene docentes" (teaching well).

My essay's claim is that this whole passage is basically a piece of immanent critique: shes not smuggling in some anachronistic secular egalitarianism, she's using the community's own stated values (modesty, scriptural authority, avoiding scandal) to force a pro-education conclusion that nobody in her actual audience could reject without contradicting themselves. I'd like comments on whether that's a fair and precise way to describe what's going on, versus me just imposing more cleverness/structure on it than is really there. And separately: is there something a little too convenient about how "conservative"-sounding this argument is, given that Sor Juana herself is obviously exactly the kind of unusually learned woman who'd be qualified to do the teaching in question? Does that self-interested angle undercut the immanent-critique reading, or are these just two different things happening at once — and if it's the latter, is there already a name in the literature for that kind of doubled rhetorical move (simultaneously a principled internal critique and a self-serving positioning)? Feel free to be harsh, I'd rather find the holes now than later.
