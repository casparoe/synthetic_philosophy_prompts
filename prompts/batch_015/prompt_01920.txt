After forty years running a lab on enzyme kinetics, I retired three years ago and have finally had time to read philosophy properly rather than in stolen evenings. Lately I have been working through David Chalmers's writing on what he calls the "meta-problem" of consciousness: the project of explaining, using only ordinary physical, computational, or evolutionary resources, why creatures like us come to believe that phenomenal experience is peculiarly resistant to explanation -- why we insist that no amount of talk about neurons and information processing could ever tell us what it is like to see red or taste coffee.

The parallel to my own corner of science seems exact to me, and I cannot quite let it go. Vitalism was not killed by a direct refutation of the idea of a vital force; it died piecemeal, as when Buchner showed in 1897 that a cell-free extract of yeast could ferment sugar into alcohol just as living cells did, meaning the enzymes alone were doing the work and nothing was left for a "life force" to contribute. The question of what activated the vital force was never actually answered -- it simply evaporated, along with the belief that anything had needed activating. The meta-problem looks to me like it is positioned to do exactly the same job for consciousness: once cognitive science gives a complete functional and computational account of why we judge, report, and insist that our experience is ineffable and irreducible, the further "hard problem" of why there is something it is like to be us should dissolve for the same reason, since nothing would be left over for it to be about.

What puzzles me is that a good many philosophers -- Chalmers himself among them, as far as I can tell -- keep insisting the hard problem could survive a complete meta-problem solution fully intact. I mentioned this to a former colleague who now sits on a bioethics committee, but she waved it off as a question for philosophers rather than scientists, which was not much help. So I would genuinely like to know: is this just a reluctance among philosophers to draw the same conclusion here that biochemistry drew about vitalism, or is there some principled disanalogy between the two cases that I am not seeing?
