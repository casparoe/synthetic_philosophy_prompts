A friend and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about a wrinkle in Robert Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia that had come up in my political philosophy reading group. In the chapter on rights as "side constraints," Nozick's whole case against utilitarianism rests on what he calls "the fact of our separate existences": an aggregate social good cannot compensate anyone for a sacrifice imposed on them, he argues, because there is no social entity that itself bears the loss and reaps the gain — "there are only individual people, different individual people, with their own individual lives" — so no one may be used merely to increase the overall good without their consent. This is meant to ground a broadly Kantian view on which persons may never be treated merely as means. Yet only a few pages later, in a much-discussed aside (p. 39), Nozick floats "utilitarianism for animals, Kantianism for people" as a way of handling the moral status of non-human animals: their welfare, unlike ours, can apparently still be maximized and traded off in the ordinary aggregative way. He seems uneasy about his own suggestion, since a few pages further on he asks whether some race of beings elsewhere in the universe that stands to us as we take ourselves to stand to animals would thereby be entitled to treat us exactly as we treat them.

My friend insisted that Nozick can consistently hold both views, so long as the side-constraint apparatus is keyed to some further capacity — rational agency, say, or the ability to frame and revise a plan for one's own life — that only humans possess among terrestrial creatures. I argued that this defense comes too late: the separateness argument, as Nozick actually states it, seems to turn entirely on the bare fact that each creature has only the one life it has, with nothing in its official formulation restricting it to rational beings. If that's right, then whatever force the argument has against aggregating harms and benefits across humans should equally well block aggregating them across animals, which would make "utilitarianism for animals, Kantianism for people" not a stable resting point but a position his own premises actually rule out. We went back and forth for the better part of an hour without settling anything.

I'd like to see the most rigorous version of my side of the dispute. Please argue that Nozick's stated rationale for side constraints gives him no principled basis, internal to that rationale, for confining them to persons rather than to all separately existing sentient creatures — so that his proposed treatment of animals is undercut by the very reasoning he uses against utilitarianism in general. Reconstruct the separateness-of-persons argument carefully enough to show exactly where a restriction to rational agents would have to be smuggled in from outside it, and then engage seriously with the most obvious rejoinder on Nozick's behalf: that rational agency, rather than mere separateness, was doing the real justificatory work all along, so that no such restriction is really an ad hoc addition.
