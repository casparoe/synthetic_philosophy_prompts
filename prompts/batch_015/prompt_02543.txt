I'm a software engineer who got into formal logic and type theory a couple of years ago through a proof-assistant side project, and that eventually pulled me into reading about Gottlob Frege, since a fair amount of the machinery I use (e.g., defining a sequence by taking the "ancestral" of a relation, which is essentially old-fashioned transitive closure) traces back to his Begriffsschrift and Grundlagen der Arithmetik. While reading around his biography, I was caught off guard to learn that in the last year of his life Frege kept a private political diary, not published until decades after his death, full of virulent antisemitism, contempt for parliamentary democracy, and open sympathy for the nascent fascist right in Weimar Germany. Apparently the material was unsettling enough that Michael Dummett, one of the philosophers most responsible for establishing Frege as a founder of analytic philosophy, described being genuinely shaken when he first came across hints of it among Frege's papers in the 1950s.

What struck me even more, reading further, is that Frege doesn't seem to have kept this walled off from his technical interests. By some accounts, he reached for the same conceptual toolkit in his late political writing that he used in logic — invoking the logic of genealogical sequences (that "ancestral" idea again, applied to lines of descent) to argue for restricting civil rights based on "Aryan" ancestry, and separately worrying about how the vagueness of ordinary predicates would complicate the drafting and enforcement of antisemitic statutes. This feels adjacent to, but not quite the same as, the long-running fight over Heidegger and Nazism: Heidegger's philosophy was always entangled with history, authenticity, and the fate of peoples, so a political reading was never a huge stretch. Frege's reputation, by contrast, rests almost entirely on being the paradigm of cold, formal, apolitical rigor — which is exactly what makes it strange to find him bending that same rigor toward eliminationist politics.

I'd like you to write an essay that actually adjudicates this controversy rather than just describing it. Lay out the strongest case that Frege's political writings are philosophically inert with respect to his standing as a logician — that judging his logicism, his sense/reference distinction, or his anti-psychologistic claim that thoughts and logical laws inhabit a mind-independent "third realm" by reference to his politics would be a version of the genetic fallacy, discrediting an idea because of its source rather than its merits. Then lay out the strongest case for the opposite view: that the diary and the writing around it give us real, non-biographical grounds to reassess something in the philosophy itself. Finally, come down on one side — I want an actual verdict, defended as persuasively as you can manage, not a survey that ends in "reasonable people disagree."
