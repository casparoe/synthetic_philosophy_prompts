I'm a software engineer, and a few colleagues and I have been working through Derek Parfit's Reasons and Persons as an informal lunchtime reading group -- it kept coming up in the rationalist-adjacent blogs some of us follow, usually in the same breath as decision theory and anthropic puzzles, so we decided to go to the source instead of relying on summaries. We just finished the teletransportation material, and before we reconvene this Friday I want to make sure I've actually understood the argument rather than just the plot of the thought experiment.

The setup, as I follow it: in the ordinary case, a machine on Earth scans your body down to the cellular level, destroys it, and radios the blueprint to Mars, where a replicator assembles an exact duplicate out of new matter, memories and personality fully intact -- essentially a checkpoint-and-restore operation on a person, serializing the state, killing the original process, and deserializing it on different hardware. Parfit then adds the "branch-line" wrinkle: the scan is taken, but the Earth body is merely damaged rather than destroyed, so for a few days there are two of you -- one starting a full life on Mars, one on Earth who knows he has only a few days left. Since the Earth-you and the Mars-you briefly coexist, they plainly can't be numerically identical to each other, which seems to me to settle things fairly decisively: ordinary one-shot teletransportation must just be death dressed up as travel, since Parfit's own example shows that the party on the receiving end is, strictly speaking, somebody else.

What I can't square is that several secondary sources describe Parfit's own reaction to the branch-line case as reassuring, almost liberating, rather than as confirmation that his teletransporter is a murder machine. That seems like more than a philosopher just being deliberately contrarian, so I assume I'm missing a step in the reasoning. Is there an actual argument for why losing strict identity shouldn't bother us as much as it seems like it should, or is Parfit's equanimity here more of a personal, slightly idiosyncratic reaction that the rest of us are free to reject even if we accept his metaphysics?
