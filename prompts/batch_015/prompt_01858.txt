I am working on my IB Extended Essay in philosophy, on Otto Neurath, and before I extend it to full length I would like some outside feedback on its central argument. My research question is whether Neurath's Isotype project is really consistent with his epistemology, or whether it secretly depends on assumptions his own philosophy of science should rule out. Below is the core section of my draft.

Neurath is remembered mainly as a member of the Vienna Circle: an opponent of Schlick's residual foundationalism in the protocol-sentence debate, and a proponent of a loose "encyclopedia" model of unified science rather than Carnap's more formal system-building. Famously, he compared scientific knowledge to a ship that sailors must rebuild plank by plank while it stays afloat, since there is no dry dock, no tabula rasa, from which to start again. It is tempting to treat Isotype — the "international picture language" of pictorial statistics that Neurath began developing in 1925 at Vienna's Social and Economic Museum with Marie Reidemeister, and which the artist Gerd Arntz joined in 1928 — as an unrelated, merely practical hobby of his. I want to argue instead that Isotype is a visual working-out of the very same anti-foundationalism.

The temptation to see it otherwise is understandable. Repeating a little worker-icon to mean "five million workers" looks like exactly the kind of unmediated, self-interpreting sign that Neurath's epistemology should rule out, and part of his case for Isotype in International Picture Language (1936) was precisely that such a sign crosses language barriers where the word "worker" cannot. That sounds like a claim that pictures give direct access to their content in a way words do not: a pictorial given.

But Isotype's actual design rules cut the other way. Its cardinal rule was that a larger quantity must never be shown by enlarging a single icon, only by repeating icons of a fixed size, because an enlarged icon invites exactly the kind of "direct," uncritical reading that makes bad graphs misleading. Repetition instead makes the sign countable and rule-bound: a viewer must already grasp the convention that one icon equals one unit before the chart means anything at all. Even the full name the system took in the mid-1930s, International System of Typographic Picture Education, yokes "typographic" — a mark fixed by convention — to "picture." On my reading, an Isotype icon functions like a protocol sentence in another medium: not a self-certifying datum but a conventionally fixed mark, revisable within the encyclopedia of unified science rather than an unrevisable foundation.

The trouble is that this leaves it unclear why Neurath thought pictures, specifically, could cross linguistic and educational boundaries that words cannot. If Isotype icons are conventional through and through, that advantage needs its own story, and I am not sure "easier to learn" is really a different claim from "not conventional," rather than just a restatement of it.

Two things I would value feedback on: first, whether the parallel I draw between protocol sentences and Isotype's design rules is doing genuine philosophical work, or whether I am forcing an analogy Neurath himself would not recognize; second, whether my last paragraph actually defuses the objection, or whether Neurath's own stated hopes for picture language as a vehicle of international and democratic communication commit him to something stronger than the conventionalism I am attributing to him. I would rather learn the argument does not work now than after I have written the rest of the essay.
