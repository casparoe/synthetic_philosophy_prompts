A friend of mine, who works on mechanistic interpretability at an AI lab, and I had a long, fairly heated argument over dinner last week, and neither of us walked away having conceded a single point. It started because I brought up Anthropic's "Golden Gate Claude" demo, in which they took a single feature found by a sparse autoencoder and turned its activation up until the model could barely answer any question without steering the conversation back to the bridge, and I said this seemed like good evidence that the model really does have a representation of the Golden Gate Bridge somewhere inside it, no more mysterious in principle than a face-selective neuron in inferotemporal cortex. I am currently taking a seminar on philosophy of cognitive science, and this is exactly the kind of case our readings on realism about mental representation keep circling back to. My friend, who spends her working days actually probing models like this one, thought I was moving far too quickly from "manipulating this direction reliably changes the output" to "this direction represents the bridge," and said I had not earned the right to that word.

The argument then ranged more widely than either of us expected. I brought up the claim, from the "Zoom In" circuits work, that features are the fundamental unit of neural networks and that circuits deserve the kind of careful, cumulative study biologists once gave the cell, to argue that interpretability is basically ordinary science discovering a previously unknown kind of structure. She countered with the superposition story: if a network's "true" features only become visible once you impose a particular sparse dictionary on its activations, that already looks less like discovery and more like construction. She then invoked the recent "interpretability illusion" result on subspace activation patching, where an intervention can look exactly like it is manipulating the right causal variable while actually just activating a separate, dormant pathway that happens to produce the same output — her point being that we currently have no reliable way of telling a genuine representation apart from an artifact of whichever method we used to go looking for one. When I tried to insist that this was an epistemic problem rather than a metaphysical one, she brought out the older Fodor-and-Pylyshyn worry that distributed vector representations may simply lack the systematicity and stable compositional structure that made "representation" a useful, load-bearing notion in cognitive science to begin with, so that calling a direction in activation space a representation might just be the sort of quiet, unearned borrowing of folk-psychological vocabulary that Ramsey, Stich, and Garon once warned would not survive contact with how connectionist networks actually store information.

Since we ended the night no closer to agreement, could you write the dialogue we should have had? Set it up as an exchange between two equally sharp interlocutors: one a representational realist, who holds that mechanistic interpretability is uncovering real, mind-independent structure and that features and circuits are a genuine natural kind; the other a deflationist, who holds that "feature," "circuit," and "representation" are useful labels we impose on high-dimensional statistics, with no further fact about whether the network "really" represents anything. Please have them work through the Golden Gate Claude/superposition case, the interpretability-illusion problem, and the systematicity objection through genuine back-and-forth, with each responding to the specific move the other has just made rather than restating their own position, and have them close by trying to establish whether what divides them is an empirical disagreement about what further evidence would settle the question, or something closer to a verbal dispute over how demanding the word "representation" ought to be. I don't want either side written as the obvious loser: the realist's position should survive contact with the illusion and systematicity worries, and the deflationist's should survive contact with interpretability's real predictive and causal successes, the way both of ours somehow did.
