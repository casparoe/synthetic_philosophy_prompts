I spent thirty-some years running a genetics lab doing genome-wide association studies, and I still remember the jolt in 2008 when the Homer et al. paper came out, showing you could figure out whether a specific person's genotype was part of a study's "case" group just from the aggregate allele frequencies that journals were cheerfully publishing at the time -- no individual data required. NIH and the Wellcome Trust changed their data-sharing rules within weeks. I hadn't thought about that episode in years, but retirement has finally given me time to read some philosophy (enthusiastically, if not always competently), and a stack of papers on "differential privacy" sent me down a rabbit hole connecting it back to that old memory and also to the 2020 Census, where a differentially private algorithm was used to release the count data and a bunch of demographers I know were furious about the noise it introduced into small towns and minority populations.

Here's what's nagging at me though. Differential privacy gives you an actual, provable mathematical guarantee -- with a clean little dial called epsilon -- of exactly how much any one persons presence in a dataset could possibly change a published statistic. That feels like a real, rigorous answer to the privacy question, not just a philosophers gut feeling about it. So why, in the philosophy course I've been auditing, is "what privacy is" and "why it matters" still being treated as this wide-open, unresolved thing? Once you can literally quantify how much information leaks, hasn't the substantive part of the debate basically been settled by the mathematicians and computer scientists, with everything left for philosophers being just detail-work about where to set the dial? My son-in-law, who's a data scientist, told me over Thanksgiving that I was "mixing up two totally different questions," but then got roped into carving duty before he could tell me which ones.
