ok so I've been on a bit of a Keynes kick lately — "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren," the whole "once scarcity's basically solved, what do we even do with ourselves" thing — and that sent me down a rabbit hole into the fact that Keynes's actual picture of the good life wasn't really his own idea, he more or less lifted it from G.E. Moore's Principia Ethica. Apparently he and the rest of the Bloomsbury lot treated Moore's final chapter, "The Ideal" (the one arguing personal affection + aesthetic enjoyment are basically the only things worth having for their own sake), as closer to scripture than the actual duty/conduct chapters of the book. So I went and read the chapter myself to see what the fuss was about, and there's a bit in there (sections 116–117) I cant fully untangle:

"Now the case which I have called an error of taste, namely, where the actual qualities we admire (whether possessed by the ‘object’ or not) are ugly, can in any case have no value, except such as may belong to the emotion by itself… In this sense, then, it is undoubtedly right to think that seeing beauty in a thing which has no beauty is inferior in value to seeing beauty where beauty really is. But the other case is much more difficult. In this case there is present all that I have hitherto mentioned as necessary to constitute a great positive good: there is a cognition of qualities really beautiful, together with an appropriate emotion towards these qualities. There can, therefore, be no doubt that we have here a great positive good. But there is present also something else; namely, a belief that these beautiful qualities exist… and further the object of this belief is false. And we may ask, with regard to the whole thus constituted, whether the presence of the belief, and the fact that what is believed is false, make any difference to its value? We thus get three different cases… (1) a belief in the existence of these qualities, of which the object, i.e. that they exist, is true: or (2) a mere cognition, without belief, that the object of the cognition, i.e. the beautiful qualities, exists: or (3) a belief in the existence of the beautiful qualities, when they do not exist.

Now all these three cases… have something in common, namely, that, in them all, we have a cognition of really beautiful qualities together with an appropriate emotion towards those qualities… And I think that the value of the second, in either of its two subdivisions, is precisely the same as the value of the element common to all three. In other words, in the case of purely imaginative appreciations we have merely the cognition of really beautiful qualities together with the appropriate emotion; and the question, whether the object cognised exists or not… seems here… to make absolutely no difference to the value of the total state. But it seems to me that the two other cases do differ in intrinsic value both from this one and from one another, even though the object cognised and the appropriate emotion should be identical in all three cases."

So near as I can tell Moore's ranking goes: correctly believing in a beautiful thing that actually exists > merely imagining a beautiful thing with no belief either way > falsely believing some ugly/nonexistent thing is beautiful — and he's explicit that the cognition and emotion are stipulated to be identical across all three. That's the part messing with me. His whole method up to now has been "isolate the thing, ask whether it's good purely in virtue of what it intrinsically is" — so if two states of consciousness are qualitatively the same, what gives him license to rank them differently based on a fact sitting entirely outside the state itself (whether the object happens to exist)? Feels like a pre-Nozick experience-machine move, kind of like the wireheading debates you get on LW, except he never has to argue for it as its own premise, it just rides in on the back of the "three cases" setup. Is there a standard way of reading this that keeps it consistent with his own method, or is it a genuine crack in the system that Keynes and the Bloomsbury crowd just inherited without noticing? And does it matter for how seriously we should take the Keynes/Bloomsbury "good life = personal affection + beauty" picture if the argument underneath it has this kind of seam in it?
