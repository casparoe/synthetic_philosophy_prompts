I'm drafting a dissertation chapter on higher-order evidence and would like to see the central debate staged as a dialogue, built around a case of my own devising rather than one of the standard textbook examples (the hypoxic pilot, the unmarked clock, and so on).

The case: a postdoc re-checks, by hand, a long multi-step algebraic derivation underlying a key lemma in a paper due at midnight. She works through it twice, at 2 a.m. and again at 3 a.m., and reaches the same stable result both times, so her first-order evidence for the lemma is about as strong as such evidence gets. Just before submitting, she recalls a robust finding from the sleep-deprivation literature: people who have been awake past the eighteen-hour mark make sign and transposition errors in exactly this sort of derivation at a substantially elevated rate, while remaining just as subjectively confident in their (erroneous) results as when rested. She herself has been awake for twenty-one hours.

Please write a dialogue between two epistemologists debating what she should believe about the lemma once this occurs to her. One should defend a level-splitting view, on which she can rationally retain high confidence in the derivation while lowering her confidence that this very confidence is well-supported -- that is, she can be rationally akratic. The other should defend an anti-akrasia ("enkratic") constraint, on which no such combination of attitudes is ever rational, so the higher-order evidence must flow down and lower her first-order confidence too.

I would like the exchange to move beyond simply asserting intuitions. Have the anti-akrasia character raise the bootstrapping worry -- that permitting this sort of split would let the postdoc convert repeated akratic episodes into an a priori track record vindicating her calculating ability even under conditions she has independent reason to distrust -- and give the level-splitter a genuine reply rather than a concession. Then have the level-splitter press the anti-akrasia character on what entitles the anti-akrasia constraint itself to be exempt from the regress that, on the level-splitter's diagnosis, threatens any epistemic rule once higher-order doubt about one's own rule-following is admitted at all. Each character should engage with the specific objection just raised rather than restate their opening position, and the dialogue should not clearly hand the win to either side.
