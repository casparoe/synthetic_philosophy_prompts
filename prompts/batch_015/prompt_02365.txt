A quirk of copyright law has stuck with me ever since a plot overlap with another novelist's book made me nervous: copyright treats independent creation as a complete defense, while patent law does not. If two novelists, each unaware of the other, produced identical manuscripts, neither would infringe the other's copyright — the law asks only whether you copied, not whether your text happens to match someone else's. The standard explanation is purely evidentiary, a workaround for the fact that copying is hard to prove directly. But I keep thinking of Borges's Pierre Menard, who arrives at a few chapters of Don Quixote word for word by his own independent route, and whom the story treats as the author of a different, richer book than Cervantes's despite the identical wording.

Please argue for a stronger claim than the evidentiary one: that copyright's independent-creation rule is tracking something true about what a novel is, rather than just papering over a problem of proof — that a literary work is individuated by the history of its making rather than by its text considered in isolation, so that two verbatim-identical manuscripts could, in full seriousness, be two distinct books.
