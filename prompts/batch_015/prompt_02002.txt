So I fell down a philosophy-of-mind rabbit hole a while back, triggered by yet another one of those AI-consciousness arguments that break out every few months on LessWrong and in ACX comment threads -- you know, "if you replicate a brain's functional organization in silicon the upload is conscious, period," basically an appeal to substrate independence. That kind of claim is leaning hard on functionalism plus multiple realizability: Putnam's old point that pain can't just BE c-fiber firing, since presumably an octopus, a silicon-based martian, or an upload could be in pain too, realized by completely different physical stuff, so mental states have to be picked out by their causal/functional role rather than their physical make-up. Fodor extended the same logic to argue the special sciences generally (not just psychology) are autonomous from physics.

Then I actually went and read Kim's 1992 paper ("Multiple Realization and the Metaphysics of Reduction"), and it turns out he uses almost the same premise to argue for the opposite conclusion. His move (if I'm summarizing it right) is: if pain-in-humans and pain-in-octopi really are realized by totally heterogeneous physical states, then "pain" isn't a single, causally unified kind at all -- its more like jade, which turned out to just be jadeite-or-nephrite with nothing metaphysically interesting binding the two together, and which nobody thinks is a real kind you could base a law on. So Kim says taking multiple realizability seriously should push you toward LOCAL, species-specific reductions (pain-in-humans = c-fiber firing, full stop; pain-in-octopuses = whatever the octopus equivalent is) rather than toward some autonomous, irreducible functional level. Which is a pretty wild dialectical move, since multiple realizability gets taught in every intro phil-of-mind class as THE knockdown argument against reductionism, and here's a serious reductionist using that very same fact to argue for a (weirder, disjunctive) kind of reductionism.

I vaguely remember there being replies on the functionalist side -- something about the functional property being a legit second-order property rather than a mere disjunction, plus some empirical pushback about whether realizers are ever really as heterogeneous as the thought experiments assume -- but I never got to the bottom of who actually wins. Could you write me an essay that lays out Putnam and Fodor's original case, Kim's rebuttal, and the strongest replies to Kim, and then actually commits to a verdict: does multiple realizability support functionalism/non-reductive physicalism the way everyone assumes, or does Kim show that it really cuts the other way? Please don't hedge out with a "reasonable philosophers disagree" ending, I want an actual position, defended.
