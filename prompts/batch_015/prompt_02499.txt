I'm reporting a piece on the "why do the laws of physics look the way they do" beat (fine-tuning, multiverse stuff, all that), and while doing background reading for it I fell down a rabbit hole on a metaphysics fight that apparently sits underneath the whole thing: the Humean vs anti-Humean debate about what a "law of nature" actually is. A friend doing a philosophy PhD walked me through the cartoon version over drinks — Lewis-style Humeans think laws are just whatever regularities come out on top when you look for the best trade-off between simplicity and strength in summarizing the total pattern of particular facts (the "Humean mosaic"), nothing is out there governing anything; Armstrong and co. say no, there's an actual necessitation relation holding between universals, so laws are metaphysically prior to their instances and genuinely produce the pattern rather than just describing it.

What I cant quite get past is that old gold-sphere/uranium-sphere case — there could in principle be a gold sphere a mile wide, it's just absurdly unlikely, whereas a uranium one that size is supposedly ruled out by critical mass, and only the second one feels like a real law rather than an accident. Could you actually walk through how each camp handles that example, and then be honest about who comes out ahead? I vaguely remember Lewis's comeback to Armstrong being something like: naming a relation "necessitation" doesn't actually make it necessitate anything, so positing it doesn't explain the difference, it just relabels the mystery. But then don't Humeans have almost the mirror-image problem — if laws are nothing but a compressed summary of the mosaic, how are they suppose to turn around and explain why the mosaic has that pattern rather than another? I don't want the standard side-by-side textbook description, I want you to actually adjudicate which view, if either, escapes its own version of this circularity.
