Ok so this has kind of been living in my head the last couple weeks. I got into one of those habit tracker apps for a bit (the little boxes you check off for meditating, not snacking, whatever) and somebody on a forum said Ben Franklin was basically doing the same thing 250 years before it was a startup idea, so I actually went and read that chunk of his autobiography instead of just trusting a random comment. And it's true, he's got this list of 13 virtues (temperance, silence, order, resolution, frugality, industry, sincerity, justice, moderation, cleanliness, tranquility, chastity, humility) and he made this little book with a page for each one, ruled into a grid, days of the week across the top, and he'd mark a black dot every time he slipped up. He'd hammer on just one virtue per week so he wasn't spreading himself thin, cycle through all 13 in thirteen weeks, then start the whole course over again, four times a year.

Here's the part that actually stuck with me though. Humility wasn't even on his original list of 12 - a Quaker friend of his (who Franklin never names in the book, kind of funny that the guy who called him out doesn't even get credit) told him flat out that people generally thought he was arrogant, so he bolted Humility onto the end as virtue 13, and the entire definition he gives it is "imitate Jesus and Socrates," which is not exactly a low bar to clear. Then later on he admits he never got anywhere on the actual "reality" of humility, only the "appearance" of it, he trained himself out of saying "certainly" and "undoubtedly" and started hedging everything with "I imagine" or "it appears to me" instead. And then he just casually drops this line near the end that even if he'd totally beaten pride, he'd probably end up being proud of his humility. Theres also this bit earlier where he compares himself to a guy who wants his axe polished but gives up halfway and decides he actually likes it "speckled," which reads like its meant as a defense of the whole imperfect-effort approach, even though it originally has nothing to do with the humility problem specifically.

I can't decide if that crack in the humility case means the whole checklist system has a hole running through it, like maybe you can drill yourself into behaving a certain way without ever actually becoming that kind of person, and that gap just happens to be more obvious with humility than with something like frugality, since grading yourself on how humble you're getting seems to feed the exact thing you're trying to starve. Could you write me a dialogue between Franklin and that unnamed Quaker friend (give him a name, Franklin never bothered to) where the friend presses him on exactly this, not just the specific humility paradox, but the bigger question of whether you can bookkeep your way into real virtue at all or whether the method can only ever train up a convincing performance of it? I want Franklin to actually defend himself well instead of just folding, he seems like way too sharp a guy to not have a real answer. Make it a genuine back and forth where they're both landing real points and responding to what the other actually just said, not two guys taking turns giving prepared speeches.
