Brouwer presented his intuitionism as a direct continuation of Kant's philosophy of mathematics, though a continuation that quietly amputates half of Kant's doctrine of pure intuition. Kant had grounded arithmetic in the a priori intuition of time and geometry in the a priori intuition of space; once non-Euclidean geometries gained wide acceptance over the course of the nineteenth century and made it doubtful that Euclidean structure could be a necessary precondition of any possible experience, Brouwer's 1907 dissertation dropped the apriority of space altogether while doubling down on the apriority of time as the sole source of all mathematical construction, arithmetical and geometrical alike. From that single temporal root he then built an entire revisionary logic: unrestricted use of the law of excluded middle was rejected for statements about infinite domains, classical existence proofs by contradiction were declared worthless unless they could be turned into constructions, and genuinely new kinds of mathematical object, such as free-choice sequences with no classical counterpart, were admitted. Heyting later gave the resulting system a formal logical calculus, and decades afterward the Curry-Howard correspondence showed that intuitionistic proofs could be read as nothing but typed computer programs, an idea now built directly into proof assistants such as Coq, Agda, and Lean.

I've been turning this lineage over since sitting in on a philosophy of mathematics seminar that spent a week closely on Kant's Transcendental Aesthetic and then simply jumped to Brouwer the following week, treating the inheritance as self-evident rather than examining where it strains. That has left me wanting to know what Kant himself, confronted with the whole trajectory from Brouwer's dissertation to a proof checked today by machine, would actually make of it: a legitimate unfolding of his own critical philosophy, or a system that betrays it at crucial points while borrowing its vocabulary?

I'm especially interested in how you think Kant would resolve three specific tensions, rather than a general verdict. First, whether space and time can be pulled apart the way Brouwer wants, given how tightly the Transcendental Aesthetic binds outer and inner sense together, or whether keeping time's apriority while discarding space's is already an incoherent move within Kant's own architecture. Second, whether he could sanction the demotion of the law of excluded middle for infinite totalities, given that in the Critique's second preface he insists that logic had not advanced a single step since Aristotle and was, to all appearances, already closed and complete, with no revision pending. Third, what he would make of the idea, unthinkable in his own century, that a mathematical construction carried out in pure intuition could be executed as a computer program with no sensible subject behind it at all. Please work out what you take to be Kant's most defensible position on each of these three points, rather than simply observing that he predates the debate.
