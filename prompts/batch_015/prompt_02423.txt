So I'm about a third of the way into a novel that has a big scene set in a Tibetan monastic debate courtyard (the kind where the monks clap and stamp to punctuate their logical moves), and I really don't want the philosophy in that scene to be decorative wallpaper — I want the argument my two monk characters are having to actually hold together. For research I've been sitting in on a little Wednesday night reading group at the zen center near me thats been slowly working through Nagarjuna for months, and last week two of the regulars nearly came to blows (verbally) over something that sounds small but apparently isn't. Dev, whos ABD in logic, and Roz, who did her MA thesis partly on Candrakirti, got into a real argument about the catuskoti, the "four corners" move where Nagarjuna rejects a claim as true, as false, as both, and as neither, and it kept going long after the group officially ended, so I wrote most of it down after.

It went roughly like this. (1) Dev opened by saying Nagarjuna is basically a proto-dialetheist, in the spirit of the Garfield and Priest reading: he's not just rejecting all four corners as some kind of rhetorical throat-clearing, he sometimes positively affirms the "both" corner too, like in the verse where he says something along the lines of everything being real and not real, both, and neither, and calls it the actual teaching, and since he also explicitly negates "neither" elsewhere, you get a flat contradiction that no amount of charitable reading should be allowed to erase. (2) Roz came back with the two-kinds-of-negation point, which she said goes back to a guy named Kajiyama and gets used heavily by Jan Westerhoff: most of Nagarjuna's "nots" in the tetralemma are the non-implicative kind, meaning they don't commit you to the opposite, they just refuse to let the predicate apply at all, kind of like how "the present king of France is neither bald nor not bald" doesn't break the law of excluded middle, it just rejects a broken presupposition — so "neither existent nor nonexistent" isn't asserting "not (P or not-P)" in the classical sense at all. (3) Dev granted that this rescues the "neither" corner but said it does nothing for the "both" corner, since a conjunction isn't a negation, there's nothing left to reinterpret, and if Nagarjuna is willing to positively assert "P and not-P" somewhere, that's a straightforward true contradiction, full stop, no scope tricks available. (4) Roz said that verse isn't one univocal assertion, it's shorthand for "true conventionally, false ultimately," the two truths doing their usual job, compressed into one line for effect, so it's equivocation across levels, not a genuine contradiction. (5) Dev said that move is awfully convenient given nothing in the verse itself marks which half goes with which truth, and that scholars reaching for the two truths exactly when consistency needs saving looks like special pleading, whereas his reading needs no rescue at all, since blowing up consistency at the limit is the whole point of running reductio on every view, including Madhyamaka's own. (6) Roz's last word was that reductio-ing every corner shows the target is conceptual proliferation itself, meaning silence, not some secretly true double claim, and that even Garfield and Priest, when you read them carefully, only ever cash out the "true contradiction" in one very narrow spot, something to do with emptiness applying to itself, rather than handing Dev a general license to assert P and not-P wherever it's convenient.

Nobody budged and I honestly couldn't tell you who came out ahead. Could you go through it move by move and say who had the better of each individual exchange, not just declare an overall winner, and flag anything you think both of them are missing? And selfishly, if you had to put one of these positions in the mouth of the "wins the debate" monk in my actual novel scene, which one would hold up better?
