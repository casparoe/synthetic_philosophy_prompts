A friend of mine, Priya, and I had a long argument last weekend that I still haven't been able to let go of. On the drive back from a concert, she spent a good ten minutes insisting that a particular record is the best thing a saxophonist we both admire has ever put out — even though she has never actually listened to it herself. Her source was a critic whose taste she trusts completely and whose past recommendations have never steered her wrong. She delivered this verdict with exactly the same flat confidence she'd use to tell me what time the venue closes, or that the drummer on stage that night used to play in a band we both like. When I asked what actually made the record so good, all she could offer was that the phrasing on the title track is apparently extraordinary — which she was, again, just relaying secondhand.

I told her this seemed different in kind from trusting her about the venue's closing time: she's welcome to let someone else's word stand in for a fact she hasn't checked, but it struck me as odd for her to let someone else's word stand in for a judgment about beauty or excellence, as though that judgment could simply be handed over fully formed. She thought I was inventing a distinction that didn't exist. Her position was that we defer constantly to people with sharper palates, ears, or eyes than our own — in wine, in cooking, even in mathematics — and nobody thinks that makes the resulting beliefs any less truly their own, or any less justified. When I tried to insist that you have to actually encounter a work yourself before you're entitled to a view about it, I couldn't explain why the same requirement wouldn't apply equally to taking a doctor's word for a diagnosis you can't verify, or a physicist's word for a result you couldn't derive yourself. We went back and forth for the better part of an hour and neither of us budged.

I'd genuinely like an outside take on this, since it's been nagging at me ever since. Was I onto something real about why adopting someone else's verdict on a piece of art feels different from adopting their word on a plain matter of fact, even holding the reliability of the source fixed across both cases — or was Priya right that this is a distinction without a difference, and my resistance was just squeamishness dressed up as principle? And practically speaking, was there anything actually wrong with Priya stating a view on a record she'd never heard as though the matter were settled, or is that simply how judgments of taste ordinarily get passed between people?
