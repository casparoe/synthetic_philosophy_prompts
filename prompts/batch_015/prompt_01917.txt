I've been working through Alan Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" on my own — I have no formal training in philosophy or computer science, just a longstanding interest — and I've gotten stuck on a part of it that I rarely see discussed: objection (9) in his list of counterarguments, "The Argument from Extra-Sensory Perception."

Turing doesn't raise this as a throwaway joke. He states plainly that "the statistical evidence, at least for telepathy, is overwhelming," and adds that if this is accepted, the assumption that our bodies obey only the known laws of physics would be among the first things we'd have to give up — which seems to threaten the mechanistic picture of thinking that the rest of the paper relies on. He even grants that, on its own terms, the objection is a strong one. Yet the actual remedy he offers is narrow and almost logistical: put the two contestants in a sealed, telepathy-proof room so that a psychically gifted human can't get an unfair advantage in the game.

I can't work out how that remedy is meant to relate to the larger worry he has just raised. Sealing off the room protects the fairness of the test as a game, but it doesn't seem to touch his own admission that, if ESP is real, bodies and minds might not run on known physical law alone, which would undercut the deeper claim that thinking is simply a kind of mechanical process. Is Turing quietly separating the question of whether the test is fair from the question of whether his whole approach is even correct, and only bothering to answer the first one? Or is there a more charitable reading of the passage in which the telepathy-proof room is doing more philosophical work than it appears to be? I'd appreciate help reconstructing the actual structure of his reasoning in this section.
