So we just finished the Hart–Devlin unit in my jurisprudence course (Wolfenden Report, Devlin's "Enforcement of Morals" lecture, Hart's reply in "Law, Liberty and Morality") and our seminar leader threw R v Brown at us as the "now apply it to a real case" exercise. Last night two people in my study group, Theo and Nadia, basically hijacked what was supposed to be a quick pre-exam review session arguing about whether Brown was rightly decided, and it want on long enough that I wrote down the gist of it, because I honestly can't tell who's ahead. Neither of them is weak on this stuff, they'd both actually done the reading, so I'd rather get an outside read then just side with whoever talked louder.

Here's roughly how it went, numbered so it's easier to point at:

(1) Theo opened with: Brown was correctly decided, and Templeman's line about society needing to protect itself against a "cult of violence" is basically Devlin's disintegration thesis wearing a wig. Even though the acts were consensual and totally private, a society is partly constituted by its shared morality, so tolerating something the reasonable-man/jury-of-twelve standard finds seriously depraved isn't a neutral non-event, it changes what kind of society this is, and a society gets to defend that the same way it defends its borders.

(2) Nadia hit back with Hart's actual reply to Devlin: that slides from "some shared morality is needed for a society to survive at all" (true, but already covered by the harm principle re violence, broken promises, etc.) to "a society just is its current morality, so any change equals its destruction" — which Hart said would license criminalizing literally any minority practice, down to every "jot and tittle" of the moral code, and which Devlin never actually backed with any evidence that a society disintegrated from tolerating stuff like this.

(3) Theo said fine, drop the crude version, but Nadia's skipping the more careful part of the majority's reasoning: Jauncey and Lowry weren't just being squeamish, they specifically worried about the thing spreading — the "corruption of young men" language, the fuss over the videotapes existing at all. That's not "any deviation equals collapse," it's a narrower worry about a culture of escalating violence normalizing itself outward.

(4) Nadia said that move quietly smuggles in the harm principle through the back door: worrying about who might get drawn in without fully consenting, or about future/unwilling participants, is exactly Hart's (and Mill's) principle, not Devlin's — Devlin's whole point was that the law can act even with zero identifiable victim. So if that's actually doing the work now, Theo's defending Hart's framework, not Devlin's.

(5) Theo pivoted to paternalism: Slynn's dissent raises it and punts it to Parliament, but Hart himself, unlike a strict Millian, accepted a fair bit of paternalism. So maybe the majority doesn't need Devlin at all — the state can stop you consenting to escalating, severe, maybe-not-fully-voluntary-in-the-moment bodily harm, full stop, no shared-morality argument required.

(6) Nadia said that's not a rescue of his original claim, it's a full retreat: a paternalism-only justification is a totally different principle from legal moralism, and it comes with its own bill to pay (why is boxing legal, then, or extreme fasting, or any number of dangerous-but-consensual hobbies?), so Theo can't just borrow Devlin's argument's credibility for a completely different argument.

I can't work out who's actually winning at each step, they both seemed to land real points. End with your overall call on its own line, exactly like this: \verdict{Theo}, \verdict{Nadia}, or \verdict{Draw}.
