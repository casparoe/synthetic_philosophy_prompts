Quick one from left field — I'm poking at a piece on the "should a thinker's ugly politics change how we read their actual work" question, and Frege turns out to be the strangest possible case study. Background in case its useful: the guy basically invented modern logic and spent his career trying to reduce arithmetic to it, the picture of dry, bloodless rationality — except in 1924, about a year before he died, he kept a private diary that's viciously antisemitic and anti-democratic, wanting Jews stripped of civil rights and pushed out of Germany, cheering on the far-right völkisch scene of the day. Dummett, who probably did more than anyone to revive Frege's reputation, apparently read the diary in manuscript back in the 1950s, decades before it was actually published, and has said it genuinely shook him, since he'd always taken Frege for a kind of pure-rationality machine. A friend doing a phil PhD tipped me off that there's a whole new book (I think its literally called Frege and Fascism) arguing this isn't just a sad footnote but actually continuous with the logic.

I tried sketching my own version of that "continuous with the logic" argument for the piece, and now I honestly can't tell if I'm onto something or just forcing two unrelated obsessions together because they rhyme nicely. Here's the paragraph:

"Frege's whole logical program rested on concepts having sharp, decidable boundaries: for any object whatever, a legitimate concept must say yes or no, with nothing left undecided — a predicate that leaves borderline cases hanging isn't really a concept at all, just fog wearing the costume of thought. Now set that next to what he spends real energy on in the diary: how you'd actually write a law that keeps Jews out of civic life, given that ancestry comes in fractions and generations and mixed marriages blur every line you try to draw. It's the same demand, relocated: reality, including a population of human beings, has to be carve-able so that every case comes out decided, no remainder left over. A decade later, Nazi bureaucrats would drown in exactly that problem while defining their 'Mischling' categories. Frege, it seems, wanted from politics the same tidiness he demanded from logic."

Does that actually hold up as an argument, or am I just yoking two unrelated things together because they sound neat side by side? And more broadly — is "which part of the philosophy caused the politics" even the right question here, versus just "a rigorous, brilliant man also happened, for reasons that have nothing to do with his rigor, to be a bigot"? Brutal honesty welcome, including "drop this angle entirely" — I'd rather know that now than after I've filed it.
