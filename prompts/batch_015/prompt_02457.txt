I'm taking a graduate course in cooperative game theory, and we recently compared Nash's bargaining solution with the Kalai–Smorodinsky (KS) solution that Kalai and Smorodinsky proposed in 1975 as a rival. Both solutions satisfy Pareto optimality, symmetry, and invariance to positive affine transformations of utility (scale invariance). Where they part ways is on a fourth axiom: Nash keeps independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA), while KS replaces it with individual monotonicity — roughly, if a player's best possible outcome improves for every payoff level the other player might receive, while the other player's own opportunities don't get worse, that player's payoff under the solution should not decrease. Nash's solution can actually violate this monotonicity property, which is exactly why Kalai and Smorodinsky introduced their alternative in the first place, but my lecture notes just present this as a technical fact about the two solutions rather than making a case for why you'd want one axiom over the other.

I'd like you to bring this disagreement to life as a dialogue between two equally capable bargaining theorists — one committed to the Nash solution, the other to the KS solution — who have been brought in to advise on a long-running royalty-sharing arrangement between a record label and an artist. A new licensing opportunity has come up that would substantially raise the artist's revenue if it were allocated to them entirely, without changing what the label could earn in its own best-case scenario. Asked whether the existing arrangement should be revisited in light of this, the two theorists disagree sharply: the Nash advocate argues that IIA gives no principled reason to move away from the existing split just because new, previously unchosen options have appeared, while the KS advocate insists that ignoring the artist's improved prospects amounts to ignoring exactly the kind of consideration their monotonicity axiom was designed to protect.

Please write this as a genuine back-and-forth rather than two parallel speeches: each theorist should give the strongest version of their own axiom's justification (for instance, IIA as a rationality or consistency constraint with parallels elsewhere in choice theory, versus monotonicity as capturing something about fairness or bargaining power that IIA misses) and should respond to the specific objections the other raises rather than restating their own position. Somewhere in the exchange, have them acknowledge the cases in which the two solutions actually coincide, so it's clear exactly where the disagreement bites, and have them work out whether their dispute is forced by the mathematics, a genuine normative disagreement about what a bargaining solution is supposed to represent, or something that dissolves once each is explicit about what they take bargaining theory to be for. Aim for roughly 700–900 words.
