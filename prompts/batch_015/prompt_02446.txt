For a feature on the philosophical assumptions buried inside tools like cost-benefit analysis and social welfare functions, I've traced the modern debate over "interpersonal utility comparisons" back to a passage I can't quite untangle on my own. It's from William Stanley Jevons's The Theory of Political Economy (1871), in the section of the Introduction headed "Measurement of Feeling and Motives":

"The reader will find, again, that there is never, in any single instance, an attempt made to compare the amount of feeling in one mind with that in another. I see no means by which such comparison can be accomplished. The susceptibility of one mind may, for what we know, be a thousand times greater than that of another. But, provided that the susceptibility was different in a like ratio in all directions, we should never be able to discover the difference. Every mind is thus inscrutable to every other mind, and no common denominator of feeling seems to be possible. But even if we could compare the feelings of different minds, we should not need to do so; for one mind only affects another indirectly. Every event in the outward world is represented in the mind by a corresponding motive, and it is by the balance of these that the will is swayed. But the motive in one mind is weighed only against other motives in the same mind, never against the motives in other minds. Each person is to other persons a portion of the outward world—the non-ego as the metaphysicians call it. Thus motives in the mind of A may give rise to phenomena which may be represented by motives in the mind of B; but between A and B there is a gulf. Hence the weighing of motives must always be confined to the bosom of the individual."

What stops me is the very next paragraph, where he adds: "though the theory presumes to investigate the condition of a mind, and bases upon this investigation the whole of Economics, practically it is an aggregate of individuals which will be treated. The general forms of the laws of Economics are the same in the case of individuals and nations."

Could you help me work out what's actually being argued in the first passage, and whether it holds together? Jevons seems to slide between at least two distinct grounds for denying interpersonal comparison — that there is no instrument or "common denominator" for measuring feeling across minds, and that such a comparison, even if it were available, simply would not be needed — and it isn't clear whether the second is meant to rescue the argument if the first fails, or whether both are supposed to support the same conclusion independently. I would also find it useful to know whether his move, one paragraph later, to treating economics as a science of "aggregates" and "nations" quietly reintroduces the very cross-mind weighing he has just ruled out, and how this particular knot relates to the later split between Robbins's flat rejection of interpersonal comparison and the attempts by economists like Harsanyi and Sen to rehabilitate it as a normative rather than empirical premise.
