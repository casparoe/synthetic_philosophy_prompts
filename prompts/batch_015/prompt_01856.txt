I sit on my state bar's character-and-fitness committee, which certifies that applicants possess the "good moral character" required for admission to practice law, largely on the strength of reference letters and a clean record. We recently split over a file: an applicant with a decade of spotless references had, on a single occasion years earlier, backdated a filing to meet a deadline at a previous job. A colleague sent me some of the situationist literature on virtue ethics afterward, arguing that cases like this are exactly what we should expect once we see how thin the evidence for stable "global" character traits really is — and now I can't tell whether our whole procedure rests on a philosophical mistake.

Write a dialogue between a virtue ethicist and a situationist brought in to advise the committee on this case. Have the situationist build their argument on specific evidence — such as Doris's distinction between local and global character traits, and studies like Isen and Levin's "dime in the phone booth" experiment and Milgram's obedience experiments — and have the virtue ethicist respond with equal rigor, for instance by pressing, as Kamtekar does, that these studies target a much thinner notion of character than the one built into a genuinely Aristotelian account centered on practical wisdom. The two should engage directly with each other's arguments rather than restating their own positions, and the dialogue should end without either side simply conceding the point.
