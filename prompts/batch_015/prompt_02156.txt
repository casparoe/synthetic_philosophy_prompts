I'm a retired physicist (spent about thirty five years doing optics research) and now that I'm retired I've finally been letting myself read all the philosophy and history-of-science stuff I never had time for during my career. Lately I've been slowly working through Galileo's 1615 "Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina," which I'd always heard described as basically the "Bible teaches how to go to heaven, not how the heavens go" essay and not much else. But near the end he does something I really wasn't expecting: he takes on the actual passage in the Book of Joshua where Joshua commands the sun to stand still so the Israelites can finish off the enemy in daylight, and argues that the miracle actually fits *better* with Copernicus than with Ptolemy. Here's the bit that's got me stuck (I'm working from an old, somewhat clunky translation I found, so please excuse the stilted phrasing, and I've trimmed it down a little):

"Now if the words of Joshua are to be taken in their bare and proper sense, it must be understood that he commanded the sun to speed up its own particular motion -- that slow yearly creeping along the zodiac which is proper to the sun itself, quite apart from the great daily sweep that carries the whole sky from east to west. And to lengthen the day by such means, this proper motion of the sun's would have to be hastened until it went as fast as the diurnal motion of the primum mobile -- that is to say, increased some three hundred and sixty-fold beyond its ordinary pace. But I do not think any sober man supposes this to have been Joshua's meaning, since he spoke to a people who knew nothing of the sun's proper motion at all, but only of the single common movement of the whole heaven, and he wished only that they should grasp the greatness of the miracle, not be instructed in the frame of the spheres.

For this reason many grave doctors -- Dionysius the Areopagite among them, and St. Augustine following him -- have held that it was not the sun alone that stood still, but the primum mobile itself, so that all the orbs it carries stood still along with it. Now let us place the sun, as Copernicus does, fixed at the center of the planets, yet not wholly without motion, but turning very slowly upon its own axis, as I take myself to have shown from the spots observed upon its body; and let us suppose further -- since the sun is in a manner the heart of the world -- that this turning of the sun's is the very fountain from which the motions of all the other planets flow, even as the motion of the heart sends motion into all the limbs of an animal. If this be granted, then to arrest the sun's turning is to arrest at a single stroke the turning of the whole system, and the day is lengthened upon the earth by the stopping of one sole and simple motion, without the least disorder introduced among the mutual positions of the heavenly bodies. Which of these two suppositions sits more easily with so great a miracle, I leave to Your Highness's own good judgment."

Two things about this have got me stuck. First, the physical picture: why does Galileo think that stopping the sun's own spin would stop the earth (and everything else) from moving too? He seems to be leaning on this "sun is the heart of the world, pumping motion into the planets" idea rather then anything like a force in the modern sense -- is that just rhetorical flourish on his part, or is he gesturing at something more substantive, maybe something that later thinkers like Kepler picked up and ran with? Second, and this is the part that really bugs me: earlier in that same letter Galileo is completely insistent that Scripture wasn't written to teach natural philosophy, that its wording is accommodated to ordinary people and isn't meant to be read literally on physical questions -- so why does he then spend all this effort arguing that the literal words of Joshua fit Copernicus better than they fit Ptolemy? Isn't he basically conceding that literal fit matters after all, which seems to undercut his own accommodation doctrine from earlier in the letter? Or is there a way of reading this passage where he isn't actually contradicting himself, like some kind of "even granting your own rules, I still win" move? Would appreciate a hand untangling this, it's been bugging me for days.
